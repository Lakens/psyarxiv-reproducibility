Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Sleep disorders are prevalent among psychiatric patients and are strongly associated with neurobiological, psychological, and behavioral processes. This chapter analyzes neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE), an approach incorporating the insights gained from neuroscience with patient education to understand and manage sleep disturbances better. To establish a scientific basis for psychoeducational interventions, we discuss central mechanisms of sleep regulation such as circadian rhythms, sleep homeostasis, neurotransmitter systems, and brain networks. It illustrates how bidirectional sleep disorders and psychiatric syndromes, including depression, anxiety, PTSD, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), are linked, and explains sleep disturbance as a transdiagnostic key clinical feature. In addition to cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, NIPE is used to provide a solution to insomnia by integrating mindfulness-based strategies and personalized interventions with the assistance of digital technologies. Implementation challenges and outcome assessment, as well as ethical dimensions of practice, are also considered. In fact, NIPE is a clinically relevant strategy that can both maximize patient engagement and ameliorate sleep disorder outcomes.

1. Introduction

Neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE) is a niche approach that synthesizes neuroscience with pedagogical modalities designed to improve patient comprehension and treatment outcomes for psychiatric disorders. Due to such complex neurobiological bases and multisystem psychosocial factors, this method attracted attention from the field of psychiatry, especially sleep disorders. NIPE is designed to enable patients to know about the neural substrate of sleep to promote self-efficacy and engage in therapeutic interventions (Ekhtiari, Rezapour, Aupperle, & Paulus, 2017).

1.1. Sleep Disturbance as a Transdiagnostic Feature of Psychiatric Disorders

From a neuroscience point of view, the phenomenon of the sleep disorder is one that is closely related to brain function and a person's sleep regulation. The whole human sleep cycle has its structure in play and is influenced by genes, cells and molecules, neurotransmitters, circadian rhythms, and certain neuroanatomical areas including the hypothalamus and brainstem. Studies suggest that some sleep-related disorders from malfunctioning of these systems may be common and include insomnia, sleep apnea, and restless legs syndrome. NIPE aims to translate these intricate biological phenomena to easily understood, digestible bits of information for patients. When a clinician translates science into terms and principles that an audience can understand and connect, it can help make disorders grounded in neurobiology less alienating, reducing the overwhelming challenges that many people in sleep science face when trying to understand them (Edinger et al., 2021; Gumport, Gasperetti, Zieve, & Harvey, 2023).

1.2. Therapeutic Value of Neurobiological Understanding

The application of NIPE is more than communication of information, as it has therapeutic significance too. Patients who understand the biological basis of their sleep disorders are more likely to appreciate the value of continued compliance with routine therapy and making necessary lifestyle changes in order to achieve sound sleep health (Kryza-Lacombe, Richards, Hansen, & Goldin, 2021). For example, knowledge about melatonin's role in the sleep-wake cycle or recognizing the impacts of stress on neurochemistry can help patients to have better sleep hygiene and decrease anxiety concerning their sleep problems. This educational context in turn seeks to integrate clinical and patient insights to generate the therapeutic alliance grounded in shared knowledge (Luca & Peris, 2020).

1.3. Psychoeducation as a Driver of Treatment Engagement

Prospective use of NIPE in clinical practice could not only lead to better treatment outcomes in sleep disorders, but also in psychiatric conditions. Increased patient knowledge has been linked to higher motivation for behavioral treatments, including cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) and other non-pharmacological treatments (Morin & Buysse, 2024; Peters, Saunders, & Jackson, 2022). Studies have illustrated that when the reasons for these interventions are well-founded and rooted in sound neuroscientific principles, it can positively influence the acceptance of these interventions with active participation and therapeutic adherence. Especially in the context of managing chronic sleep disorders, where involvement and self-regulation are key to sustained improvement (Riemann et al., 2022; Sun & Wu, 2023).

1.4. Integrating Sleep and Mental Health Perspectives

With the integration in psychiatric settings of NIPE for sleep disorders, it not only promotes knowledge to the patients but also encourages a holistic understanding of mental health. Sleep and mood disorders are often interrelated; thus, addressing sleep from a neurobiological standpoint can unveil the reciprocal influences of sleep quality on psychological wellbeing. Through understanding these relationships in more detail, clinicians can better tailor treatment approaches, enhancing both sleep and overall psychiatric outcomes (Bersani et al., 2017).

Hence, NIPE plays a key role in the management of sleep disorders. Such a comprehensive approach to patient education aligns with current psychiatric practices that prioritize patient involvement in their care (Khawaja et al., 2013). With progress ongoing, the incorporation of neuroscientific principles within psychoeducational frameworks is likely to significantly contribute to the enhanced understanding and management of sleep disorders and therefore enhance patient outcomes and quality of life (Wong et al., 2017).

1.5. Rationale for a Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducational Approach

Sleep is a multifaceted physiological process that has been intimately associated with numerous neurobiological pathways. Dysregulation of these processes is implicated in the pathogenesis and maintenance of numerous sleep disorder states. To provide insight into its interplay, it is paramount to delve into the biology behind sleep, in particular neural pathways, neurotransmitter systems and hormones.

Human brain sleep-wake circuitry is at the heart of sleep management and it is typically driven by two systems of neural circuits involved in the central nervous system (CNS). The homeostatic component illustrates that sleep is required because we have been awake previously, largely via adenosine availability; as waking frequency rises, adenosine accumulates, fueling sleepiness. In contrast, the production of adenosine will diminish during sleep illustrating a physiological feedback loop that is essential for the regulation of sleep homeostasis (Freeman, Sheaves, Waite, Harvey, & Harrison, 2020; Meyer et al., 2024).

The circadian rhythm, which is regulated in the hypothalamus via the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), controls the timing of sleep and wake according to physical or environmental signals. Such circadian disruptions may precipitate or worsen sleep disorders including insomnia and circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders (Meyer et al., 2024). Melatonin, a hormone secreted by the pineal gland in reaction to darkness, is essential for controlling circadian rhythms and indicates that sleep is imminent. Lifestyle-related dysregulation of melatonin production, as well as exposure to artificial lighting, has a significant impact on sleep onset and quality (Moon, Kim, Partonen, & Linnaranta, 2022).

Neurotransmitter systems are equally central to sleep regulation. GABA (gammaaminobutyric acid), the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter, facilitates the transition into sleep by reducing neuronal excitability. In contrast, neurotransmitters like norepinephrine and serotonin are more active during wakefulness and exhibit reduced levels during sleep . In some sleep disorders, this equilibrium is altered and a marked reduction in GABAergic activity has been seen in patients with insomnia, suggesting a biological substrate for the hyperarousal state often reported in these individuals (Li et al., 2025; Siegel, 2004).

2. Neurobiological Foundations of Sleep and Psychiatry

>Neurobiological Foundations of Sleep and Psychiatry

2.1. GABAergic Mechanisms and Sleep Initiation

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), one of the key neurotransmitters involved in sleep regulation. GABA, the key inhibitory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system, is implicated in initiation and maintenance of sleep. It works on GABA receptors scattered across the brain including the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) of the hypothalamus important to start sleep. Activation of the VLPO leads to increased GABAergic signaling that inhibits wake-promoting neuropeptides, thus facilitating sleep (Oishi, Saito, & Sakurai, 2023).

2.2. Serotonergic Contributions to Sleep Regulation

Serotonin, another important neurotransmitter implicated in sleep regulation, is predominantly synthesized in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem. Serotonin levels exhibit a diurnal variation, peak during daytime, and decrease at night, thereby influencing the sleepwake cycle. Serotonin acts during sleep initiation as well but is also involved in the regulation of many cycles of sleep including stabilization of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep (Hyndych, Koval, Dzeruzhynska, & Mader, 2025; Vaseghi et al., 2022).

2.3. Norepinephrine and the Regulation of Arousal Systems

Norepinephrine, secreted mainly from the locus coeruleus in the brainstem, is also critical for sleep architecture. Notably, norepinephrine levels are elevated during wakefulness and decrease significantly during sleep. This neurotransmitter plays a role in attention and alertness and its inhibition is associated with the process of REM sleep switch. The antagonistic link between norepinephrine and GABA, their reciprocal inhibition, plays a pivotal role in regulating the balance of wakefulness and sleep (Fernandes, Lam, Park, Drummond, & Sur, 2025; Nassar, 2024).

2.4. Adenosine and Homeostatic Sleep Pressure

In addition, the deposition of adenosine in the basal forebrain during wakefulness acts as the homeostatic impetus for sleep. Adenosine receptors when stimulated encourage sleepiness and sleep initiation. Adenosine builds up in prolonged wakefulness because a body needs to recover and restore after activity, as the prediction states sleep fulfills a restorative role (Huang et al., 2024; Reichert, Deboer, & Landolt, 2022).

2.5. Hypothalamic and Circadian Control of Sleep

Besides neurotransmitters various brain structures also play a big role in controlling sleep. The hypothalamus consists of several nuclei of the brain critical for sleep regulation, the most significant of which is the VLPO, which activates sleep-stimulating pathways, and the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), which orchestrates the circadian rhythm and time to sleep and synchronizes the internal timing of sleep with environmental light/dark cycles. The SCN serves as the master circadian clock, responding to light incoming from the retina, affecting when sleep and wakefulness should occur (Adamantidis & de Lecea, 2023; Leal, Carvalhas-Almeida, Álvaro, & Cavadas, 2024).

2.6. Thalamocortical Dynamics and Non-REM Sleep

Thalamocortical Dynamics and Non-REM Sleep. Another key mediator of sleep regulation, especially during non-REM sleep, is the thalamus. It participates in the synchronization of cortical activity and processing of sensory information throughout sleep (Lambert et al., 2022).

2.7. Integrative Neurobiology of Sleep Regulation

This interaction between these neurotransmitters and brain structures collectively demonstrates that sleep regulation is complex. Such concepts provide a basis for future neuroscience-informed psychoeducational interventions to treat sleep disorders. With some understanding of the nervous system's mechanisms, mental health workers can be better able to help guide patients in their sleep and help them adopt healthier patterns and behaviors (Anih, Yakubu, Arowora, Abah, & Chinekwu, 2025). The integration of neuroscience into psychoeducation for sleep disorders requires a solid theoretical framework to inform practice. Several theoretical perspectives help to provide a basis for an understanding of how NIPE can improve patient outcomes. This section then explains the major frameworks that form the foundation of this integration and discusses their implications.

2.8. Translating Neurobiology into Psychoeducational Frameworks

Neurobiological mechanisms of sleep include the involvement of various neurotransmitters, neuropeptides, and brain structures as part of sleep responses and their regulation. GABA and melatonin are important in encouraging sleep, for example, while norepinephrine and cortisol both contribute to being awake. Research indicates that dysfunctional neurochemical systems can precipitate sleep disorders such as insomnia and hypersomnia (Harvey, 2022). This knowledge reinforces the importance of psychoeducation which includes biological underpinnings in order to enable a more holistic understanding of sleep disorders at the patient level.

3. Defining Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation

>Defining Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation

3.1. Conceptual Role of Psychoeducation in Sleep Medicine

Psychoeducation works by equipping patients with knowledge about their conditions, empowering them to engage in healthier sleep behaviors and making informed choices regarding treatment options. Research has suggested that patients who understand the neurobiological basis of their sleep issues may experience greater efficacy from cognitivebehavioral therapies (CBT) and pharmacological interventions, as they can better contextualize their experiences and anticipate therapeutic outcomes (Nielson, Perez, Soto, Boyle, & Dzierzewski, 2023; Soalheiro et al., 2025; Ugwuanyi & Formella, 2024). Thus, integrating neuroscience into psychoeducational interventions could enhance adherence to treatment regimens and support patients in their journey towards improved sleep health.

3.2. Core Components of a NIPE Framework

In practice, implementing a NIPE framework involves several key components. Firstly, healthcare providers must utilize accessible language to explain complex neurobiological concepts to patients, avoiding excessive jargon while ensuring that essential information is conveyed. Visual aids, such as diagrams depicting sleep cycles or the neurochemical processes involved in sleep regulation, can facilitate understanding. Moreover, psychoeducational sessions can be complemented with interactive elements such as case discussions and role-playing scenarios, which may aid in the retention of information (Rezapour, McLean, Psederska, Chokshi, et al., 2025; Rezapour, McLean, Psederska, Maleki, et al., 2025; Takizawa et al., 2022).

3.3. Group-Based Psychoeducation and Social Learning

Furthermore, research supports the idea that group psychoeducation sessions can be particularly beneficial, as they foster social support and allow individuals to share their experiences, thereby normalizing their struggles with sleep disorders (Baourda et al., 2022). Group dynamics can also facilitate a deeper understanding of the neurobiological effects of sleep through shared insights. Therefore, the incorporation of neuroscientific principles into these sessions can lead to a more empathetic approach to sleep health, ultimately benefiting individuals suffering from sleep disorders.

3.4. Evidence Supporting Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducational Interventions

Several studies have indicated that psychoeducation interventions that incorporate neuroscientific data may improve treatment outcomes. For example, a systematic review by Bai et al. (2024) demonstrated that patients receiving digital psychoeducation alongside standard treatment for insomnia exhibited significant reductions in symptom severity compared to those who received treatment alone (Bai, Cao, Zhang, Liu, & Yin, 2024). These findings reinforce the premise that a thorough grasp of the neurobiological drivers of sleep can enhance both the understanding of one's condition and the overall efficacy of therapeutic interventions.

3.5 Practical Challenges and Implementation Considerations

While the integration of neuroscience into psychoeducation presents numerous benefits, challenges remain. It is essential to assess the individual needs and learning preferences of patients and to modify educational interventions accordingly. Furthermore, the rapid evolution of neuroscientific research necessitates that healthcare providers stay abreast of emerging findings to ensure that the psychoeducational material remains current and relevant (Higgins et al., 2022). Such continuous professional development fosters an environment where patients can engage in informed discussions about their health, thus promoting a patient-centered approach to sleep disorder management., The understanding and treatment of sleep disorders have evolved significantly from ancient interpretations of sleep phenomena to contemporary neuroscience-informed frameworks (Levenson & Williamson, 2023).

3.6. Emergence of Modern Sleep Medicine

The establishment of sleep medicine as a distinct field occurred alongside advances in neurology and psychology in the 20th century. Notably, it was the discovery of REM (rapid eye movement) sleep by Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman in the 1950s that provided a pivotal understanding of sleep stages and their significance to health (Schulz, 2022). This discovery propelled a wave of research into various sleep disorders, including insomnia, narcolepsy, and obstructive sleep apnea, promoting a shift towards a biomedical model in treatment (Iranzo, 2022).

3.7. Behavioral Models of Sleep Disorders

The mid-20th century also saw the emergence of behavioral therapeutic approaches, spearheaded by researchers such as Arthur Spielman and his associates. Their work in cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) emphasized the role of cognitive and environmental factors in the perpetuation of insomnia, providing a counterpoint to purely medical interpretations and introducing interventions that were both psychologically and behaviorally informed (Perlis & Lichstein, 2003). However, this paradigm operated largely independently of the advancements in neuroscience, which continued to evolve concurrently, bolstered by insights gained from neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies (Mohammad Nami et al., 2022).

3.8. Integration of Neuroscience into Contemporary Sleep Psychoeducation

In more recent decades, there has been a concerted effort to integrate neuroscience findings into psychoeducational approaches for sleep disorders. The emergence of the biopsychosocial model has allowed for a more comprehensive framework that recognizes the interplay between biological, psychological, and social factors influencing sleep (Zarski et al., 2025). NIPE enhances the understanding of insomnia and related disorders by elucidating the neurophysiological processes underpinning sleep regulation, circadian rhythms, and the impact of stress and anxiety on sleep quality (Ehrampoush et al., 2019).

4. Psychiatric Populations Most Likely to Benefit

>Psychiatric Populations Most Likely to Benefit

4.1. Psychiatric Comorbidity and the Rationale for NIPE

There is compelling evidence supporting the value of NIPE in psychiatric populations who struggle with co-morbid sleep disorders. These clinical populations comprise patients who are diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder, Anxiety Disorders, Schizophrenia and Psychotic Disorders, Bipolar Disorder, PTSD, and ADHD (Freeman et al., 2020).

4.2. Major Sleep Disorders and Clinical Relevance

Current therapeutic strategies increasingly leverage this integrated knowledge base. Approaches now often include informational components that educate individuals on the neurobiological underpinnings of sleep disorders. They help patients understand the mechanisms behind their experiences and provide evidence-based techniques for managing symptoms. This neuro-informed psychoeducation not only empowers patients to engage more effectively with their treatment but also reduces stigma and misconceptions surrounding sleep disorders (Ehrampoush et al., 2019). Thus, the historical journey from supernatural interpretations to NIPE underscores a significant transformation in understanding sleep disorders and informs contemporary practices in the field., Sleep disorders encompass a diverse array of conditions that significantly impair both the quality and duration of sleep. Among the most commonly recognized sleep disorders are insomnia, sleep apnea, and narcolepsy, each presenting unique clinical features and challenges, along with pronounced implications for mental health.

4.2.1. Insomnia and Psychiatric Comorbidity

Insomnia is characterized by difficulty in initiating or maintaining sleep, resulting in daytime impairment or distress. The prevalence of insomnia in the general population ranges between 10-30%, with higher rates observed in certain populations, such as older adults and individuals with chronic health conditions (Vaziri et al., 2021). Insomnia can present in various forms, including acute insomnia, which typically lasts for a short duration and is often triggered by stressful events, and chronic insomnia, which endures for at least three months and recurs at least three times per week (Ghermezian et al., 2023). The impact of insomnia extends beyond mere fatigue; it has been empirically linked to various mental health issues, including anxiety disorders, depression, and cognitive impairment (Behzadi, Ahmed, & Nami, 2025). In this context, the heightened arousal state associated with insomnia may perpetuate a vicious cycle of stress and sleep disruption, further exacerbating mental health concerns.

4.2.2. Obstructive Sleep Apnea and Psychiatric Burden

Another prevalent sleep disorder is obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), a condition characterized by repeated episodes of partial or complete obstruction of the upper airway during sleep, leading to disrupted sleep and reduced oxygen saturation levels. The prevalence of OSA is estimated to be between 2-4% in the general adult population, although it often remains underdiagnosed (Gupta & Simpson, 2015). Risk factors for OSA include obesity, male gender, advanced age, and anatomical abnormalities of the upper airway. The consequences of untreated OSA are far-reaching, as studies indicate a robust association between OSA and various psychiatric disorders, including increased rates of depression and anxiety (Mohammad Nami et al., 2015). The recurrent awakenings associated with OSA prevent the attainment of restorative sleep phases, contributing to daytime sleepiness and impaired cognitive function, which may further complicate pre-existing mental health conditions (Kaufmann, Susukida, & Depp, 2017).

4.2.3. Narcolepsy and Psychosocial Impact

Narcolepsy is a less common but equally impactful sleep disorder characterized by excessive daytime sleepiness and often accompanied by sudden episodes of sleep, cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle tone), sleep paralysis, and hallucinations. The prevalence of narcolepsy is estimated at 0.02-0.06% of the population, with a higher incidence noted in teenagers and young adults (Mundt, Franklin, Horsnell, & Garza, 2025). Narcolepsy is believed to be linked to a deficiency of hypocretin, a neurotransmitter essential for regulating arousal and wakefulness. The psychological burden of narcolepsy can be profound, as individuals often report social stigmatization, anxiety, and depression stemming from the unpredictable nature of their sleep episodes (Davidson, Biddle, Nassan, Scammell, & Zhou, 2022). The resultant excessive daytime sleepiness not only disrupts daily functioning but can also lead to significant challenges in occupational and educational settings.

Taken together, sleep disorders represent a significant public health issue, characterized by multifaceted impacts on sleep quality, daytime functioning, and mental health outcomes.

Understanding the prevalence and clinical nuances of insomnia, sleep apnea, and narcolepsy offers essential insights for the development of neuroscience-informed psychoeducational interventions aimed at improving sleep and its associated mental health comorbidities. Such interventions can play a critical role in enhancing the overall well-being of affected individuals and mitigating the societal burden posed by sleep disorders.

4.3. Theoretical Frameworks Informing NIPE in Psychiatric Sleep Populations

The regulation of sleep is a complex interplay between various neurotransmitters, hormones, and brain structures, which collectively orchestrate the sleep-wake cycles fundamental to human health. This section delves into the neuroscience underlying sleep regulation, focusing on the key players in this intricate system.

4.3.1. Biopsychosocial Model

One prominent theoretical perspective is the Biopsychosocial Model of Health. This model posits that health outcomes are influenced by a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors (Verma, Singh, & Srivastava, 2022). In the context of sleep disorders, this model underscores the importance of considering neurobiological underpinnings, such as the role of neurotransmitters and circadian rhythms, alongside psychological factors like anxiety and cognitive distortions, as well as social influences such as lifestyle and support systems. For instance, Perrault et al. (2015) demonstrated how stress and social dynamics impact sleep quality, suggesting that interventions should address both the biological basis of sleep disorders and the psychosocial aspects that may impede recovery. By embracing this model, clinicians can tailor psychoeducation strategies that promote an integrated understanding of sleep health among patients (Perrault et al., 2025).

4.3.2. Cognitive Behavioral Theory

Another influential perspective is Cognitive Behavioral Theory (CBT), which has been widely adopted in the treatment of sleep disorders, particularly insomnia. CBT posits that maladaptive thoughts and behaviors significantly contribute to sleep disturbances (Kamp, Viaene, & Verhoeven, 2024).
