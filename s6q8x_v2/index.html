Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Governments, schools, hospitals, companies, and myriad other organizations structure institutional life and mobilize large numbers of people to accomplish what they cannot do alone. Institutions also regulate something less visible, but equally consequential: the emotions of their constituents. We introduce institutional emotion regulation as the formal processes by which institutions, through organizations, seek to influence the emotions people have, when they have them, and how they experience and express these emotions. Research across the social sciences has examined how particular organizations attempt to shape affective experiences, but these literatures have largely developed in disciplinary silos or domain-specific settings. We propose that these phenomena reflect a distinct level of emotion regulation, characterized by emotion goals held by organizations and pursued through formal institutional means. We develop a framework that crosses four established stages of emotion regulation -targeting situations, attention, appraisals, and responses -with three distinctive vectors of institutional influence: policies, the rules and structures that shape people's environments; proclamations, communications directed toward the public; and proxies, the agents who act on behalf of the organization. Our framework provides a common language for understanding how institutions influence emotional life at scale and suggests novel directions for psychological study while surfacing broader questions for society around the appropriate use of this influence.

Researchers across the social sciences have long recognized that governments, businesses, and other groups shape people's emotions (e.g., Ahmed, 2004; Bagozzi et al., 1999). This work has considered specific emotions (e.g., 'happiness guarantees' for consumer products) as well as broader affective states (e.g., customer satisfaction or feelings of trust), and we cast a wide net in the affective phenomena we consider here. Crucially, these strands have often been pursued in disciplinary silos. For example, research from public health has developed an extensive literature on the use of fear appeals to evoke fear among the public to motivate protective health behavior (Witte & Allen, 2000), whereas research on emotional labor has examined how employees' friendliness can generate positive feelings among customers, but potentially at a cost to those employees (Grandey & Gabriel, 2015). Each tradition provides depth within its domain, but their different terminologies, regulatory actors and targets, and outcomes can obscure what they share: organizations attempt to influence emotions in pursuit of their goals, and do so with similar formal mechanisms, even across disparate domains.

This fragmentation has limited an integrated approach and made it difficult to ask systematic questions about why organizations shape emotion, how they do so, what factors shape when those efforts succeed or fail, and what the broad-ranging outcomes might be. We propose that seemingly disparate phenomena can be understood as instances of a common process of institutional ER. We use institutional not simply to denote the setting in which emotions are regulated (Destin & Haase, 2024), but to describe the broad scale and the formal means of regulation itself 1 . Institutional ER therefore represents a distinct level of regulation where the emotion goals -goals concerning which emotions are being regulated -are held by an organization and pursued through formal means of policy, proclamation, and proxies. These three vectors encompass the formal ways in which organizations regulate constituents, which is the focus of our framework; these formal paths are distinct from other informal influences of institutions (such as norms and social roles) which have received more attention in the psychology literature (Stephens et al., 2014).

We build from decades of research that have examined how individuals regulate their own and other's emotions (Gross, 2015; Koole, 2009; Thompson, 1994). Recent theorizing highlights the utility of scaling up individual-level regulation frameworks (Duckworth & Gross, 2020) and extending the emotion regulation framework to the institutional level can provide a common language to study how emotion is shaped at scale. Using this framework, scholars and practitioners can identify the emotional goals that organizational actors hold, and the mechanisms through which they pursue them, including generating predictions about when and why those efforts will succeed or fail. This framework also enriches psychology by articulating how regulatory processes operate when emotion goals and regulatory power reside not just in individuals, but in the organizations that structure social life within institutions.

In this paper, we first articulate a model of institutional ER and situate it within the broader landscape, distinguishing it from related phenomena including other forms of dyadic, extrinsic, and collective ER and other ways in which emotion is regulated at scale, including by culture (Goldenberg & Cao, 2026; Mesquita et al., 2007; Nozaki & Mikolajczak, 2020; Zaki & Williams, 2013). We then consider why organizations regulate emotions, including factors that shape the different emotion goals being pursued. Then, we consider how these emotion goals are pursued, drawing on research and examples across institutional settings. Our model organizes institutional ER along two intersecting and orthogonal frames: the stages of emotion generation that institutions can target (situations, attention, appraisals, and responses) and the distinctive vectors of formal institutional influence through which they can target them (policies, proclamations, and proxies). We conclude by considering the implications of institutional ER for research and society, highlighting future directions across disciplines.

Institutional Emotion Regulation: An Overview

Institutional ER is distinctive because of the powerful levers that organizations have to change emotion in the public at scale: policies, proclamations, and proxies. These vectors intersect orthogonally with established targets of ER to map the diverse ways organizations seek to shape emotion (Figure 1). We build from a process model of ER (Gross, 2015), which describes how emotions unfold: People encounter situations, attend to particular aspects, appraise their meaning, and an emotional response follows (gray boxes, Figure 1). Each stage provides a target for regulation: changing the situation, changing what receives attention, changing how the situation is appraised, or changing the resulting emotional response, including its experiential, behavioral, or physiological components (blue boxes, Figure 1). Although these targets are well established in individual-focused affective science, applying them at the institutional level reveals the breadth of ways organizations can shape people's emotional lives.

First, organizations can regulate emotion by policy.

Policies regulate emotion by changing the formal rules, procedures, and structures that organize people's environments, thereby shaping the public's situation, attention, appraisals, or even overt responses. Policies include not only laws and government regulations, but also organizational rules and procedures across institutional domains. For example, ER can be undertaken via school curricula, hospital procedures, workplace policies, or eligibility requirements for public benefits.

Second, organizations can regulate emotion by proclamation. Proclamations regulate emotion through official communications from the organization to the public. Organizations can speak to millions of people at once through public addresses, campaigns, announcements, or warnings, but proclamations can also be directed toward smaller audiences as well. Critically, these communications carry the voice and authority of the organization, whether in the form of a government warning, corporate apology, university announcement, or public-health campaign.

Finally, organizations can regulate emotion by proxy. Proxies are specific agents who are authorized to interact with constituents, and regulate their emotions in myriad ways, on the behalf of the organization. These include teachers, police officers, or "street level bureaucrats" in government (Lipsky, 1983), "principal agents" deputized to represent an organization (Miller, 2005), or "frontline" employees who interact with customers on a regular basis. Despite their varied roles across domains, these agents share two essential features: they are points of contact between organizations and their constituents, and they exercise personal discretion while also acting on behalf of the organizations they represent (Camp, 2024; Camp et al., 2025).

Situating Institutional Emotion Regulation

Together, these three vectors comprise the distinctive means through which organizations act on constituents' emotions, integrating insights from organizational research on institutional influence and domain-specific scholarship in areas such as law, politics, health care, education, and business. Institutional ER connects these research traditions to psychological research on emotion and emotion regulation. Although the organizational vectors are distinctive, the regulatory processes they target are shared across forms of ER, from self-regulation to regulation within social networks. Two dimensions help situate institutional ER relative to other forms of emotion regulation: the formal hierarchical structure in which regulation occurs and the number of people targeted by the regulation (Figure 2).

Formal Hierarchy Structures

First, institutional ER is embedded within formal hierarchical structures (y-axis, Figure 2).

Institutions, enacted through organizations, confer roles, authority, and power that persist beyond any particular individual. Importantly, these structures need not involve steep hierarchies of authority: even relatively horizontal organizations, such as artist cooperatives, have formal governance structures that establish rules, responsibilities, and procedures for collective decision-making. These structures provide the foundation for the distinctive means through which organizations can pursue emotion goals. This distinguishes institutional ER from regulation occurring within informal hierarchies, such as when parents regulate children (Morris et al., 2018), or when group social status is salient such as cross-race interactions (Richeson & Shelton, 2007), as well as from contexts involving little or no hierarchy, such as when individuals regulate themselves or their close others. This hierarchical distinction persists even when institutional ER occurs through proxies.

Contacts between proxies and constituents resemble interpersonal forms of ER, but proxies are also tethered to an organization, acting in an institutional capacity and in service of the organization's http://goals.in/ (Camp, 2024). Notably, institutional ER does not require the organization to explicitly direct each regulatory act. Proxies may exercise discretion over how best to pursue organizational goals. Thus, when a parking attendant responds to a distressed driver with leniency, that regulation occurs as part of an institutional encounter: the attendant is exercising authority granted by the organization, and their actions constitute the organization's treatment of that constituent. Even within interactions where an individual proxy appears lower in power than the constituent -say, a customer service representative and a customer --there remains an asymmetry between the relatively powerful organization and that constituent.

Emotion Regulation at Scale

Institutional ER is distinctive in its capacity to operate at scale (x-axis, Figure 2). Policies and proclamations can target the emotions of thousands or millions of people through a single regulatory act. Even when institutions regulate emotion through proxies, a particular regulatory episode may involve only two people (a physician and a patient, for example) or small group (a teacher and a classroom), but effects can accumulate significantly across repeated interactions: a hospital employs dozens of physicians who see hundreds of patients each year and a school system employs thousands of teachers who teach hundreds of students each year. Collectively, these interactions allow institutions to influence constituents' emotions at scale and shape constituents' relationships with the larger institutions the proxies represent. This is not to say that institutional ER is the only form of large-scale ER. Individuals with substantial informal power -prominent artists, authors, or pundits, for example -may seek to shape the emotions of mass audiences without acting through a formal institutional hierarchy (left-most yellow boxes, Figure 1). Collective ER can operate across large groups through emergent features of networked relationships (Goldenberg & Cao, 2026). Individuals, groups, and institutions alike are embedded in larger cultural systems of regulation (Mesquita et al., 2007), with cultural norms and practices that may overlap with collective and institutional ER.

What distinguishes institutional ER is not simply the relative influence of the people involved, or the setting in which ER occurs, but the structures through which regulation operates: Institutional ER emphasizes emotion goals pursued through the formal, top-down capacities of organizations whereas collective ER emphasizes regulatory dynamics that emerge through informal, bottom-up interactions among members of a collective. While collective ER can take place within organizational hierarchies, the organization itself is not a regulating agent. In contrast to collective ER, institutional ER can operate in the absence of a social relationship or network, and the relationship between an organization and its constituents -such as between a government and its citizens -would not typically be characterized as a tie in such a network. In practice, collective and institutional ER often co-occur (e.g., participants in a religious service may simultaneously be regulated by a church organization and their co-congregants), and also interact with each other (e.g., organizational regulatory attempts may amplify or dampen bottom-up collective regulatory dynamics, and informal norms and cultural beliefs can shape an organizations' goals or tactics).

Distinguishing institutional ER from other forms of ER clarifies how organizations influence the emotional life of their constituents and highlights understudied questions in organizational scholarship. Organizations have long sought to cultivate desired emotions among their employees, as illustrated by Arlie Hochschild's foundational work on "the managed heart" (1983), to which our title pays homage. Whereas subsequent studies of emotional labor have elaborated on the methods and costs of employees' self-regulation, our framework foregrounds organizations as regulators of emotion, asking how they impose these emotional demands in the first place. Organizations' efforts to influence employees' emotions through workplace policies, communications, and managerial oversight fall squarely within our definition of institutional ER (gray box within blue institutional ER box, Figure 1). While this inward-facing institutional ER is important, our framework further highlights a comparatively understudied form of institutional influence: organizations' efforts to shape emotion are not limited to employees, but extend to the broad populations with whom they interact and whose lives they structure.

Taken together, institutional ER is distinguished not simply by its setting, target, or scale, but the source and structure of regulatory influence: emotion goals are pursued through the formal capacities of organizations. Next, we turn to the foundational question of what emotions organizations want people to feel and why, before considering the institutional means through which they are pursued.

The Emotional Goals of Organizations

Emotion regulation is, by definition, intentional: one has the goal of regulating their own or another's state towards a desired emotion, or away from an undesired one (Tamir, 2016). So too, is institutional ER, with the distinction that goals reside at the organizational level. People readily understand organizations as agentic entities that are capable of forming intentions and pursuing goals (Rai & Diermeier, 2015). Although organizations cannot feel emotions themselves, they can seek to affect the emotions of others. These goals can be hedonic, aimed at increasing pleasure or reducing discomfort. For example, Intuit tax software seeks to "design for delight". These goals can also be contra-hedonic, aimed at increasing discomfort or reducing pleasure. For example, Mothers Against Drunk Driving places totaled vehicles outside schools as visible reminders of the consequences of drunk driving, seeking to elicit fear and discourage teenagers from driving while intoxicated.

Organizations may pursue emotion goals for their downstream consequences, for the value placed on particular emotional states themselves, or for some combination of both. Often, organizations target constituents' emotions instrumentally, as a means to influence constituents' behavior. In the examples above, Intuit seeks to instill delight to create a more appealing product experience and the shock of a wrecked car is intended as not just as a spectacle, but as a warning to discourage drunk driving. In other cases, emotions are the ultimate target of regulation. For instance, palliative care organizations explicitly seek to relieve patients' emotional and psychological suffering as part of their core mission (World Health Organization, 2020), and the kingdom of Bhutan lists increasing "Gross National Happiness" as an explicit policy goal (Ugyel et al., 2024).

Whereas the source of individual emotional goals can be relatively straightforward -a person decides they want to influence an emotion -the processes through which organizations form their own goals are more varied and complex. Not only are organizations composed of more than one person, but they also adopt different structures to organize decision making and power is delegated, concentrated, or distributed across individuals in different ways. As such, accounts of organizational goal formation range from bureaucratic agenda-setting (Weber, 1947), to negotiations between coalitions (Cyert & March, 1963), to ad-hoc convergences of problems and solutions (Cohen et al., 1972), or simple mimicry (Meyer & Rowan, 1977).

How, then, can an emotion goal be meaningfully attributed to an organization, and not simply to the individuals within it? Whatever their source, these goals become attached to the organization as a form of joint intent (Schweikard & Schmid, 2021). Joint intention arises when people recognize and commit to a common coordinated goal. A planned date is one example of joint intent, in that neither person can form the intention on their own; it arises from the pair's common understanding of the date. Joint intentionality appears to be a uniquely human capacity (Warneken et al., 2006), and one that has been scaled up to institutional collectives built on shared representations and commitments. Formal organizations create and sustain joint intent by fostering a common mission among their members and procedures they believe will generally be followed by one another (List & Pettit, 2011; Pettit, 2001). Indeed, joint intent is what allows an organization to act coherently across time and individual members. Thus, saying that an organization "has" an emotion goal does not require that the goal originate with the organization or through any particular decision-making process. What matters is that the goal ultimately comes to be pursued by the organization.

In sum, institutional ER cannot be reduced to ER performed by individuals, nor is it an incidental byproduct of organizations seeking to maximize benefits and minimize harms to its stakeholders. Rather, emotion goals become organizational goals through joint intent, allowing organizations to pursue them coherently across individuals and over time. Once established, these goals can be pursued through a distinctive suite of regulatory tools available to organizations, to which we turn next.

Organizations can pursue emotion goals through three institutional vectors -policies, proclamations, and proxies -each of which can intervene at different stages of constituents' emotional responses (Figure 1). Several themes emerge from these examples. First, organizations often combine policies, proclamations, and proxies in pursuit of the same emotion goal: a policy may be announced through a proclamation and then implemented by proxies. Second, the lines between vectors themselves can be blurred. Third, a single institutional action may target multiple stages of constituents' emotional responses. Organizations Influence Constituents' Emotional Situations

Emotion regulation strategies that target situations involve changing the environments or affordances people encounter so that particular emotional responses become more or less likely. For example, an individual seeking to reduce the stress of a frustrating coworker may avoid interacting with that person at work, removing the opportunity to interact with them altogether. Organizations, on the other hand, can alter situations on a much greater scale via policies, proclamations, and proxies: creating, removing, or restructuring the environments in which their constituents' emotions unfold.

Institutional policies can powerfully shape the situations constituents encounter, and in turn, their emotions. A 2025 executive order by President Trump, for instance, sought to remove distressing classroom content by restricting federal funding from K-12 schools that might lead children to "feel guilt, anguish, or other forms of psychological distress" on account of their race or gender (Trump, 2025). By removing classroom content, such policies can preclude students from encountering, attending to, and appraising emotionally challenging material. Situational policies can be considerably more subtle. Much user experience (UX) design, for example, is oriented towards designing procedures that make mundane tasks more pleasant for users.

Conversely, policies can also be designed to inflict pain and shame on constituents, by way of "administrative burdens" (Moynihan et al., 2015) -procedural steps imposed to add friction to constituents' interactions with the state. Many social aid programs in the U.S., for instance, have been intentionally designed to dissuade the "undeserving" poor -and Black Americans in particular -from seeking assistance by requiring difficult-to-obtain documentation, limiting hours of service, and subjecting applicants to high levels of scrutiny (Moynihan et al., 2015; Ray et al., 2023). Indeed, connections between informal prejudice and formal practices, such as discriminatory policies and selective enforcement, are a defining feature of institutional racism writ large (Ray, 2019; Sewell, 2016).

Proclamations can also influence emotional situations. For example, a declared moment of silence creates an opportunity for constituents to feel collective grief, while an anthem creates an opportunity for collective pride; both proclamations foster these emotions to affirm commitment to the state. Town halls and listening sessions can similarly create situations where constituents are able to feel valued and heard by an institution. Here, the proclamation is doing more than just conveying (or receiving) information, the proclamation itself helps generate an emotional event.

Finally, proxies can also alter emotional situations through their presence and actions, and as a resource or an obstacle. Consider Disney "cast members", who roam the theme parks to evoke customers' delight, or peer navigators, who help alleviate the psychological costs of administrative burdens by shepherding clients through bureaucracies (DeLuca et al., 2023).

Indeed, the mere presence of institutional agents can shift constituents' emotions: deploying police officers, for example, may make an environment feel safer to some constituents while making the same environment feel more threatening or surveilled to others. In these cases, the proxy becomes part of the situation to which constituents emotionally respond.

Organizations Influence Constituents' Attention

ER strategies that target attention involve directing focus toward or away from features of the environment so that particular emotional responses become more or less likely. An individual might regulate their own emotion by distracting themselves from upsetting news, or attempt to cheer up a sad friend by reminding them of an upcoming vacation. Organizations can also shape which information constituents notice, dwell on, or ignore -but can do so for large numbers of people at once. Through policies, proclamations, and proxies, organizations can highlight or prioritize emotionally consequential information, or delay, restrict or remove such information from constituents' attention.

Policies can structure what information captures constituents' attention, or reaches them at all. Mandated cigarette warning labels, for example, use graphic images and prominent text to direct attention toward the aversive consequences of smoking, eliciting emotions such as fear and disgust to discourage tobacco use (Noar et al., 2016). Increasingly, attentional policies are implemented algorithmically: social media platforms curate users' exposure to affectively charged content, shaping their emotional experiences (Brady et al., 2020; Kramer et al., 2014).

Conversely, institutions may restrict exposure to emotionally consequential information. For example, schools and law-enforcement agencies may limit details released following a suicide

to reduce contagion risk (American Foundation for Suicide Prevention & Suicide Prevention

Resource Center, 2018). In each case, organizational rules and procedures govern what constituents are likely to attend to and, in turn, shape the emotions they are likely to feel.

Proclamations provide an especially direct way to capture and direct constituents' attention. Ad campaigns, for example, make products salient to generate emotions such as excitement and desire (Achar et al., 2016). Other proclamations deliberately call attention to suffering that might otherwise escape public awareness. Truth and reconciliation initiatives publicly foreground histories of collective harm, eliciting grief or outrage in the service of acknowledgment and longer-term reconciliation (Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada, 2025). Across such cases, an organization uses its communicative "megaphone" not simply to provide information, but to determine which emotionally consequential information occupies constituents' attention.

Finally, proxies can direct constituents' attention toward or away from emotionally consequential information. Museum docents, for example, draw visitors' attention toward exhibit features that cultivate curiosity or awe. Conversely, proxies may distract constituents from information likely to evoke unwanted emotions. For example, in a study of Chinese social media posts, researchers found that the ruling party influenced public discourse through distraction: instructing party members to post positive non-political content during times of social conflict to divert attention away from discontent or criticism of the government (King et al., 2017).

Notably, constituents need not recognize these individuals as institutional proxies. Whether operating overtly, as with docents, or covertly, as with coordinated social-media posters, proxies shape what captures attention and what recedes from view.

Organizations Influence Constituents' Appraisals

ER strategies that target appraisals involve changing how people interpret the meaning of a situation so that particular emotional responses become more or less likely. An individual anxious about an upcoming job interview, for example, might remind themselves that the interview is an opportunity rather than a test, reframing the situation to make it feel less threatening. Organizations can similarly shape how constituents interpret events, including what is at stake, how threatening or controllable a situation is, who is responsible for it, and what it means for the constituent. Through policies, proclamations, and proxies, organizations can therefore influence emotion by shaping the meaning people ascribe.

Policies can formalize particular ways of interpreting emotionally consequential situations. For example, educational institutions can establish grading policies that frame setbacks and failure as opportunities for learning and growth rather than evidence that students lack ability, thereby fostering hope and reducing discouragement (e.g., Clark County School District, n.d.). In such cases, organizations embed particular interpretations of events within their formal practices, making some appraisals (and the emotions that follow from them) more likely than others.

Proclamations can shape appraisals even more explicitly by telling constituents how an event should be understood. During crises, governments may reassure the public that a threat is under control, shaping appraisals of severity and controllability in ways that reduce fear or anxiety (Government Communications, 2020; Yan Jin, 2010). Corporations may describe layoffs as "strategic restructuring," framing an event that could signify organizational failure or instability as purposeful adaptation (EnerSys, 2025, e.g.). Public apologies offer another form of appraisal change: by acknowledging wrongdoing and accepting responsibility for it, an institution can publicly affirm appraisals that a harm occurred and the organization bears responsibility for it, providing recognition to those harmed and supporting reconciliation (Blatz et al., 2009; Schumann & Ross, 2011). In each case, proclamations regulate emotion by offering constituents a particular interpretation of what an event means.

Finally, proxies can shape constituents' appraisals through direct interpersonal interactions. Customer-service representatives, for example, may be trained to de-escalate angry customers by acknowledging their frustration while reframing an offending action as a mistake or misunderstanding rather than intentional mistreatment. Similarly, medical institutions establish procedures for physicians to break bad news that govern not only what patients are told but how difficult information is communicated, to help patients emotionally process threatening diagnoses (Baile et al., 2000). Conversely, classic research on bill collectors describes their strategic use of inflammatory language to heighten debtors' appraisals of urgency and threat, thereby eliciting anxiety in the service of securing payment (Sutton, 1991).

Across these cases, proxies regulate emotion by shaping the meaning constituents construct from an event and, in turn, influencing the emotions that follow.

Organizations Influence Constituents' Emotional Responses

ER strategies can also target emotional responses as they occur, directly shaping how emotions are experienced, expressed, or manifested behaviorally or physiologically. An individual might suppress signs of anger during an argument or slow their breathing to reduce physiological arousal when anxious. Organizations can similarly seek to amplify or dampen constituents' emotional responses. Unlike strategies that change the situation, redirect attention, or alter its meaning, these strategies intervene directly on the emotional response itself.

Policies can establish rules and procedures governing how constituents express their emotions. Workplaces, schools, courts, and other organizations, for example, commonly establish codes of conduct that require constituents to suppress or constrain emotional expressions deemed inappropriate or disruptive (Rule 5.03 -Courtroom Decorum, 2025). Such policies need not operate only on emotional expression. Hospitals, for example, may standardize paced-breathing exercises as part of preoperative care, using guided breathing to directly reduce patients' physiological arousal and anxiety before surgery (Mayo Clinic, n.d.).

Here, the institution seeks to regulate emotion by targeting the bodily response itself: slowing physiological arousal as a means of calming the broader emotional response.

Proclamations can also directly encourage constituents to alter how they respond emotionally. The British Ministry of Information's 1939 "Keep Calm and Carry On" poster, for example, was designed for deployment during severe wartime crises to encourage the public to constrain fear and maintain composure (Imperial War Museums, n.d.). Organizations may similarly encourage constituents to publicly express pride, grief, or gratitude during collective events, including through moments of silence, commemorative rituals, or symbolic displays (Proclamation of a Day of Mourning for Her Late Majesty Queen Elizabeth the Second, 2022).

Finally, proxies can intervene directly to shape a constituent's emotional behavior or physiology. Indeed, the enforcement of policies regulating emotional expression often falls to proxies, such as teachers, courtroom officials, or religious leaders instructing constituents to regain composure or maintain decorum. Emergency dispatchers, for example, may instruct distressed callers to slow down and take a breath so that they can communicate critical information and follow instructions (Feldman, 2021).

Institutional Emotion Regulation's Implications for Science and Society

The breadth of examples reviewed above attests both to the ubiquity of institutional ER, and to the extent to which it -or elements of it-have attracted scholarly attention in the social sciences. Our framework identifies the connective tissue between seemingly-disparate corners of research. A teacher helping a student to reconstrue a bad grade may seem far removed from a company urging consumers to take advantage of a limited time offer, yet both seek to shape emotional appraisals, albeit through different vectors (proxies and proclamation, respectively).

Organizations operating in completely different fields may nonetheless share goals of regulating their constituents' emotions, target similar stages of their constituents' emotional response, or use the same vectors to pursue their goals.

Our framework also highlights opportunities for interdisciplinary exchange. Social sciences can draw on decades of work in affective science that articulates how emotions are constructed, experienced, and acted upon, and the methods used to measure them.

Psychology, which has traditionally centered individual minds as the units of analysis, will similarly benefit by attending to fields which analyze variability among firms or governments.

Extending ER from individuals to dyads and groups has revealed regulatory processes that emerge at each new level of analysis. Moving outward even further to institutions likewise raises important questions for future research, where we turn to next.

When Will Institutional Emotion Regulation Be Successful?

What does it mean for institutional ER to succeed? For intrinsic ER, success typically refers to whether people achieve their own emotion goals (Tamir, 2016). Institutional ER complicates this picture, as organizations are so different from their constituents, and often engage in emotion regulation to advance other objectives. Adding to this complexity, the scale and breadth of institutional ER lends itself to divergent outcomes -intentional or otherwiseamong subgroups of the population. For example, state punishment may be intended to elicit shame among criminal offenders but also intended to foster wider public moral contempt towards their behavior (Dunlea & Heiphetz, 2021), while political messages designed to instill hope in some audiences may inadvertently demoralize others. For now, we focus on success as defined by whether organizations are most likely to produce their intended emotional change, while recognizing that such success does not imply that all (or any) of their constituents' emotion goals will be met. The success of institutional ER is influenced by several features which are uniquely relevant at the institutional level: organizations have an advantage in regulating emotions 'upstream' of individuals, and also face challenges when constituents become aware of emotional influence attempts or view regulation as illegitimate.

Theories of ER propose that intervening earlier within the emotion-generation process can be particularly effective (Sheppes et al., 2014; see also Duckworth et al., 2016).

Organizations may be especially well positioned to capitalize on this advantage given their capacity to structure the situations in which emotions arise. Consistent with this idea, a metaanalysis comparing behavior change interventions at both individual and institutional levels found that interventions were most successful when they targeted peoples' access to desired behaviors, upstream of individuals' thoughts and beliefs (Albarracín et al., 2024). Interestingly, these types of situational strategies have been relatively understudied within the ER field compared to cognitive strategies targeting attention or appraisal We propose that situational intervention is precisely where institutional ER may have a particular advantage:. By determining which situations people encounter and shaping their affordances, organizations can make downstream efforts to redirect attention, alter appraisals, or modify emotional responses easier or possibly even unnecessary. Such influence may be especially powerful precisely because constituents need not recognize it as ER at all.

Earlier-stage regulation may be powerful partly because it is less visible, whereas explicit proclamations ("don't panic," "be grateful," "feel proud") reveal regulatory goals and may provoke resistance from constituents. A broad literature tells us that if people become aware of regulation attempts, it threatens their autonomy and invites reactance (Wicklund, 1975). We speculate that institutional ER may be particularly susceptible to reactance, precisely because emotions are seen as innately and essentially human qualities (Berent, 2021) that organizations lack. Individuals could thus find it more objectionable to have these qualities targeted by organizations (vs. by other people via interpersonal ER). Such attempts to target emotions could even be seen as more intrusive than efforts to target constituent's behaviors or beliefs.

Institutional ER may therefore be least vulnerable to reactance when its regulatory purpose is unobtrusive (vs. salient).

The controversy surrounding Facebook's large-scale emotion experiment illustrates this possibility: Facebook removed emotional content from nearly 700,000 users' News Feeds without their awareness, causing changes in user's emotional expressions (Kramer et al., 2014).

And when the study became public, it provoked widespread criticism over the platform's covert attempts to influence users' emotions (Verma, 2014). Thus, institutional ER may become a target of resistance when its regulatory purpose is made explicit. Such resistance may be particularly pronounced when the organization's emotion goals conflict with constituents' own: for example, when an organization benefits from fear, guilt, or outrage that constituents themselves would prefer not to experience (or the converse, see Čehajić-Clancy et al., 2016).

A further challenge concerns perceived legitimacy: whether constituents perceive the institution has the right to influence their emotions. Even a clearly detected regulation attemptincluding those that conflict with the constituents own emotion goals -may elicit little resistance when the institution is perceived as legitimately entitled to intervene. Advice to remain calm from an emergency dispatcher, for example, may be welcomed because distress regulation falls within the perceived authority of that role, whereas covert attempts by a social-media platform to manipulate user's affect may be rejected as exceeding their institutional sphere of influence.

Thus, the success of institutional ER may depend not only on when the regulation happens, and whether constituents recognize their emotions are being targeted or what to feel that way, but also on whether they accept the institution's right to try to shape their emotions at all.

Connecting institutional ER with research on organizational control and individual selfregulation offer paths for identifying the factors underlying its success or failure. Future research can delineate which vectors are most effective at different stages of emotion regulation, under what conditions, and for which constituents. But doing so also requires understanding the assumptions that guide institutional attempts to regulate emotion in the first place. Just as individuals hold beliefs about which emotions are desirable and how they can be changed (Ford & Gross, 2019), organizations may develop shared assumptions about which emotions their constituents should feel, how those emotions can be produced, and what behaviors they motivate. These assumptions do not emerge in a vacuum: they should be shaped by informal institutional norms and broader cultural beliefs of which emotions are useful (Mesquita et al., 2007; Tsai et al., 2025; for an example, see Bencharit et al., 2019). These assumptions need not be psychologically sophisticated or even correct to affect constituents' emotions.

Understanding these assumptions and their downstream impacts, however, hinges upon being able to measure them -a topic we turn to next.

Challenges in Measurement: For Researchers and Organizations

The asymmetries between organizations and individuals raise unique challenges in explicit. An executive order seeking to eliminate federal funding for schools that promote what it describes as "radical indoctrination," for example, frames its purpose as protecting children from "guilt or anguish" (Trump, 2025). In other cases, an organization may have little incentive to disclose its emotion goal. For example, administrative burdens that make accessing public benefits frustrating or demoralizing to discourage uptake are not stated as an official objective (Lowrey, 2026). Requiring organizations to explicitly acknowledge their emotion goals would therefore exclude important cases of institutional ER precisely when they have reason to obscure their aims.

Researchers must instead infer institutional intent from multiple sources, triangulating official statements, policies, internal documents, historical records, interviews with organizational actors, and patterns of institutional behavior: a preponderance of evidence rather than a smoking gun. To do so, psychologists can borrow methods developed by other social sciences for studying organizational goals that are not explicitly acknowledged (Heller, 2023).

Emerging computational techniques can also capitalize on the extensive linguistic traces organizations leave of their attempts to shape constituents' emotions. For example, natural language processing can analyze these materials at scale, identifying not only emotional content (e.g., Billet et al., 2025, who analyzed church sermons), but also language reflecting particular ER strategies. This may be especially useful for verbally instantiated ER, such as cognitive change strategies that reframe or reinterpret events in ways to alter constituents' appraisals and, in turn, their emotional responses (Corbani et al., 2026; Nook et al., 2017).

These methods offer exciting opportunities to understand ER at scale, and even across history.

Organizations face the inverse measurement problem: how can they know what constituents actually feel? Unlike individuals, organizations cannot infer emotions through interpersonal cues and empathic processes. In cases of instrumental ER, organizations may circumvent this problem by measuring the downstream behavior of interest without measuring a targeted emotion: whether constituents purchase a product, comply with a request, remain engaged with a platform, or abandon a welfare application. When organizations do gauge emotions, however, they must make subjective experiences organizationally legible through metrics that can be standardized, aggregated, and compared. Scholars at the macro and microlevel have observed that organizations tend to value what can be readily quantified, with metrics potentially reifying the qualities they measure as important in their own right (Beer, 2016; Chang et al., 2024; Scott, 1998). Applied to emotion, this raises provocative questions about how organizations translate emotional experiences into metrics, and how those metrics, in turn, shape what organizations understand emotion to be. The discrete reactions that social-media platforms provide -hearts, likes, emojis, and the like-may not only shape what information the platform learns about users' emotions, but also shape the subsequent institutional actions taken in response to them. Going a step further, emotional metrics imposed by organizations could influence how constituents themselves express, label, or understand what they feel. To borrow from the example above, are the reactions available on the platform to measure emotion lead users to view their own or others' emotions through those categories?

Institutional Power and Institutional Change

Perhaps one of the most consequential implications of institutional ER is the sheer scale of its influence over individuals' emotions, often with little oversight, accountability, or even awareness. Large-scale interventions from organizations can occur without the systematic testing or ethical constraints that would accompany attempts by researchers to manipulate emotion. Such scrutiny is all the more pressing because organizational goals need not align with the goals of constituents. Indeed, there is little reason to assume that these goals will routinely align. Organizations may benefit when constituents feel calm, hopeful, afraid, guilty, or angry, regardless of whether constituents themselves want to feel those emotions. Moreover, when ER serves instrumental ends, the consequences of goal misalignment can extend well beyond how constituents feel. For example, government communications that characterize concerns about climate change as alarmist or exaggerated (Choi, 2020) may reduce public concern and in turn reduce support for environmental protections (Office of Management and Budget, 2025). Such effects can have stark downstream consequences for constituents and their communities..

Evaluating the outcomes of institutional ER thus benefits from a broad approach that considers not only constituents' immediate emotions and actions but also the consequences that follow.

Institutional ER may also shape the stability and transformation of institutions themselves. Organizations have strong incentives to preserve their legitimacy, authority, resources, and continued operation (Meyer & Rowan, 1977), and regulating constituents' emotions may help them do so.

When constituents feel anger, fear, dissatisfaction, and other negative emotions, it can signal that existing systems are failing and motivate behaviors ranging from complaints and withdrawal to protest and collective action to create change in those institutions. But, by reducing these emotions -or fostering positive emotions towards the institution, such as gratitude (Eibach et al., 2015)-institutional ER may improve constituents' immediate emotional experiences while also reducing their motivation to demand institutional change. Similar trade-offs between well-being and collective action have been observed when individuals regulate their own emotions within institutional contexts (Ford et al., 2019 (Ford et al., , 2023)).

When initiated by the institutions themselves, these processes may play out at a massive scale, helping protect the institution and maintain the status quo.

However, institutional self-preservation need not require preservation of the status quo.

When widespread dissatisfaction threatens an organization's legitimacy, resources, or continued authority, institutional change may become a means of self-preservation. Collective action may therefore shift organizations' emotion-regulatory calculus: although institutions can attempt to reduce the emotions motivating demands for change, they can also respond substantively to the conditions producing those emotions. Institutional ER is therefore not a oneway path of influence: constituents' emotions can be targets of institutional influence, but can also be forces capable of reshaping the institutions seeking to regulate them.

Conclusion

Institutions and the organizations that enact them are not merely the settings in which emotions unfold. They are active regulators of emotional life, with distinctive goals, resources, and means of influence. Recognizing institutional ER as a level of analysis in its own right can expand the science of emotion while helping us better understand and navigate the powerful role that institutions play in shaping how people feel.
