Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Approximately 10% of early adolescents in the UK report lifetime cannabis use. However, contemporary UK research in this population remains limited due to recruitment, consent and safeguarding challenges. We explored the feasibility of conducting research about cannabis use in UK adolescents aged 13-15 years.

Methods: This exploratory feasibility project comprised an online quantitative survey and qualitative interviews on cannabis use and mental health. Participants aged 13-15 years were recruited through schools, drug services, social media, posters and word-of-mouth. Participants consented through either a standard parent-and-adolescent pathway or an independent Gillick-competent pathway. Feasibility outcomes included total recruitment, cannabis-user recruitment, successful data collection, and the extent of safeguarding actions required.

Results: 175 participants completed the survey (Mage = 14.35, 54.9% female) and n=13 (7.4%) reported lifetime cannabis use. 16 participants (Mage=14.56, 75.0% female) completed the qualitative interview study, including 11 lifetime cannabis users. Two and four cannabis-using participants used the Gillick-competent pathway in the quantitative and qualitative studies, respectively. In the quantitative study, 38/175 participants (21.7%) triggered low-intensity signposting and one participant received additional safeguarding actions. Three qualitative participants received safeguarding actions. Both studies successfully elicited detailed information on cannabis use, mental health and contextual experiences.

We recruited nearly 200 13-15-year-old adolescent participants, including those who used cannabis, collected rich data about illegal cannabis use and mental health using indepth surveys and interviews, while keeping participants safe. We therefore proved we can feasibly conduct research about cannabis in <16-year-olds in the UK.

Introduction

Cannabis is the most widely used illicit drug among school-aged adolescents in the UK. In England, 7% of adolescents aged 11-15 years have used cannabis during their lifetime, and 8% of 15-year-olds have used cannabis in the past month (NHS Digital, 2024). In Scotland and Wales, 19% of 15-year-olds/Year 11 students have used cannabis at least once (Inchley et al., 2023; Page et al., 2023). Of lifetime cannabis users in the UK, survey results suggest that about one-third first tried cannabis before the age of 15 (Spinazzola et al., 2025). However, in the UK, contemporary research about cannabis in under-16-year-olds is extremely limited.

Consuming cannabis frequently carries psychiatric risks, including an increased risk of psychotic disorder (Marconi et al., 2016) and a small but increased risk of mood disorders (Lev-Ran et al., 2014). Earlier initiation of cannabis use is specifically associated with a greater chance of psychotic disorder (McDonald et al., 2024), cannabis use disorder (Lawn et al., 2022) and later substance abuse (Lynskey et al., 2003). In England, among the 16,212 under-18-year-olds who received formal support at a drug and alcohol service between 2024 and 2025, 71% of them received this support because of cannabis, and around 50% of these cannabis-using service users were under the age of 16 (Office for Health Improvement and Disparities [OHID], 2025).

While national survey statistics in the UK repeatedly demonstrate that a non-negligible proportion of early adolescents are using cannabis (NHS Digital, 2024; Public Health Wales, 2026), contemporary British academic research providing details on cannabis use in <16year-olds remains sparse. Most existing literature has either reported data from older cohorts when participants were adolescents in the early 2000s (Hines et al., 2023; Lees Thorne et al., 2025) or combined all illegal drugs without separately reporting cannabis use (Thornton et al., 2026; Ward et al., 2026). There are some exceptions, however; Page et al. (2021) reported an increase in past-month cannabis use in Wales through a national school survey of 11-16-year-olds in Wales. While Hotham et al. (2023) and Hines et al. (2024) reported associations between type of cannabis use and mental health outcomes, they used data from a whole-school, all-substance use prevention trial. Due to their broader focus, these studies lack detail on cannabis use behaviour and details. Humphreys et al. (2025) investigated cannabis experiences and motives in 55 students aged 13-16 years in an alternative provision (AP) school using online surveys. This appears to be the only contemporary study that specifically set out to investigate early adolescent cannabis use and offers rich insight into cannabis use in a younger age group in the UK. In short, only a handful of academic papers have studied cannabis use in under-16s over the past 20 years in the UK, and most involved analysis of large-scale existing datasets. This usually limits the cannabis-related detail available, as well as avoiding major ethical and safeguarding responsibilities of the researcher collecting data. We believe that given the size of the early adolescent cannabis user population in the UK, this group deserves dedicated, detailed research attention.

Internationally, more recent research has investigated under-16 cannabis use (Ashdown-Franks et al., 2019; Son et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2019; Forman-Hoffman et al., 2017).

Qualitative studies that gain rich data in younger age-groups also exist in US, Canada and Norway (Jenkins et al., 2017; Haines-Saah et al., 2014; Friese, 2017; Bilgrei et al., 2022).

Nonetheless, most papers report findings from large health-general surveys or include mixed age ranges, including those over 16. Thus, there appears to be limited international research that specifically investigates cannabis and focuses on under-16s. We believe there is particular need for cannabis-oriented research in under-16s, given this is when cannabis use often begins and the heightened harms of an earlier onset of cannabis use (Hosseini & Oremus, 2019). This lack of detailed cannabis research into early adolescents may reflect the unique challenge of researching illegal drug use among <16-year-olds. Potential participants may worry that their disclosures of illegal drug use are told to caring adults, which reduces willingness to participate and compromises honesty of answers (Berlan & Bravender, 2009; Fisher, 2003).

Full confidentiality can never be guaranteed due to the moral and legal obligation to protect <16-year-old adolescents when they are at risk of serious harm (Children Act, 1989). Good practice is to carefully and candidly explain to adolescents how sensitive disclosures will be handled, as in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study in the US (Clark et al., 2018; Clark et al., 2026). However, given the complexity and ambiguity of safeguarding timing, effectively teaching younger participants these confidentialitybreaking rules can be difficult. Therefore, deciding when to break confidentiality and how to safeguard, versus maintaining confidentiality, in early adolescent research studies is challenging (Hiriscau et al., 2014). This is often referred to as 'Gillick competence' (Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech Area Health Authority, [1985]), although its application to observational non-medical research is not a formal, legal term. These policies have been used by researchers who investigate vulnerable adolescent populations, for example, studies on LGBTQ+ populations (e.g. Todd, 2024; Cwinn et al., 2021 (Canada); Mustanski et al. (2017) (US)). However, in practice, British Research Ethics Committees (RECs) may default to requiring parental consent (Barker et al., 2025).

Consequently, most UK under-16 adolescent research utilises the parental consent with child assent approach, even though recruitment or data quality may be compromised by obligating the involvement of parents when studying a sensitive topic (Tigges, 2003; Rojas et al., 2008).

Alternatively, parental opt-out consent procedures are often used in large survey studies (NHS Digital, 2024).

Researchers also meet recruitment challenges. It is difficult to establish reliable routes to ethically and effectively invite early adolescents to participate in research, especially cannabis users. Schools provide access to large numbers of early adolescents, but participation is constrained by time limits and awkwardness in publicly discussing topics like illegal drug use (Hatch et al., 2023; Tyler & Davies, 2013). Similarly, despite caring for early adolescents who use drugs, researchers may find it hard to successfully recruit from healthcare and drug services due to time and clinical pressures (Loades et al., 2019).

To summarise, cannabis use during early adolescence in the UK is not rare. Many UK lifetime cannabis users try cannabis for the first time before 16, and initiating frequent use at an earlier age is predictive of worse outcomes (Hosseini & Oremus, 2019). Despite this, very little research has been published on early adolescent cannabis use, particularly contemporary research and research that specifically focuses on cannabis use behaviour. This is likely due to ethical and logistical challenges. Therefore, we evaluated the feasibility of conducting exploratory research into cannabis use among UK early adolescents through both a quantitative questionnaire study and a qualitative interview study about cannabis use and mental health. The indicators of feasibility were: (i) recruitment, (ii) data collection success and (iii) extent of sign-posting and safeguarding actions required. We report the strategies we undertook to achieve our goals, namely: our multi-route recruitment method, the independent Gillick-competent consent pathway, and a tiered safeguarding protocol.

Method

Timeline

Quantitative questionnaire data collection began in December 2024 and ended in July 2026, including two active data collection periods that cumulatively totalled approximately 10 months. Qualitative data collection began in June 2024 and ended in July 2026, including two active data collection periods that cumulatively totalled 5 months.

Consent Processes

Participants could provide their consent through two pathways. First, through the standard combined parental consent and adolescent assent pathway. Alternatively, the adolescent could consent via the Gillick-competent pathway (HRA, 2026). In this approach, participants could consent independently after they passed an interview in which they proved they understood the aims, content, risks and benefits, confidentiality and safeguarding protocol of the study. Competency interviews were run over the phone or via MS Teams and comprised questions to test whether they genuinely understood the study, and how confidentiality, signposting and the safeguarding process work. This consenting process is based on the Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech Area Health Authority [1986] legal case.

Signposting and Safeguarding

All participants received default, generic signposting to substance use and mental health services in the information sheet. We then informed participants in advance of participation about when confidentiality would be broken, what safeguarding action would be taken, and what information could or could not be shared with others. This signposting and safeguarding protocol was informed by Clark et al.'s (2018) approach and received further input from clinicians, adolescent research academics, and a school safeguarding lead.

The actions are based on qualitative disclosures or high quantitative questionnaire scores.

Study design

The quantitative study was an online survey combining questionnaires about cannabis use, drug and mental health, and computerised cognitive tasks. The survey was for 13-15-year-olds regardless of their past cannabis or drug use. We evaluated recruitment, cannabis user recruitment, successful data collection, and safeguarding outcomes. We collected descriptive findings on mental health and cannabis use outcomes.

Ethics

Ethical approval was granted by King's College London REC, ID: 43622.

Participants

Recruitment. The quantitative study was mainly advertised through voluntary school assemblies about cannabis presented by WL. WL gave about 25 assemblies to about 4,000 secondary school students in 2024-2026. The study was also advertised through physical posters, collaboration with drug services, paid advertisements on social media platforms (in 2024), and word of mouth. We spent around £1,500 on Snapchat and TikTok advertisements in summer 2024. Three drug services said they would help with recruitment. Participants could join both quantitative and qualitative studies.

Measures

Feasibility outcomes.

Recruitment.

Recruitment was defined by the number of participants we recruited who started the survey.

Cannabis user recruitment.

Cannabis-user recruitment was defined by the number of lifetime cannabis user participants we recruited who started the survey.

Successful data collection.

Successful data collection was defined by the proportion of people who started the survey and completed it, demonstrating acceptability of the survey to the participants. Furthermore, successful data collection was assessed by preliminary descriptive results.

Safeguarding outcomes.

Safeguarding outcomes were defined as the number of participants requiring different levels of safeguarding actions.

Cannabis use and drug use variables.

Cannabis use and use history.

Cannabis use was measured through standard questions assessing lifetime cannabis use, time since last use, lifetime cannabis use days, cannabis use initiation age and cannabis use frequency. Cannabis users also reported their typical weights (in grams) of cannabis consumed on a day of use from 0.1 to 5+, the type of cannabis they commonly used (skunk, hash/resin, herbal/Thai/ditch-weed or other), the route of administration (vape, eat or smoke) and the location they used cannabis (home, school, friend's House or park) through multiple-choice questions. Cannabis use was always kept confidential, regardless of extent, and did not trigger individualised signposting or safeguarding.

Problem cannabis use.

Cannabis users reported problematic cannabis use in the past 6 months through the CUDIT-R (Adamson et al., 2010). It comprises 8 multiple-choice items on hazardous cannabis use and dependence symptoms, and each item is coded from 0 to 4.

The final score is the sum of all ratings.

Cannabis use motive.

Cannabis users reported past-year cannabis use motivation was measured using the adapted BCAMM (Bartel et al., 2023). After adaptation, six 5-point Likertscale items each identify a specific reason for cannabis use. Participants rated the frequency based on the extent to which their cannabis use was driven by these aims from 'never' to 'always' and were coded from 1 to 5.

Offered cannabis.

All participants are asked whether they have been offered cannabis.

Perceived norms.

All participants reported how normal it is to use cannabis (Not at all to a lot, or don't know) and how easy it is to get cannabis (Very easy to very hard) in the UK.

Harm perception.

All participants are asked how much they think smoking cannabis will harm them (No harm to a lot of harm, or don't know) and to what extent they think regular cannabis use will harm their mental health (No risk to great risk, or don't know) (Wadsworth & Hammond, 2019).

Drug history.

Drug use history other than cannabis was collected through a drug history questionnaire which assessed lifetime/yearly/monthly substance use including alcohol, tobacco, CBD, magic mushrooms, nitrous oxide/laughing gas, ketamine, cocaine, MDMA and E-cigarette. For participants who report use in the last month, they are asked for the exact number of days that they used that substance. Any participants who reported a frequency of using these drugs equal to or higher than 2 days/week were individually signposted.

Problematic alcohol use.

Problematic alcohol use was assessed by AUDIT-C (Bush et al., 1998). Participants completed three five-point multiple-choice questions assessing the frequency, quantity, and frequency of heavy alcohol consumption during the past year.

Answers for each question were converted to ratings from 0-4, and the score is the sum. Any participants who scored 5 or above were individually signposted.

Mental health variables.

Depression. Depression in the past week was measured through the DASS-21 -depression subscale (Henry & Crawford, 2005). This subscale includes seven 4-point Likert-scale items, each ranging from 0 (Not apply at all) to 3 (Apply very much). The score is two times the sum. Any score of 21 or above was considered severe and were individually signposted.

Anxiety.

Anxiety was measured through the GAD-7 (Spitzer et al., 2006). GAD-7 comprises seven 4-point Likert-scale questions, each asking the perceived frequency of anxiety symptoms in the past 2 weeks from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). The anxiety score was the sum of all items, and any score of 15 or above was considered severe and were individually signposted.

Psychotic-like symptoms.

Psychotic-like symptoms were measured through the CAPE-15 (Capra et al., 2017). CAPE-15 contains 15 4-point Likert scale items, measuring the frequency and the distress of positive psychotic-like symptoms over the past 3 months from 0 to 3. The psychotic-like symptom score was the sum of all frequency items. A score of 22 or above (average≥1.47) was considered severe (Bukenaite et al., 2017) and individually signposted.

Other.

Demographics. Demographic information included gender, age, sexual orientation, and ethnicity.

We also measured impulsivity, personality, cannabis self-identity, school engagement, and police interactions with questionnaires, and used cognitive task measures of working memory and response inhibition, but these are not reported in this manuscript.

Procedure

Participants received an information sheet and were asked which consent route they preferred after contacting the research team. Participants who wanted to consent independently underwent the Gillick competency interview. After providing their consent, they received a Gorilla link to the survey. Participants did the survey at home in their own time. Participants were encouraged to do the survey on a laptop or computer, but could do it on a smartphone if required. Participants first confirmed that they had not used cannabis in the preceding 12 hours and provided their consent again. They then completed the survey.

Participants who reported lifetime cannabis use received extra questions on cannabis use details, cannabis use motives and problematic cannabis use. The total time commitment is around 30-45 minutes. Participants received a £20 Amazon voucher as compensation for their time. Before April 2025, participants received £30 (n=35), but this was reduced to £20 to better reflect the time commitment. The team then checked mental health questionnaires to determine if thresholds were crossed. If thresholds were crossed, the PI conducted individualised signposting, and other safeguarding actions were considered as appropriate.

Data Analysis

All quantitative analyses were conducted using Python and IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 32).

One cannabis-using participant did not receive the CUDIT-R. 33 participants were not asked about: being offered cannabis, ease of getting cannabis and normality of cannabis questions, due to receiving an earlier version of the survey being used. No other data were missing. We report descriptive statistics for demographics, feasibility outcomes, and drug use and mental health questionnaire outcomes. Categorical outcomes were summarised using frequencies and percentages, while continuous outcomes were summarised using means, standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals. Recruitment progression is visualised through a Sankey plot and safeguarding decisions visualised through a Venn diagram.

Qualitative Study

Study design

The qualitative interview study involved a semi-structured interview with (i) frequent cannabis users and (ii) non-users who had been offered cannabis but declined it. We explored cannabis use motives, perception of harm and contexts of use, as well as reasons for declining cannabis and perceptions of cannabis users. We use similar feasibility indicators as in the quantitative study. We report descriptive quotes based on topics discussed in the interview.

Ethics

Ethical approval was granted by both King's College London REC (ID: 35145) for recruiting participants through non-NHS sites and the HRA and the Liverpool Central NHS REC (IRAS project ID: 365406, REC reference: 26/NW/0090).

Participants

Recruitment. The recruitment method was the same as the quantitative survey study.

The only difference was that cannabis users were prioritised in the qualitative study.

Eligibility Criteria. Inclusion criteria were: (i) aged 13-15 years, (ii) speak English, and (iii) either be a (1) current weekly cannabis user or recent weekly user, or (2) have never used cannabis but have been offered it. The exclusion criteria are: 1) History of psychosis; 2) Suicidal thoughts or self-harm; 3) Previous use of heroin, crack cocaine or methamphetamine; 4) Unable to be interviewed for 1.5 hours, as judged by the participant, parent/carer, or researcher; and 5) Says that participation in the study will endanger them.

Measures

Feasibility Outcomes.

Recruitment.

Recruitment was defined by the number of participants who started the interview.

Cannabis user recruitment.

This was defined by the number of lifetime cannabis user participants who completed the interview.

Successful data collection.

Successful data collection was defined by the proportion of people who started the interview and completed it, demonstrating acceptability. It was also reflected by the preliminary descriptive content of the interview responses.

Safeguarding outcomes.

Safeguarding outcomes were defined as the number of participants triggered at each level of the signposting & safeguarding protocol.

Interview schedule

The topics covered during the interview are determined through discussions with clinicians, safeguarding leads, academics and through a review of existing literature. The interview topic guide was written by co-authors, including an experienced qualitative researcher (ML). Topics included: (i) reasons for cannabis initiation, (ii) reasons for continued use, (iii) perceived shortterm effects, (iv) perceived long-term effects, (v) context of cannabis use, (vi) knowledge of cannabis and (vii) the impact of cannabis use on their relationship with their family among cannabis users. For non-users, the five main topics in the interview schedule were: (i) their experience with cannabis, (ii) reason for not using cannabis, (iii) harm perception of cannabis, (iv) thoughts on peers who use cannabis, and (v) policymaking around cannabis use.

Procedure

Interviews were offered online or in person, conducted by a postgraduate researcher (LF or AS). The interview was expected to take around 1 hour. Interviews were audio-recorded with consent. We collected demographics and cannabis use days per week before the interview.

After the interview, participants received a £20 Amazon voucher. In 2024, the reward was £30 (n=9).

Quantitative results for qualitative study

We report quantitative demographics, cannabis use days per week, and age of first cannabis use. Then we report the recruitment progression with reference to consent and recruitment route through a Sankey plot. We reported interview length for users and non-users to check whether the interviews had been conducted within the expected duration. Safeguarding outcomes are also reported descriptively.

Raw qualitative data description

We reported illustrative qualitative results as example quotes, without conducting thematic analysis. We presented example quotes for 7 interview topics for users and 5 for non-users.

Discussion

This study explored the feasibility of conducting research on cannabis use among early adolescents in the UK. In spite of the sensitive nature of the research and ethical and logistical challenges, we successfully recruited 187 13-15-year-old participants, through a range of recruitment methods. We recorded information from 13 lifetime cannabis users in our quantitative survey and 11 frequent cannabis users in our qualitative interview study. We utilised a Gillick-competent independent consent pathway for some participants. We collected data about illegal cannabis and other drug use, mental health and contextual cannabis-related factors. We maintained full confidentiality about cannabis use, while safeguarding participants with a tiered safeguarding protocol relating to mental health and drug use.

Overall, our findings support the feasibility of specifically researching cannabis and other illegal drug use, in detail, among adolescents under 16 years old in the UK.

We demonstrated that we can gain ethical approvals to study illegal cannabis use in children in the UK and then demonstrated we can successfully collect quantitative and qualitative data on cannabis-related details in under-16s, including both cannabis users and non-users. This is despite the fact that parental involvement negatively affects engagement (Rojas et al., 2008) and adolescents may lie about their cannabis use (Delaney-Black et al., 2010). Beyond secondary analysis of existing survey data (Page et al., 2021) and baseline trial data (Hines et al., 2024; Hotham et al., 2023), our study is one of the few contemporary studies that investigate cannabis use in <16-year-olds in the UK. Even worldwide, it is one of the first studies that specifically set out to investigate <16-year-old cannabis use without relying on large epidemiological surveys. This is a significant research achievement, given (i) the prevalence of early adolescent cannabis use and its impact on wellbeing, and (ii) the ethical and logistical challenges of conducting such research.

Early adolescents are hard to reach when considering the competing goals in recruitment sources (schools and health services), and cannabis users could be harder to reach due to confidentiality concerns. Despite this challenge, we recruited 175 adolescents to the quantitative study and 16 for qualitative interviews within a limited time window, with 13 lifetime and 11 frequent cannabis users, respectively. The quantitative sample size therefore supports using school assemblies to recruit participants for studies on sensitive topics and demonstrates the potential of social media and third-sector clinical services to enlarge sample size and recruit substance users. Meanwhile, engagement from third-sector clinical services is sometimes inconsistent, suggesting that a major recruitment method (e.g., school assemblies) alongside clinical services is necessary. It is notable that many parents or carers were willing for their child to participate in our research, specifically about cannabis use and mental health, even under the knowledge that they would not be informed about any cannabis use. This demonstrates the feasibility of working with parents, and explaining that their child's cannabis use will not be disclosed to them, due to research confidentiality.

The Children Act 1989 obligates researchers to safeguard under-16s. These duties offer protection but may also worry potential adolescent participants, especially those who use cannabis, that their answers will be told to adults to protect them (Fisher, 2003). Nonetheless, cannabis users comprised 7.4% of our quantitative sample, with an average age of 14.3 years. This percentage falls within the 2023 SDD nationally representative data, which reports a rate of 7.1% lifetime cannabis use for 14-year-olds and 16.9% for 15-year-olds (NHS Digital, 2024), suggesting our recruitment efforts were able to reach early adolescent cannabis users, and tentatively suggests participants were being honest. We also proved that questionnaires and interviews were well accepted by early adolescents by finding a high completion rate, even when focusing on sensitive topics.

A potential explanation for participants' willingness to disclose their cannabis use could be our safeguarding strategy. Built on Clark et al. (2018), we explain to participants in what condition confidentiality will be broken and describe different levels of safeguarding action in advance. Cannabis use was never a reason to signpost or safeguard, regardless of frequency or intensity of use. In this context, one in five participants crossed at least one mental-health or non-cannabis-drug-use threshold, and one in four disclosed events that required signposting or safeguarding actions. This result stressed the necessity of planning a safeguarding protocol before data collection, but the proportion and the fact that we encountered no severe adverse events also showed the risk is manageable.

After we conducted corresponding safeguarding actions, we did not receive any complaints from participants. In general, appropriate safeguarding is compatible with substantial and sensitive data collection within early adolescents.

Another factor supporting our recruitment of cannabis users can be the Gillick-competent consent pathway. In past adolescent substance use research, it has been used mainly in substance use intervention studies (Deluca et al., 2015; Watson et al., 2015), while its use in observational adolescent drug use studies is rare. It is also sometimes difficult to implement this method when considering the subjectivity in assessing Gillick competence and the conflicts between RECs and researchers (Barker et al., 2025; Hunter & Pierscionek, 2007).

Nonetheless, the approval of both the quantitative and qualitative components by King's College London REC and NHS REC and the HRA demonstrates that combining Gillick competence into sensitive research on early adolescents can be conducted within an ethically acceptable framework. We believe RECs should be applauded for enabling this research, as other RECs should also be praised for enabling studies involving other sensitive topics like sexual orientation in LGBTQ+ child participants (Todd, 2024). Our work is particularly timely given the recent clarification the HRA released in July 2026 (HRA, 2026).

The quantitative data we collected provide preliminary insights into cannabis use profiles during early adolescence. Quantitative data reveal kind of cannabis was consumed, where it was consumed, methods of administration, quantities consumed, motives for use, and other drug use, as well as illuminating norms and harm perceptions amongst non-users. This demonstrates the value of a more detailed cannabis-and drug-focused survey.

We recruited 11 frequent cannabis users in our qualitative study, with a current average frequency of 3 days/week and an average age of cannabis use onset of just over 13 years. This is a young and potentially vulnerable sample. Within a reasonable, less-than-one-hour interview length, the qualitative interview provided contextual information about cannabis use initiation, environments, motives, personal experiences, and attitudes. These findings illustrate the level of detail and nuance that can be feasibly collected from this age group. For example, the frequent disclosure of difficult and drug-related experiences by users and experiences of being offered cannabis but rejecting it by non-users showed the kind of rich data we can receive from early adolescents. Taken together, specialised research into early adolescents' drug use provided unique findings that went beyond binary or other simple measures offered by large epidemiological studies. These studies show the promise that these research efforts have for uncovering important, undiscovered elements of early adolescent cannabis use.

Although we have preliminarily demonstrated feasibility, several limitations remain. The number of participants who consented through the Gillick-competent pathway was small. One explanation is that cannabis-using participants did not have to disclose their cannabis use to their parents because they did not have to be cannabis users to take part, which is a strength of the study design in the first place. Nonetheless, the assessment of Gillick competence can be improved by ensuring participants understand this option from the start and reducing burden of the competency interview. Another concern is our use of school assemblies to recruit, which were about the potential harms of cannabis use and were delivered by the principal investigator (WL). We showed that we can approach many under-16s with cannabis use experience through school assemblies and it is a cost-effective way of reaching hundreds of teenagers. However, it comes with the limitation of potentially biasing the students and potential participants into not disclosing cannabis use to the cannabis education expert, who is also the researcher. Furthermore, past literature reported strong correlations between frequent cannabis use and truancy and exclusion from mainstream schools (McCrystal et al., 2005; McCrystal et al., 2007), although we did also advertise in alternative provision schools to address this issue.

Conclusion

Many under-16 early adolescents in the UK have used cannabis. However, research about cannabis with this age group in the UK is extremely limited. We prove we can feasibly recruit these participants and study cannabis use in detail, despite ethical and logistical challenges.

These findings provide a methodological basis for larger studies that investigate the onset, continuation, consequences, and treatment of cannabis use during early adolescence. It also contributes to the growing literature on how to study sensitive topics among <16-year-old children.
