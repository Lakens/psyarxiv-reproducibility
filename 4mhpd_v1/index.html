What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

To examine these factors, incarcerated participants completed the Prison Climate Questionnaire (PCQ), assessing perceptions of autonomy, safety, relationships, and opportunities within prison (Bosma et al., 2020).

Only men were recruited to the prisoner groups.

All eligible candidates received oral and written information and provided written informed consent.

For incarcerated participants, consent procedures were conducted without staff present to minimize perceived links with the judicial system, and participants were explicitly informed of the research team’s independence from the penitentiary and judicial system.

On average, outgoing participants had spent 1,378 days in detention (SD = 1,411).

Incoming participants were significantly younger than outgoing participants (t = -2.34, p = .027).

Incoming participants were relatively evenly distributed across remand/pre-trial detention (37.9%), sentences of 1-3 years (20.7%), 3-5 years (20.7%), and 5-10 years (20.7%).

In contrast, outgoing participants were predominantly serving longer sentences, with 69.2% reporting sentence lengths of 5-10 years.

The most frequently reported types of offence in incoming participants were violent offences (27.6%), followed by drug-related, non-violent property and sexual offences (24.1% each).

During the encoding phase (Fig. 2A), participants were introduced to four fictional characters: two with incarceration experience and two without.

Narratives were randomized across sessions so that all five narratives per group were presented across the longitudinal follow-up.

Stimuli represented ethnic diversity, with character’s ethnicity (Afro-descent or European descent) alternating across sessions so that all participants were exposed to both ethnic groups across the longitudinal assessment.

After the encoding phase, participants completed memory checks requiring them to match narratives with the corresponding faces to ensure they correctly remembered the identity of the characters (Fig. 2B).

To familiarize themselves with the structure of the task, participants completed twelve practice trials before the main experiment, using socially neutral character narratives and scenarios.

The absence of an overall group difference in social choices and the absence of a group-specific longitudinal FMθ change (Group × Session) in outgoing participants were not statistically equivalent to zero (TOST p > .05).

The longitudinal analyses in this group were therefore underpowered, and null effects among outgoing participants are consequently reported as inconclusive on the basis of equivalence tests.

To facilitate longitudinal follow-up, participants provided contact details on paper-based forms.

Participants still in prison were contacted through institutional channels, whereas released participants were contacted using the information provided at baseline.

Incoming participants were notified of upcoming follow-up sessions through letters sent via the prison communication system (Prison Cloud) or via institutional intermediaries.

At approximately 3 months post-release, participants were contacted to schedule follow-up assessments.

In contrast, for outgoing participants, all participants were sentenced and nearing release, making this distinction less relevant.

research_questions • It has been repeatedly shown that perceiving an individual as an outgroup member tends to reduce prosociality towards that individual compared to ingroup members (Stürmer et al., 2005, Hein et al., 2010, Pech et al., 2022), an effect that has been referred to as the intergroup prosociality bias. Indeed, prisoners are biased towards intergroup prosociality, as they display behaviors consistent with in-group favoritism (Balafoutas et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). However, given the absence of longitudinal evidence and the heterogeneity in identification with the inmate group, we formulate bidirectional hypotheses regarding changes in intergroup behavior over time.
H1.A : For individuals entering prison (incoming group), incarceration may either strengthen or weaken intergroup bias. Specifically, over the course of 8 months, prisoners may (a) show progressively reduced prosocial behavior toward outgroup individuals (i.e. who have never been incarcerated) and increased prosocial behavior toward ingroup individuals (i.e. who currently are or have been incarcerated), alongside increased antisocial behavior toward outgroup individuals and decreased antisocial behavior toward ingroup individuals; or (b) exhibit the opposite pattern, reflecting reduced identification with the inmate group.
H1.B : For individuals leaving prison (outgoing group), changes in intergroup bias following release may also be bidirectional. Over the course of 8 months after release, former prisoners may (a) show increased prosocial behavior toward outgroup individuals and decreased prosocial behavior toward ingroup individuals, alongside decreased antisocial behavior toward outgroup individuals and increased antisocial behavior toward ingroup individuals; or (b) maintain or reinforce ingroup-oriented patterns, depending on their level of identification with the inmate group and their reintegration context.

• Choosing between an individual from an outgroup (i.e., someone one does not identify with) and an ingroup (i.e., someone one identifies with) involves cognitive conflict (Pech & Caspar, 2022; Pech et al., 2022). Previous EEG studies have shown that participants experience greater conflict, as indexed by increased midfrontal theta power, when selecting an outgroup individual over an ingroup individual in prosocial contexts, suggesting that prosocial behavior toward outgroup members may be less intuitive (Pech & Caspar, 2022; Pech et al., 2022).
H2.A : At the neural level, we will examine how cognitive conflict associated with prosocial and antisocial decisions evolves over time in both incoming (during incarceration) and outgoing (after release) individuals. Given the absence of longitudinal evidence in these populations, and the paradigm including antisocial decisions being new, we formulate bidirectional hypotheses. Specifically, we predict that over time, individuals may exhibit either an increase or a decrease in cognitive conflict when making antisocial or prosocial decisions, as reflected by changes in midfrontal theta activity.
H2.B : In addition, we hypothesize that cognitive conflict will differ depending on whether decisions target ingroup or outgroup individuals. However, because group identification may vary substantially across individuals and across time (e.g., during incarceration vs. reintegration), we adopt a bidirectional hypothesis for both groups. That is, antisocial decisions may become either more or less conflictual when directed toward outgroup compared to ingroup individuals, and similarly, prosocial decisions toward outgroup members may elicit either increased or decreased conflict over time, in both groups.
H2.C : Importantly, we expect that these effects may follow different trajectories in incoming and outgoing groups. In incoming individuals, incarceration may alter the salience of ingroup–outgroup distinctions, whereas in outgoing individuals, these effects may persist, diminish, or reverse during reintegration. Overall, this approach allows us to test whether changes in cognitive conflict reflect adaptation to the prison environment, disengagement from it, or individual differences in social identification.
study_design_overview The study is a repeated-measures design with time as a within-subject factor (Baseline, 4 months, 8 months). Participants include both incoming detainees assessed shortly after incarceration and outgoing detainees assessed prior to release, recruited from French and Flemish prisons in Belgium. In addition, a community control group is included to account for repeated task exposure effects, with one 4-month interval re-test only. Incoming participants are recruited within the first three to seven weeks following incarceration, whereas outgoing participants are assessed a month prior to release. All incoming and outgoing participants are followed longitudinally at 4- and 8-month intervals.
Participants complete a modified version of the intended prosociality task (Pech et al., 2022), lasting approximately 20 minutes. During this task, participants are introduced to four fictional characters: two representing an ingroup (individuals with current or past incarceration experience) and two representing an outgroup (individuals without incarceration experience and from a different socio-economic background). At the beginning of the task, participants rate the extent to which they identify with each character.
Participants are then presented with a series of prosocial and antisocial scenarios, followed by a pair of characters. These pairs either consist of either one ingroup and one outgroup member (free-choice condition) or two individuals from the same group (forced-choice condition). For each trial, participants decide toward which individual they would direct the prosocial or antisocial action.
Behavioral measures include the proportion of choices directed toward ingroup versus outgroup individuals (separately for prosocial and antisocial contexts) and reaction times for each decision. Neural activity is recorded throughout the task using electroencephalography (EEG).
Behavioral and neural data are analyzed at the trial level using linear and generalized linear mixed-effects models ((G)LMMs), accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data (trials nested within participants) and variability in the number of observations across groups. Neural analyses focus on response-locked midfrontal theta power as index of cognitive conflict. Behavioral analyses examine choice patterns using the same set of predictors, including decision type (prosocial vs. antisocial), session, and group (incoming, outgoing).

The assignment of fictional characters is also randomized across participants. Across sessions, scenarios are randomly sampled from a predefined pool, such that participants are exposed to overlapping but not identical sets of scenarios over time.

sample_size_rationale The target sample size was determined based on a series of a priori power analyses conducted using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007), reflecting the different components of the study design.

This analysis used a mixed repeated-measures framework with 2 groups and 6 repeated measurements, reflecting the combination of the 3-level session factor and the 2-level scenario factor. For detecting a medium effect size (f = .25), with 95% power, the required total sample size was estimated at 28 participants (i.e. 14 per group).
Second, an additional power analysis included the community control group for additional analyses of practice effects. Because control participants were assessed at two sessions, this analysis used a mixed repeated-measures framework with 3 groups and 4 repeated measurements, corresponding to the 2-level session factor and the 2-level scenario factor. Under the same assumptions, the required total sample size was estimated at 45 participants (i.e. 15 per group).
However, this project is part of a larger study for which, keeping all other factors constant, an additional power analysis was conducted focusing on within-subject effects across 3 sessions, with 2 conditions (i.e. each task of the study includes at least 2 conditions), and was performed separately within each detainee group. This analysis yielded a required sample size of 28 participants per group.
Given the high attrition expected in longitudinal prison research, substantially larger samples are recruited at baseline to ensure sufficient statistical power at follow-up.

For the practice effect analysis, fixed effects will include Group (3-level), Session (2-level), Scenario (2-level), and their interactions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Social decision-making is central to successful reintegration following incarceration, yet whether imprisonment changes how people treat individuals with versus without a criminal history remains unclear. In this preregistered longitudinal study, newly detained (incoming, n = 34) and newly released (outgoing, n = 13) individuals completed a social decision task with EEG at three sessions over eight months, choosing whether to direct prosocial or antisocial actions toward characters described as having an incarceration history (here termed incarceration-history targets) or not (community targets). Frontal midline theta (FMθ) was recorded as an index of choice-related conflict. Prison-climate and quality-of-life measures were also collected. Across groups, participants identified more strongly with (formerly) incarcerated individuals, yet directed more antisocial intentions toward incarceration-history targets and more prosocial intentions toward community targets. Incoming participants showed a reduction in prosocial community intentions after four months that rebounded by eight months. Incoming participants showed higher FMθ than outgoing participants early in incarceration, with a progressive reduction over time in all groups, consistent with reduced decision conflict. Exploratory analyses linked social intentions to prison-environment and well-being factors. Because the target types differed in criminal history and, inevitably, in how positively they were described, these effects are best understood as responses to stigma linked with incarceration-history rather than to group membership. These findings suggest that incarceration and release involve distinct adaptations in social decision-making toward (ex-)prisoners, with dissociable behavioural and neural markers.

Introduction

Attitudes toward people with a criminal history are the social dimension most directly implicated in reintegration. A criminal record marks a person as a member of a devalued category, attracting stigma that constrains access to housing, employment, and relationships and that (ex-)prisoners may internalise themselves (LeBel, 2008; Moore et al., 2016; Pager, 2003). Reintegration therefore depends not only on how the community treats those who have offended, but on how incarcerated and recently released individuals themselves come to regard, approach, or avoid others who share, or do not share, that history (Abo et al., 2022; Keene et al., 2018; Visher & Travis, 2011). Accordingly, the present study operationalises this social dimension as prosocial and antisocial intentions directed toward characters described as having a criminal history versus a conventional life, and asks whether incarceration and release shift these attitudes linked with incarceration-history. Social identity offers one interpretive lens on such attitudes as well.

According to Social Identity Theory, part of the self-concept derives from group membership, and social categorization fosters preference for one's own group and, at times, prejudice toward others (Tajfel & Turner, 1986), reflecting a basic need to belong (Baumeister & Leary, 1995; Cacioppo & Patrick, 2008). Such tendencies may become particularly pronounced in environments characterized by exclusion, uncertainty, and social threat, such as incarceration. As such, prisons represent a particularly relevant context for examining how social identities emerge, evolve, and shape interpersonal behaviour, with direct implications for reintegration. Examining how incarcerated individuals orient prosocial and antisocial behaviour toward fellow incarcerated and non-incarcerated individuals may therefore provide important insights into the social adaptations associated with imprisonment and release.

Imprisonment is characterized by social exclusion (Aureli et al., 2020; Traversa et al., 2026), where prisoners are physically separated from society (Schliehe et al., 2022). As they are forced to cohabit with other prisoners under constrained and often overcrowded conditions (Schaefer et al., 2017), prisoners are progressively embedded within a closed social environment in which fellow prisoners may become one of the few sources of affiliation and social support (Traversa et al., 2025). Consequently, incarceration may foster the development of a distinct prisoner identity, potentially strengthening identification with other incarcerated individuals while simultaneously increasing distance toward nonincarcerated individuals. Therefore, an important scientific and societal challenge is to understand how incarceration shapes social identity and, in turn, influences prosocial and antisocial tendencies toward individuals with and without incarceration history. Criminal Social Identity (CSI) applies SIT to offending and incarceration contexts (Boduszek, Adamson, Shevlin, Mallett, et al., 2013). Previous work suggests that CSI is shaped by complex interactions between individual and environmental factors, including peer relationships, social exclusion, stigma, and prior experiences of rejection (Boduszek, Adamson, Shevlin, Mallett, et al., 2013). Importantly, these processes are unlikely to be static. Rather, they may be dynamically influenced by the prison environment itself, particularly during critical transitions such as prison entry and release.

Despite growing interest in prisoner social identity and prosociality, important gaps remain in the literature. First, most existing studies rely on self-report measures assessing identity, trust, stigma, or prosociality (Boduszek, Adamson, Shevlin, Mallett, et al., 2013; Mosso et al., 2024; Traversa et al., 2026), while relatively few investigate actual social decision-making behaviour using ecological or incentivized paradigms (Birkeland et al., 2014; Chmura et al., 2017; Khadjavi & Lange, 2013). Second, studies examining prosocial or antisocial tendencies in prisoners produced inconsistent findings. Some studies found levels of altruism and cooperation comparable to non-incarcerated populations, both when prisoners interacted with other prisoners and with individuals from the general population (Birkeland et al., 2014; Chmura et al., 2017). In contrast, other work reported reduced cooperation, trust, and reciprocity among offenders both when interacting with individuals outside the criminal justice system (Clark et al., 2015), or with incarcerated offenders (Scheeff et al., 2018). Conversely, several studies suggest that prisoners may display increased prosociality under specific contextual conditions. For example, incarcerated women showed higher cooperation rates with anonymous partners than students in Prisoner's Dilemma paradigms (Khadjavi & Lange, 2013), while organized crime members displayed particularly strong cooperation and loyalty compared to ordinary offenders and non-criminal controls (Nese et al., 2018). However, only a limited number of studies have explicitly examined the intergroup dimension of prisoner social behaviour. Among these, Guo et al. (2020) reported evidence of ingroup favouritism, showing that prisoners exerted greater effort when helping individuals with a shared prisoner identity compared to individuals from outside the prison. These inconsistencies may partly stem from the fact that most studies do not explicitly consider the intergroup context in which social decisions occur. Specifically, little is known about whether incarceration shapes the distinction prisoners make between individuals with and without incarceration history.

Importantly, intergroup biases are expressed not only behaviourally but also neurally.

Research on intergroup empathy bias consistently shows reduced neural responses to outgroup relative to ingroup suffering (Amodio & Cikara, 2021; Vollberg & Cikara, 2018), and helping or empathizing with outgroup members appears to induce greater cognitive conflict than ingroup-directed behaviour (Amodio et al., 2008). Past electroencephalography (EEG) research has consistently shown that stronger cognitive conflict is associated with increased frontal midline theta (FMθ) activity (4-8 Hz), a neural marker implicated in conflict monitoring and cognitive control processes (Cavanagh & Frank, 2014; Cohen & Cavanagh, 2011; Cohen, 2014; Nigbur et al., 2012). Extending these findings to prosocial decision-making, Pech and Caspar (2024) showed that participants exhibited increased FMθ activity and longer reaction times during prosocial intentions toward outgroup compared to ingroup individuals, consistent with greater cognitive conflict when helping outgroup members. Related work further demonstrated reduced neural empathic responses toward outgroup pain and that it can be voluntarily modulated depending on motivational and contextual factors (Caspar et al., 2024).

This preregistered longitudinal study investigates how incarceration and release influence prosocial and antisocial intentions toward people with versus without a criminal history, and the neural activity associated with conflict and control demands during these choices. Newly detained ("incoming") and newly released ("outgoing") individuals were followed over eight months at three sessions, to test whether these attitudes shift during early imprisonment and reintegration. Participants performed a modified version of the intended-prosociality task (Pech & Caspar, 2024), directing prosocial and antisocial actions toward characters described as having a criminal history or a conventional life. Neural activity was simultaneously recorded using EEG, with analyses focusing on FMθ activity. The highly interdependent nature of prison life may promote solidarity and affiliation among incarcerated individuals, potentially strengthening identification with fellow prisoners and increasing prosocial intentions toward fellow prisoners and distance from the wider community (Boduszek, Adamson, Shevlin, Mallett, et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2020; Jetten et al., 2017). Conversely, prisoners may actively distance themselves from the prisoner identity because of its stigmatized and socially devalued nature (Mosso et al., 2024), and orient toward the community, especially after release, when stigma and disrupted relationships are common barriers (Keene et al., 2018; Visher & Travis, 2011), and a sense of belonging supports reintegration (Abo et al., 2022).

Given these competing theoretical possibilities, bidirectional hypotheses regarding the effects of incarceration and release were preregistered.

Importantly, individuals differ considerably in their pre-existing psychosocial characteristics, which can impact their experience of the prison environment (Liebling & Maruna, 2005). Social tendencies may therefore also be associated with perceived prison climate and reintegration conditions. To examine these factors, incarcerated participants completed the Prison Climate Questionnaire (PCQ), assessing perceptions of autonomy, safety, relationships, and opportunities within prison (Bosma et al., 2020). Perceptions of post-release conditions were evaluated with the Good Lives Questionnaire (GLQ), which captures dimensions related to quality of life, social integration, and access to meaningful resources after imprisonment (Harper et al., 2021). Exploratory analyses investigated whether individual differences in individual and environmental factors were associated with longitudinal changes in social decision-making. Overall, the current study aims to provide the first longitudinal investigation of how incarceration shapes prosocial and antisocial behaviour at both behavioural and neural levels. Using experimental social neuroscience methods, the study examines whether imprisonment strengthens affiliation among prisoners and reinforces exclusion toward the general population, or instead produces more complex patterns of social adaptation.

Methods and Materials

Participants Three hundred and fifty (N = 350) male prisoners from 15 penitentiary establishments in Belgium participated in the study. Only men were recruited to the prisoner groups. Women make up roughly 4% of the Belgian prison population (World Prison Brief, 2024) and are held in only a small number of establishments. Therefore, recruiting women would have introduced bias on gender, and their concentration in a few prisons would have confounded gender with prison site. The prisoner groups were therefore restricted to men. The study protocol was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) on August 10, 2023 (DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/M4TS9). Power analyses with G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) for the Group (2level: Incoming/Outgoing) × Session (3-level) × Scenario (2-level: Pro-/Anti-Social) interactions, using a repeated-measures approximation, indicated a total target sample size of 28 participants (14 per group) to detect medium-sized effects (f = .25) with 95% power.

However, G*Power misspecifies designs that combine between-and within-subject interactions(Brysbaert, 2019), and our confirmatory analyses used linear mixed-effects models (LMM). Therefore, additional simulation-based power analyses were conducted for the actual model. This analysis was not part of the preregistered plan and is reported as a deviation (see Supplementary S1). To avoid circularity, the assumed effect size was fixed independently of the observed results. Simulating the Group × Session × Scenario interaction on the binomial choice model (64 free-choice trials per scenario per session), and assuming a small interaction (odds ratio = 1.5) with moderate between-participant variability in the session change, 80% power was reached at approximately 30 participants per group. Main effects and lower-order interactions required fewer participants. Because longitudinal prison research entails high attrition, baseline recruitment targets were set substantially higher than these completing-sample targets. Details on sample size estimation, recruitment and inclusion/exclusion criteria are provided in Supplementary S1.

Communication with prisoners was facilitated by prison staff to arrange appointments. All eligible candidates received oral and written information and provided written informed consent. For incarcerated participants, consent procedures were conducted without staff present to minimize perceived links with the judicial system, and participants were explicitly informed of the research team's independence from the penitentiary and judicial system.

Incoming and outgoing prisoners completed behavioural tasks with EEG recordings and questionnaires at each session and received financial compensation (€10 for questionnaires, €10 for tasks). The study was part of a broader longitudinal project on executive functioning in incarceration, including multiple cognitive paradigms.

Incoming prisoners were assessed within four to seven weeks following incarceration (M = 31.4 days, SD = 13.9). This recruitment window allowed the baseline to capture early-stage incarceration while ensuring sufficient stability in participants' status. The outgoing group consisted of prisoners assessed shortly before release (M = 19.7 days before release, SD = 35.0). To specifically examine the effects of prolonged incarceration, only participants who had been detained for at least six months prior to baseline were included. On average, outgoing participants had spent 1,378 days in detention (SD = 1,411). To facilitate follow-up, several retention strategies were employed (see Supplementary S2 for details, including attrition rates). The right to withdraw from the study at any given time without any consequences for the participants was emphasized. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ghent University (2023-085A).

Complete datasets with the three experimental sessions were collected for 34 incoming and 13 outgoing participants. Mean S1-S2 retest intervals were 129.7 days (SD = 19.3) for incoming, and 139.7 days (SD = 23.7) for outgoing participants. Mean S2-S3 retest intervals were 134.4 days (SD = 38.9) for incoming and 150.4 days (SD = 44.8) for outgoing participants.

For EEG analyses, participants with excessive noise or insufficient usable trials were excluded, resulting in final longitudinal EEG samples of 29 incoming and 11 outgoing participants.

Descriptive statistics for individual and incarceration-related variables are reported in Supplementary S4. Incoming participants were significantly younger than outgoing participants (t = -2.34, p = .027). Age was therefore included as a covariate in statistical analyses. Education levels did not differ significantly between groups (p = .762) with the majority of both incoming and outgoing participants reporting secondary education (58.6% and 53.8%, respectively), whereas smaller proportions reported no formal education, primary education, or higher education.

Sentence-related characteristics differed significantly between groups. Among incoming participants, 62.1% were convicted at baseline, whereas 37.9% were on remand or pre-trial detention. Sentence lengths were significantly longer in outgoing compared to incoming participants (U = 69.0, p < .001). Incoming participants were relatively evenly distributed across remand/pre-trial detention (37.9%), sentences of 1-3 years (20.7%), 3-5 years (20.7%), and 5-10 years (20.7%). In contrast, outgoing participants were predominantly serving longer sentences, with 69.2% reporting sentence lengths of 5-10 years. Incoming participants with sentences shorter than one year were intentionally excluded during recruitment in order to reduce attrition risk across longitudinal follow-ups. Re-incarceration rates did not differ significantly between incoming and outgoing participants (U = 164.0, p = .498). Among incoming participants, 31.0% reported only the current incarceration, whereas 27.6% reported two incarcerations and 41.4% reported three to five incarcerations. Similarly, among outgoing participants, 30.8% reported only the current incarceration, 23.1% reported two incarcerations, and 46.2% reported three to five incarcerations. Overall, repeated incarceration was common in both groups (incoming: 69.0%; outgoing: 69.2%). This variable reflects participants' self-reported history of previous incarcerations and should not be interpreted as a measure of recidivism, for which rates can vary depending on the definition, follow-up period, and outcome considered.

Participants could report multiple offences, therefore percentages should be interpreted independently. The most frequently reported types of offence in incoming participants were violent offences (27.6%), followed by drug-related, non-violent property and sexual offences (24.1% each). Among outgoing participants, the most commonly reported were non-violent property and sexual offences (38.5% each), followed by drug-related (30.8%), life and violent offences (23.1% each).

Procedure and Material

Testing sessions within prisons were conducted in available rooms, including classrooms, consultation rooms, and activity spaces (Fig. 1). Follow-up sessions outside prison took place at participants' homes, social housing centers, workplaces, or rented testing spaces. Each session lasted approximately two hours and included five cognitive tasks with EEG recordings (1-1.5 hours), followed by 30-60 minutes of questionnaires. Tasks were administered on laptops using PsychoPy (Python 3.10). Two laptops were used simultaneously during data collection (Lenovo ThinkBook 15 G3 ACL and Dell Latitude 5530; 15.6-inch screens; 1920×1080 resolution; 60 Hz refresh rate). Responses were provided using a standard computer mouse to minimize head movements and facilitate participation for individuals with limited computer experience. Task order was randomized across participants to reduce fatigue and order effects. Questionnaires were completed on paper before or after the tasks depending on practical constraints and participant availability.

Social Task

The Intended Pro-/Anti-Sociality Task was adapted from Pech and Caspar (2024) to investigate social decision-making towards individuals with and without incarceration history. This task was designed to accommodate participants who were less familiar with computer use than the convenience samples typically recruited in psychological research, and was therefore specifically adapted to the present population. During the encoding phase (Fig. 2A), participants were introduced to four fictional characters: two with incarceration experience and two without. Participants read short narratives about each character and rated to which extent they identified with them on a 4-point scale ("No", "Not really", "Partly", "Yes").

Narratives were randomized across sessions so that all five narratives per group were presented across the longitudinal follow-up. Stimuli represented ethnic diversity, with character's ethnicity (Afro-descent or European descent) alternating across sessions so that all participants were exposed to both ethnic groups across the longitudinal assessment. Face stimuli were randomly selected from the Chicago Face Database (Ma et al., 2015). Crucially, the incarceration-history and community targets narratives differed not only in incarceration history but, inevitably, in valence. Indeed, characters with incarceration experience were described through a criminal record, including an offence, sometimes along with a sentence, whereas characters without incarceration experience were described through a conventional life history. This mirrors how incarceration-history information is encountered in everyday life. However, it also means that target group status and the positivity of the descriptions are intertwined by design. For clarity, the two target types with incarceration experience are referred to as incarceration-history characters and characters without such a history as community targets. These labels denote the incarceration-history contrast between the targets throughout. After the encoding phase, participants completed memory checks requiring them to match narratives with the corresponding faces to ensure they correctly remembered the identity of the characters (Fig. 2B). Incorrect or omitted responses had to be repeated until correct. Characters were reintroduced twice, following each break, to maintain familiarity throughout the task. To familiarize themselves with the structure of the task, participants completed twelve practice trials before the main experiment, using socially neutral character narratives and scenarios.

The main task consisted of selecting toward which character an action should be directed (Fig. 2C). Four prosocial (e.g. "These two people have asked your help for moving. Who are you helping move?") and four antisocial action scenarios (e.g. "These two people are on the opposing team during a football game. Who do you tackle, so that you hurt them and they cannot play?") were randomly sampled from a list of 16 scenarios and alternated across sessions so that all scenarios were eventually presented during the longitudinal study. Each scenario was repeated twice across 16 blocks. Scenarios were displayed for 10 seconds, accompanied by illustrative images and optional audio recordings to reduce cognitive load.

Examples of characters' narratives and scenarios are presented in Supplementary S3.

Each block contained 16 trials corresponding to all combinations of character pairings. During each trial, participants chose between the two characters on screen by clicking on their name with the mouse. After each trial, a fixation cross was displayed for 0.8-1.2 seconds and the mouse cursor was reset to the center of the screen to ensure sufficient time for EEG analyses.

Trials included free-choice conditions (incarceration-history vs. community target) and forced-choice conditions (same target presented twice). The task included 128 free-choice trials and 128 forced-choice trials, for a total of 256 trials (~20 minutes). Forced-choice trials were included to facilitate comparison of neural activity associated with decisions in pro-and antisocial contexts towards incarceration-history and community target, in case some participants never selected one character in some experimental conditions. After reading one scenario, they were instructed to select the individual toward whom they would direct the action. Exploratory Questionnaire Measures

Participants in each group completed a series of questionnaires examining prison and postrelease life conditions, well-being, and individual characteristics. These measures were included as exploratory predictors of social decision-making at the behavioural and neural levels.

Perceptions of the prison environment were assessed using the validated Prison Climate Questionnaire (PCQ), administered at baseline in both cohorts and at follow-ups in incoming participants only. The PCQ evaluates several dimensions of prison climate, including autonomy, safety, meaningful activities, contact with the outside, and relationships with staff and fellow prisoners (Bosma et al., 2020). Participants also reported perceived detention severity and psychological distress using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6).

Outgoing participants reported post-release quality of life using the Good Lives Questionnaire (GLQ) at S2-S3 to evaluate five domains including inner peace, agency and energy, social connectedness, leisure, and spirituality (Harper et al., 2021).

Baseline assessments further included demographic and detention-related variables, while substance use and incarceration history were collected longitudinally. Reward and punishment sensitivity were measured using the Behavioural Inhibition System/Behavioural Activation System (BIS/BAS) Scale (Carver & White, 1994). Additional methodological details are provided in Supplementary S5.

EEG Recording and Processing

EEG was recorded using a 32-channel electrode cap with ActiveTwo system (BioSemi) system arranged according to the international 10-20 layout and sampled at 2048 Hz. Preprocessing was performed in MNE-Python (Gramfort et al., 2013). The full procedure is detailed in Supplementary S6. Prior to preprocessing, raw recordings were visually inspected to remove excessive noise. Data were then processed using an automated pipeline. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was performed to remove ocular and muscular artifacts.

Components were classified automatically using ICLabel (Li et al., 2022; Pion-Tonachini et al., 2019) and only components labelled as "brain" or "other" with a probability ≥ .70 . Data were re-referenced offline using the reference electrode standardization technique (REST).

For time-frequency analyses, data were filtered between 1-40 Hz and segmented into response-locked epochs (-2 to 2 s relative to the keypress). Trials containing artifacts were rejected using automated outlier detection. Time-frequency representations (TFRs) were computed at the single-trial level and FMθ was subsequently extracted within a pre-response window (time: -1.0,-0.25 s ; frequency: 3-7Hz) identified from grand-average activity (Fig. 3) using a post-response baseline (0.0-1.0s). Analyses focused on fronto-central channels (FC1, FC2, Fz, Cz), commonly associated with FMθ activity (Cavanagh & Frank, 2014; Cohen, 2014).

Participants contributing fewer than 10 valid trials per condition or fewer than 30 valid trials overall were excluded from EEG analyses. Statistical Analysis

As a validation check of the task manipulation, character identification ratings were analysed using an LMM to verify whether participants identified more strongly with targets with or without incarceration history across sessions and groups, fit on the 47 participants with complete data at all three sessions (34 incoming, 13 outgoing).

The main behavioural outcome consisted of trial-level incarceration-history versus community target choices during free-choice trials. Choices were analysed using a generalized LMM (GLMM) with a binomial link function. Fixed effects included Group (incoming/outgoing), Session (S1-S3), Scenario Type (pro-/anti-social), and their interactions.

Decision times (DT) and mean-centred age were included as covariates. Random effects included participant-level intercepts and slopes for within-subject factors Session and Scenario Type. The model was fit on the same 47 participants (34 incoming and 13 outgoing).

The main neural outcome was trial-level FMθ amplitude during forced-choice trials. FMθ was analysed using an LMM including fixed effects of Group, Session, Scenario Type, Target (incarceration-history/community), and their interactions, with DT and age included as covariates. Random effects included participant-level intercepts and slopes for Session, Scenario Type, and Target. Prior to modelling, trial-level FMθ values were screened for withinparticipant outliers, excluded when at least two of three criteria were met: an absolute zscore above 3, a deviation exceeding three median absolute deviations, or a value beyond 1.5 times the interquartile range. To assess the robustness of the FMθ results to this exclusion, all neural models were also refit including outlier trials; these sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplementary S8. Planned contrasts were examined using estimated marginal means (EMMs) with Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons. The model was fit on the 40 participants with usable EEG at all three sessions (29 incoming and 11 outgoing).

Exploratory analyses examined the influence of questionnaire-derived individual and contextual predictors separately in incoming and outgoing participants. Missing questionnaire and covariate data were handled using multiple imputation. For outgoing participants, PCQ variables reflected baseline (pre-release) assessments, whereas GLQ variables from the first (S2) and second (S3) post-release assessments were evaluated as separate candidate predictors. Candidate predictors were first tested individually into behavioural and neural mixed-effects models as interactions with the primary experimental factors (Session × Scenario for behavioural analyses; Session × Scenario × Target for neural analyses). For each predictor, model fit was compared against the corresponding baseline model using pooled D2 tests across imputed datasets. To account for multiple testing across predictors, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied separately within each analysis.

For predictors surviving FDR correction, follow-up estimated marginal means and simple slope analyses were conducted to identify the specific Session × Scenario (and Session × Scenario × Target for FMθ) conditions driving the effects.

Because a non-significant result cannot by itself indicate the absence of an effect, the nonsignificant effects were additionally examined with equivalence tests (two one-sided tests) (Lakens, 2017; Lakens et al., 2018). These tests were not part of the preregistered analysis plan and the equivalence bounds, procedure, and full results are provided in Supplementary S11. Effects that were neither statistically significant nor statistically equivalent were interpreted as inconclusive. Full details regarding models specifications, including imputation procedures and exploratory analyses are provided in Supplementary S7.

Discussion

The main objective was to examine how incarceration and release shape prosocial and antisocial intentions directed toward people with versus without incarceration history, combining behavioural and neural markers. Although both incoming and outgoing participants consistently identified more strongly with characters who had a criminal history than with community characters, this stronger identification did not translate into uniform behavioural favouritism toward them. Instead, the results revealed a dissociation between subjective identification, social behaviour, and conflict-related neural activity.

Across groups and sessions, participants identified more strongly with incarcerated than nonincarcerated characters, and this pattern was already present shortly after incarceration in incoming participants and remained stable in outgoing participants after release (equivalence tests). The design cannot establish whether this identification follows incarceration or precedes it, as individuals entering prison may already identify with marginalized or justiceinvolved groups. CSI is thought to develop progressively through pre-existing peer affiliations and marginalization, with incarceration and prisonization potentially reinforcing and stabilizing it over time (Boduszek, Adamson, Shevlin, Mallett, et al., 2013; Boduszek & Hyland, 2011; Walters, 2003). Besides, criminalized populations often remain tied to these networks after release for material support, housing, and protection against isolation (Michel, 2024).

Identification with (formerly) incarcerated others may therefore reflect both pre-existing social positioning and subsequent adaptation to incarceration. Importantly, this stronger identification did not translate into a behavioural preference for (formerly) incarcerated targets. On the contrary, participants directed prosocial intentions toward community targets and antisocial intentions toward incarceration-history targets, so that behaviour tracked the valence of the targets' descriptions rather than the group with which participants more strongly identified. A strict social-identity account would predict the opposite, namely an ingroup favouritism toward incarceration-history targets. The observed pattern can be explained by a incarceration-history identity as ambivalent or stigmatized. It could also be that, because target type was confounded with the valence of the descriptions, the effects observed reflect responses to the stigma attached to an incarceration history rather than to group membership per se. The social-identity account is therefore one possible interpretation rather than a demonstrated mechanism (Balliet et al., 2014; Boduszek, Adamson, Shevlin, Mallett, et al., 2013; Tajfel & Turner, 1986). Read through this lens, several features of the data are informative. Identification did not entail unconditional solidarity with (formerly) incarcerated targets or hostility toward non-incarcerated others. Rather, it appears an incarceration-history identity may be ambivalent, providing belonging while carrying stigma and social devaluation (Jetten et al., 2017; Mosso et al., 2024; Traversa et al., 2026). Higher antisocial intentions toward incarceration-history targets do not necessarily reflect hostility.

Instead, they may be perceived as less socially costly, reflecting expectations of reciprocity or tolerance among familiar others (Balliet et al., 2014; Imada et al., 2024). Indeed, several participants spontaneously reported assigning unpleasant actions to individuals "who would not mind", although anecdotal, it suggests perceived relational safety rather than dislike. Such orientations are nonetheless highly context-dependent (Durrheim et al., 2023). Prison environments, marked by overcrowding, tension, and chronic stress (Beyens, 2019; Haney, 2001), may further shape these dynamics, as prolonged forced cohabitation can erode idealized solidarity and heighten interpersonal fatigue or distrust (Haney, 2006). Finally, testing by non-incarcerated researchers may have prompted identity management or socially desirable responding, including avoidance of overt alignment with a stigmatized "criminal" identity.

Whereas the behavioural findings were specific to the type of target and the valence of the scenario, the neural results operated at a general group level. One reliable effect was a group difference as incoming participants showed higher FMθ amplitude than outgoing participants, across conditions and independent of whether the target had a criminal history. Because this difference did not vary by scenario or target, it is unlikely to index the processing of incarceration-history stigma itself. It more plausibly reflects the overall cognitive demand of the early-incarceration period. Elevated FMθ is a well-established marker of conflict monitoring and cognitive control (Cavanagh & Frank, 2014; Cohen, 2014). Its elevation early in incarceration is consistent with a state of heightened vigilance, uncertainty, and effortful regulation during initial adaptation to the prison environment. This corresponds to the timewindow in which incoming participants also showed the most behavioural movement in how they oriented toward community targets. From this perspective, the pattern is consistent with greater general cognitive engagement during early incarceration rather than target-specific conflict processing. On the other hand, the valence-driven orientation toward specific targets is expressed behaviourally. The smaller group difference observed near release would be consistent with a gradual settling of these adaptive demands. However, because this is a between-groups comparison and the outgoing sample was underpowered, this interpretation remains speculative. FMθ amplitude also declined across sessions overall, independent of group, scenario, or target. However, because this effect did not survive the outlier-retention sensitivity analysis, it should be interpreted with caution. If reliable, such a non-group-specific decline would more plausibly reflect a general reduction in cognitive engagement across repeated assessment, such as habituation or task familiarity, rather than an incarcerationspecific adaptation.

Exploratory analyses further suggested that social decision-making may be shaped by institutional experiences and individual characteristics. These analyses are reported to generate rather than test hypotheses. Among incoming participants, greater satisfaction with outside contacts and more positive staff-prisoner relationships were associated with more prosocial and fewer antisocial intentions toward community targets. These associations align with evidence that maintaining connections with the outside world and supportive institutional relationships promote well-being, reduce institutionalization, and facilitate reintegration (Cochran, 2014; Liebling & Arnold, 2004). One interpretation is that sustained contact with non-incarcerated others reduces the social distance and stigma attached to a incarceration-history label, fostering more positive orientations toward community targets (Allport, 1954; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). Conversely, greater availability of meaningful activities (at four months) and stronger prisoner-prisoner relationships were associated with fewer prosocial and more antisocial intentions toward community targets. One valence-based interpretation is that deeper embedding in the prison social world makes incarcerationhistory descriptions more familiar and less negatively valenced, so that prosocial intent is extended more readily toward incarceration-history targets and, correspondingly, less toward community targets. This is also one context in which a social-identity account fits well, as shared in-prison activities and peer bonds may reinforce identification with fellow (formerly) incarcerated individuals and a correspondingly sharper differentiation from community targets (Guo et al., 2020; Walters, 2003). This pattern was not uniform over time, however, as meaningful activities were associated with fewer antisocial intentions toward community targets at eight months, which may reflect the nature of some activities. For instance skillsbased programmes or activities facilitated by non-incarcerated volunteers may increase contact with, and hence more positive orientations toward, the outside (Allport, 1954; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). Among outgoing participants, greater inner peace was associated with more prosocial intentions toward community targets over time, consistent with reintegration frameworks emphasizing the role of psychological well-being, meaning-making, and personal resources in social adaptation following release (Keene et al., 2018; Visher & Travis, 2011).

At the individual level, being diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder was associated, in both incoming and outgoing participants, with fewer prosocial intentions toward community targets, particularly early in incarceration and around release. This is consistent with evidence linking psychiatric symptoms, particularly depressive symptoms, to social withdrawal and altered social cognition (Cruwys et al., 2014). Accordingly, the social-cure framework holds that identification with meaningful social groups can support health by providing belonging, support, and purpose (Haslam et al., 2018). Hence, incarcerated individuals with psychiatric difficulties may both struggle to maintain positive social relationships and, as a result, show reduced prosocial orientation toward community targets. Higher reinforcement sensitivity was similarly associated with fewer prosocial intentions toward community targets. Higher BAS scores among incoming participants at eight months and outgoing participants at baseline, and higher BIS scores among outgoing participants at four and eight months. These associations fit evidence that social interactions engage reward-and punishment-processing systems, positive interactions can act as rewards (Baumeister & Leary, 1995; Krach et al., 2010), whereas social rejection or exclusion may be experienced as aversive (Dickinson & Dearing, 2014; Duffy et al., 2018). Similarly, individual differences in reward and punishment sensitivity have been linked to variation in social motivation and functioning (Bijttebier et al., 2009; Gray, 1984). Thus, altered reinforcement sensitivities may influence how individuals evaluate and engage with non-incarcerated others during incarceration and reintegration.

Among incoming participants, older age was the only predictor associated with higher FMθ amplitude. Given the relatively young sample, this effect is unlikely to reflect age-related cognitive decline. Instead, greater FMθ in relatively older prisoners may reflect increased recruitment of cognitive-control resources or more deliberate engagement during social decision-making (Zając-Lamparska et al., 2025). This interpretation is consistent with work suggesting age-related changes in FMθ recruitment and flexibility during executive-control tasks (Darna et al., 2025; Depestele et al., 2022). In addition, older age was also associated with behaviour. At baseline, older incoming participants showed fewer prosocial and more antisocial community target intentions. This could reflect the number of incarcerations, as previous imprisonments showed an association with social intentions, although the post-hoc contrasts were not statistically significant. Age may additionally index the accumulated duration of lived experience of social marginalization and stigma, which accumulates independently of the number of incarcerations. Indeed, prolonged exposure to stigma and social exclusion has been linked to reduced trust and withdrawal from non-stigmatized groups (Keene et al., 2018; Link & Phelan, 2001). A complementary social-identity reading is also well suited here. Attitudes and social identities tend to become more stable and resistant to change with age (Krosnick & Alwin, 1989). Although prosociality often increases with age it also becomes increasingly directed toward one's own group (Cutler et al., 2021). Reduced prosocial and heightened antisocial orientation toward community targets in older prisoners may therefore reflect an age-related entrenchment of orientation toward incarcerationhistory targets rather than a general decline in prosociality.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. Most importantly, the two target types were confounded since incarceration-history characters were described with a criminal record and community characters with a conventional life, so they differed in the valence of their descriptions as well as in incarceration-history status. This has two consequences. First, effects cannot be attributed to group membership itself.

Therefore they are interpreted primarily as responses to the incarceration-history descriptions and their associated stigma, and treat social-identity or intergroup-bias accounts as one possible interpretation rather than a demonstrated mechanism. Conversely, because incarceration-history status and description valence covary, the present design cannot fully separate responses to the stigma of a criminal history specifically from responses to negatively valenced descriptions more generally. Disentangling the two would require a design that orthogonalizes valence and incarceration-history status, for example, including negatively described community targets and positively described incarceration-history targets. That participants nonetheless identified more strongly with incarceration-history characters indicates that this distinction was subjectively meaningful and not reducible to description valence alone. Secondly, behavioural responses may have been influenced by social desirability or identity-management processes. Testing was conducted individually in the presence of two researchers in the room, who may have been perceived as members of the non-incarcerated community. Even though the experimenters were not keeping track of the participant's responses to avoid a social presence effect (Bond & Titus, 1983; McCambridge et al., 2014), it is possible that participants may have partly regulated their responses to avoid overt alignment with stigmatized "criminal" identities or antisocial attitudes. Third, the present task assessed hypothetical social intentions rather than real interpersonal behaviour. Although the scenarios were designed to maximize ecological validity within prison settings, they inevitably simplify the complexity of real-world social interactions. Prior work suggests that emotions such as guilt emerge after experiencing the emotional consequences of one's actions rather than during hypothetical situations (Andrade & Hoyle, 2022). More immersive or ecologically realistic paradigms may therefore better capture prison interpersonal behaviour, but such approaches also raise important ethical challenges in incarcerated populations, particularly in specific antisocial or provocative situations. Moreover, few experimental paradigms have attempted to examine prosocial and antisocial social decisions while preserving ecological realism in incarcerated populations, limiting the availability of validated task frameworks for comparison. Future studies would benefit from combining experimental paradigms with qualitative approaches or immersive virtual social environments to better capture the contextual complexity of prison social cognition (Pan & Hamilton, 2018). Fourth, attrition was a substantial challenge, especially in the outgoing group (n = 13 at S3) which fell below both the preregistered target and the simulation-based estimate of approximately 30 participants per group. The longitudinal analyses in this group were therefore underpowered, and null effects among outgoing participants are consequently reported as inconclusive on the basis of equivalence tests.

Voluntary participation may additionally have introduced selection bias. Dropout nonetheless appeared to be driven primarily by practical follow-up constraints rather than disengagement from the study. However, in outgoing participants, loss to follow-up after release may also reflect differences in post-release reintegration trajectories, including variability in housing stability, social functioning, or responsiveness to institutional contact. As a result, participants retained at later follow-ups may not fully represent the broader population of released prisoners. Because the prisoner groups were restricted to men by design, the present findings may not generalise to incarcerated women, whose experiences of incarceration-history stigma and reintegration trajectories may differ. Despite these limitations, the present findings provide novel evidence that social adaptation during incarceration is not well captured by a simple strengthening of in-group preference toward (formerly) incarcerated others. Instead, prosocial and antisocial intentions tracked the incarceration-history status and valence of the targets (Balliet et al., 2014; Turner et al., 1979).

Although both incoming and outgoing participants consistently identified more strongly with incarcerated individuals, the behavioural consequences of this identification appeared limited. At the same time, antisocial decisions were more frequently directed toward individuals with incarceration history, highlighting that stronger prisoner identification does not necessarily translate into straightforward behavioural solidarity. At the neural level, the early-incarceration period was marked by globally higher conflict-related activity in incoming than outgoing prisoners, without target-specific modulation. The exploratory findings further suggest that social adaptation is shaped not only by how individuals relate to a incarcerationhistory identity, but also by prison experiences, especially in terms of social environment, and reintegration conditions. Indeed, factors such as relationships with staff and fellow prisoners, contact with the outside world, psychological well-being, and psychiatric vulnerability were all associated with social decision-making, highlighting the importance of the broader social environment in shaping how (former) prisoners relate to both individuals with versus without an incarceration or a criminal history.

These findings may have important implications for reintegration. Continued identification with incarcerated or formerly incarcerated individuals may influence social belonging, trust, and perceptions of stigma after release, while successful reintegration may depend in part on maintaining positive social connections and supportive environments both inside and outside prison. Future research should therefore examine how reciprocal interactions between prisoners, former prisoners, and broader society contribute to social exclusion, stigma, and long-term reintegration outcomes.

S2. Retention strategies

To facilitate longitudinal follow-up, participants provided contact details on paper-based forms. Comprehensive tracing details included participants' residential address, mobile phone number, email address, and social media profiles. When possible, participants also provided contact details for alternative contacts (e.g., relatives, friends, social workers, or services they anticipated contacting upon release). Similar strategies are commonly used in longitudinal studies involving marginalized or high-risk populations to ensure effective followup (Aitken et al., 2008; Stewart et al., 2021; Teague et al., 2018). Researchers ensured that participants understood how their secondary contacts' details would be used and that sensitive information, such as their criminal history, would not be disclosed to these individuals.

Several retention strategies were employed to maintain participant engagement throughout the follow-up period. Participants still in prison were contacted through institutional channels, whereas released participants were contacted using the information provided at baseline. When possible, participants transferred to another prison or re-incarcerated during follow-up were traced through the national penitentiary database (SIDIS) and reassessed in their new institution. Participants could withdraw at any time without consequences.

For participants remaining incarcerated

Incoming participants were notified of upcoming follow-up sessions through letters sent via the prison communication system (Prison Cloud) or via institutional intermediaries. This allowed participants who were employed within the prison to request time off or schedule testing outside of working hours. During ongoing recruitment periods, participants eligible for follow-up were also contacted directly on recruitment days to confirm their continued interest and availability.

Participants transferred to a different penitentiary institution were identified through the SIDIS penitentiary database, and authorization to conduct testing in the new institution was obtained from the Belgian Federal Public Service Justice (Direction générale EPI -

Établissements pénitentiaires).

For participants released from prison Participants who were scheduled for release within three weeks following baseline testing were asked to provide contact information, including a telephone number, email address, and/or residential address. When available, contact details of a social worker, community center, or health service were also recorded.

At approximately 3 months post-release, participants were contacted to schedule follow-up assessments. If initial contact attempts were unsuccessful, up to three follow-up messages were sent. For participants who could not be reached through the provided contact information, secondary contacts were used to obtain updated contact details when available.

Letters were also sent to participants' last known residential address.

Participants who were re-incarcerated during the follow-up period were identified through the SIDIS database, provided they had consented to this monitoring in the informed consent procedure. Participants who were re-incarcerated before S2 were excluded from follow-up analyses, whereas those re-incarcerated between S2 and S3 remained eligible for the final assessment.

For both incarcerated and released participants, they were considered lost to follow-up if they cancelled or were absent from two consecutive scheduled appointments, or did not respond to contact attempts for three consecutive weeks. S6. EEG Recording and Preprocessing

EEG was recorded during task performance using a 32-electrode BioSemi ActiveTwo system (see http://www.biosemi.com/ for hardware details), positioned according to the international 10-20 system and sampled at 2048 Hz. Two additional external electrodes were used to record mastoid activity. Raw EEG data were acquired with ActiveView Software and converted to BIDS format. Continuous data were resampled from 2048 to 512 Hz.

Preprocessing was conducted in MNE-Python (Gramfort et al., 2013) using an automated pipeline. Prior to preprocessing, raw recordings were visually inspected to verify data quality and identify, visually and from prior records, bad channels.

For ICA decomposition, a separate copy of the continuous data was band-pass filtered between 1 and 30 Hz using an infinite impulse response (IIR) filter. Power line noise was removed using notch filters at 50 Hz and its harmonics (up to 250 Hz). ICA was computed using the Picard algorithm on EEG channels excluding bad channels. Components were then automatically classified using the ICLabel classifier (Li et al., 2022; Pion-Tonachini et al., 2019).

Only components labelled as "brain" or "other" with a classification probability ≥ .70 were retained, all remaining components were removed. Bad channels were interpolated using spherical spline interpolation, and data re-referenced using the reference electrode standardization technique (REST), implemented through a spherical head model and forward solution (Gramfort et al., 2013; Yao, 2001; D. Yao et al., 2019). Data were band-pass filtered between 1 and 40 Hz and segmented into response-locked epochs (-2 to 2s). Analyses focused on frontocentral electrodes (FC1, FC2, Fz, Cz), which are commonly associated with frontal midline theta activity (Cavanagh & Frank, 2014; Cohen, 2014).

Epochs containing artifacts were excluded using a fully automated procedure applied to frontocentral electrodes (FC1, FC2, Fz, Cz) within a -1.5 to 1 s window surrounding the response. Artifact detection was based on three signal properties: peak-to-peak amplitude, mean activity, and signal slope. For each participant and electrode, trials exceeding an interquartile range (IQR)-based threshold (±2 × IQR) on any metric were automatically identified as outliers and removed from further analyses. Across S1 to S3, trial rejection was 9.10% across incoming and outgoing participants included in the longitudinal EEG sample (IN: M = 8.77%, SD = 1.72; OUT: M = 9.84%, SD = 2.07).

Time-frequency representations (TFRs) were computed at the single-trial level using Morlet wavelets with frequencies ranging from 2 to 30 Hz (80 logarithmically spaced frequency bins) and logarithmically spaced cycles (4-14). Power estimates were z-score normalized relative to a post-response baseline window (0.0-1.0 s post-response). Trial-level theta estimates were extracted from frontocentral electrodes (FC1, FC2, Fz, Cz) within a time-frequency window identified from grand-average plots across all groups and sessions. Specifically, theta activity was quantified within the 3-7 Hz and -1.0 to -0.250 s pre-response window for subsequent statistical analyses. Participants contributing fewer than 10 valid trials per condition or fewer than 30 valid trials overall were excluded from EEG analyses.

Each predictor model was compared against the corresponding baseline model using D2 model-comparison tests pooled across imputations (Grund et al., 2016). The D2 statistic evaluates whether inclusion of a predictor significantly improves model fit while accounting for uncertainty introduced by multiple imputation. To control for multiple testing across predictors, p-values from D2 model comparisons were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure separately for each analysis. Multiple Imputations of Questionnaire Data

Missing questionnaire and covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) implemented with the mice package in R (Van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). Imputation was performed using the futuremice function to allow parallel computation. Overall, missingness was low (1.83% on average for incoming participants and 2.52% for outgoing participants). Higher missingness proportions (up to ~40%) were observed for a limited number of variables, mainly reflecting sparse or rare categories (e.g., specific medication or substance-use indicators).

For the incoming prisoner sample, 150 imputed datasets were generated, whereas 100 imputed datasets were generated for the outgoing prisoner sample, reflecting the smaller sample size. Imputed datasets were generated with 400 iterations per imputation chain to ensure stable convergence of the algorithm. The imputation model used predictive mean matching (PMM) as the primary imputation method (White et al., 2011). For the statistical analyses reported in the manuscript, 20 imputed datasets were retained and pooled using Rubin's rule (D. B. Rubin, 2018).

The imputation model used a custom predictor matrix to specify which variables could be used to predict missing values. This allowed to remove collinear and constant variables from the imputation model. In addition, a masking rule was used to restrict imputation to testing sessions which were actually completed. For a small number of variables that were constant (e.g., rare drug-use categories or offence types), imputation using the mice algorithm was not feasible. In these cases, deterministic imputation using either the mean or the mode was applied to allow construction of composite indicators (e.g., binary indicators). Participants with missing questionnaires in early sessions, but observed data at later sessions, were included in the imputation procedure. Missingness in these cases primarily reflected procedural factors (e.g., questionnaires not returned or lost) rather than participant refusal.

On the other hand, participants who withdrew from the study or became ineligible for followup were not imputed forward. One incoming participant, whose baseline and follow-up questionnaires were lost in transit (sent by post), could not be imputed. This participant is therefore excluded from all questionnaire-based analyses (descriptive summaries and covariate/exploratory models), was retained in analyses requiring only task data.

S8. Sensitivity of the Neural Analyses to Outlier-trial Removal

To assess robustness, the FMθ model was refit on the full dataset including all trials. Under this sample the random-effects structure retained by-participant intercepts and a Session slope (non-singular). The main effect of Group remained, with incoming participants showing higher FMθ amplitude than outgoing participants (F(1, 40.2) = 5.09, p = .030), as did the positive association with age (F(1, 39.9) = 20.68, p < .001). The main effect of Session, by contrast, was reduced to non-significance (F(2, 40.1) = 2.87, p = .068), indicating that the session-related decline observed in the primary analysis was not robust to outlier retention.

Decision time reached significance under this specification (F(1, 6797.5) = 5.20, p = .023), although it was non-significant in the primary model. No other main effect or interaction was significant. The Group and age effects are therefore robust to outlier handling, whereas the Session and decision-time effects are not and are interpreted with caution.

Sample Size Estimation (deviation from preregistration).

The preregistered sample-size target was derived from an a priori power analysis in G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) (medium effect, f = .25, 95% power). Because G*Power approximates such designs as repeatedmeasures ANOVAs and misspecifies interactions combining between-and within-subject factors (Brysbaert, 2019), and because the confirmatory analyses used (generalised) linear mixed-effects models, an additional simulation-based power analysis was conducted for the design actually used. This analysis was not preregistered and was performed after data collection. To avoid circularity, the effect size was set by convention rather than from the observed estimates. For each candidate sample size, datasets from a binomial GLMM were simulated with the full Group × Session × Scenario structure, a by-participant random intercept and random slopes for Session and Scenario, and 64 free-choice trials per scenario per session (aggregated to binomial counts within each participant × session × scenario cell).

The three-way Group × Session × Scenario interaction was tested with a likelihood-ratio test comparing the full model against a model retaining all two-way interactions. Power was the proportion of 500 simulated datasets in which the interaction was detected at α = .05.

Assuming a small interaction (odds ratio = 1.5) and moderate between-participant variability in the session change (random-slope SD = 0.4 on the logit scale), 80% power for the threeway interaction was reached at approximately 30 participants per group in the present longitudinal design (two groups × three sessions). Main effects and lower-order interactions reached 80% power at smaller samples. The achieved longitudinal samples met the requirement for incoming participants (n = 34 with complete behavioural data; n = 29 with usable EEG across all three sessions) but fell below it for outgoing participants (n = 13 and n = 11, respectively); accordingly, null effects in the outgoing group are interpreted using equivalence tests as inconclusive rather than as evidence of absence (see Results).

Recruitment and Eligibility Criteria. Participants were recruited across 15 penitentiary establishments in Belgium, which varied in security level, regime, and capacity. Eligibility for the study was restricted to individuals who had sufficient understanding of Dutch, French, or English and did not experience visual or motor impairments. Participants who exhibited inappropriate behaviour towards the researchers were excluded, and in rare cases, prison staff denied participation for security or disciplinary reasons. If they had already been approached by the researchers during a prior incarceration period, those candidates were excluded. Participants were not originally excluded if they had a mental disorder, however those who experienced active psychosis during testing, who were transferred to a psychiatric unit, who were unable to complete the task, or who were non-compliant were excluded.

Additional eligibility criteria for the incoming group included transient incarceration, such as cases involving imminent release, extradition, or deportation. For outgoing participants, eligibility was determined based on end-of-sentence dates and anticipated parole release, in consultation with prison staff, to ensure that follow-up assessments could be conducted after release. When release was delayed by up to six months beyond baseline, the second assessment was conducted while participants were still incarcerated (n=2), although this assessment was not included in the final analyses. Participants who were released more than six months after baseline were excluded from the cohort. To capture the potential effects of prolonged incarceration on cognitive and social processes, only outgoing participants who had been incarcerated for a minimum of six months prior to release were included in the final sample. Individuals released under limited detention regimes were excluded because this status simultaneously involves elements of incarceration and community reintegration.

Recruitment timelines, and prison specifications are detailed in Supplementary Table S1-1. Timelines differed across institutions due to logistical and administrative constraints, particularly related to prison overcrowding and repeated disruptions in prison access. Age Incoming participants were significantly younger than outgoing participants. An independent-samples t-test revealed a significant group difference (t = -2.34, p = .027). Therefore, age is included as a covariate in all the confirmatory analyses.

Education

A non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test indicated no significant difference between incoming and outgoing participants (p = .762). Overall, both groups were predominantly characterised by secondary-level education (Supplementary Figure S4-1).

Sentence Length and Conviction Status

Among incoming participants, 62.1% were convicted at baseline (n = 18) , whereas 37.9% were on remand or pre-trial detention (n = 11). Sentence length differed significantly between incoming and outgoing participants (Supplementary Figure S4-2). A non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test revealed that outgoing participants had significantly longer sentence lengths than incoming participants (U = 69.0, p < .001), with median sentence length categories corresponding to 1-3 years for incoming participants and 5-10 years for outgoing participants. Importantly, prisoners who had already been convicted and received sentences shorter than one year were excluded during recruitment in order to minimise the likelihood of attrition across follow-up assessments. Consequently, participants with sentences below one year were underrepresented in the incoming prisoner sample.

S11. Equivalence Tests for Null Effects (Deviation from

Preregistration)

The preregistered analysis plan did not include a procedure for quantifying evidence for the null. A non-significant result cannot distinguish a genuine absence of effect from insufficient statistical power. Thus, equivalence tests were conducted using the two one-sided tests (TOST) procedure (Lakens, 2017; Lakens et al., 2018) for the three non-significant effects, including (i) the stability of identification with incarceration-history targets across sessions, (ii) the absence of overall group (incoming vs. outgoing) differences in social intentions, and (iii) the absence of longitudinal FMθ change in outgoing participants. Because these analyses were not preregistered, they are exploratory.

As no equivalence bounds were preregistered, smallest effects of interest (SESOI) were defined based on measurements rather than a priori. Namely, it corresponds to half a point on the 4-point response scale (±0.5 points) for identification ratings, an odds ratio of 1.5 for social intentions on the log-odds scale (±log(1.5)), and a small effect in z-scored power of ±0.2 z for FMθ amplitude. Following Lakens et al. (2018), an effect was considered statistically equivalent to zero when both one-sided tests were significant at α = .05, equivalently when its 90% confidence interval fell entirely within the equivalence bounds. Effects that were neither statistically significant nor statistically equivalent were interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence for the null. Because the chosen SESOIs are necessarily somewhat arbitrary, they should be treated as reasonable reference values rather than definitive thresholds. Full results for all contrasts are reported in Table S11-1.
