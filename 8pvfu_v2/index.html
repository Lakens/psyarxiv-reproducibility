Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Introduction: Prehabilitation aims to optimise psychological status, but psychological provision largely addresses existing distress and what preparation for treatment consists of is unclear as prehabilitation services scale up. We asked how patients experience it, what enables or undermines it, and how their circumstances shape what they can use.

We searched CINAHL, MEDLINE and PsycINFO from inception, last searched 22 May 2026, for peer-reviewed English-language qualitative studies of adults' experiences of multimodal cancer prehabilitation. Eligible programmes combined at least two of exercise, nutrition and psychological support, and either targeted psychological outcomes or included an element with a stated psychological function; measurement alone did not qualify. Two reviewers appraised reports using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist plus four domains covering staff training, fidelity, adherence and participants' words. We synthesised findings by meta-ethnography. No external funding. Registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023434123).

Results: Seventeen reports (2021-2026), predominantly surgical and from six high-income countries, drew on 14 independent samples and 230 patients. Across three constructs and 12 subthemes, patients experienced psychological preparation as having something to act on, discovering what their bodies could do, understanding and managing what was coming, being taken seriously and kept going, and not being alone. These functions arose through exercise, dietary support and relationships with staff and peers, not only explicitly psychological input. Prehabilitation added to preparations already under way. Relevance, timing, burden and continuity after surgery enabled or undermined preparation, depending on patients' beliefs, capacity, demands and support networks. Discussion: Evidence came almost entirely from engagers, and staff training, adherence and fidelity were sparsely reported. Psychological preparation did not map onto a discrete component; it arose where what a programme offered matched what a patient could use. Services should build psychological function into how prehabilitation is delivered, who delivers it, and how it is matched to patients.

INTRODUCTION

Prehabilitation is proactive preparation, before and during cancer treatment, intended to build the physical, nutritional and psychological reserve patients need to withstand and recover from treatment [1, 2]. It involves screening and needs-based assessment, enabling individualised, often multimodal prescription of exercise, nutrition and psychological support [1, 2]. Psychological status is therefore part of what prehabilitation is for, not an optional extra, and prehabilitation differs from general psychosocial care in being organised around an impending treatment.

Anxiety, depression, self-harm and suicide are more common after a cancer diagnosis, particularly in the first year [3], and in more deprived groups [4]. Depression after diagnosis is also associated with poorer survival [5]. Psychological input is also less readily taken up once distress has appeared and treatment has begun [6]. Psychological prehabilitation therefore has two aims: to treat existing difficulties, and to reduce future ones [1]. The revised UK guidelines accordingly ask services to assess symptoms alongside illness perceptions, coping and self-efficacy, which predict later distress [1, 7].

But what optimised psychological status amounts to is not specified. Exercise and nutrition have proximal targets linked to recovery -fitness, strength, nutritional markers [2]. Of 140 prehabilitation studies, 89% gave a fitness rationale and 33% a psychological one [8]. Where it is assessed, psychological status is defined reactively, around difficulties already present [1, 9] rather than around readiness for future treatment, which patients described as a "mindset to have surgery": confidence in what to expect and in coping, motivation, and anxiety that is expected but manageable [10]. Programmes containing a psychological component do reduce overall postoperative complications (RR 0.73, 95%CI 0.60-0.89) [11], without showing what that component contributed.

This gap is about to scale up, as prehabilitation moves from trials into policy and services in England. The National Cancer Plan for England commits to a national, digital-first prehabilitation offer from 2028 and to a personalised assessment of every patient's physical, mental health and social needs [12], taking forward the Ten-Year Health Plan's shifts from sickness to prevention, hospital to community, and analogue to digital [13]. Services elsewhere are on the same trajectory [14]. A programme nevertheless delivers only what patients can use. [1], and what is delivered is often poorly reported [9]; so trials tell us little about what preparation consists of for patients or what within a programme produces it. Two qualitative syntheses have asked what determines participation in prehabilitation before major surgery [15] and adherence to it [16], coding patient accounts into existing behavioural frameworks. Neither examined psychological preparation as an outcome or looked for it outside components labelled psychological.

Few of the interventions tested for efficacy reflect what is done in practice

To address these gaps, we synthesised qualitative studies of patients' experiences of multimodal prehabilitation with a psychological function, appraising how far each report described the programme it studied. We asked how people with cancer experience psychological preparation, which components, relationships and delivery processes enabled or undermined it, and how patients' circumstances, capacity and the timing of the offer shaped what they could use.

METHODS

We follow PRISMA reporting [17], registered the review with PROSPERO (CRD42023434123; which is the protocol), and took a critical realist approach. Searches to January 2024 informed an earlier version (Hussain et al., preprint, https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/8pvfu_v1). After the provisional 2024 synthesis, we respecified the question around psychological preparation and dropped the requirement to distinguish experiences of psychological components (preparation extended beyond them). PROSPERO records and explains amendments.

Eligibility followed the PI(C)OS framework:  Population: adults (18+) with a cancer diagnosis;  Intervention: programmes comprising at least two of the three core guideline domains-exercise, nutrition and psychological support [1]-and either aiming to address psychological outcomes or including an element with a stated psychological or psychosocial function; psychological outcome measurement alone did not qualify.

We focused on multimodal programmes to inform the combined model of care recommended in UK guidance [1], examining patients' experiences of psychological preparation when different intervention domains are offered together. We defined domains by content rather than how it was delivered, so a single resource addressing more than one domain qualified. Eligibility concerned the programme offered, not the number of domains each participant received. Programmes extending into rehabilitation were eligible if they included an identifiable prehabilitation phase; where phases could not be separated, we retained the report and treated attribution to prehabilitation as uncertain;  Outcomes: participants' experiences of prehabilitation; and,  Study type: qualitative studies using a formal method of analysis, from which data on the programme could be extracted.

Search Strategy

On 13 January 2024 and 22 May 2026, we searched CINAHL, Medline, and PsycINFO from inception for peer-reviewed, English-language studies, using PI(C)OS terms combined with Boolean logic and a subject librarian's support (Supporting Information Table S1 gives the scripts). We hand-searched relevant reviews and included reports.

Selection process

At each stage one reviewer screened all titles and abstracts (AH in 2024, ZU in 2026) and a second screened them independently (MB). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, refining the criteria. One was adjudicated by MR.

Data extraction

AH (2024) and ZU (2026) extracted data using an a priori form, cross-checked by MB (Tables 2 and 3). For the update, we also recorded the provider, label, and level (universal, targeted or specialist) of psychological or psychosocial elements. We retained extracts describing a psychological or psychosocial experience, need or impact, for example, emotion, appraisals of control, agency or self-efficacy, coping, motivation, identity, and relational support. We included physical, practical, and nutritional material only when its psychological function or bearing on engagement was explicit. In mixed samples, accounts from others (professionals, partners) provided context but not first-or second-order data unless patient contributions were independently identifiable.

Quality appraisal

We used the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) qualitative checklist [18, 19], which applies across diverse qualitative designs and theoretical positions, to characterise, rather than score or exclude, studies. 1a Having something to act on: agency and control

Prehabilitation gave patients something to act on in a period they could not otherwise influence. Where everything seemed "unmanageable and hopeless", it let them focus on the important things and do something before surgery that might pay off afterwards (N) (Q07). What they acted on was small and concrete. Patients put a cross on a leaflet every day, wrote down what they had managed on a day when not much else happened (A, B) (Q03, Q02), kept a food journal with the dietitian (O) (Q08), or took direction about exercise, nutrition and coping (K) (Q06). Enrolment, appointments and regular contact gave "rhythm" (P). It also took focus away from cancer (H), and completing meaningful tasks gave a sense of control (C). What patients found controllable was their own body and conduct, not the disease: "something you can control yourself, because you can't control the cancer" (E, P) (Q04, Q05).

1b Finding out what I can do: confidence and capability

Patients also found out what they and their bodies could do, and their confidence was based on their lived experience. Repeated assessment made capability visible, including to patients who had thought themselves fit enough already (M). So did being able to "clearly feel the improvement" and find themselves "the first one up the hills" within weeks (N) (Q13). Others felt better able to "fight" (K) (Q12), or came to value a strong body despite feeling "really bad" on chemotherapy (O) (Q14). Reports named this effect differently: selfefficacy (E), or confidence and self-esteem (H, D) (Q09, Q10). Only one report, with the most transparent sampling and the fullest account of its own conduct, highlighted the adverse side to this effect: where patients could feel no change, confidence fell and habits formed during the programme lapsed within days (P) (Q11, Q15). The other form was acting on feelings directly. Grounding and breathing filled the "void" between the facts on paper and living through them (Q) (Q17, Q18). Patients still used stress-management recordings during adjuvant chemotherapy (K) (Q20), and exercise itself became a moment to relax before a "nerve-wracking procedure" (P) (Q24).

1d Being taken seriously and kept going: relational safety and supportive accountability

Patients experienced relationships with staff as psychologically active, and professional involvement supported their motivation and adherence (A, B, C, E, F, H, I, J). What made it work was being treated as a person with a view. Patients described a nurse who "took everything I said seriously" (D) (Q27), staff "like having a friend there all the way through" (M) (Q28), and a service where "no one feels judged" (L) (Q32). Being known in this way made it safe to ask, to disclose and to keep taking part.

Expertise removed specific fears. Patients would not trust an ordinary trainer with "this particular situation" (K) (Q31) and wanted someone "educated in that field" who saw them "as a human being" (Q) (Q29). A surgeon's confirmation that a four-week wait carried no risk was itself a relief (N).

Relationships also sustained effort, and whether that helped depended on how it was negotiated. Regular appointments and a little push to go the extra mile supplied accountability when motivation dipped (K, P) (Q33). One woman said "there had to be real pressure" and that staff had to "keep an eye on me" (B) (Q25); another that patients do not want to be "pigeonholed" (C) (Q26). The relationship took time to build: where staffing was short, staff "answer questions and that's probably it" (O) (Q30).

1e Not being alone: social connection within the programme

Programmes brought patients together to deliver exercise, and patients found a social effect that was rarely designed for. Being among others in the same position gave validation, knowledge, and a change of perspective (B, E, G, H, Q). Patients drew "mental strength" from watching post-operative patients back in the class and doing well (G) (Q34), and encouraged each other "without realizing it" (P) (Q38). Classes were where surgery and difficult feelings could be discussed (D). Meeting someone who had already had a difficult operation provided reassurance (F), and where patients attended hospital alone the programme helped them feel supported (K) (Q35).

What patients accepted depended on what the group was for and on who was in it. A group "working to the same goal" of getting fit was welcome, but the same patient would not have joined one where she had to "compare cancers, or stages" (O) (Q37). T2 Conditions that enabled or undermined preparation

What enabled preparation and what undermined it were often the same featuresrelevance, timing, burden, continuity -working in opposite directions for different patients.

2a Relevance and tailoring

Patients wanted a programme that recognised their situation and fitted their circumstances (A, B, E, F, G, I). They wanted material to be relevant to "my situation" rather than general (B) (Q40), and classes had to feel like "personal classes just for you" even in a group (G) (Q41). Patients resented what had been organised without considering what they wanted (I) (Q42) and mismatches limited engagement with the whole programme (C, D). Psychological support was most acceptable where it was available and "not labelled" as such (H) (Q39).

Relevance could fail at several levels. Patients were given information without being told what prehabilitation was for (M, O) (Q45). What they wanted differed between the exercise, dietary and psychological parts (K), and some equated prehabilitation with exercise, knowing little of its other elements (N). How much information was right differed between patients even in the same report: some were "deluged with information" at diagnosis, others found it enough because "you can choose yourself" (M) (Q43, Q44). Format divided them the same way: remote delivery widening access for some and removing personal contact for others (O).

Psychological input too had to match the amount patients wanted (Q). Where it did not, it did not register as support: a drop-in chat was not what one patient needed (O) (Q46), and a request for psychological help before surgery was never acted on (O) (Q47).

2b Timing within diagnostic and treatment overwhelm

Patients often wanted prehabilitation introduced early, but not all at once. Reports agreed that early introduction was valuable (C, E, F, I) (Q48, Q49), ideally around diagnosis as a "crucial time" (Q) (Q55), but briefly rather than in detail that "can end up putting people off" (Q50). It allowed patients to prepare at their own pace during a four-week wait (N) and one patient thought it "better to know everything than only bits and pieces" (O) (Q54).

Against early introduction, being overwhelmed was the most common reason for not taking part at all, with information arriving while patients were "still digesting the diagnosis" (K) (Q53). Interviewees in one report were told everything at once when they were "too ill to deal with it" (M) (Q51), and an early offer close to surgery led patients to think the classes would be of little use (D).

What seemed to separate these positions was not when the offer arrived but whether it matched what the patient could use. A psychosexual consultation held before surgery was about "what might happen" rather than "my own situation" (N) (Q52).

2c Burden against capacity

Where prehabilitation took place mattered largely because of what it cost patients to attend (A, B, C, D, E, J) (Q56, Q57, Q58). Treatment side effects were the most common barrier: fatigue and post-surgical tiredness meant patients "would just sleep all day long" (K) (Q61) or could not re-engage (P). The programme's own demands could exhaust patients, one coming out "completely exhausted" and learning to "set your own boundaries" (P) (Q60).

What reduced the cost was largely administrative. Appointments were scheduled alongside existing hospital visits, with travel reimbursement and parking vouchers (K), moved online where patients could not attend (M, O) (Q62), or someone worked "in the background to organise all the visits" (Q) (Q63). But capacity could be depleted before the offer was made, with one patient already "mentally exhausted" and wanting to be left alone (I) (Q59).

2d Carrying preparation into recovery

Support that ended at surgery was experienced as loss rather than completion. Patients contrasted "fantastic treatment in hospital" with being "kind of on your own" afterwards, sometimes feeling abandoned or feeling responsible for re-establishing contact (D) (Q64, Q65). Other endings were as abrupt: being held "by the collar over a cliff" (P) (Q67), or simply "goodbye" after recovery (M) (Q68). Where support continued, patients valued it.

Access to the prehabilitation team on discharge reinforced the changes they had made (E) (Q66), and one patient still in psychotherapy found it brought "pre-surgery" and "after surgery" into context, working better for her afterwards than it would have before (O) (Q69). Some resumed the prehabilitation routine by themselves (K), while post-operative advice to rest sometimes undid what the programme had started (P).

Their beliefs about themselves, the demands already on them, and the people around them determined what counted as preparation and how it worked out. 3a The person who arrives: identity, beliefs and valued futures Patients did not arrive as blank slates, and what they already believed about themselves governed whether prehabilitation seemed necessary. Expecting "physio care afterwards" to suffice (I) (Q71), feeling already fit enough, and judging psychological support unnecessary reduced perceived need ( C, D, H). Being "very independent and resilient" meant not seeing a need, though not that others would not benefit (M) (Q73). Beliefs about psychology shaped uptake of it as a distinct component. A referral could be read as the care team's judgement that the patient was not coping (Q). Seeing oneself as a strong person could work either way: protective for some, and for others a route to avoidance and later overwhelm, one patient who thought herself "relatively a strong person" unable to recognise her own anxiety (Q) (Q74).

Patients also differed in the kind of future they valued. Most spoke of returning to normality, but one rejected this because "normality for me is not going to be enough" (O) (Q75). For others, anticipated incontinence and loss of sexual function mattered more than cure: one would rather risk not being cured than lose "a proper life" (N) (Q76). Once surgery was decided, those patients engaged eagerly in preparation. Others approached the future more provisionally -"we'll see how it goes" -which shaped how far motivation and support became action (P) (Q77).

3b Other preparations and competing demands

Patients prepared in ways the programmes did not propose: housekeeping, packing hospital bags, work plans, wills, insurance papers and funeral arrangements. Preparing in these ways gave patients security and control in a situation without them otherwise (A, B, C -linked reports from one research group) (Q78, Q80). An independent fourth report confirms it: one patient got her will straight because it "hangs over you" and surgery "spurred me on" (O) (Q82).

Time and competing priorities constrained what was possible, so programmes had to fit flexibly around the work, commitments and appointments patients already had (A, B, C, D, E, J) (Q79). Life stage, dependants and work determined how much patients could absorb, younger ones struggling "to work and raise kids and deal with this at the same time" (Q) (Q83). And though prehabilitation was free where it was studied, continuing the activities afterwards was "way too expensive" (P) (Q81).

3c Family and friends: scaffold and substitute

Family and friends worked both as scaffold to the programme and as substitute for it. Including family members was considered in A, B, C, E and J. As scaffold, family members supported engagement and adherence (A, B, E, J) (Q84) and supplied accountability and structure. One patient exercised "every day because my family made me" (K) (Q87), another made a schedule with his wife (N) (Q89), and a husband joined the dietetic consultation so that he knew what was being asked of her (O). A partner could be the second set of ears where "there's a lot you don't take in" (Q) (Q91) and support from others sustained motivation, one patient calling it "indispensable" (P) (Q86).

As substitute, family and friend support reduced the perceived need for professional help.

One patient with a strong network "didn't really approach any professionals" (M) (Q88), and another declined the offer because she had "the most supportive family that listen completely" (O) (Q92). Those without a network described isolation itself as their motivation to take part (M).

Support went in the opposite direction too. Where a partner was more affected by the diagnosis than the patient herself, she "stopped fighting him for help" (O) (Q90). One patient had not talked about her diagnosis with her husband or children, and would rather not "bother" the friends she could have turned to (C) (Q85).

Line of argument

Patients prepared in their own ways as well as the programme's.

They organised their households and work, packed for hospital, made wills and drew on the people around them, which gave them security and control. Prehabilitation added to this. It gave patients something concrete to do, evidence of what their bodies could manage, ways to understand and handle what was coming, and relationships with staff and peers that made it easier and less lonely to keep going. No component carried its psychological function on its own. This function arose between what a programme offered and what a patient could recognise and use as preparation. Patients saw little need if they thought themselves fit, expected physiotherapy afterwards to be enough, already had people who listened, or found preparing itself too threatening. Where the offer did fit, preparation depended on what patients could use at that moment, demands within their capacity, and professionals who respected their judgement rather than overriding it. Professional attention could feel like supportive accountability or pressure. Early introduction could orient patients or overwhelm them. The same element did different psychological work for different patients and at different times. Psychological preparation was not something completed before surgery, nor a separate component alongside exercise and nutrition. It could continue through treatment and recovery, and it depended across the pathway on what patients could use. Where support continued after surgery, some patients carried the changes and routines they had built into recovery. Abrupt endings left others feeling abandoned, or stopped the momentum they had built. The reports could not isolate what any single component did. What they showed is how patients experienced psychological preparation across a whole programme and against everything else in their lives.

DISCUSSION

In patients' experience, psychological preparation involved agency, confidence in capability, orientation to what was coming and help managing how it felt, relationships in which they felt known, and belonging among other patients. It extended beyond reducing distress to preparedness for treatment and recovery, developing accounts of psychological readiness as a "mindset to have surgery": knowing what to expect, confidence in coping, motivation, and anxiety that remains manageable [10].

Two things follow. Psychological function arose through exercise, dietary contact, staff relationships and peer contact, as well as explicitly psychological input. And it materialised where what a programme offered met what a patient could recognise and use, which is why the same element prepared one patient and burdened another. What patients could recognise depended on what they already believed about prehabilitation and about psychological support. Our synthesis examined what taking part in cancer prehabilitation does for psychological preparation, extending what we know from earlier syntheses on who participates and who continues [15,16].

Relationships with staff did psychological work, yet few reports described how staff were prepared for it. In one programme, co-ordinators delivering universal emotional and nutritional support had been trained for the role, yet described lacking experience with patients at diagnosis, and gave less attention to both elements because they saw them as outside their expertise (F). Nurses delivering prehabilitation report limited training, absent specialist roles and staffing shortages [42], and psychological input delivered through existing roles can be effective where staff are trained and supervised [43].

Contact with other patients prepared people where they could recognise themselves in the group, and mismatched groups deepened isolation. This may reflect a social identity process shown to influence health outcomes [44].

Implications

Most of these effects arose outside elements designated as psychological and varied with staffing, matching, and timing. Services should therefore support these psychological functions deliberately through routine programme design and delivery rather than leave them to chance. Within the guidelines' stratified model [1], universal provision would mean psychologically informed delivery across programme components; targeted provision, firstline input for identified needs from staff trained and supervised to provide it through their existing roles (e.g., exercise); and specialist provision, assessment and intervention by psychological professionals for more complex needs. A whole-programme approach complements rather than replaces discrete psychological care. Where services extend across surgery, continuity should also be supported.

Group composition needs attention too. Programmes already utilise groups, so creating a common purpose, connecting patients' resources for mutual benefit, and choice about taking part could turn an incidental effect into a planned one.

What patients could use was constrained by travel, cost, treatment effects, work, and caring responsibilities. Remote provision reduced attendance burdens for some but removed valued contact for others. Services can address or exacerbate these inequalities [45], and criteria for equitable provision exist [46]. As prehabilitation moves towards nationwide, digital-first provision [12], digital delivery should widen choice rather than replace personal contact.

Research should treat every element of a programme as a candidate influence on psychological preparation, and report what is delivered, by whom, and with what effect, evaluating process alongside outcome [47], including for patients who decline. That is how the field will identify prehabilitation's psychological mechanisms and optimise them as deliberately as it optimises physical and nutritional status.

Limitations

Almost all the evidence came from patients willing to take part who received at least some of a programme, and few studies examined how education, employment or deprivation shaped engagement. Only one report explicitly identified programme non-engagers within its interview sample (I; n=2). All studies were conducted in high-income countries, often within established or research-supported services, which limits transferability to other health systems.

Reporting on staff training, fidelity, and adherence was sparse, so we cannot say whether programmes were delivered as intended or how much each patient received. Most accounts were retrospective and gathered within the delivering services, so may be positively biased. Two reports contributed thin data for our research question and five drew on two samples, so the independent evidence is narrower than seventeen reports suggests. Our interpretations also rest on the quotations and analyses the original authors chose to publish. Support was thinnest for continuity after surgery and family involvement. We included only peer-reviewed reports in English and excluded programmes with neither a stated psychological aim nor an element with a stated psychological function.

Conclusion

Psychological preparation did not map onto a discrete component. It arose where what a programme offered matched what a patient could use. Services should therefore build psychological function into how prehabilitation is delivered, who delivers it, and how it is matched to patients, and optimise it as deliberately as they optimise physical and nutritional status.
