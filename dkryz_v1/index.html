Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

AI chatbots are increasingly used to deliver health information and persuasive interventions, yet the consequences of making these systems appear humanlike remain unclear. Drawing on the Computers Are Social Actors and Media Are Social Actors paradigms, mind perception theory, and psychological reactance theory, this research examined whether anthropomorphism in AI chatbots shapes reactance through two competing pathways: trust in the chatbot and perceived persuasive intent. An online between-subjects experiment was conducted among U.S. adults who currently used e-cigarettes. Participants in the main study (N = 586) were randomly assigned to interact with either an anthropomorphic or a non-anthropomorphic AI chatbot, or to view a static vaping cessation message. The results showed that interacting with an anthropomorphized chatbot reduced reactance compared to reading a static message, although this advantage did not extend to attitudes toward vaping or intentions to quit. Additionally, while anthropomorphism can generally reduce psychological reactance, its two dimensions, mindful and mindless anthropomorphism, operated in different paths. Mindful anthropomorphism was associated with greater trust and, indirectly, lower reactance. Mindless anthropomorphism was directly associated with lower reactance. These findings support the potential use of anthropomorphic AI chatbots as a communication approach for vaping cessation. Furthermore, by distinguishing mindful from mindless anthropomorphism and clarifying their distinct mechanisms, this research offers a more nuanced explanation of how humanlike AI cues shape psychological reactance and provides practical guidance for designing AI-mediated health interventions.

1. Introduction

AI chatbots have become widely used in customer service, e-commerce, education, and health care (Adam et al., 2021; Adamopoulou & Moussiades, 2020; Labadze et al., 2023; Laymouna et al., 2024). As chatbots take on more service and support roles traditionally performed by people, designers often equip them with humanlike social cues to make interactions feel more natural and engaging. These cues can take verbal, visual, auditory, or behavioral forms, including human names, facial expressions, small talk, and first turn (Feine et al., 2019). As AI chatbots increasingly evolve beyond providing information to advising and encouraging users, an important question arises: whether making these agents more humanlike can improve their effectiveness as persuasive communicators.

The growing use of humanlike design seems to be grounded in the assumption that anthropomorphism will improve users' experience and responses to AI chatbots. Indeed, some existing research has found that chatbot anthropomorphism can elicit greater social presence, trust, satisfaction, acceptance, and compliance (Adam et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2023; Ischen et al., 2020; Song et al., 2024; Zhang & Sheng, 2026). However, in persuasive communication, anthropomorphism may produce backfire effects. On one hand, a humanlike chatbot may appear more competent and benevolent, thereby increasing trust and reducing reactance to its recommendations (Murtaza et al., 2024; Park et al., 2023). On the other hand, humanlike qualities may lead users to perceive the chatbot as possessing greater agency and acting with intentions to influence (Gray et al., 2007; Waytz et al., 2010). This heightened perception of perceived persuasive intent could, in turn, increase users' psychological reactance and weaken their attitudes toward and intentions to follow the recommendations (Reynolds-Tylus, 2019).

Anthropomorphism may therefore facilitate or undermine persuasion depending on the strength of these two competing pathways.

The proposed negative pathway is centered around psychological reactance, referring to "the motivational state that is hypothesized to occur when a freedom is eliminated or threatened with elimination" (Brehm & Brehm, 1981, p. 37). When an individual's freedoms are perceived to be threatened or eliminated, the motive to reassert them will be aroused (Brehm, 1966). Individuals who experience threats to their freedoms would attempt to restore their lost or threatened freedoms (Brehm, 1966; Engs & Hanson, 1989; Worchel & Brehm, 1971; Brehm & Brehm, 1981, pp. 101-106). More specifically, they may experience anger and generate negative thoughts toward the message, its source, or the advocated position (Bensley & Wu, 1991; Dillard & Shen, 2005; Kim et al., 2020; Miller et al., 2007). Consequently, they may reject the recommendation, develop less favorable evaluations of the advocated behavior, or become more committed to maintaining their current behavior (Ma et al., 2025; Rains, 2013; Steindl et al., 2015; Worchel & Brehm, 1971).

These consequences are particularly concerning in health communication, where reactance to recommendations such as quitting vaping, reducing alcohol consumption, or getting vaccinated can directly undermine individuals' health outcomes. Understanding which pathway between anthropomorphism and reactance predominates is therefore vital for designing AI chatbots that effectively promote health behavior change while minimizing reactance.

Before exploring the relationship between anthropomorphism and psychological reactance, it is necessary to first understand anthropomorphism. Broadly speaking, anthropomorphism refers to the attribution of humanlike characteristics, motivations, intentions, or emotions to nonhuman entities (Epley et al., 2007). Although most existing studies treated anthropomorphism as a single construct, some scholars have distinguished between mindful and mindless modes of anthropomorphism (Kim & Sundar, 2012). Mindful anthropomorphism involves deliberate attribution, whereas mindless anthropomorphism occurs more automatically (Kim & Sundar, 2012; Lombard & Xu, 2021).

Existing studies have yielded mixed evidence regarding whether chatbot anthropomorphic cues elicit both modes in the same or opposite direction (Araujo, 2018; Kim & Sundar, 2012; Li et al., 2023). This indicates that mindful and mindless anthropomorphism may capture distinct aspects of the construct, and both should be measured in the study. Therefore, the present study examines the relationship between anthropomorphism and psychological reactance through two models: one treating anthropomorphism as a single general construct, and the other distinguishing mindful and mindless anthropomorphism as two correlated individual constructs.

Estimating and comparing the two models provides a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms through which anthropomorphism shapes psychological reactance and its downstream outcomes.

With the development of Large Language Models (LLMs), AI chatbots have emerged as a promising approach to smoking/vaping cessation due to their accessibility and capacity to deliver personalized, real-time conversations (Liu et al., 2025). However, limited research has investigated the effectiveness of AI chatbot-mediated interventions for vaping cessation.

Moreover, vaping cessation is a particularly relevant context in which to examine psychological reactance. Unlike commercial chatbot interactions, vaping cessation conversations aim to change users' existing behaviors, which can directly threaten their perceived freedom. This makes reactance more likely to occur, providing an appropriate context in which to test whether and how anthropomorphism shapes reactance and its outcomes.

Given that AI chatbot-mediated persuasion is a relatively new area of research, important questions remain unanswered about how anthropomorphism shapes persuasive outcomes. In particular, little research has examined the competing pathways through which anthropomorphism may influence psychological reactance and its downstream outcomes, and even less has distinguished how mindful and mindless anthropomorphism may function differently in this process.

Therefore, to address the research gap, this study investigated two broad questions: First, does interacting with an AI chatbot produce different outcomes than viewing a static message?

Second, does anthropomorphism shape users' psychological reactance after interacting with an AI chatbot (anthropomorphic vs. non-anthropomorphic) about vaping cessation, and if so, do perceived persuasive intent and trust in the chatbot explain this relationship?

To answer these two questions, adult e-cigarette users were assigned to interact with either an anthropomorphic or non-anthropomorphic AI chatbot or to read a static cessation message. The study estimated two structural models, one treating anthropomorphism as a single construct and the other distinguishing mindful and mindless anthropomorphism as two correlated constructs, and examined perceived persuasive intent and trust in the AI chatbot as competing pathways to psychological reactance, with attitudes toward vaping and intentions to quit as downstream outcomes.

2. Literature Review

>Literature Review

2.1 AI Chatbots in Persuasive Health Communication

AI chatbots have been used to promote physical activity, healthy eating, weight management, medication adherence, vaccination, mental health, and tobacco cessation (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2024; Oh et al., 2021). Several characteristics make chatbots suitable for health persuasion. They can use real-time user input to tailor information and feedback, remain available outside clinical hours, and allow people to discuss sensitive issues with lower fear of social judgment than they might experience with another person (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Carrasco-Hernandez et al., 2020; Maher et al., 2020). Conversational language and responsive turn-taking may also provide a sense of emotional support.

Evidence of effectiveness is promising but inconsistent. Reviews have found improvements in selected health attitudes and behaviors, but effects vary across outcomes, intervention duration, chatbot capability, personalization, and comparison conditions (Fitzpatrick et al., 2017; Fulmer et al., 2018; Ly et al., 2017; Tawfik et al., 2023; Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., 2022; Prochaska et al., 2021; Tong et al., 2025). In vaccination research, for example, chatbot interventions have improved attitudes more consistently than intentions (Chan et al., 2023).

Studies of smoking-cessation chatbots have reported higher cessation rates, increased selfefficacy, and greater abstinence (He et al., 2025; Olano-Espinosa et al., 2022). Some chatbots also perform poorly when empathic language is not supported by adequate response quality, which can make the interaction seem inauthentic (Trzebinski et al., 2023).

More directly relevant to the present study, emerging research has compared conversational AI with static information, but findings have been mixed. Hwang et al (2026) found that information delivered by a conversational agent produced less psychological reactance and greater agreement than information presented as static text, in the context of environmental protection. In health contexts, Xu et al. (2026) found that personalized AI dialogues were more than twice as effective as the CDC brochure at increasing vaccinations, whereas Sehgal et al. (2026) found that AI chatbot conversations increased immediate HPVvaccination intentions compared to no intervention but did not outperform well-designed public health materials.

Given these mixed findings and the absence of direct evidence in the context of vaping cessation, the present study examines whether psychological reactance, vaping attitudes, and quit intentions differ across the anthropomorphic-chatbot, non-anthropomorphic-chatbot, and staticmessage conditions.

2.2 AI Chatbots as Social Actors

The Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) paradigm explains why people may respond socially to interactive technologies. CASA argues that people often apply interpersonal scripts and social norms automatically when computers display recognizable social cues, even when they know they are interacting with a machine (Nass & Moon, 2000; Nass et al., 1994). Two conditions are especially important. The technology must present enough cues to invite a social response, and users must perceive the technology as an autonomous source of communication rather than only a channel carrying another person's message (Gambino et al., 2020; Nass & Steuer, 1993).

Contemporary AI chatbots are capable of eliciting social responses because they satisfy both criteria. First, AI chatbots can present sufficient social cues, as they communicate through natural language in a conversational, turn-taking manner. Second, AI chatbots can be perceived as autonomous message sources by design, as they generate their own responses in real time rather than transmitting messages authored by humans. These features lead users to respond to AI chatbots socially, even when they consciously recognize that they are interacting with an artificial system (Nass & Moon, 2000).

2.3 Mindful and Mindless Anthropomorphism

CASA was initially explained through mindlessness: people automatically apply familiar interpersonal scripts when technologies display social cues. Early CASA scholars generally distinguished this process from anthropomorphism, which was often understood as a conscious attribution of human qualities to nonhuman entities. However, drawing on Sundar's (2004) earlier observation that anthropomorphism could result from users' long-term social associations with computers, Kim and Sundar (2012) directly challenged this distinction, arguing that anthropomorphism does not need to be conscious. In a 2×2 experiment, they manipulated the presence of a human-like agent and the level of interactivity to examine whether these cues would produce mindful and mindless anthropomorphism. Mindful anthropomorphism was measured by asking participants whether the website seemed humanlike, natural, or lifelike, whereas mindless anthropomorphism was measured using four items that asked whether the website appeared likeable, sociable, friendly, and personal. The results showed that participants exposed to the humanlike agent reported lower mindful anthropomorphism but higher mindless anthropomorphism compared to those who were not exposed to the humanlike agent.

Additionally, participants in the high-interactivity condition reported higher mindless anthropomorphism but the same level of mindful anthropomorphism compared to those in the low-interactivity condition. The results revealed that mindful and mindless anthropomorphism are empirically distinguishable. Users who explicitly deny that an agent is humanlike can still perceive it in a social way (Kim & Sundar, 2012).

Based on these findings, Lombard and Xu (2021) extended the CASA paradigm by proposing that mindlessness and anthropomorphism are not competing explanations but complementary mechanisms within the MASA paradigm. When social cues are high in quality and quantity, users may respond automatically, reflecting mindless anthropomorphism; when social cues are weaker or less sufficient, users may respond deliberately, reflecting mindful anthropomorphism (Kim & Sundar, 2012; Lombard & Xu, 2021).

Building on the theoretical origins above, mindful and mindless anthropomorphism represent two distinct modes through which users perceive media agents as humanlike. Mindful anthropomorphism refers to the deliberate and thoughtful attribution of human traits and characteristics to the media agent, whereas mindless anthropomorphism refers to the nonconscious, intuitive, and spontaneous attribution of such traits and characteristics (Nass & Moon, 2000; Kim & Sundar, 2012; Lombard & Xu, 2021).

Empirical findings support the distinction but do not show one fixed relationship between the dimensions. Kim and Sundar (2012) found that humanlike and interactive cues affected mindful and mindless anthropomorphism differently. Araujo (2018), by contrast, found that anthropomorphic cues in a text-based customer-service chatbot increased both dimensions.

Research on health conversational agents has also found that both dimensions can increase while predicting different downstream outcomes (Li et al., 2023). These findings justify examining a general anthropomorphism construct while also estimating mindful and mindless anthropomorphism separately.

2.4 Anthropomorphism and Perceived Persuasive Intent

The three-factor theory of anthropomorphism posits that anthropomorphism operates as an inductive inference process through which people apply knowledge about humans to nonhuman targets (Epley et al., 2007). Through this process, people may attribute humanlike characteristics, motivations, intentions, and emotions to the imagined or actual behavior of nonhuman agents (Epley et al., 2007). Mind perception theory further organizes these attributed capacities into two categories: experience and agency (Gray et al., 2007). Experience refers to the capacity to sense and feel, encompassing capacities such as hunger, fear, pain, and pleasure.

Agency, in contrast, refers to the capacity to think, plan, and act intentionally, encompassing capacities such as self-control, morality, planning, and thought (Gray et al., 2007; Waytz et al., 2010).

Perceived agency of chatbots deserves attention in the context of persuasion because it may shape how users interpret the chatbot's intentions. When users attribute agency to a chatbot, they may be more likely to interpret its recommendations or messages as deliberate attempts to influence their attitudes or behaviors, resulting in greater perceived persuasive intent. Heightened perceived persuasive intent may subsequently increase psychological reactance, potentially weakening the persuasiveness of the messages. Perceived persuasive intent refers to "a target's perception of an agent's strategic behavior in presenting information designed to influence someone's beliefs, attitudes, decisions, or actions" (Friestad & Wright, 1994). Empirical evidence suggests that higher perceived persuasive intent can heighten psychological reactance (Moyer-Guse & Nabi, 2010; Reynolds-Tylus, 2019; van Noort et al., 2012). AI sources may be perceived differently from human sources. Lu et al. (2025) found that counterattitudinal messages attributed to AI were perceived as less biased, more informative, and having less persuasive intent than the same message attributed to human sources. Similarly, Candrian and Scherer (2023) found that threatening information triggered less reactance when delivered by an AI rather than a human source. However, little research has tested whether anthropomorphic features change perceived persuasive intent within AI chatbot interactions. Pizzi et al. (2021) found that anthropomorphic digital assistants produced less reactance than non-anthropomorphic assistants, but they did not test persuasive intent as the mechanism.

The present study therefore considers the possibility that anthropomorphism increases reactance by increasing perceived persuasive intent: H1. Anthropomorphism is positively associated with perceived persuasive intent.

H2.

Perceived persuasive intent is positively associated with psychological reactance.

H3.

Perceived persuasive intent mediates a positive association between anthropomorphism and psychological reactance.

Because mindful and mindless anthropomorphism may operate through different processes, the study also asks: RQ3. Do mindful and mindless anthropomorphism have different indirect associations with reactance through perceived persuasive intent?

2.5 Anthropomorphism and Trust

Trust is particularly relevant when people receive health information from an AI chatbot.

The processes that generate a model's response are difficult for most users to inspect, and users must decide whether the system is capable of providing reliable assistance and whether it is oriented toward their interests. Trust in automation can therefore be understood as willingness to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations that an agent can help achieve a goal under uncertainty (Lee & See, 2004; Rousseau et al., 1998). In the present study, trust in the chatbot reflects perceived competence and benevolence.

Existing studies have found mixed results regarding whether anthropomorphic cues increase trust in or the perceived trustworthiness of AI agents. Reuter et al. 's (2025) scoping review found that eight studies reported significant positive effects of anthropomorphism on trust, four found no significant effect, and seven reported mixed or conditional effects. Across these studies, communication style and voice appeared to influence trust more consistently than names or appearance.

Previous research has found the direct or indirect positive association between anthropomorphism and trust in the AI agents, even though they measured trust in chatbots in subtly different ways. Janson (2023), for example, conceptualized trusting beliefs along three dimensions: competence, benevolence, and integrity. The study found that anthropomorphic chatbot design positively influenced these trusting beliefs through increased social presence.

Similarly, Murtaza et al. (2024) modeled trust in two dimensions: competence and honesty, after Cheng et al. (2021). They found that, consistent across the three studies, perceived anthropomorphism mediated the positive effect of socially oriented communication on trust, which, in turn, affects intention to use the chatbot. Differently, Park et al. (2023) measured trust globally through assessments of trust and honesty in the chatbot's information, and they also found a positive impact of anthropomorphism on trust.

In turn, greater trust may reduce psychological reactance. When users perceive a chatbot as capable of providing reliable services and as oriented toward their interests, they may be less likely to view its recommendations as threatening to their freedom and experience less reactance (Brendel et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2021). Therefore, trust in the AI chatbot is expected to mediate the effects of mindful and mindless anthropomorphism on psychological reactance. This reasoning leads to the following hypotheses: H4. Anthropomorphism is positively associated with trust in the AI chatbot.

H5.

Trust in the AI chatbot is negatively associated with psychological reactance.

H6.

Trust mediates a negative association between anthropomorphism and psychological reactance.

Because mindful and mindless anthropomorphism may operate through different processes, the study also asks: RQ4. Do mindful and mindless anthropomorphism have different indirect associations with reactance through trust in the AI chatbot?

2.6 Psychological Reactance and Persuasion Outcomes

Proposed by Brehm in 1966, psychological reactance theory (PRT) suggests that individuals believe they have certain behavioral freedoms. When these freedoms are threatened or eliminated, the motive to reassert them will be aroused (Brehm, 1966). This restoration can occur directly or indirectly (Dillard & Shen, 2005): Direct restoration of the freedom involves adopting a position or behavior opposite from the position or behavior advocated (Worchel & Brehm, 1971). In addition, freedoms may be restored indirectly by increasing the perceived attractiveness for the threatened choice (Brehm et al., 1966; Hammock & Brehm, 1966), derogating the source of threat (Kohn & Barnes, 1977; Schwarz et al., 1980; Smith, 1977; Worchel, 1974), denying the existence of the threat (Worchel & Andreoli, 1974; Worchel et al., 1976), or by exercising an alternative freedom to gain feeling of control and choice (Wicklund, 1974).

In the context of vaping cessation, greater reactance may cause users to maintain more favorable attitudes toward vaping and reduce their willingness to quit (Rains, 2013; Ma et al., 2025). Yao and Rui (2024) found that high controlling language triggered reactance, which caused negative attitudes and subsequently lower intentions to quit smoking in both China and America. Therefore, users who experience greater reactance may attempt to restore their threatened freedom by resisting the chatbot's recommendation. Consequently, they may develop less negative attitudes toward vaping and, in turn, weaker intentions to quit. Accordingly, H7 and H8 are proposed:

H7. Greater psychological reactance is associated with less negative attitudes toward vaping.

H8. More negative attitudes toward vaping are associated with stronger intentions to quit vaping.

The trust and persuasive-intent pathways imply opposing indirect associations between anthropomorphism and reactance. The study therefore asks:

RQ5a: What is the total association between general anthropomorphism and psychological reactance?

RQ5b: Do the total associations of mindful and mindless anthropomorphism with psychological reactance differ significantly? The above research questions also extend to downstream outcomes:

RQ6a: What are the total associations between general anthropomorphism and attitudes toward vaping and intentions to quit?

Method 3.1 Study context This study investigated the proposed hypotheses in the context of vaping-cessation messages delivered by AI chatbots. Vaping cessation was selected for two reasons. First, ecigarette use remains a substantial public health concern in the United States. In 2024, approximately 7.0% of U.S. adults reported current e-cigarette use (National Center for Health Statistics [NCHS], 2025). Vaping has been associated with cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, and oral-health risks (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2025; Nagy et al., 2025; Seiler-Ramadas et al., 2020), underscoring the continued need for effective cessation communication.

Second, vaping cessation provides a theoretically relevant setting for examining psychological reactance. Although communication campaigns and interventions have sought to discourage e-cigarette use (Ma et al., 2023; Mylocopos et al., 2024; Reiter et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024), strongly worded recommendations may threaten users' perceived freedom and produce resistance. For example, ever-vapers exposed to dogmatic public service announcements reported greater perceived freedom threat and psychological reactance than those exposed to suggestive messages (Clayton et al., 2020; Clayton et al., 2023). Vaping cessation is therefore a suitable context for investigating whether the design of a persuasive technology can reduce resistance to health recommendations. AI chatbots were chosen as the communication format because emerging evidence suggests that conversational agents may support tobacco-cessation efforts. Engagement with Aipaca, a GPT-4-powered cessation chatbot, was associated with improvements in health-risk knowledge, cessation knowledge, self-efficacy, and readiness to quit (Liu et al., 2025). Similarly, a pilot randomized controlled trial found higher three-month smoking-abstinence rates among QuitBot users than among users of the National Cancer Institute's SmokefreeTXT program (Bricker et al., 2024). These findings suggest that AI chatbots may offer a promising channel for delivering cessation information and support.

3.2 Study stimuli 3.2.1. AI chatbot conditions

To create a real-time conversational experience, the GPT-4.1-mini API was embedded in the online survey. Participants were informed that they would interact with an AI chatbot and that the messages displayed during the interaction were AI-generated. Participants in both chatbot conditions completed three conversational rounds.

Anthropomorphism was manipulated through the chatbot's system instructions, which were developed using the COSTAR prompting framework (Teo, 2025). The manipulation varied the chatbot's assigned role, communication style, and tone. In the anthropomorphic condition, the chatbot was instructed to act as a warm, friendly, and personally engaged conversational partner, communicate naturally, demonstrate attentiveness, and refer to information previously shared by the participant. In the non-anthropomorphic condition, it was instructed to act as a neutral informational tool and communicate in a clear, impersonal, task-focused, and robot-like manner without conveying warmth, personality, or social presence.

All other major instructions were held constant across the two chatbot conditions. Both chatbots were instructed to provide accurate information about vaping risks and cessation strategies, tailor their responses to participants' input, and help participants consider quitting.

Responses were limited to 120-150 words and were required to remain consistent with guidance from the NIH, CDC, or FDA. The chatbots were also instructed not to diagnose participants or provide medical advice and to refer users to qualified professionals when appropriate. The complete system prompts are provided in the Appendix A.

Before the interaction, participants answered an open-ended question about their experiences with vaping and thoughts about quitting. Their response was provided to the chatbot to support personalized communication. Conversation history was retained and transmitted to the model during each round, allowing subsequent responses to incorporate participants' earlier statements. Although the exact wording and information generated by the chatbot varied across participants, the two conditions shared the same persuasive objective, authoritative information sources, response-length requirements, safety constraints, number of conversational rounds, and access to participants' previous input (specific prompts can be found in Appendix A).

3.2.2. Static-message condition

Participants in the static-message condition read a fixed 127-word vaping-cessation message. The message was developed based on content patterns identified during repeated developmental interactions with both chatbot versions and addressed three topics commonly covered across the chatbot conversations: the health risks of vaping, the benefits of quitting, and possible cessation strategies. Consistent with the chatbot instructions, the message presented information derived from authoritative health sources. The complete static message is provided in Appendix A.

The static condition served as a noninteractive benchmark rather than a fully dosagematched control. Unlike participants in the chatbot conditions, participants in this condition did not receive personalized responses or engage in multiple conversational rounds. Therefore, comparisons between either chatbot condition and the static-message condition capture the broader effects of chatbot-based communication, including interactivity, personalization, and additional message exposure. However, the comparison between the anthropomorphic and nonanthropomorphic chatbot conditions provides a more controlled test of the anthropomorphism manipulation.

4. Method

>Method

4.1 Procedure

An online experiment was conducted using a between-subjects design with three conditions: a non-anthropomorphized AI chatbot condition, an anthropomorphized AI chatbot condition, and a static message control condition. Participants were recruited from Prolific, an online crowdsourcing platform designed for research purposes, from April 16 -17, 2026.

Eligible participants were Prolific users who were 21 years or older, current e-cigarette users, living in the United States, and had a previous approval rate of at least 98%. Current e-cigarette users were defined as individuals who used e-cigarettes in the past 30 days and had not already quit (Cuccia et al., 2021). Qualified participants who agreed to participate were offered $2.20 for completing the 15-minute study.

After screening and providing informed consent, eligible participants reported their vaping status and perceptions of vaping. They were then randomly assigned to one of the three conditions. Participants in the chatbot conditions completed three rounds of interaction about vaping with a pre-prompted chatbot powered by the GPT-4.1-mini API (Costello et al., 2024), whereas those in the control condition read a static persuasive message discouraging vaping.

Following exposure, chatbot participants reported perceived anthropomorphism, response coherence, perceived intelligence, trust, perceived persuasive intent, psychological reactance, attitudes toward vaping, and intentions to quit. They also reported general trust in and familiarity with AI chatbots; these covariates were measured after exposure to minimize priming.

Participants in the static-message condition reported psychological reactance, vaping attitudes, and quit intentions. All participants then provided demographic information and received a study debriefing.

4.2 Participant

The primary structural equation modeling (SEM) analyses included participants assigned to the two chatbot conditions. Following the recommendation of at least five observations per estimated free parameter (Bentler & Chou, 1987), the 74-parameter model required at least 370 chatbot participants. Approximately 185 additional participants were targeted for the staticmessage condition, producing a total target of at least 555 qualified participants.

A total of 692 participants were recruited. 89 did not meet the eligibility criteria, and 17 failed at least one of two attention checks. The final analytic sample comprised 586 participants: 189 in the non-anthropomorphic chatbot condition, 197 in the anthropomorphic chatbot condition, and 200 in the static-message condition. Participants had a mean age of 38.56 years (SD = 11.51); 44.4% identified as male and 54.8% as female. Additional sample characteristics are reported in Table 1.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the author's institution. All participants provided informed consent electronically before participating in the study.

4.3 Measures 4.3.1 Anthropomorphism

Mindful Anthropomorphism and Mindless Anthropomorphism were measured using items adapted from Nass and Moon (2000). Mindful anthropomorphism was assessed with three 7point semantic differential items (e.g., machinelike -humanlike; Cronbach's α = .943). Mindless anthropomorphism was assessed using four 7-point Likert-type items capturing perceived likability, sociability, friendliness, and personalness, with responses ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) (Cronbach's α = .936). In the SEM examining general anthropomorphism, all seven mindful and mindless anthropomorphism items were specified as indicators of a single latent construct (Cronbach's α = .951).

4.3.2 Trust in the AI chatbot

Trust in the AI chatbot was measured by five items adapted from Chi & Hoang Vu (2022), using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). A sample item was "I trust that the AI chatbot I just interacted with will take care of me" (Cronbach's α = .910).

4.3.3 Perceived persuasive intent

Perceived persuasive intent was measured by three items adapted from van Noort et al.

(2012), using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). A sample item was "These responses generated by the AI chatbot were to persuade" (Cronbach's α = .920).

4.3.4 Psychological reactance

As a motivational state, latent variable psychological reactance cannot be measured directly. Instead, anger and negative cognitions are two manifest predictors of psychological reactance. Anger was measured by having respondents rate on semantic-differential scales, ranging from 0 representing none of this feeling to 4 indicating a great deal of this feeling, to what degree the responses produced by the AI chatbot made them feel: (a) angry, (b) irritated, (c) annoyed, and (d) aggravated (Dillard & Shen, 2005, Cronbach's α = .898). Negative cognitions were measured with a 7-point semantic-differential scale, using two items developed from Quick et al.'s (2015): "My thoughts about the responses generated by the AI chatbot are … ([1 = negative] to [7 = positive]);" "My thoughts about the responses generated by the AI chatbot are … ([1 = unfavorable] to [7 = favorable])" (Cronbach's α = .887).

4.3.5 Attitudes toward vaping

Attitudes toward vaping were measured with a 7-point semantic-differential scale, using four items developed by Ajzen (2002): (a) harmful -beneficial; (b) unpleasant -pleasant; (3) bad -good; (4) worthless -valuable (Cronbach's α = .865).

4.3.6 Intentions to quit vaping

Intentions to quit vaping were assessed with three items adapted from Yang (2023), rated on a 7-point scale from 1 (very unlikely) to 7 (very likely). Participants indicated whether they intended, expected, and would try to quit vaping within the next 12 months (Cronbach's α = .956).

4.3.7 Additional chatbot evaluations

Response coherence and perceived intelligence were measured as additional evaluations of the chatbot. Response coherence refers to the degree to which the AI chatbot's outputs are informative, relevant, specific, and clear (Xiao et al., 2020). Perceived intelligence refers to users' assessments of the AI chatbot's functional cognitive capabilities, encompassing its ability to understand "user's requests and responds without continuous user intervention and with a certain awareness of the physical and virtual worlds, is able to adapt to change and learn from newly acquired information and the user's behavior, and is able to successfully complete tasks within a favorable timeframe for the user (Moussawi et al., 2020). Both variables were identified here because they could be unintentionally influenced by the manipulation and, in turn, affect the mediators (i.e., trust in the AI chatbot and perceived persuasive intent; Alagarsamy & Mehrolia, 2023; Ma et al., 2025).

Response coherence was assessed with four 7-point items capturing the informativeness, relevance, specificity, and clarity of the chatbot's responses (Xiao et al., 2020; Cronbach's α = .868). Perceived intelligence was measured with five 7-point items assessing the chatbot's responsiveness, comprehension, communication, information-processing ability, and usefulness (Moussawi et al., 2020; Cronbach's α = .876).

4.3.8 Control variables

Control variables included vaping status, familiarity with AI chatbots, and general trust in AI chatbots.

Vaping status was categorized according to the number of days participants had used ecigarettes during the previous 30 days: infrequent use (1-5 days), intermediate use (6-27 days), or daily use (28-30 days; Amato et al., 2016). Familiarity with AI chatbots was measured with two 7-point items assessing participants' understanding of the underlying technology and familiarity with using AI chatbots (Cronbach's α = .746). General trust in AI chatbots was measured with five items adapted from Chi and Hoang Vu (2022), using a 7-point agreement scale (Cronbach's α = .907). Unlike chatbot-specific trust, this measure assessed participants' general trust in AI chatbots, such as ChatGPT and Gemini. Demographic characteristics included age, sex assigned at birth, education, and income. Descriptive statistics, reliabilities, and correlations among all variables are reported in Table 2.

4.4 Data analyses

Randomization was evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance for age and chisquare tests for categorical demographic variables. Sparse categories were combined or excluded when necessary to satisfy expected-frequency requirements (Cochran, 1954).

To evaluate the anthropomorphism manipulation, independent-samples t-tests compared the two chatbot conditions on general, mindful, and mindless anthropomorphism. Additional ttests examined whether the conditions differed in response coherence or perceived intelligence.

Normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated using Q-Q plots, Shapiro-Wilk tests, and Levene's tests; Welch's t-tests were used when equal variances could not be assumed.

Additionally, internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha and corrected itemtotal correlations. An alpha of .70 or higher was considered acceptable (Nunnally, 1978). All items exhibited acceptable corrected item-total correlations and were retained for subsequent analyses.

To address RQ1 and RQ2, ordinary least squares regressions (OLS) examined the effects of experimental conditions on psychological reactance, attitudes toward vaping, and intentions to quit. Condition was entered categorically, with the static-message condition serving as the reference group. Models adjusted for vaping status, age, sex, education, and income. Linearity, homoscedasticity, residual normality, and multicollinearity were evaluated using residual plots, Q-Q plots, and variance inflation factors.

To address RQ3 to RQ6, SEM was conducted among participants in the two chatbot conditions (n = 386) using the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). Following Anderson and Gerbing's (1988) two-step approach, the measurement models were evaluated before the structural relationships were tested. Model fit was assessed using the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Values of CFI and TLI ≥ .95, RMSEA ≤ .06, and SRMR ≤ .08 indicated acceptable fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Factor loadings of at least .50 were retained (Hair et al., 2019). Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed using average variance extracted and the Fornell-Larcker criterion (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

Two models were estimated. The first specified general anthropomorphism as a single latent construct indicated by all seven mindful and mindless anthropomorphism items. The second specified mindful and mindless anthropomorphism as correlated but distinct latent constructs. Direct, indirect, and total associations were estimated, with indirect effects evaluated using 5,000 bootstrap resamples. Statistical significance was evaluated at p < .05.

5.1 Preliminary results

5.1.1 Randomization check

The three conditions were comparable in size: non-anthropomorphic chatbot (n = 189), anthropomorphic chatbot (n = 197), and static message (n = 200). Randomization checks indicated no condition differences in age, F(2, 582) = 1.69, p = .186; sex (male vs. female), χ²(2, N = 581) = 1.47, p = .479; race/ethnicity (White, Black, Asian, or other), χ²(6, N = 586) = 0.71, p = .994; income, χ²(10, N = 585) = 13.18, p = .214; or vaping frequency, χ²(4, N = 586) = 5.29, p = .259. Education differed across the three conditions, χ²(8, N = 586) = 16.34, p = .038, but not between the two chatbot conditions, χ²(4, N = 386) = 5.45, p = .244. Accordingly, no demographic covariates were added to the SEM, whereas education was included with the other prespecified covariates in the regressions comparing all three conditions.

5.1.2 Manipulation check

Because the anthropomorphism measures were nonnormally distributed and had unequal variances across conditions, Welch's t tests were used. Participants in the anthropomorphized chatbot condition perceived the chatbot as more generally anthropomorphic (M = 5.20, SD = 1.26) than those in the non-anthropomorphized chatbot condition (M = 4.27, SD = 1.70), t(345.62) = 6.09, p < .001, d = 0.62. They also reported higher mindful anthropomorphism (M = 4.79, SD = 1.58 vs. M = 4.16, SD = 1.93), t(363.37) = 3.49, p < .001, d = 0.36, and higher mindless anthropomorphism (M = 5.51, SD = 1.20 vs. M = 4.35, SD = 1.66), t(341.21) = 7.86, p < .001, d = 0.81. These results confirmed the effectiveness of the manipulation.

5.1.3 Additional chatbot evaluations

Participants in the anthropomorphic condition reported slightly greater response coherence (M = 5.91, SD = 0.86) than those in the non-anthropomorphic condition (M = 5.67, SD = 1.21; p = .027, d = 0.23). They also perceived the anthropomorphic chatbot as more intelligent (M = 6.06, SD = 0.74 vs. M = 5.69, SD = 1.15), p < .001, d = 0.39. Because these post-exposure evaluations differed between conditions, they were retained in the SEM.

Familiarity with AI chatbots did not differ between the chatbot conditions (p = .175), and was excluded for model parsimony. General trust in AI chatbots differed between conditions (p = .019) and was retained. Because general trust, response coherence, and perceived intelligence were measured after exposure, they were modeled as outcomes of condition and as predictors of trust in the specific chatbot and perceived persuasive intent.

5.1.4 Measurement model analysis

Two confirmatory factor analyses were conducted among participants in the chatbot conditions (n = 386) using robust maximum likelihood estimation. The first model specified general anthropomorphism as a single latent construct indicated by all seven mindful and mindless anthropomorphism items. This model showed mixed fit: χ²(333) = 1103.66, p < .001, CFI = .922, TLI = .911, RMSEA = .077, 90% CI [.072, .083], SRMR = .059. Although the SRMR indicated acceptable fit, the remaining indices did not meet the preferred criteria specified in Section 4.4.

The second model specified mindful and mindless anthropomorphism as correlated but distinct constructs and demonstrated stronger fit: χ²(327) = 763.63, p < .001, CFI = .956, TLI = .949, RMSEA = .059, 90% CI [.053, .064], SRMR = .058. Across both models, all standardized factor loadings exceeded .50 and were statistically significant. Average variance extracted values ranged from .637 to .878, and all constructs satisfied the Fornell-Larcker criterion, supporting convergent and discriminant validity.

Prior to finalizing the model, several theoretically motivated modifications were made suggested by model fit diagnostics and modification indices. The model was respecified by adding a direct path from psychological reactance to quit intentions, consistent with reactance theory and improved model fit (Brehm & Brehm, 1981). Response coherence, perceived intelligence, and general trust in AI chatbots were modeled as condition-predicted endogenous variables and as predictors of trust in the specific chatbot and perceived persuasive intent. 5.2.2 H1 to H3, and RQ3: perceived persuasive intent pathway

H1-H3 examined whether general anthropomorphism was indirectly associated with psychological reactance through perceived persuasive intent. General anthropomorphism was not significantly associated with perceived persuasive intent (B = -.008, SE = .091, p = .93), failing to support H1. 5.2.3 H4 to H6, and RQ4: trust pathway

H4-H6 examined whether general anthropomorphism was indirectly associated with psychological reactance through trust. Consistent with H4, general anthropomorphism was positively associated with trust in the chatbot (B = .637, SE = .142, p < .001). Trust was negatively associated with psychological reactance (B = -.441, SE = .109, p < .001), supporting H5. The indirect association between anthropomorphism and reactance through trust was also negatively significant (B = -.281, SE = .085, p < .001), supporting H6.

In terms of RQ4, different patterns for mindful and mindless anthropomorphism were revealed. Mindful anthropomorphism was positively associated with trust (B = .615, SE = .178, p < .001) and had a significant negative indirect association with reactance through trust (B = -.294, SE = .121, p = .015). In contrast, mindless anthropomorphism was not associated with trust (B = .024, p = .893) and its trust-mediated indirect association was nonsignificant (B = -.011, p = .900). Thus, only mindful anthropomorphism was associated with lower reactance through greater trust.

5.2.4 H7 and H8: downstream effects on attitudes and quit intentions

H7 and H8 examined the downstream effects of psychological reactance on attitudes and quit intentions. Psychological reactance was not significantly associated with attitudes toward vaping (B = .011, SE = .028, p = .699), failing to support H7. Consistent with H8, more negative attitudes toward vaping were associated with stronger quit intentions (B = -.386, SE = .065, p < .001). H8 was supported.

5.2.5. RQ5a and RQ5b: total associations with psychological reactance

RQ5a examined the total association between general anthropomorphism and psychological reactance. General anthropomorphism had significant negative indirect (B = -.284, SE = .097, p = .003) and direct associations (B = -.927, SE = .229, p < .001) with reactance. Its total association with reactance was therefore negative and significant (B = -1.212, SE = .261, p < .001).

RQ5b examined mindful and mindless anthropomorphism separately. Mindful anthropomorphism had a significant combined indirect association with reactance based on its bootstrap confidence interval (B = -.266, SE = .139, p = .055, 95% CI [-.568, -.049]), although its direct and total associations were nonsignificant. In contrast, mindless anthropomorphism had no significant combined indirect association with reactance, but it had a significant negative direct association (B = -.876, SE = .325, p = .007), and a significant negative total association (B = -.920, SE = .363, p = .011). Thus, mindful anthropomorphism was associated with lower reactance indirectly, primarily through trust, whereas mindless anthropomorphism was associated with lower reactance through a direct pathway.

5.2.6. RQ6a and RQ6b: total associations with attitudes and quit intentions

RQ6a examined the total associations between general anthropomorphism with attitudes toward vaping and intentions to quit vaping, including both direct and indirect pathways. General anthropomorphism had no significant total association with attitudes toward vaping (B = -.013, SE = .034, p = .698), but had a significant positive total association with quit intentions (B = .323, SE = .057, p < .001).

RQ6b examined the respective total associations of mindful and mindless anthropomorphism with attitudes toward vaping and intentions to quit vaping. Neither mindful nor mindless anthropomorphism had a significant total association with attitudes toward vaping.

Mindful anthropomorphism was also not significantly associated with quit intentions in total (B = .066, SE = .056, p = .238). Mindless anthropomorphism, however, had a significant positive total association with quit intentions (B = .240, SE = .071, p < .001).

In summary, neither mindful nor mindless anthropomorphism was significantly associated with attitudes toward vaping in total. However, mindless anthropomorphism showed a significant positive total association with intentions to quit vaping, whereas mindful anthropomorphism did not.

6. Discussion

This study examined whether and how AI chatbot anthropomorphism shapes psychological reactance to vaping-cessation messages. Participants assigned to the anthropomorphic chatbot condition reported less reactance than those who read the static message, whereas the non-anthropomorphic chatbot did not differ significantly from the static message.

The SEM results further clarified the mechanisms underlying these responses. General anthropomorphism was associated with greater trust in the chatbot, which was subsequently associated with lower reactance. By contrast, anthropomorphism was not associated with perceived persuasive intent, although perceived persuasive intent was associated with greater reactance. Moreover, mindful and mindless anthropomorphism followed distinct pathways: mindful anthropomorphism was indirectly associated with lower reactance through trust, whereas mindless anthropomorphism was directly associated with lower reactance. These findings demonstrate that anthropomorphism is not a unitary process and that its dimensions may facilitate receptivity to persuasive AI communication in different ways.

6.1. Theoretical Implications

This study extends research on chatbot anthropomorphism to health persuasion. Unlike many commercial interactions, vaping-cessation communication explicitly encourages users to change an established behavior and may therefore threaten perceived freedom. The finding that an anthropomorphic chatbot elicited less reactance than a static message suggests that conversational and humanlike communication may help reduce resistance to health recommendations, even when the advocated behavioral change is potentially freedom threatening.

The findings also distinguish two mechanisms through which anthropomorphism could influence reactance. General anthropomorphism was associated with greater trust, which, in turn, was associated with lower reactance. Users who perceived the chatbot as more humanlike may have regarded it as more credible, dependable, or responsive to their needs and consequently experienced its recommendations as less threatening. Trust therefore appears to be an important mechanism connecting perceived anthropomorphism with receptivity to health messages. In contrast, anthropomorphism was not associated with perceived persuasive intent. This null finding is theoretically informative because it suggests that perceiving humanlike qualities in an AI chatbot does not necessarily lead users to infer that the system is attempting to influence them.

A central contribution of this study is its distinction between mindful and mindless anthropomorphism. The measurement and structural models separating the two dimensions fit the data better than the models treating all seven items as a single anthropomorphism construct.

More importantly, the dimensions exhibited different relationships with trust and reactance.

Mindful anthropomorphism was associated with lower reactance primarily through greater trust, whereas mindless anthropomorphism was instead directly associated with lower reactance.

These patterns extend the CASA/MASA perspective by showing that conscious perceptions of humanlikeness and automatic social responses are not interchangeable. Treating anthropomorphism as a single construct could conceal the different processes through which humanlike AI communication shapes persuasion. Future theory and measurement should therefore specify which form of anthropomorphism is expected to operate and why.

6.2. Practical and Methodological Implications

The findings offer several implications for the design of health-focused AI chatbots.

Designers may be able to reduce reactance by developing chatbots that communicate in a socially responsive and personable manner while cultivating users' trust. In particular, the distinct role of mindful anthropomorphism suggests that humanlike communication may reduce reactance when it strengthens trust. The direct association between mindless anthropomorphism and lower reactance further highlights the potential value of making chatbots appear or sound likeable, friendly, sociable, and personal.

The absence of an association between anthropomorphism and perceived persuasive intent is also encouraging, although it should not be interpreted as evidence that anthropomorphic design is universally risk free. In the present context, increasing anthropomorphism did not appear to make the chatbot's persuasive purpose more salient.

Designers may therefore be able to incorporate humanlike conversational features without necessarily increasing users' perceptions that the chatbot is attempting to manipulate them.

Methodologically, this study demonstrates a prompt-based approach to manipulating AI chatbot anthropomorphism. Rather than relying exclusively on fixed names, avatars, or scripted language, the chatbot was instructed to communicate in a more humanlike manner while adapting its responses to each conversational turn. The manipulation was effective. This approach preserves the responsiveness of LLM-enabled interactions and may be extended to experimental manipulations of other perceived characteristics, such as warmth, empathy, or humor. Nevertheless, researchers should continue to assess possible effects on response coherence, perceived intelligence, and other chatbot evaluations.

6.3. Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited through Prolific and were limited to U.S. adults who reported current e-cigarette use. The sample was younger, more highly educated, and included a greater proportion of White participants than the general U.S. population (U. S. Census Bureau, 2024a Bureau, , 2024b)). Generalization to the broader population, adolescents, or other groups of people who vape should therefore be approached cautiously.

Second, the study did not measure dispositional anthropomorphism because of questionnaire-length constraints. Random assignment should have minimized systematic differences between conditions, but individuals may differ in their general tendency to attribute human qualities to nonhuman agents (Waytz et al., 2010). Future research should examine whether this tendency moderates the effects of chatbot design on mindful and mindless anthropomorphism, trust, and reactance. The study also did not measure perceived threat to freedom, a central antecedent of reactance (Dillard & Shen, 2005; Rains, 2013). Including this construct would permit a more complete test of whether anthropomorphic design reduces reactance by making the message appear less freedom threatening.

Third, participants interacted with the chatbot for only three conversational rounds. The limited exposure may have prevented the effects of anthropomorphism from fully developing.

Additionally, the study captured users' responses immediately after the interaction and therefore could not assess whether the observed effects persisted over time. Further research should employ longer interactions and longitudinal designs to examine how users' responses to anthropomorphic chatbots evolve with repeated exposure and whether reductions in reactance are long-lasting beyond the immediate interaction.

Finally, the study examined one health topic using the GPT-4.1-mini API. Replication across vaccination, medication adherence, cancer screening, and other health contexts is necessary to establish generalizability. Comparisons across LLMs are also important because models may differ in their conversational styles, capabilities, and adherence to experimental prompts. Cross-model sensitivity analyses would help determine whether the observed patterns reflect general processes of chatbot anthropomorphism or characteristics of the particular model used.

7. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that interacting with an anthropomorphic AI chatbot reduced psychological reactance compared with reading a static health message, although this advantage did not extend to attitudes toward vaping or intentions to quit following a brief interaction. More importantly, the findings reveal distinct pathways linking anthropomorphism to psychological reactance: mindful anthropomorphism was indirectly associated with lower reactance through greater trust in the chatbot, whereas mindless anthropomorphism was directly associated with lower reactance. Neither dimension was significantly associated with perceived persuasive intent, although greater perceived persuasive intent was associated with higher reactance. By distinguishing these pathways, this study extends anthropomorphism and CASA/MASA research and advances our understanding of how humanlike characteristics shape responses to AImediated health persuasion. Overall, these findings support the potential effectiveness of anthropomorphic AI chatbots as a communication approach for vaping cessation and, more broadly, for health communication.
