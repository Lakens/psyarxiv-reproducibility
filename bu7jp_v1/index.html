Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

There is growing recognition of the importance of designing work environments that promote the well-being of diverse employees. Although the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model has contributed substantially to work engagement research, there remains scope to extend it to address the challenge of creating inclusive work environments. We examined the associations of workplace tolerance and individual autonomy with work engagement, including the potential mediating roles of psychological safety and intrinsic motivation. We used data from 1084 Japanese workers collected across four waves of online surveys and conducted causal mediation analyses. As a result, the presence of tolerant coworkers was associated with work engagement through workplace psychological safety (total effect = 0.296; proportion mediated = 29.7 %), whereas the absence of intolerant coworkers was not clearly associated with work engagement. Individual autonomy was also associated with work engagement through intrinsic motivation (total effect = 0.444), although the proportion mediated was modest (14.5 %). These findings suggest that positioning workplace tolerance and individual autonomy as social and personal resources, respectively, may extend the JD-R model by enabling it to describe the conditions under which employees with diverse characteristics and circumstances can engage in their work on an equitable basis.

Introduction

Contemporary societies are experiencing declining birth rates and population aging simultaneously, and these demographic changes are contributing to a shrinking workforce. At the same time, populations are becoming increasingly diverse. Against this background, there is growing recognition of the importance of designing work environments that prioritize the well-being of diverse individuals alongside conventional outcomes such as labor productivity (Cabinet Office, Government of Japan, 2019; Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, 2024; OECD, 2020).

In response to these demographic changes and the accompanying needs to secure an adequate workforce and promote equity, organizational psychology and management research have accumulated a substantial body of knowledge and practice concerning work engagement and diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI). Work engagement is defined as "a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind that is characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption" (Schaufeli et al., 2002). Work engagement has been associated with employees' health and well-being, including psychological distress and life satisfaction, as well as important work-related outcomes such as job satisfaction, organizational commitment, turnover intentions, and productivity (Eguchi et al., 2015 (Eguchi et al., , 2020;; Halbesleben, 2010; Kuroda et al., 2021; Mazzetti et al., 2023; Shimazu et al., 2008). Thus, work engagement is not only a component of well-being that is worth pursuing in its own right but is also associated with a range of important organizational outcomes.

The Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model is a central framework in the work engagement research (Bakker et al., 2023; Hakanen & Roodt, 2010; Schaufeli & Bakker, 2004). Within this model, resources are aspects of work or the individual that facilitate goal attainment, help employees manage job demands, and promote personal growth and development (Bakker et al., 2023). These include social, work-related, developmental, and personal resources (Mazzetti et al., 2023).

However, the JD-R model has developed primarily by examining how job demands and resources influence motivational and health-related outcomes. It has paid comparatively less attention to identifying resources that enable employees with diverse characteristics and circumstances to engage in their work on an equitable basis. Thus, although the JD-R model has contributed to addressing organizational concerns such as workforce sustainability and productivity, there remains scope to extend it to address the challenge of creating work environments in which diverse individuals have equitable opportunities to achieve well-being.

A substantial body of research has also examined the DEI in organizational contexts. Cox and Blake (1991) established a perspective that regards diversity management not as a cost or obligation but as a strategic organizational resource. Shore et al. (2011) conceptually distinguished diversity from inclusion and defined workplace inclusion as a state in which employees' needs for both belongingness and uniqueness are satisfied. Subsequent research has shown that diversity practices are associated with employee engagement through an organizational climate of trust (Downey et al., 2015), that a climate for inclusion is associated with reduced interpersonal bias and conflict and sustained unit-level satisfaction in genderdiverse groups (Nishii, 2013) and that inclusive leadership is positively associated with psychological safety (Carmeli et al., 2010; Hirak et al., 2012; Nembhard & Edmondson, 2006). Thus, diversity management requires more than increasing demographic representation through recruitment and promotion; increasing attention has been paid to whether everyday interactions and workplace environments create inclusive conditions that enable employees to participate and contribute fully (Ely & Thomas, 2020; Mor Barak, 2015; Shore et al., 2018).

Despite the growing recognition of the importance of inclusion, limitations in current DEI practices have also been identified. Organizational practice has tended to emphasize the implementation of discrete programs, such as training, mentoring, networking initiatives, and grievance procedures. However, these initiatives may fail to produce sustained change and can sometimes have unintended adverse consequences (Benschop et al., 2016; Dobbin & Kalev, 2017; Ely & Thomas, 2020; Leslie, 2019). In response to these concerns, attention has increasingly shifted from simply asking what programs have been implemented to examining how DEI initiatives can transform workplace learning, psychological safety, and power structures. Implementation gaps-including the lack of interventions tailored to organizational needs through processes such as needs assessment and the oversimplification of evaluation metrics-have also been identified as important challenges (Ely & Thomas, 2020; Roberson, 2019).

Against these backgrounds, researchers working in Japan have introduced a new perspective on inclusion from outside the conventional contexts of organizational psychology.

Drawing on discussions in political theory (Galston, 2005; Hobhouse, 1911 Hobhouse, /2010;; Raz, 1988), Costantini et al. (2024) and Ono et al. (2026a) focused on the concepts of tolerance and autonomy as conditions for enabling diverse individuals to achieve well-being. Ono et al.

(2026a) defined tolerance as "respecting, caring, and accommodating diverse individuals within a society to maintain or enhance their well-being." Autonomy, by contrast, was defined as "an individual agency that balances one's dependence portfolio for one's own well-being." Based on these definitions, Ono et al. (2026a) developed self-administered questionnaire scales assessing the extent to which individuals are surrounded by tolerant people and the extent to which they exercise autonomy. They also found that being surrounded by more tolerant people and having greater autonomy were associated with higher well-being, and these associations were broadly consistent among diverse people. These findings suggest that fostering tolerance within society and enhancing individual autonomy may be important for achieving well-being in a society characterized by diversity.

Although Ono et al. (2026a), examined tolerance and autonomy in the context of social life in general, their conceptualization may offer a means of extending the JD-R model to better address the demand for equity. Tolerance and autonomy do not correspond directly and respectively to the concepts of belongingness and uniqueness proposed by Shore et al. (2011).

Nevertheless, an environment in which individuals are respected and can receive care, accommodation, and support when needed, together with the capacity to manage relationships with others according to one's own interests and circumstances, may constitute complementary resources that support both belongingness and uniqueness. Moreover, according to the classification of resources proposed by Mazzetti et al. (2023), workplace tolerance and individual autonomy may be regarded as social and personal resources, respectively.

Thus, the conceptualization proposed by Ono et al. (2026a) is conceptually related to the inclusion framework of Shore et al. (2011) and is also compatible with the classification of resources within the JD-R model. It therefore has the potential to provide a conceptual bridge between the JD-R model and workplace inclusion. Positioning workplace tolerance and individual autonomy within the JD-R model would bring the conditions required to create inclusive workplaces-where people with diverse characteristics and circumstances can engage in their work-within the scope of the model. Accordingly, we proposed the following hypotheses: Hypothesis 1: Workplace tolerance is associated with work engagement. Hypothesis 2: Individual autonomy is associated with work engagement.

In addition, workplace tolerance and individual autonomy are also conceptually related to psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999) and intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000 , 2020), respectively-two constructs that have been extensively studied in organizational psychology.

The psychological safety is regarded as important for supporting diversity within organizations and has also been associated with work engagement (Sasaki et al., 2022).

Although the relationship between workplace tolerance and psychological safety has not been sufficiently investigated, inclusive leadership has been shown to be positively associated with psychological safety (Carmeli et al., 2010; Hirak et al., 2012; Nembhard & Edmondson, 2006). Taken together, these findings suggest that workplace tolerance may be associated with work engagement through psychological safety. We therefore hypothesized a pathway in which greater workplace tolerance enhances employee's psychological safety, which in turn is associated with greater work engagement.

Hypothesis 3: Workplace tolerance is associated with work engagement through workplace psychological safety.

The intrinsic motivation refers to engaging in an activity because it is inherently interesting, enjoyable, or satisfying rather than because of external incentives, such as pay or performance evaluations (Ryan & Deci, 2000 , 2020). The satisfaction of the basic psychological needs for autonomy, relatedness, and competence is considered important for fostering intrinsic motivation. Although autonomy as conceptualized by Ryan and Deci (2020) is not identical to that proposed by Ono et al. (2026a), the two constructs share a core emphasis on making decisions and acting in accordance with one's own interests, values, and orientations. We therefore hypothesized that individual autonomy as conceptualized by Ono et al. (2026a) would be associated with work engagement through intrinsic motivation.

Hypothesis 4: Individual autonomy is associated with work engagement through intrinsic motivation.

Examining these mediations may provide theoretical insight into the mechanisms through which workplace tolerance and individual autonomy are associated with work engagement.

In addition, it may clarify how practical approaches targeting workplace tolerance and individual autonomy can be positioned in relation to existing organizational practices.

The aim of this study was to examine the associations of workplace tolerance and individual autonomy with work engagement, including the potential mediating roles of psychological safety and intrinsic motivation. Specifically, we tested the four hypotheses described above, with data collection and analysis guided by the directed acyclic graph presented in Figure 1. As described below, T0, T1, T2, and T3 denote the time points at which the surveys were conducted, corresponding to the baseline survey and the three followup surveys, respectively.

Method

Settings and participants

Data from four online surveys were used in this study. The baseline survey was conducted in March 2025, followed by three follow-up surveys at six-month intervals.

For the baseline survey, 2500 participants were recruited from a survey company's panel of approximately 13 million registered individuals. Quotas were established for 22 strata defined by age (11 five-year age groups ranging from 15-19 to 65-69 years) and sex (men and women) so that the sample distribution would correspond to that of Japan's employed population. The distribution of the employed population was calculated by the authors using data from the Japanese Labour Force Survey for October 2024. The baseline survey was conducted on March 13, 2025.

In every survey, informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants prior to participation. Specifically, participants were required to carefully read the study description and indicate their consent by checking a designated box; only those who provided consent were allowed to proceed to the questionnaire.

Participants who failed some attention-check items at baseline were excluded from followup because their responses were considered indicative of satisficing-that is, responding with insufficient attention or effort in an attempt to complete the survey quickly. Satisficing has been reported to bias indicators of reliability and validity (Hamby & Taylor, 2016). The attention check questions were specially designed items for which a specific correct option could be identified if the question was read attentively.

Three follow-up surveys were conducted at six-month intervals after the baseline survey: the first in September 2025, the second in March 2026, and the third in September 2026. In this article, the baseline survey is denoted as T0, and the three follow-up surveys are denoted as T1, T2, and T3, respectively.

A change in workplace during the follow-up period would make it difficult to appropriately examine the associations between workplace tolerance or individual autonomy and the mediators and outcomes assessed at subsequent time points. Therefore, at each follow-up survey, participants were asked whether their primary workplace had changed since T0. The primary analyses were restricted to participants who did not change their primary workplace at the third follow-up survey. Details of the item used to assess workplace change are provided in Supplementary material 1.

Participants younger than 18 years were also excluded from the final analyses because working conditions and the social and legal context of employment differ between minors and adults. Although individuals younger than 18 years participated in the baseline and follow-up surveys, they were excluded from the final analytical sample.

Measurements

Workplace tolerance

We adapted the Tolerance of Surroundings Scale (TSS) developed by Ono et al. (2026a) to assess perceived tolerance in the workplace. The original TSS is a valid and reliable selfadministered instrument that assesses the extent to which respondents perceive themselves as being surrounded by tolerant and intolerant people at the micro and meso levels. Although the micro-level component of the original scale encompasses coworkers, the present study required a measure specifically focused on the workplace. Moreover, if the tolerance of surrounding people is considered a potential target of organizational intervention, organizations have limited capacity to influence people outside an employee's workplace. We therefore modified the introductory instructions and item wording by changing the referent from "people around you" to "coworkers." Details of the adapted scale, hereafter referred to as the Workplace Tolerance Scale, are provided in Supplementary material 1.

The Workplace Tolerance Scale consists of 10 items representing two factors: the presence of tolerant coworkers (PTCW) and the absence of intolerant coworkers (AICW). In the present study, we examined its two-factor structural validity and internal consistency using the T0 and T1 data. The results supported the proposed two-factor structure and demonstrated good internal consistency for both subscales. Details of these analyses are provided in Supplementary material 1.

Subscale scores were calculated by summing the corresponding item scores. The PTCW score ranges from 0 to 16, with higher scores indicating that the respondent is surrounded by more tolerant coworkers. The AICW score ranges from 0 to 24, with higher scores indicating that the respondent is surrounded by fewer intolerant coworkers.

Individual autonomy

We used the 12-item version of the Autonomy Scale developed by Ono et al. (2026b). This scale is a valid and reliable self-administered instrument comprising three factors: selfdependence in decision-making and practice, interdependence with others, and selfdependence in emotion and cognition. The scope of individual behavior assessed by this scale is not restricted to the workplace; therefore, the scale was administered in its original form. This decision was based on the premise that, when individual autonomy is considered a potential target of organizational intervention, organizations may support employees' autonomy both within and outside the workplace through employee support and benefit programs.

In the present study, the autonomy score was calculated by summing the 12 item scores.

The total score ranges from 0 to 36, with higher scores indicating a greater capacity to maintain personal agency in decision-making, role performance, and the regulation of emotions and thoughts while also being able to rely on others.

Mediators and outcome variables

The psychological safety was assessed using the Japanese version validated by Sasaki et al. (2022) of the workplace psychological safety measure developed by O'Donovan et al. (2020).

We summed the items referring to coworkers or other team members and the team as a whole to calculate the psychological safety score (PS score). The intrinsic motivation was assessed using the Japanese version (Kotera et al., 2022 (Kotera et al., , 2024) ) of the Work Extrinsic and Intrinsic Motivation Scale developed by Tremblay et al. (2009). The mean of items assessing intrinsic motivation was used as the intrinsic motivation score (IM score).

The work engagement was assessed using the nine-item Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES Schaufeli et al., 2002; Shimazu et al., 2008). In addition, we assessed workaholism as a secondary outcome using the Dutch Workaholism Scale (Schaufeli et al., 2009) because previous research has emphasized the importance of distinguishing work engagement from workaholism (Shimazu et al., 2009 (Shimazu et al., , 2012 (Shimazu et al., , 2015)). The item scores for each scale were summed to calculate the UWES score and workaholism score, respectively.

Detailed descriptions of these measures and their scoring procedures are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Control variables

We measured individual-and workplace-level characteristics as control variables. Detailed descriptions of how these variables were measured are provided in Supplementary material 1.

Individual characteristics included age, gender identity, sexual orientation, marital status, living alone, number of children being raised, caregiving responsibilities, disability status, physical health, years of education, and household income.

Workplace characteristics included the number of employees, staffing workload balance, trust in senior management, fairness of performance appraisal and availability of career development training. The last three variables were measured by items in Workplace Strengths Checklist (Shimazu, 2019).

Because workplace tolerance and individual autonomy are relatively new constructs, prior evidence regarding their determinants remains limited. Guided by the modified disjunctive cause criterion, we selected pre-exposure control variables that, based on subject-matter knowledge, were considered plausible causes of the exposure, the outcome, or both (VanderWeele, 2019; Inoue et al., 2025).

Statistical Analyses

We conducted causal mediation analyses as the primary analyses to estimate the average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), total effect, and proportion mediated. In each analysis, the exposure was measured at T1, the mediator at T2, and work engagement at T3. The models were adjusted for the mediator and work engagement measured at T0, as well as the prespecified individual-and workplace-level control variables measured at T0.

As supplementary analyses, we used inverse probability-of-censoring-weighted linear regression to examine the associations of the T1 exposures with work engagement and workaholism at T3 while accounting for potential selection bias due to loss to follow-up.

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption and estimates from 30 imputed datasets were pooled using Rubin's rules. All analyses used the original score metrics without standardization or normalization.

Accordingly, the magnitudes of the estimated associations were interpreted with reference to the observed standard deviations of the corresponding exposure and outcome scores.

Details of statistical analyses are described in Supplementary Material 1.

Discussion

Responses to the four hypotheses

Turning first to workplace tolerance, the findings partially supported Hypotheses 1 and 3.

The total effect of the PTCW score was 0.296, and the estimated proportion mediated was 29.7%. The 95%CIs for both the ACME and ADE excluded zero. In contrast, although the estimated proportion mediated for the AICW score was 29.6 %, its total effect was 0.066, with a 95%CI that included zero. Thus, PTCW was associated with work engagement through psychological safety, whereas AICW was not clearly associated with work engagement. The supplementary regression analyses yielded consistent findings: the PTCW score was associated with the work engagement, whereas the AICW score was not. Accordingly, Hypotheses 1 and 3 were partially supported in that PTCW was associated with work engagement through workplace psychological safety, whereas AICW was not associated with work engagement.

For individual autonomy, the total effect was 0.444, and the estimated proportion mediated was 14.5 %. The 95%CIs for both the ACME and ADE excluded zero. The supplementary regression analysis also showed an association of a similar magnitude between individual autonomy and work engagement. These findings indicate that individual autonomy was associated with work engagement through intrinsic motivation. However, the proportion of the association mediated by intrinsic motivation was modest. Thus, Hypotheses 2 and 4 were supported in that individual autonomy was associated with work engagement partly through intrinsic motivation, although the contribution of this mediating pathway was relatively limited.

Contributions to the JD-R model and DEI practice

The principal theoretical contribution of this study lies in positioning workplace tolerance and individual autonomy as a social resource and a personal resource, respectively, within the JD-R model and examining their longitudinal associations with work engagement. The findings showed that the PTCW and autonomy scores were associated with work engagement through psychological safety and intrinsic motivation, respectively-existing constructs that are related to the two proposed resources. However, these mediating pathways accounted for only 29.7% and 14.5% of the respective total associations. These findings suggest that workplace tolerance and individual autonomy, although related to proximal established constructs such as psychological safety and intrinsic motivation, may capture resources that are not fully reducible to those constructs. Incorporating tolerance and autonomy as social and personal resources may enable the JD-R model to describe more explicitly the conditions under which employees with diverse characteristics and circumstances can engage in their work on an equitable basis.

Extending the JD-R model in this way may also provide a conceptual basis for assessing inclusion-related conditions at both the social and individual levels. Specifically, the PTCW and individual autonomy scores may serve as complementary dimensions capturing a social resource within the work environment and a personal resource through which employees manage their relationships with others. Used together, these dimensions may support organizational needs assessment and the tailoring of interventions to the specific needs of each organization. This approach may help address implementation gaps in DEI practice, including the limited adaptation of interventions to organizational needs and the reliance on overly simplified evaluation metrics (Ely & Thomas, 2020; Roberson, 2019).

However, the present study did not examine whether the distributions of workplace tolerance and individual autonomy or their associations with work engagement differed across employee subgroups. Such heterogeneity should not be investigated solely by dividing participants into broad categories such as older employees, people with disabilities, parents, family caregivers, or LGBTQ+ individuals. Rather, future studies should define theoretically meaningful subgroups based on relevant configurations of individual characteristics, job demands, and resources and ensure adequate sample sizes within each group. Such investigations would help clarify more concretely how incorporating workplace tolerance and individual autonomy into the JD-R model can contribute to understanding and assessing equitable and inclusive work environments.

Practical implications and needs of further studies

This study was a longitudinal observational study in which the exposures were modeled as continuous variables. Therefore, before these findings can be translated into workplace practice, interventions targeting workplace tolerance and individual autonomy must be developed and evaluated in experimental studies. Nevertheless, the present findings offer implications for the design and practical application of such future studies.

First, the finding that the PTCW score was associated with work engagement whereas the AICW score was not suggests that, specifically for promoting work engagement, increasing tolerant behaviors among employees may be more important than merely reducing intolerant behaviors. More specifically, promoting tolerant behaviors-such as respecting diverse perspectives, helping one another, and adapting one's behavior in response to others' requests-may be particularly important, in addition to discouraging intolerant behaviors such as discriminatory conduct or the rejection of diversity. However, AICW might have relevance within particular subgroups, including groups that have historically experienced discrimination. This possibility further underscores the value of conducting stratified analyses in theoretically meaningful groups as discussed above.

Second, the concept of individual autonomy adopted in this study may offer a perspective on work engagement that differs from autonomy as conceptualized in self-determination theory. The concept proposed by Ono et al. (2026a) refers to a state in which individuals can combine a sense of personal ownership over their decisions and actions with the ability to rely on others. The present findings therefore suggest a potentially new approach: enabling employees to take ownership of their decisions and actions while relying on others when needed. Although this approach must be evaluated in studies designed to estimate its causal effects more rigorously, an approach based on a concept of autonomy that incorporates not only self-determination but also reliance on others may support employees' needs for both belongingness and uniqueness (Shore et al., 2011), thereby contributing to the creation of more inclusive workplaces.

Third, the PTCW and autonomy scores were associated with the work engagement but not with the workaholism score. Additionally, the corresponding point estimates and the upper bounds of their 95%CI were close to zero. Taken together, these findings suggest that approaches targeting workplace tolerance and individual autonomy may have the potential to enhance work engagement without producing an unintended increase in workaholism.

Last, the magnitude of the observed associations has implications for how future experimental studies should evaluate interventions. Using the total effects estimated in the mediation analyses, the ! ! coefficients estimated in the supplementary regression analyses, and the standard deviations of the exposures and outcome, we calculated the standardized association corresponding to a one-standard-deviation increase in each exposure. The resulting values were approximately 0.11 for PTCW and 0.20 for individual autonomy. These associations were modest in magnitude.

Nevertheless, even an intervention with a small individual-level effect may produce a substantial aggregate benefit if it reaches a large number of employees. Furthermore, the JD-R model proposes a positive gain cycle in which greater engagement promotes proactive employee behaviors, which in turn generate additional resources (Bakker et al., 2023).

Similarly, the qualitative findings of Ono et al. (2026a) led to the hypothesis of a positive cycle in which tolerance among surrounding people enhances individual autonomy and individuals with greater autonomy behave more tolerantly toward others. These perspectives suggest that workplace tolerance and individual autonomy may generate spillover effects whereby employees who benefit from these resources subsequently influence others.

Evaluations of interventions targeting workplace tolerance and individual autonomy should

therefore consider not only the extent to which each employee's work engagement improves but also the overall impact of the intervention in relation to its reach and potential spillover effects.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a panel maintained by an online survey company. Although recruitment quotas were used to align the age and sex distribution of the sample with that of Japan's working population, the possibility of sampling bias cannot be excluded. Therefore, when generalizing the findings, differences between the study participants and the target population to which the findings are applied should be carefully considered.

Second, although we used statistical procedures to adjust for potential confounders and selection bias, these procedures do not necessarily establish that the estimated associations represent causal effects. We also assumed treatment-version irrelevance-that different ways of attaining the same exposure score would not result in different outcomes. In addition, the exposures were modeled as continuous variables, and linear relationships between the exposures and outcomes were assumed. Given their dependence on these assumptions, the findings should be interpreted as covariate-adjusted longitudinal associations rather than causal effects.

Third, the estimated associations may remain affected by common method bias. To mitigate this risk, we used scales with established content validity, avoided ambiguous wording, administered the survey anonymously, and allowed respondents to leave questions unanswered. In addition, the control variables, exposures, mediators, and outcomes were measured at different time points. These procedures may have reduced social desirability bias, response pressure, and shared covariance arising from simultaneous measurement, thereby limiting common method bias to some extent (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Nevertheless, the exclusive use of self-report questionnaires remains a limitation.

Conclusions

Using data from four waves of online surveys, this study examined the associations of workplace tolerance and individual autonomy with work engagement, including the potential mediating roles of psychological safety and intrinsic motivation. PTCW was associated with work engagement through workplace psychological safety, whereas AICW was not associated with work engagement. Individual autonomy was also associated with work engagement through intrinsic motivation, although the proportion mediated by intrinsic motivation was modest.

The findings suggest that positioning workplace tolerance and individual autonomy as social and personal resources, respectively, may extend the JD-R model by enabling it to describe the conditions under which employees with diverse characteristics and circumstances can engage in their work on an equitable basis. Future research should define theoretically meaningful subgroups and examine the distributions of workplace tolerance and individual autonomy and their associations with work engagement within these groups. Interventions targeting workplace tolerance and individual autonomy should also be developed and evaluated in future experimental studies.

4. Structural validity and internal consistency for workplace tolerance scale.

The workplace tolerance scale was adapted from the original Tolerance of Surroundings Scale to assess perceived tolerance among people in the workplace. The only modification concerned the reference group that respondents were asked to consider: "people around you" was replaced with "your coworkers." We therefore expected the adapted scale to have psychometric properties similar to those of the original scale Nevertheless, before conducting the analyses of interest (i.e., the mediation and regression analyses), we conducted a preliminary evaluation of the structural validity and internal consistency of the workplace tolerance scale.

First, we conducted confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) using complete-case data separately at T0 and T1. These analyses revealed residual correlations between items Tw1 and Tw2 and between items Tw9 and Tw10. 5. Measurements of Mediators and outcome variables

>Measurements of Mediators and outcome variables

Psychological safety

We used the Workplace Psychological Safety Scale developed by O'Donovan et al. (2020). This self-administered instrument assesses psychological safety in the respondent's workplace and consists of nine items concerning the team leader, seven items concerning coworkers or other team members, and three items concerning the team as a whole. The validity and reliability of the Japanese version were demonstrated by Sasaki et al. (2022).

Because this study focused on the association between workplace tolerance and work engagement, we considered psychological safety in relation to coworkers and the team as a whole to be the components most relevant to the hypothesized mediation mechanism. We also considered that some respondents might work in settings without an identifiable team leader or might themselves serve as team leaders. Therefore, the present survey used the seven items concerning coworkers or other team members and the three items concerning the team as a whole. The scores for these 10 items were summed to calculate the psychological safety score (PS score) used in the analyses.

Intrinsic Motivation

We used the Short Work Extrinsic and Intrinsic Motivation Scale. The scale assesses respondents' motivation toward work. Although the original version developed by Tremblay et al. (2009) consists of 18 items, a Japanese version and a shortened 12-item version were subsequently developed (Kotera et al., 2022 (Kotera et al., , 2024)). The Japanese version has a three-factor structure and has demonstrated good internal consistency.

Although all 12 items were administered in the present survey, only the eight items assessing intrinsic motivation were used in the analyses. The mean for these eight items was used as the intrinsic motivation score (IM score).

Work engagement

We used the nine-item version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES; Schaufeli et al., 2002). The UWES assesses three dimensions of work engagement: vigor, dedication, and absorption. The original 17-item version has been used in 32 countries and is among the most widely used instruments in work-engagement research. In the present study, we used the nine-item version, which contains three items for each dimension.

A validated Japanese version of the nine-item UWES is available and has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity (Shimazu et al., 2008). The scores for all nine items were summed to calculate the UWES score used in the analyses.

Workaholic

Although measuring work engagement was sufficient to address the primary objectives of this study, previous research has emphasized the importance of distinguishing work engagement from workaholism, which is characterized by compulsive and excessive working (Shimazu et al., 2009 (Shimazu et al., , 2012 (Shimazu et al., , 2015)). An approach toward inclusive workplace should contribute to employees working with energy and fulfillment without promoting compulsive or excessive working. It was therefore important to examine whether workplace tolerance and individual autonomy were associated with work engagement but not with workaholism.

Accordingly, workaholism was included as a supplementary outcome.

Workaholism was assessed using the Dutch Workaholism Scale (DUWAS; Schaufeli et al., 2009). The DUWAS consists of 10 items representing two factors: working excessively and working compulsively. The total score ranges from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating a stronger tendency to work excessively and compulsively. A Japanese version of the DUWAS was developed by Schaufeli et al. (2009). In the present study, the scores for all 10 items were summed to calculate the workaholism score used in the analyses.
