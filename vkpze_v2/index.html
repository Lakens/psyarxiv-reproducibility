What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. 1. Introduction

Student agency has gained increasing attention in higher education (HE) research, driven by its recognized importance for learning, well-being, and students' capacity to engage meaningfully with complex societal challenges (Inouye et al., 2023; Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Marín et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2023). Beyond academic performance, agency has been linked to students' personal growth, sense of connectedness, and preparedness for transitions from education to work life (Ross et al., 2024; Schoon & Heckhausen, 2019).

Accordingly, educational policy documents emphasize student agency as a central goal of education (OECD, n.d.). Yet agency is not only an educational outcome. It is enacted within everyday learning situations, making it important to understand how course-level pedagogical conditions relate to students' agency.

Nevertheless, understanding of agency remains conceptually and empirically fragmented: Prior reviews have highlighted persistent challenges, including undertheorization, reliance on narrow or unidimensional representations of agency, and limited integration between theoretical models and empirical measurement (Marín et al., 2020; Stenalt & Lassesen, 2021). Empirical research has often focused on specific groups of students or isolated pedagogical interventions, leaving open questions about how agency is experienced across different pedagogical settings and how it relates systematically to teaching practices. There is limited understanding of how student agency is constituted in course-level micro-contexts that organize students' work and participation and how it can be meaningfully supported through pedagogy.

This study addresses these gaps by examining university students' perceptions of their agency within a multidimensional framework comprising three analytically distinct components: resources for agency, agentic will, and agentic action. The study contributes to understanding how these perceptions vary across students and course contexts, how the three components of agency relate to one another, and how they are associated with the teaching practices students encounter in courses within their fields of study. A studentinformed perspective is particularly important because students are uniquely positioned to evaluate both their perceived opportunities for agency and their own volitional orientations and actions in educational contexts.

To address these aims, we pose the following research questions (RQ): RQ1: How do university students perceive their agency across the three components of agency: resources for agency, agentic will, and agentic action (i.e., are there differences in the levels of students' perceptions across the subdimensions)?

RQ2:

To what extent is variation in these perceptions attributable to student-and courselevel differences?

RQ3: How are the three components of agency and their subdimensions interrelated?

RQ4.

How are students' perceptions of agency associated with their ratings of teaching practices with respect to course design transparency, different types of learning activities, and student-teacher roles in their courses?

Our conceptualization draws on core qualities of agency highlighted in the literaturesnamely, views of agency as subjectively experienced, socially embedded, and dynamically evolving. First, we understand agency as extending beyond observable action or participation and as rooted in students' experiences of ownership, influence, and possibilities for learning (e.g., Case, 2015; Cook-Sather, 2020; Su, 2011). Second, we view agency as being formed in interaction with social, relational, and structural conditions rather than evolving solely as an individual attribute (e.g., Archer, 2003; Matusov et al., 2016). Third, we see that agency unfolds in the dynamic interplay between learners and their environments, with the potential for both adaptation and transformation (Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Mezirow, 2000).

Based on these qualities, we conceptualize student agency as a dynamically evolving and contextually shaped capacity for purposeful learning and transformation, which is enabled by students' self-perceived resources for agency, manifested in their agentic will and realized through their agentic action. Operationally, we approach this capacity as comprising three interrelated components: (1) students' self-perceived resources for agency (e.g., experiences of interactional support, personal capabilities, and environmental affordances for influence), (2) their self-reported agentic will (e.g., willingness to set demanding goals, invest effort in interactive learning, and seize opportunities to engage in joint problem-solving and to influence learning practices), and (3) their self-reported agentic action through which they intentionally advance their learning, contribute to knowledge co-construction with other learners, and influence learning processes and conditions in their courses. While conceptually distinct, these components and their respective subdimensions are assumed to be dynamically interconnected in the enactment of student agency. Together, they form the Agency of University Students -Resources, Will, and Action (AUS-RWA) model (Fig. 1), developed as part of our ongoing research on student agency (Jääskelä, et al., 2017 (Jääskelä, et al., , 2020 (Jääskelä, et al., , 2023)). By examining the subdimensions of agency and their associations with teaching practices while taking into account the clustering of students within courses, the study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how agency is shaped and supported in HE.

2. Conceptualization of student agency in higher education

>Conceptualization of student agency in higher education

2.1. Core qualities the underlying understanding of student agency

Student agency is widely acknowledged to be grounded in learners' subjective experiences and sense of becoming subjects of their own learning (Case, 2015; Cook-Sather, 2020; Lee, 2024; Su, 2011). From a person-centered perspective, agency can be understood as students' experienced capacity to shape their learning through constructing personal goals and meanings and positioning themselves within learning environments and social relationships (Su, 2011; Vaughn, 2020 Vaughn, , 2021)). This capacity is shaped not only by cognitive processes but also by affective experiences and motivational aspects, including self-efficacy, intentionality, and interest (Bandura, 2018; Mercer, 2012). Students' sense of ownership and efficacy are considered central to becoming learners who can influence both their own learning and the learning of others (Case, 2015; Cook-Sather, 2020; Lee, 2024).

On the other hand, sociocultural and sociological perspectives emphasize that agency is shaped in relation to interaction with social and structural conditions. This means that rather than being an inherent individual trait, agency emerges through engagement with relational, institutional, and epistemic structures that provide varying opportunities for participation and influence (Archer, 2003; Berger & Luckmann, 1966 /1994; Giddens, 1984; Matusov et al., 2016). In HE, these structures are concretely instantiated in degree programs and course-level pedagogical arrangements, such as curricula, teaching practices, and organized forms of participation (Damşa et al., 2010; Eteläpelto & Lahti, 2008). These structures may either enable or constrain students' possibilities to exercise agency.

A further defining quality of student agency is its dynamic nature, which includes the potential for transformation. Agency develops through learners' ongoing participation, interaction, and reflection in educational settings. These processes may lead leaners to reconsider their assumptions, expand their ways of knowing, and transform how they engage in knowledge construction (Klemenčič, 2017; Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Mezirow, 1997 Mezirow, , 2000)). Consequently, agency entails actively navigating opportunities and constraints and shaping one's engagement in relation to them. Bandura's (1999 Bandura's ( , 2001 Bandura's ( , 2008) ) social cognitive theory further highlights the roles of intentionality, forethought, self-reactiveness, and self-reflectiveness in enabling learners to participate purposefully in learning activities and act on opportunities in their learning environment.

We argue that the core qualities of subjectivity, social and structural embeddedness, and dynamic development of agency connect individuals' experiences with the structural conditions of learning environments. This implies that agency is likely to vary across educational contexts, highlighting the importance of examining both individual-and course-level variation.

2.2. A multidimensional model of student agency

Our multidimensional conceptualization of student agency distinguishes between three interrelated components: students' perceived resources for agency, their agentic will, and the action through which agency is enacted. The AUS-RWA model (Fig. 1) brings these components together within a unified framework. As shown in Fig 1, the model comprises several subdimensions, with nine pertaining to resources for agency, three to agentic will, and three to agentic action. Informed by Klemenčič's (2015 Klemenčič's ( , 2017 Klemenčič's ( , 2023) ) distinction between the power to act and the will to act, we conceptualize the resources component of our model as capturing personal capabilities and contextual affordances that contribute to students' power to act. These resources may enable or constrain their exercise of agency within learning environments. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the resources component comprises four broader categories of resources: personal, relational, participatory, and transformative resources.

Personal resources refer to students' perceptions of their own capabilities for learning and agency, whereas relational, participatory, and transformative resources represent contextual resources embedded in social relationships, educational practices, and learning environments. This distinction is consistent with sociological and educational perspectives that view agency as emerging through the interplay of personal capacities and social and epistemic environments (Archer, 2003; Giddens, 1984; Matusov et al., 2016).

The conceptualization of personal resources draws on approaches and constructs of social cognitive theory, such as self-efficacy and competence beliefs, which underpin students' perceived capacity to pursue meaningful learning goals (Bandura, 2008 (Bandura, , 2018;; Schunk & Zimmerman, 2012). Contextual resources, by contrast, concern the opportunities and supports available within learning environments for participation, influence, collaborative knowledge construction, and transformative learning (Damşa et al., 2010; Eteläpelto & Lahti, 2008; Lee, 2024; Lipponen & Kumpulainen, 2011; Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Mezirow, 1997 Mezirow, , 2000)).

Relational resources for agency refer to students' perceptions of teacher and peer support, trust in others, and balanced power relations among peers that provide social conditions conducive to agency (Fig. 1). Drawing on sociocultural perspectives, these resources are grounded in social relationships and interactions that shape participation and learning (Eteläpelto & Lahti, 2008; Lipponen & Kumpulainen, 2011). Relational resources characterized by accessibility, responsiveness, and learning support (Juutilainen, et al., 2018) provide a secure basis for expressing uncertainty and for critically examining assumptions and alternative perspectives central to transformative learning (Mezirow, 1997 (Mezirow, , 2000;; Lipponen & Kumpulainen, 2011).

Participatory resources for agency refer to opportunities for interactive learning, and for students' perspectives and interpretations to be heard and taken seriously (Damşa et al., 2010; Lee, 2024). These resources shape students' opportunities to contribute to shared activities, influence learning processes, and participate as legitimate contributors to joint meaning-making and knowledge construction. Participatory resources can support students' capacity for meaningful influence, deepen collaborative sense-making, and provide opportunities to reconsider existing understandings (Lipponen & Kumpulainen, 2011; Mezirow, 1997 Mezirow, , 2000)).

Transformative resources for agency refer to opportunities to participate in epistemic activities that challenge and expand students' existing ways of understanding and acting (Fig. 1). These resources involve affordances for critical reflection, collaborative development of ideas, engagement with complex questions, and exploration of new ways of understanding and acting, as well as students' perceptions that such opportunities are meaningful and worthwhile (Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Wigfield et al., 2012). By supporting the reconstruction of existing understandings, these resources create conditions for transformative learning and the development of agency within and beyond the learning context (Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Mezirow, 1997 Mezirow, , 2000)).

In the AUS-RWA model, agentic will represents the relationally and motivationally mediated volitional impulse-that is, an intentional willingness and orientation toward engagement in agentic action within specific learning situations. Drawing on Klemenčič's (2015 Klemenčič's ( , 2017 Klemenčič's ( , 2023) notion of will to act, we conceptualize agentic will as students' contextually shaped intentional orientation toward learning activities that serve epistemic aims and meaningful learning. Whereas Klemenčič conceptualized the will to act broadly as a general agentic orientation toward action, our conceptualization of agentic will focuses more specifically on students' readiness and commitment to mobilize available capabilities, opportunities, and situational resources toward purposeful learning-related action. This conceptualization builds on a relational understanding of agency, which views agency as emerging from the dynamic interplay between individuals and their social, cultural, and material environments (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Emirbayer & Mische, 1998).

Accordingly, in the AUS-RWA model, we see that students' agentic will develops in relation to their perceptions of personal capabilities, relational resources, and opportunities for agency. The perceived resources provide a basis for agentic will but do not fully determine it. Thus, motivationally, agentic will concerns the value students attribute to engaging agentically in learning and striving to take up and tackle challenges. Volitionally, will concerns students' willingness to invest effort and sustain commitment despite challenges or competing demands. In alignment with Bandura's (1999 Bandura's ( , 2001) ) notions of intentionality and forethought, agentic will reflects students' readiness to formulate agentic goals and anticipate the value of effort in committing themselves purposefully to action they deem meaningful and worthwhile.

Striving for understanding (Fig. 1) refers to a form of agentic will characterized by an intentional willingness and orientation toward learning in which developing understanding is valued as an important educational goal. This dimension reflects the intentional and purposive nature of human agency (Bandura, 2001) and aligns with Klemenčič's (2017) notion of agentic orientation, whereby students consciously direct themselves toward valued educational goals. However, in our model, this orientation is directed specifically toward epistemic aims and meaningful learning. Within the will component, the subdimension of interactive learning (Fig. 1) captures students' willingness to engage with others in ways that support purposeful learning.

Research within the sociocultural tradition emphasizes that learning and knowledge construction are fundamentally mediated by social interaction (Vygotsky, 1978; Rogoff, 1990), through dialogue, feedback, and the exchange of perspectives. Consistent with Klemenčič's (2017) characterization of agency as relational, this dimension captures students' intentional willingness and orientation to engage in dialogue, exchange perspectives, and build understanding together with others.

Finally, the subdimension of will to engage in joint problem-solving reflects students' willingness to participate in collective efforts aimed at exploring complex questions and new ways of thinking (Damşa, 2014; Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Mezirow, 1997 Mezirow, , 2000;; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). This subdimension emphasizes willingness to collaboratively address challenging issues (Damşa, 2014; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). It also reflects an openness to enrich and potentially transform one's own understanding through collaboration and engagement with alternative perspectives (Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Mezirow, 1997 Mezirow, , 2000)).

In the AUS-RWA model, agentic action refers to intentional and goal-directed activities through which students seek to influence and shape their learning and the conditions under which it occurs (see Fig. 1). In line with social cognitive conceptions of agency, this activity involves self-regulatory and self-reflective efforts aimed at advancing learning goals (Bandura, 2006). Agentic actions are not limited to individual initiatives but may also involve collaborative efforts in which students jointly negotiate meanings and contribute to shared knowledge advancement (Damşa et al., 2010).

We maintain that agency becomes visible in students' intentional action and active participation in learning processes. Reeve and Shin (2020) argued that students enact agency when they contribute constructively to the flow of instruction by asking questions, expressing ideas, communicating preferences, or encouraging the participation of others.

Agency may also be manifested in action that supports peer engagement and learning, such as building on others' ideas, creating space for diverse contributions, and sustaining productive collaboration (Damşa et al., 2010).

Research suggests that collaborative practices, such as dialogic interaction, collective knowledge construction, and joint problem-solving, may provide opportunities for students to exercise transformative forms of agency (Lund & Vestøl, 2020). Damşa et al. (2010) described such participation as shared epistemic agency, whereby students contribute to the advancement of collective knowledge through collaborative idea development, negotiation of meanings, and problem-solving. Within the AUS-RWA model, the subdimension of active and impactful participation captures students' efforts to influence shared learning processes by contributing ideas and perspectives, participating in joint problem-solving, taking initiatives, and voicing views on issues that shape learning and study practices. In this view, collaboration is not merely a context for agency but a primary site where students exercise agency by influencing shared learning processes and the conditions under which learning progresses.

2.3. Teaching practices in the formation of student agency

Given that agency is shaped by the interplay between individuals and social and structural conditions (Archer, 2003), and that within HE, these conditions are typically manifested in course-level pedagogical arrangements and organized forms of participation (Damşa et al., 2010; Eteläpelto & Lahti, 2008), teaching practices constitute an important focus of the present analysis. Rather than viewing teaching practices as mere resources for agency, we conceptualize them as pedagogical arrangements that structure the opportunities and conditions through which student agency emerges and can be enacted.

Although previous research has not always examined teaching practices explicitly through the lens of agency, several pedagogical features appear relevant for supporting students' participation, influence, and active involvement in learning (e.g., Goodyear & Dudley, 2015; Moses et al., 2020; Nieminen et al., 2022a; Sjølie et al., 2022). The present study focuses on three such features: transparency of course design, active learning activities, and student-teacher roles.

Aspects of transparent course design, such as clear articulation of learning objectives, alignment between goals and assessment, and explicit communication of expectations, have been linked to increased student engagement and understanding (Anderson et al., 2013; Levinsson et al., 2024; Winkelmes et al., 2016). Although agency has not been typically examined as an outcome in this line of research, transparent course design may create conditions that enable students to take a more active role in their learning by making purposeful choices about their goals and study strategies and strengthening their sense of control over learning.

Learning activities that involve authentic problem-solving, collaboration, and reflection may also support student agency. Collaborative problem-solving, inquiry-based tasks, and project-based learning provide opportunities for students to take responsibility and engage in meaningful knowledge construction (Jiang et al., 2022; Nieminen et al., 2022a; Sjølie et al., 2022). Similarly, practices such as self-assessment and co-creation can support students' evaluative judgment, self-regulation, and sense of ownership (Boud & Molloy, 2013; Bovill et al., 2016; Nieminen & Tuohilampi, 2020).

Student-teacher roles can also shape how opportunities for agency are created and distributed within learning environments. A shift toward facilitative and partnershiporiented teacher roles has been linked to greater opportunities for student participation, expressing one's own voice, and having an impact in learning processes, thereby creating conditions that support the enactment of agency (Goodyear & Dudley, 2015; Moses et al., 2020; Robertson, 2017). Importantly, structured teacher guidance does not preclude student agency; rather, when enacted through dialogic and collaborative practices, it can support students' engagement as active contributors (Reeve & Shin, 2020).

Although these strands of research highlight several pedagogical features that may support student agency, empirical work examining their relationships with multidimensional conceptualizations of agency across course contexts remains limited.

This study addresses this gap by analyzing how students' perceptions of teaching practices relate to students' perceived resources for agency, agentic will, and agentic action.

3. Methods

>Methods

3.1. Participants and procedure

This study involved 1,140 students enrolled in 47 undergraduate courses (primarily bachelor's degree programs) at a Finnish research university. Of these, 337 (29.6%) were male, 801 (70.3%) were female, and two did not disclose their gender. The participants' ages ranged from 18 to 66 years (M = 24.59, SD = 6.89). The courses represented the academic fields in natural sciences, information technology, business and economics, educational sciences, humanities, and sport and health sciences. On average, 24.3 students participated in each course (range = 10-72, SD = 15.8).

The students were informed of the study aims and the voluntary nature of participation, and written consent was obtained from all participants. Data were collected via an online questionnaire administered at the latter part of each course between spring 2023 and spring 2024. The questionnaire included items measuring students' perceptions of their agency, as well as items asking about their perceptions of teaching practices in the course they were attending at the time of completion. Demographic information was retrieved from the university's student register.

3.2. Measures

>Measures

3.2.1. Student Agency

Student agency was measured using the AUS-RWA Scale (Appendix A), an extended version of the original AUS Scale (Jääskelä, et al., 2017 (Jääskelä, et al., , 2020)). The construction of the scale involved conceptualizing, operationalizing, and validating resource factors (Authors, 2023). In our previous research we have documented the construct validity and sound psychometric properties of the AUS Scale (e.g., measurement invariance) (Jääskelä, et al., 2017 (Jääskelä, et al., , 2020 (Jääskelä, et al., , 2023)). Before the present data collection, we made modifications to the original AUS questionnaire for instance by unifying the number of items on the scales. In addition, we incorporated new items into the agency resources subscale to capture opportunities for agentic transformation in the courses (Lee, 2024; Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Su, 2011). Note that in the Methods and Results sections, the theoretical components presented in Figure 1 are referred to as AUS-RWA subscales, and the subdimensions within these components are referred to as factors.

The AUS-RWA student questionnaire consists of 60 items across three subscales: AUS-

Resources (AUS-R, 37 items); AUS-Will (AUS-W, 11 items); and AUS-Action (AUS-A, 12 items). Students responded to each item on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (fully disagree) to 5 (fully agree).

The AUS-Resources subscale comprises the following nine factors, which form four conceptual categories that derive from prior literature (e.g., Bandura, 2008; Mercer, 2012; Klemenčič, 2015 Klemenčič, , 2017;; Lund & Vestøl, 2020; Vaughn, 2020) and our earlier modelling of the AUS scale (Jääskelä, et al., 2017 (Jääskelä, et al., , 2020 (Jääskelä, et al., , 2023)) The AUS-Will subscale comprises three factors: (1) Striving for understanding (e.g., willingness to invest effort in learning); (2) Will to engage in interactive learning (e.g., willingness to participate in building a common understanding); and (3) Will to engage in joint problem-solving (e.g., willingness to participate in generating new or creative solutions).

The AUS-Action subscale comprises three factors: (1) Intentional goal-directed learning (e.g., working with determination to achieve the learning goals); (2) Collaborating with and supporting others (e.g., providing support to other students; and (3) Active impactful participation (e.g., expressing views or taking positions on issues).

3.2.2. Teaching practices

Teaching practices were measured using student ratings of three dimensions: (1) learning activities, (2) course design transparency, and (3) student-teacher roles in their course.

Learning activities (LA) were assessed by asking students to rate the extent to which different types of learning activities were included in their course. They responded to 15 items on a five-point Likert scale, with1 = not at all,2 = little or rarely,3 = moderately,4 = a lot or often, and5 = very much or continuously. The items were developed based on previous studies that found positive links between single aspects of student agency (e.g., self-efficacy beliefs, agentic goal-oriented action, influential participation) and active learning practices (e.g., content knowledge-focused, collaborative, (self)regulative, feedback activities) (e.g., Jiang et al., 2022; Lipponen & Kumpulainen, 2011; Moses et al., 2020; Nieminen & Tuohilampi, 2020; Sjølie et al., 2022). In the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) three factors were identified (Appendix B): (1) Tasks demanding higherorder thinking (four items; e.g., problem-solving tasks, critical evaluation of knowledge); (2) Interactive student collaboration (four items; e.g., small-group work around learning tasks, sharing reasoning or reflections, feedback on other's work); and (3) Monitoring and support for personal learning (seven items; e.g., self-assessment of learning, teacher identification of student challenges, and teacher guidance of individual students).

Course design transparency (CDT) was examined by asking the students to rate the extent to which five course design elements were made explicit and negotiated with them in their course: (1) learning objectives, (2) working methods, (3) timetable, (4) evaluation criteria, and (5) assessment methods. Transparency in these elements has been shown to promote student agency by helping students orient to their studies, make informed decisions, and engage in their work (e.g., Anderson et al., 2013; King et al., 2024). The students responded to the five items using a four-point scale:1 = not explicitly dealt with at all,2 = only mentioned in the materials,3 = explicitly introduced by the teacher, and4 = mutually agreed upon by the teacher and students. Factor analysis indicated a single factor (Appendix B).

Student-teacher roles (STR) were measured using one multiple-choice item with four response options in which the students selected the description that best reflected the distribution of responsibilities between the teacher and themselves in their course. The abbreviated multiple-choice options were as follows: (1) The teacher manages and assesses the achievement of learning outcomes. Students' role is to complete the given tasks. ( 2)The teacher has the role of the supervisor of learning by determining course implementation and assessment but adapting to students different learning needs and making modifications. Students' role is to commit to course work and assessment. (3) The teacher and students engage in jointly defined objectives, implementation, and/or assessment. Students act as experts and engage with peers. (4) The course is built on students' self-defined objectives, and their knowledge construction. Students are responsible for choices of methods, and self-assessment. The teacher provides support.

These four role descriptions were derived from a continuum of learning modes in prior literature, ranging from teacher-controlled to teacher-student interactive, and finally, to teacher-supported and student-self-controlled (Goodyear & Dudley, 2015). The latter modes are associated with active learning and enhanced student agency (Goodyear & Dudley, 2015; King, 1993; Morrison, 2014; Moses et al., 2020; Robertson, 2017). For analysis, dummy variables were created for response options (2), (3), and (4), with option (1) serving as the reference.

4. Data Analyses

Descriptive statistics-including the mean (M), standard deviation (SD), confidence interval (CI), skewness, kurtosis, and Cronbach's alpha (α)-were computed using SPSS version 28. Effect sizes for mean differences were calculated with Cohen's d, interpreted as 0.20 (small), 0.50 (medium), and 0.80 (large). These were used to compare mean levels across agency subdimensions (RQ1), based on latent factors.

As a preliminary step, scale structures (including AUS-RWA) and their psychometric properties were analyzed; details are provided in the Appendix B. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using Mplus version 8.11 with the weighted least square mean and variance adjusted estimator (WLSMV; Muthén & Muthén, 1998 -2017). SEM was used to model latent constructs (student agency [AUS-RWA] and teaching practices [LA, CDT, STR]) and to examine interrelations among agency dimensions across levels (RQ3) and their associations with teaching practices (RQ4) within a multilevel framework.

WLSMV was selected because the items were ordinal and, in some cases, responses were skewed, with answers limited to three of the five categories. The sample size (n = 1,140) was sufficient for this procedure, with a minimum group size of 10 individuals (Park, 2023). Covariance coverage values were high: .97-1.00 for the AUS-RWA Scale, .95-.96 for the learning activities (LA) scale, .96 for the course design transparency (CDT) scale, and .97 for the student-teacher roles (STR) scale. Missing values were assumed to be missing at random.

As the data had a nested structure (individual students situated in groups), multilevel analyses were considered appropriate. To address the second research question, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to examine the extent to which variation was attributable to student-and course-level differences. ICC represents the proportion of between-group variance relative to the total variance (i.e., the variance at both the student and group levels) (Lüdtke et al., 2009). In our data (see Results section), the ICC values yielded a design effect greater than 2, the recommended cutoff (Raykov & DiStefano, 2021), thereby justifying the use of multilevel analyses.

The online supplementary file (Appendix B) provides detailed information on the measurement models, including the invariance test results across nested data levels, model fit indices, standardized factor loadings, and factor correlations. It also compares two estimation methods: (1) WLSMV, which treats items as categorical variables, and (2) maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR; full information maximum likelihood), which treats items as continuous variables. MLR was additionally employed, as it is commonly used for Likert-scale items (Beauducel & Herzberg, 2006).

To address the fourth research question, we examined associations between agency and teaching practices using multilevel SEM. In the first regression model, the latent CDT factor was specified as an independent variable, with the latent student agency factors as dependent variables. In the second regression model, the three latent LA factors were specified as independent variables, with the latent student agency factors as dependent variables. Because the LA factors (F1-F3) were highly correlated (Appendix B), multicollinearity was addressed using Cholesky decomposition, which enables hierarchical regression within the SEM framework and yields more interpretable results (de Jong, 1999). Wald tests were conducted to compare mean differences in the latent student agency factors across STR categories. All analyses controlled for between-course variation using multilevel modeling.

Model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). The following thresholds were applied to indicate a good model fit: CFI and TLI > .95, RMSEA < .06, and SRMR < .08 (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Effect sizes for associations with LA were interpreted as small (β < 0.20), moderate (β 0.20-0.50), and large (β ≥ 0.50).

6. Discussion

This study examined student agency in higher education (HE) as a multidimensional and multilevel phenomenon comprising students' perceptions of their agency resources, their agentic will, and agentic action. Moving beyond approaches that focus on observable manifestations or outcomes of agency-such as active participation or agentic engagement-the study conceptualizes agency as a dynamic configuration through which students make interpretations and position themselves within learning environments and thereby develop their multidimensional capacity for purposeful learning. The findings contribute to research on agency in three ways: First, they provide empirical support for conceptualizing agency as a multidimensional construct with analytically distinct yet interrelated components. Second, our findings demonstrate the need to take into account the multilevel nature of agency as students' perceptions of their agency were found to vary both within and between courses, indicating that agency is responsive to contextual conditions. Third, the present study identified systematic associations between teaching practices and different dimensions of agency, suggesting that pedagogical arrangements in the courses play an important role in shaping the conditions under which student agency is enacted.

6.1. Levels of student agency

Overall, students reported moderate to high levels of agency across HE courses.

However, variation across subdimensions revealed some interesting differences or tensions in how agency is experienced and enacted. The high perceived power balance among students in their courses indicates that learning environments were typically perceived socially safe and relatively non-hierarchical. Yet, lower ratings for peer support -and notable differences compared to ratings of power balance (d = 0.80) and trust in teacher support (d = 0.66)-suggest that the perceived power balance among students does not automatically translate into experiences of supportive peer resources. Relational perspectives presented in prior literature emphasize that agency is shaped by the positions individuals are afforded within social practices and by their access to participation (Eteläpelto & Lahti, 2008). Therefore, students' experiences of power balance appear to mostly reflect equal opportunities to participate in study groups, whereas supportive peer resources are more likely to come into view where joint work is orchestrated and facilitated (Damşa et al., 2011; Lipponen & Kumpulainen, 2011), especially when attention is also directed to fostering constructive peer support.

A further tension was observed between the students' will to engage and their perceived opportunities for interaction. Students reported relatively high willingness to engage in interactive learning, but this was only moderately reflected in their ratings of perceived opportunities for interactive learning (d = 0.52), and even less in their ratings of active impactful participation-the lowest-rated subdimension. Similarly, while students recognized opportunities for renewal of thinking, these did not consistently translate into enacted participatory agency (d = 0.94). This gap between potential and enactment indicates that agency is not solely an individual disposition but depends on how learning environments structure opportunities for action. Previous studies have shown that dialogic engagement and the co-construction of knowledge rarely emerge spontaneously. Mercer (2012) demonstrates that such forms of participation develop when teachers establish shared norms and routines for interaction, while Chang and Brickman (2018) highlight that collaborative learning more broadly depends on clear task structures and instructional support.

Dimensions directly linked to teachers' action-such as students' trust in teacher support and opportunities for being heard and having influence-formed a strong relational foundation for agency. On average, students perceived that they were listened to, could express their views, and received support from the teacher when facing challenges.

However, the gap between being heard and active participation (d = 0.33) may imply that courses differ in the extent to which they invite participatory or transformative forms of agency. This interpretation is consistent with previous research, which indicates that participation depends on reciprocal interaction and the gradual construction of shared understanding (Mercer, 2012; Damşa et al., 2011) and may remain limited by insufficient self-efficacy (Bandura, 2006).

Taken together, the findings indicate that agency-supportive conditions are unevenly realized across their dimensions. While teacher-student relations and supportive structures appear relatively well established, peer support and active participation remain less developed. This highlights the importance of pedagogical practices that actively foster dialogic engagement and collaborative knowledge construction; without such orchestration, available resources may not fully translate into enacted student agency (Mercer, 2012; Damşa et al., 2011).

6.2. Variation in agency within and between courses

Our multilevel analysis demonstrated that student agency is not solely an individuallevel phenomenon. While a substantial proportion of variance was attributable to differences between students, the significant between-course variation was also nonnegligible, particularly in students' opportunities for interactive learning; being heard and having impact; their competence beliefs; opportunities for renewal of one's thinking; and their active, impactful participation. These patterns suggest that students' experiences of their agency partly vary across course contexts, supporting relational perspectives that view agency as emerging through the interplay between learners and the educational environments in which they participate. This aligns with prior research indicating that learning environments and pedagogical practices shape students' opportunities for participation and influence and thereby condition how agency can be enacted in different contexts (Damşa et al., 2011; Juutilainen, et al., 2018; Klemenčič, 2017; Stenalt & Lassesen, 2021).

At the same time, substantial individual-level variation-especially for self-efficacy beliefs, relational resources, and students' will to engage in and contribute to (co)learning-indicates that agency is also shaped by how students perceive and take up these opportunities. Consistent with prior literature, this highlights that individual differences remain central to how students interpret and act within given conditions (Bandura, 2018; Mercer, 2012). Mercer, 2012).

Taken together, the findings support a multilevel understanding of agency as both individually experienced and pedagogically structured. Rather than being a stable individual attribute, agency presents itself as a context-sensitive phenomenon that emerges through the interaction between students' agentic will and their perceived opportunities afforded by features such as course design and pedagogical practices.

6.3. Interrelations among agency components and subdimensions

Our analyses show that the subdimensions capturing agency resources, students' agentic will, and agentic action are empirically related in ways that are consistent with the proposed multidimensional conceptual model. Correlational patterns at both the individual and course levels indicate systematic associations across subdimensions, although the strength of these associations varies. Particularly strong associations were observed between striving for understanding (will) and intentional, goal-directed learning (action).

These findings contribute to the literature by providing a novel multidimensional conceptualization of student agency that brings together agency resources, agentic will, and agentic action into a unified framework. The results lend support to the conceptualization of agency as a multidimensional construct comprising analytically distinct yet interrelated components. At the same time, some subdimensions appear to be more closely associated than others. For example, the strong association between striving for understanding and intentional, goal-directed learning suggests that students' willingness to invest effort in learning and embrace challenges tends to co-occur with purposeful, goaldirected learning and reflective engagement in learning, which represent conceptually distinct subdimensions of agency.

In the AUS-RWA model, resources for agency, agentic will, and agentic action constitute three interconnected components of agency. The observed correlations among the components support their empirical connections, suggesting that students' perceptions of resources for agency in their course, their agentic will, and their agentic action are interrelated. In the AUS-RWA framework, resources capture both personal capabilities and contextual opportunities for agency, while agentic will reflects students' willingness to mobilize these resources toward purposeful learning, and agentic action represents the enactment of this will in specific learning situations. However, the present findings do not allow conclusions about the directionality or underlying mechanisms of these relationships.

Future longitudinal, process-oriented, and comparative research is needed to examine how these dimensions interact and develop over time and whether the patterns observed in this study can be replicated across different educational and cultural contexts.

6.4. Associations between teaching practices and student agency

The associations between teaching practices and student agency suggest that pedagogy shapes conditions under which agency can evolve. Different practices appear to activate or support different dimensions of agency.

From the teacher orchestrated learning activities organized in the courses, those requiring higher-order thinking, such as problem-solving and critical evaluation of knowledge, were more strongly associated with student agency. These activities showed consistent links with student-perceived opportunities to renew thinking (resource), as well as with students' agentic will and agentic action, suggesting that cognitively demanding tasks in particular may activate higher education students' willingness to invest effort and engage in intentional action. This aligns with prior research showing that inquiry-based and problem-based learning foster students' active engagement and responsibility for their own learning (Jiang et al., 2022; Lipponen & Kumpulainen, 2011).

The relatively weak association between higher-order tasks and competence beliefs suggests that cognitively challenging tasks alone do not automatically foster a sense of capability. This highlights the importance of instructional scaffolding and feedback that help students view demanding tasks as manageable: without sufficient support demanding tasks require effort but do not strengthen perceived competence.

Student perceptions of collaborative learning practices showed a complex pattern where interactive student activities, such as small-group work and joint project work, were positively associated with high perceived resources of peer support (both receiving and offering it). However, collaborative activities had weaker links to students' willingness to invest effort and engage in collaborative work, and enact intentional, goal-directed learning, and they were negatively associated with trust in teacher support. This suggests that interactive working methods do not automatically foster agentic engagement. Rather, collaboration may require task framing and guidance to translate into active participation (Chang & Brickman, 2018; Damşa et al., 2011; Lipponen & Kumpulainen, 2011). Prior research similarly suggests that collaborative practices can support epistemic agency when appropriately designed (Nieminen et al., 2022a).

Practices focused on monitoring and supporting personal learning had moderate associations with trust in teacher support and perceptions of being heard and having impact (resources) but no significant relationships with other agency factors.

Transparency in course design was positively, albeit modestly, associated with all subdimensions of agency, particularly when course features were negotiated with students.

These findings align with previous research demonstrating that transparency enhances students' understanding and engagement, and sense of control (Anderson et al., 2013; Evans, 2016; Winkelmes et al., 2016). The present results extend this work by indicating that course transparency supports student agency across multiple dimensions.

Finally, teacher roles emerged as a central contextual factor. When teachers were perceived primarily as managers of learning processes, student agency was consistently lower. In contrast, supervisory and partnership-oriented roles were associated with higher levels of agency, especially in participatory dimensions. These findings resonate with prior research emphasizing facilitative and autonomy-supportive teaching (Reeve & Shin, 2020), and the role of teachers as partners in learning (Robertson, 2017).

The findings extend prior research by showing that pedagogical practices shape not only students' participation but also the conditions and orientations through which different forms of agency become possible. In this sense, the study shifts the focus from asking whether students are agentic to understanding how teaching practices contribute to shaping the conditions under which agency can be and is enacted across higher education contexts.

7. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the crosssectional design precludes conclusions about causality and directionality. Future research should incorporate longitudinal data to examine both short-and long-term effects of pedagogical practices on student agency. Second, the data were collected from a single university, which limits generalizability across broader institutional and sociocultural contexts. At the same time, the inclusion of multiple courses across different disciplinary settings provides substantial within-institutional variation, enabling analysis of how student agency is differentially structured across pedagogical contexts. Extending the analysis to multiple institutions would further strengthen the generalizability of these findings. Third, the number of clusters in the multilevel analysis (47 courses) was relatively small in relation to the complexity of the model. Future studies using complex multilevel models would benefit from larger numbers of groups to strengthen parameter stability. Taking together, these limitations indicate that while the findings provide robust evidence of associations and multilevel patterning, further research is needed to examine causal pathways and the conditions under which these relationships hold across contexts.

8. Conclusion

This study advances research on higher education by conceptualizing student agency as a multidimensional and multilevel phenomenon comprising three interrelated components: resources for agency, agentic will, and agentic action. By integrating these components within a single framework, the study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of how agency is structured, experienced, and enacted in higher education. These findings demonstrate that student agency is not a fixed individual attribute but a context-sensitive and unevenly distributed phenomenon that varies both across individuals and between courses. By examining variation across 47 courses spanning different disciplinary contexts, the study provides empirical evidence indicating that agency is systematically shaped by course-level conditions.

Importantly, the results show that teaching practices contribute to conditions under which student agency is enacted. Transparent course design, cognitively demanding learning activities, and teacher roles which emphasize supervision and partnership were consistently linked to multiple dimensions of student agency. These findings suggest that pedagogical arrangements shape opportunities available to students and can contribute to their agentic will and agentic action realized within learning environments.

Taken together, the findings of the study provide insights into the relationships among different components of student agency and propose a model for examining the dynamics of agency through the interplay between the agency resources afforded by pedagogical design, the students' agentic will, and the agentic action enacted within learning environments and student communities. This underscores the need to design courses that integrate transparent structures, cognitively challenging tasks, and meaningful interaction in order to foster students' capacity for purposeful learning and transformation in higher education.

By providing a multidimensional, multilevel, empirically grounded framework, the study offers a basis for further research on how student agency is constituted, and how it varies across contexts, and can be supported through pedagogical practices.

Analyses of the measurement models for the scales used in this study. This supplement presents the analysis phases in the measurement models for the following scales: • Agency of University Students -Resources, Will, and Action (AUS-RWA) Scale; • Learning Activities (LA) Scale; and • Course Design Transparency (CDT) Scale.

Factor model for the AUS-RWA Scale

The factor model of the AUS-RWA Scale was developed through a three-step process.

In the first phase, the AUS-Resources (AUS-R) Subscale was analyzed, followed by separate analyses of the AUS-Will (AUS-W) Subscale and the AUS-Action (AUS-A)

Subscale. In the final phase, the AUS-RWA instrument was analyzed using a comprehensive 15-factor model comprising all scales.

To identify a refined theory-based model, consecutive exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) were conducted to establish a theoretically concise, consistent, and interpretable model for students' agentic resources, agentic will, and agentic action (e.g., Hoyle, 2023). Items were omitted or replaced in some scales to ensure that each final factor would have at least three items. The AUR-RWA Scale initially included 80 items: 53 assessing agency resources, 14 assessing agentic will, and 13 assessing agentic action. The items were selected based on three criteria. The first concerned the item's statistical properties in the model-items were excluded if they did not load onto any factor, showed strong cross-loadings to several factors, or had high residual covariance with other items. The second criterion was the use of modification indices in the analyses. The third criterion was the application of a theoretically based interpretation to ensure that the items would form a conceptually coherent factor and that the factors would align with the theoretical framework.

All analyses were conducted using Mplus Version 8.11 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998 -2017). The EFA/CFA factor model was estimated with two-level specifications (withinand between-group levels), and the equality of factor loadings was tested across levels.

Model parameters were calculated using the weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator with chi-square testing. WLSMV was chosen because the items were ordinal, and some were skewed (e.g., responses were limited to three of five categories). The large sample size (n = 1,140) and the minimum group size of 10 individuals supported this approach (see Park, 2023). Missing values ranged from 0 to 2.9% and were assumed to be missing at random. Maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR) was also applied, as it is commonly used for Likert-scale items (Beauducel & Herzberg, 2006).

Nine latent factors of agentic resources (AUS-R Subscale) were derived from 37 items, situated within four conceptual categories of agency (with nine factors in the first-order factor model) derived from the literature: ( 1 Opportunities for interactive learning and Being heard and having impact); and (4) Transformation (two factors: Interest and utility value, and Opportunities for renewal of one's thinking). Based on modification indices, 14 residual covariances were added to the model at both the within-and between-group levels. All fit indices (Table B1, WLSMV) indicated that the nine-factor solution for the AUS-R Subscale fit the data well, despite a high SRMR at the between-group level (level 2). Small changes in CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR (within) suggested that factor loadings could be set equal at the between levels 1 and 2. Treating items as continuous and using the MLR estimator produces a slightly weaker model than WLSMV.

For the AUS-W Subscale, three latent factors based on 11 items provided a good model fit after the addition of two residual covariances (Tables B1, B4, and B5). The confirmed AUS-W Subscale model encompassed three latent factors: Striving for understanding, Will to engage in interactive learning, and Will to engage in joint problem-solving. The standardized factor loadings ranged from .60 to .87, indicating strong associations between the items and their respective factors. The residual correlation between the items skmo2 and skko3 was 0.681, and between skmo2 and skko4, 0.253. These correlations were estimated only at the within-group level because the variance of skmo2 was zero at the between-group level. At the between-group level, the standardized factor loadings ranged from .54 to 1.0, reflecting variations in the residual variances. Some residual variance estimates were small and negative, and therefore fixed to zero.

The ICCs for the AUS-A Subscale factors were .095 (Intentional goal-directed learning), .145 (Collaborating with and supporting others), and .242 (Active impactful participation). All ICCs were statistically significant at the p = .05 level. Active impactful participation correlated strongly with Intentional goal-directed learning and Collaborating with and supporting others at both the within-and between-group levels.

Combining the AUS-R, AUS-W, and AUS-A Subscales with the same model (using the specifications presented earlier in this supplement) yielded good model fit: χ 2 (3016) = 4026.70, p < .001, RMSEA = .017, CFI = .957, TLI = .952, SRMR(W) = .052, and SRMR(B) = .168.
