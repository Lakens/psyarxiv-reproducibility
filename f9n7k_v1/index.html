Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
ps > .05 Fisher’s exact tests showed that the demographic distributions between both groups were statistically comparable (all ps > .05; see Table 1, Supplementary Table S2).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
ps > .05 Fisher’s exact tests showed that the demographic distributions between both groups were statistically comparable (all ps > .05; see Table 1, Supplementary Table S2).
p = .082 However, group differences in noisy environments were not significant (U = 5687.50, Z = -1.74, p = .082).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Auditory environments shape autistic people's everyday experiences and quality of life (QoL).

However, existing literature tends to characterize these experiences primarily as 'hypersensitivity', lacking nuance in how everyday auditory environments shape lived experience. Studies focusing on the experiences of autistic people who are intermittently-/non-speaking are also scarce. This study examined auditory experiences in the presence or absence of competing background noise, operationalized through noisy and quiet everyday environments, among intermittently-/nonspeaking and speaking autistic adults. 419 autistic adults (Mage=32.2 yrs, SDage=11.3) completed an online survey including a bespoke questionnaire on everyday sound experiences and a soundrelated QoL measure. Of these participants, 383 (91%) predominantly used speech while 36 (9%) were intermittently-/non-speaking. Open-text responses from intermittently-/non-speaking participants were analyzed using content analysis. Statistical analyses revealed that intermittently-/non-speaking participants reported greater sound-related impacts on QoL, greater difficulty in quiet environments, and elevated pain hyperacusis compared with speaking participants. Content analysis contextualized these findings: participants often sought predictable environments to support regulation. Adaptive strategies, including selective withdrawal, preparation, and environmental modification, were framed as proactive self-regulation. Together, the findings position sensory accessibility as an environmental design consideration rather than an individual burden, while centering autonomy in managing auditory input to foster inclusion and wellbeing.

This study explored how intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults experience everyday environments with and without competing background noise. We also examined sound-related quality of life and different types of sound sensitivity and compared these experiences with autistic adults who predominantly use speech.

We analysed survey responses from 419 autistic adults. This included 36 people who identified as intermittently-or non-speaking. Participants completed questionnaires about everyday listening experiences, sound-related quality of life, and different types of sound sensitivity. We also analysed written responses from intermittently-/non-speaking participants. This helped us understand their experiences in their own words.

Intermittently-/non-speaking participants reported that sounds had a greater impact on their quality of life than participants who predominantly used speech. They also reported more difficulty in quiet environments. They were also more likely to report pain in response to sound. Their written responses showed that they actively shaped their environments. They planned ahead, changed their surroundings, or left overwhelming situations when needed. These strategies were not simply about avoiding sound. They helped people regulate their sensory experiences, communicate more effectively, and take part in everyday life.

These findings show that auditory experiences are shaped by more than sound alone. Autonomy, communication, and control over the environment also matter. Creating more sensory-accessible environments may improve quality of life for intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults. Understanding these experiences is an important step towards more inclusive research, services, and communities.

Lay Abstract

Introduction

Many autistic people find everyday sounds challenging. These experiences can affect daily life and quality of life. However, most research has focused on autistic people who communicate using speech. We know much less about the experiences of intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults.

Introduction

Autistic people frequently report significant differences in how they perceive and process sound, with sensory differences being a primary component of the autistic profile (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Kapp, 2025; Poulsen et al., 2024). Autistic people also report that many auditory environments encountered in everyday life are aversive and, at times, disabling (MacLennan, Woolley, et al., 2022; Takach et al., 2025). These experiences have been linked to impacts on emotional wellbeing, participation, social connection, and quality of life (Costa-López et al., 2021; Poulsen et al., 2025). While research has begun to characterise the autistic auditory profile, the sensory literature persistently excludes the experiences of intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults, particularly first-person accounts from this population (Blasko, 2025; Carson et al., 2021; Shoener et al., 2008). We have chosen the term intermittently-/non-speaking as it most closely reflects the experiences of participants included in the current study. It was also endorsed by our authors with lived experience, and aligns with current accounts of terminology in the literature (Bottema-Beutel et al., 2025).

Auditory experiences are shaped not only by the properties of individual sounds but also by the characteristics of the environments in which those sounds occur (El-Kaim et al., 2015). Autistic adults often describe their responses to sound as influenced by features beyond sound parameters, including the predictability and complexity of auditory information, as well as the non-sensory demands of the sensory environment, including social engagement (MacLennan, Woolley, et al., 2022; Masiero et al., 2025; Rosas-Pérez et al., 2023 , 2025). Yet, most empirical research has focused on hyper-responsivity or auditory-isolated in-lab tasks, with very little attention given to how autistic adults experience auditory settings within everyday environments (Cantiani et al., 2016; Haigh et al., 2015; Sokhadze et al., 2017).

One environmental characteristic of particular relevance to everyday auditory experience is the presence or absence of competing background noise. Background noise can affect listening and attention by introducing competing auditory information. During communication, background noise can acoustically compete with a target speech signal, increasing the demands of identifying and processing speech (Anderson & Kraus, 2011; Evans et al., 2014). During activities requiring concentration, background sounds can also cognitively compete for attentional resources and interfere with task-relevant processing (Hernandez et al., 2020). These effects appear to be particularly relevant in autism, with autistic people reporting and demonstrating greater difficulty than non-autistic people in the presence of competing auditory information, including during speech-in-noise and other listening tasks (Brewer et al., 2016; J. Chen et al., 2019; Ruiz Callejo & Boets, 2023). This suggests that the auditory environment itself can shape listening and attentional demands, rather than auditory challenges being attributable only to individual sound sensitivity.

Examining auditory experiences in the presence or absence of competing background noise may therefore provide important insight into how auditory context influences everyday experience in autism.

Individual differences in auditory experiences may also shape how autistic people experience and respond to auditory environments. Autistic adults also experience a greater likelihood of cooccurring auditory conditions, including hyperacusis, misophonia, phonophobia, and soundinduced pain (Ahmmed & Mukherjee, 2021; Brueijs et al., 2024; Danesh et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, existing studies have largely focused on hyperacusis -more commonly termed hyper-sensitivity or hyper-responsivity -with far fewer investigations into misophonia, phonophobia, or pain-related responses (Brueijs et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2025). However, despite growing recognition of both the role of auditory environments and the heterogeneity of auditory conditions in autism, these experiences have not been equally represented within the research, including those who are non-speaking or have complex communication needs.

Intermittently-/non-speaking autistic people account for approximately 25-30% of the autistic population (Kebede et al., 2026; Koegel et al., 2020). Most existing studies rely on spoken interviews, standardised self-report questionnaires, or clinical observations that often presume spoken language as the primary mode of communication (Harrop et al., 2018; Patten et al., 2013; Weitlauf et al., 2017). Additionally, adults who communicate primarily through typed, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), gesture-based, or other methods have had limited opportunities to directly report their lived sensory experiences (Jaswal et al., 2026; Shoener et al., 2008). This gap is particularly concerning given that sensory, communicative, and environmental demands may intersect differently for intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults than for those who primarily communicate using speech (Lebenhagen, 2020; Zhang et al., 2019).

Expanding the use of accessible methodologies are therefore essential for ensuring that the perspectives of intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults are represented in sensory research.

To this end, the aim of the current study was to compare the impact of sound sensitivity on QoL, experiences of auditory environments in the presence or absence of competing background noise (i.e. quiet versus noisy environments), and auditory condition profiles (hyperacusis, misophonia, phonophobia, and pain hyperacusis) between intermittently-/non-speaking and speaking autistic adults. Specifically, we asked whether these aspects of auditory experience differed according to communication profile. These were examined using a sound-related QoL measure (Williams, Cascio, et al., 2021), selected items of a bespoke auditory experiences questionnaire (Poulsen et al., 2025), and a psychometrically validated measure of auditory condition profiles (Williams et al., 2025). Further, qualitative data were obtained through open-text responses to explore first-hand accounts of auditory experiences among intermittently-/non-speaking autistic people. Given the limited research examining auditory experiences among intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults, analyses were primarily exploratory. Together, these analyses sought to broaden understanding of autistic auditory experiences across communication profiles and identify whether aspects of auditory experience may be overlooked when intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults are underrepresented in sensory research.

Methods

Study design

This study used an online survey design that obtained quantitative and qualitative data to examine auditory experiences in the presence or absence of competing background noise, auditory conditions, and sound-related QoL among autistic adults. Ethics approval for the study was granted by the [blinded for review] Human Research Ethics Committee (ref. 52023563048250).

Participants

Participants were drawn from a dataset reported in [blinded for review] (2025) combined with additional participants recruited to improve the representation of sex assigned at birth who were male. The original study examined relationships between auditory environments, autistic traits, and quality of life across the full autistic sample. The present study focuses specifically on participants' communication profiles, comparing autistic adults who identified as intermittently-/non-speaking with those who predominantly used speaking as their main form of communication. The study was designed to be inclusive of autistic people with diverse communication profiles, including intermittently-/non-speaking participants. However, it was not originally designed specifically to compare communication groups, and the intermittently-/non-speaking subgroup comprised participants who identified this communication profile within the broader sample. The present analyses were subsequently developed to examine whether auditory experiences differed according to communication profile, given the underrepresentation of intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults in existing auditory research.

In our original study [blinded for review], 296 participants were initially recruited through autistic community networks, disability organizations, and social media. Eligibility criteria included adults aged 18 years or older, who could complete the survey in English and were either formally diagnosed with autism or self-identified as autistic. The inclusion of self-identifying autistic adults aligns with growing evidence highlighting the importance of self-identification for equitable participation in autism research (Ardeleanu et al., 2024; English, Poulsen et al., 2025; Overton et al., 2023). We intentionally sought to recruit a diverse cohort and encouraged participation from underrepresented populations (e.g., those who are culturally diverse, gender diverse, or with cooccurring conditions such as ADHD). An additional 123 male-assigned-at-birth participants were subsequently recruited from Prolific (Prolific, 2026) with the same criteria as stipulated in the 2025 study to further balance the demographics.

Participants' communication profile was established using a single-choice item ("I use speech as my preferred communication method") with four response options: "always", "often", "sometimes" or "never". In this study, participants who selected "sometimes" or "never" were classified as intermittently-/non-speaking, while those who selected "always" or "often" were classified as speaking. This approach, informed by consultation with intermittently-/non-speaking autistic people, was used to ensure the item was straightforward and interpretable for participants with diverse communication profiles.

Recruitment and data screening procedures for the original dataset are described in [blinded for review]. No additional exclusions were applied to the original dataset for the present analyses, and all participants recruited through Prolific who completed the survey were included. 296 were from the first data collection [blinded for review] and 123 were from the additional recruitment from Prolific. The final sample comprised 419 autistic adults aged between 18 and 72 years (M=32.2, SD=11.33). Of whom, 383 autistic adults were classified as "speaking", with 36 autistic adults classified as "intermittently-/non-speaking". All participants who provided consent and completed the survey were included in analysis (see Table 1). Fisher's exact tests showed that the demographic distributions between both groups were statistically comparable (all ps > .05; see Table 1, Supplementary Table S2). Socioeconomic status was not collected in the present study.

Auditory Environments Survey

The Auditory Environments Survey is a bespoke 15-item questionnaire developed to capture everyday auditory experiences across a range of real-world listening contexts (Poulsen et al., 2025).

The four factors identified in the original study comprised items spanning both quiet and noisy environments and therefore did not permit direct comparison of auditory experiences in the presence or absence of competing background noise. These comprised pairs of items assessing one's ability to follow a conversation with one person, follow a conversation with two or more people, concentration, and distraction by small sounds in quiet or noisy environments. Selection of these items was further informed through consultation with intermittently-/non-speaking autistic community members to ensure the included listening contexts reflected experiences considered meaningful and relevant to the study aims. Items assessing experiences specifically associated with talking were not included because these experiences were contingent on spoken communication and were therefore not appropriate for between-group comparison.

The eight selected items were grouped according to auditory environment to produce Quiet and Noisy scores, representing experiences in the absence and presence of competing background noise, respectively (see Supplementary Table S1). These item groupings were defined based on the environmental context represented by the items rather than derived empirically using factor analytic methods, as the aim was to compare experiences across quiet and noisy environments rather than measure underlying constructs. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from Definitely Disagree to Definitely Agree, with higher scores reflecting greater challenge or disruption within that environment.

Internal consistency was moderate for both the Quiet (Cronbach's α = .60) and Noisy (Cronbach's α = .65) groupings.

Sound-related quality of life (QoL) impact was assessed using an eight-item measure developed to capture the extent to which auditory experiences affect daily activities, stress, participation, and relationships. This questionnaire was in the original MIST-A (Williams, Cascio, et al., 2021). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting greater impact on QoL. Internal consistency of this questionnaire for the current sample was excellent (α = .93).

Procedure

The survey was administered through REDCap (Harris et al., 2009 (Harris et al., , 2019)). No identifying information was obtained beyond consent, and data were screened to ensure data quality (e.g., duplicate response detection). The first page of the REDCap survey contained the Participant Information Sheet and Consent Form. Participants reviewed this information and provided informed consent by agreeing to continue before commencing the survey. Participants first completed demographic questions, followed by the questionnaires in the order presented in this manuscript. Optional open-text response boxes were presented following selected questionnaire sections, inviting participants to elaborate on their responses or describe their experiences in their own words.

To support inclusion and accessibility, participants could request adjustments such as offline response options or assistance from a support person. Participants recruited through the original survey were not compensated for their time as funding was not available for participant reimbursement at the scale of the original study. No participants requested additional adjustments, but did utilise the "save and return later" function in REDCap, to be able to complete the survey in multiple sittings. Participants recruited through Prolific received financial compensation in accordance with Prolific's recommended payment guidelines.

Data analysis

Quantitative Analysis

Data were screened prior to analysis for missingness, duplicate entries and out-of-range values. No duplicate responses were identified. All variables were retained in their raw form, and no transformations were applied. Nonparametric analyses were selected a priori due to unequal group sizes and the ordinal nature of the questionnaire responses. Differences in the demographic variables, co-occurring psychological conditions, known auditory conditions, and use of auditory support strategies between speaking and intermittently-/non-speaking groups were examined using Fisher's Exact Tests, due to small, expected cell counts in the intermittently-/non-speaking group.

These comparisons were conducted to characterize the sample and assess whether the communication groups differed on demographic or clinical characteristics that may influence interpretation of subsequent group differences in auditory experiences and quality of life (see Table 2, Supplementary Table S2).

Quantitative data were analysed using SPSS (Version 31.0.1.0). Group differences between speaking and intermittently-/non-speaking participants were examined across three domains: sound-related quality of life, auditory environment experiences, and auditory conditions.

Autistic trait profiles were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests on CATI total and subscale scores.

Sound-related quality of life was analysed using the overall QoL score. Group differences were examined using Mann-Whitney U tests. Individual QoL items were also examined (see Supplementary Table S3).

Auditory condition profiles were examined using T-scores from MIST-A subscales, including Hyperacusis, Misophonia, Pain Hyperacusis, and Phonophobia. Group differences were analysed using Mann-Whitney U tests. Descriptive comparisons against published clinical cut-offs were also reported to contextualise the level of sound sensitivity across the sample. Statistical significance was determined using an alpha level of .05 for the primary domain-level comparisons. Where multiple item-level comparisons were conducted within a measure, Bonferroni corrections were applied to control for multiple testing. For all Mann-Whitney U tests, effect sizes were calculated as r = Z/√N and interpreted using Cohen's (1988) guidelines, with values of .10, .30, and .50 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

Qualitative Analysis

Open text responses provided by intermittently-/non-speaking participants specifically were analysed using directed qualitative content analysis, following the approach outlined by Hsieh and Shannon (2005). This method was selected to allow the qualitative data to elaborate and contextualise patterns identified in the quantitative analyses, while still permitting inductive development of new concepts arising from participants' descriptions. The initial analytical framework was informed by the key domains examined in the survey, particularly QoL, communication preferences, and auditory environments. Initial coding focused on identifying narratives relating to these domains. The emergent QoL categories were then used to examine how participants' descriptions of communication preferences and auditory supports explained, reinforced, or extended their reported QoL experiences. Coding remained open to concepts that were not represented within the initial analytical framework. Using NVivo (Version 15.3.2), coding was conducted at the level of meaning units, with attention to participants' language and the context in which experiences were described.

Although the analysis was guided by the quantitative structure, the coding process remained open to content not represented in the structured measures. When participants introduced experiences that did not align with the initial domains, additional codes were created to capture these elements.

Codes were iteratively reviewed and grouped into higher-level categories to reflect patterns across responses. This approach allowed the qualitative findings to deepen understanding of the quantitative results while also capturing aspects of auditory experience, communication and quality of life that extended beyond the survey measures. Coding was conducted by co-author [blinded for review], with the coding framework and category structure iteratively reviewed and refined in collaboration with the research team to ensure consistency and alignment with the data. The final categories therefore reflect a combination of theory-informed and data-driven elements grounded in the reported experiences of intermittently-/non-speaking Autistic adults.

Community involvement

Our research program, including the survey used in this study, was led by autistic and non-autistic researchers and developed in collaboration with autistic advisors with diverse communication profiles and support needs. Autistic advisors contributed to the refinement of survey language, accessibility, and the relevance of constructs relating to the measures used. Their input shaped the wording of items, the response scales, and the overall layout of the survey materials, as well as interpretation of the data, manuscript writing and dissemination. See Supplementary Table S5 for the GRIPP2 (short form) that details our participatory practices (Staniszewska et al., 2017).

Positionality

This study was undertaken by a multidisciplinary team of autistic and non-autistic researchers with expertise in autism research, psychology and neuroscience, qualitative methods, and participatory approaches. Additionally, the original online survey was designed with a group of four autistic community members, one describing themselves as intermittently speaking. The author team also included researchers with lived experience of intermittently-/non-speaking communication.

Throughout the study, we recognized communication as a fundamental human right and approached communication preferences from the position that speech is one of many valid forms of communication and should not be regarded as a proxy for competence, autonomy, or capacity.

This perspective informed the design, analysis, and interpretation of the study, including our use of accessible survey methods and our interpretation of communication preferences as meaningful individual differences rather than deficits. Consistent with the principles advocated by CommunicationFIRST (2023), we recognize the importance of respecting diverse communication methods and ensuring that people who cannot rely on speech alone are able to participate meaningfully in research (Communication FIRST, 2023; McLeod, 2018).

Qualitative Analysis

Content analysis -QoL impact

From the codes generated during inductive coding for the intermittently-/non-speaking group's responses, we identified four overarching impacts on QoL: relationships, wellbeing, participation, and autonomy. These four impacts captured the major issues concerning how participants' auditory experiences affect their overall quality of life.

Relationships. The 'relationships' impact captures both the positive effects of having loved ones who support your sensory needs: "Many of my loved ones are understanding and will warn me when they're going to use a blender or understand when I don't want to go to loud public places" (ID39), as well as the detrimental consequences that arise when they are not understanding of auditory challenges: "...other loved ones gaslight me and tell me it's not a big deal..." (ID39).

The impact of supportive or unsupportive relationships on quality of life was also evident in how much others understood and accommodated participants' preferred communication methods, with one participant describing "I use a combination of mouth words and sounds (which reflect my internal state of emotion, want, need, and/or desire) or body gestures to communicate with my trusted loved ones in safe settings such as home." (ID247). When preferences were accepted and used, participants felt heard and acknowledged, experienced increased wellbeing, and faced reduced executive functioning demands than if they were required to communicate in a nonpreferred format.

Wellbeing. This impact included topics relating to emotional, mental, and physical health, and how these are affected by sound and sensory overwhelm: "I am so traumatized by sounds that I worry the stress of it is going to kill me and ruin my already failing body" (ID182). For some participants, the impact of sound depended on factors such as its pitch and how frequently, or how likely, the sound was to occur (ID64; ID236). One participant described how seasonal changes in sound, such as insects becoming louder during summer months, could affect their capacity for extended periods and create negative anticipation of seasonal changes (ID236). Sound was also seen to impact sleep and rest, especially when quiet spaces were not respected by family members (ID119). Uncomfortable environments were often manageable, but required significant recovery time afterwards, (ID221). Several participants described experiencing extreme discomfort and anxiety as a direct result of an unsupportive auditory environment: "I have had multiple mental breakdowns, self-harmed and experienced suicidal ideation and attempts as a direct result of living in cities, suburbs and communities that are not conducive to my sensory needs" (ID247). Together, these accounts demonstrate how auditory experiences could have substantial and sustained impacts on participants' wellbeing.

Participation. This impact captured how sound can influence participation in activities, including how preparing to visit overwhelming environments may cause anticipatory anxiety or lead to avoidance of certain locations: "I actively avoid going to cities, or crowded places like restaurants" (ID539); and: "I can't go to events with lots of toddlers or crying babies because I am so uncomfortable with the sounds" (ID64). The role of sound on choosing whether to deal with or avoid an activity often depended on what support was available (ID119). Sound was also reported by participants as impacting access to remote and in-person education (ID119). Auditory experiences could therefore influence whether, where, and how participants were able to participate in everyday activities.

Autonomy. This impact describes the difference in quality of life when participants had autonomy over their location, auditory support tools and ability to adapt their environment to better Participants also experienced clear benefits when they had access and autonomy over their use of auditory support tools, like earplugs (ID64), often making environments that would otherwise be overwhelming, a manageable experience (ID280; ID3). Some participants also lowered their expectations of their own capacity to manage sensory sensitivities, aligning their goals with what they felt able to achieve (ID23). Participants who could choose to work from home could avoid many disruptive sounds (ID236), highlighting the benefits of choice, whereas those who could not were compelled to endure noisy environments and their associated challenges (Study ID63). Autonomy therefore shaped participants' capacity to respond to their auditory needs and make environments more manageable.

Discussion

This study examined the auditory experiences of intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults, including sound-related QoL, experiences of quiet and noisy auditory environments, and auditory condition profiles, compared with speaking autistic adults. Intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults reported greater sound-related impacts on quality of life than speaking participants, despite showing comparable autistic trait profiles, as measured by the CATI. Furthermore, while no significant between-group difference was detected in noisy environments, intermittently-/nonspeaking participants reported greater difficulty in quiet environments than speaking participants, indicating that between-group differences were context-specific rather than pervasive. Across auditory condition profiles, both groups showed elevated sensitivity relative to clinical norms, with a specific difference emerging for pain hyperacusis in the intermittently-/non-speaking group. The qualitative findings complemented these group-level patterns by demonstrating how auditory experiences were embedded within participants' everyday contexts, including their communication needs, degree of environmental control, and the strategies they used to manage auditory demands and support participation.

The overall findings challenge existing literature that has primarily characterized autistic auditory experience in terms of general hypersensitivity or responses to loudness specifically, by demonstrating that auditory experiences may vary across environmental contexts. Further, the findings highlight differences that have not previously been described for intermittently-/nonspeaking autistic adults (MacLennan, Woolley, et al., 2022; Poulsen et al., 2025; Rosas-Pérez et al., 2025). Importantly auditory research has largely excluded intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults, with existing evidence predominantly derived from studies of children via clinical or parent observations (Gunderson et al., 2025; Harrop et al., 2018; Lane et al., 2014; Ludlow & Wilkins, 2016; Patten et al., 2013; Sanz-Cervera et al., 2015; Siper et al., 2017; Stewart et al., 2016), or laboratory-based paradigms using isolated auditory stimuli such as single frequency waveforms, white noise, or other non-naturalistic paradigms (Cantiani et al., 2016; Matsuba et al., 2024; Matsuzaki et al., 2019; Yau et al., 2015). As a result, there has been limited understanding of how auditory experiences are structured and managed in everyday environments, particularly for intermittently-/non-speaking people.

Quality of life

Intermittently-/non-speaking participants reported greater sound-related impacts on QoL than speaking participants, indicating that the burden associated with auditory challenges may be more pronounced in this group. Previous research has linked aversive sensory environments with reduced wellbeing, participation, and daily functioning in autistic people (Dunlop et al., 2016; Sanz-Cervera et al., 2015; Strömberg et al., 2022) but has typically not distinguished between communication profiles or has focused on different populations and contexts. As such, direct comparisons between intermittently-/non-speaking and speaking autistic adults have not previously been demonstrated. Importantly, greater sound-related QoL impacts were observed despite no significant group difference in autistic trait profiles as measured by the CATI, suggesting that the observed difference cannot be accounted for simply by higher overall autistic trait scores in the intermittently-/non-speaking group. Instead, auditory experiences may intersect with communication access, support availability, environmental control, and autonomy, with compounding consequences for everyday life. This interpretation is consistent with broader research demonstrating that the impact of sensory experiences is shaped by social and environmental context and the opportunities available to communicate and respond to sensory needs (Y. Chen et al., 2024; MacLennan, O'Brien, et al., 2022; Mandy, 2019; Mechkaroff et al., 2022).

The qualitative findings provide further insight into the everyday auditory experiences of intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults beyond that captured by the quantitative measures. The analysis identified impacts across four interconnected areas of quality of life: relationships, wellbeing, participation, and autonomy. Auditory experiences were described as occurring within broader social and environmental contexts, with their impact influenced not only by sound itself, but by whether sensory and communication needs were understood, supported, and able to be acted upon. Similar contextual influences on sensory experiences have been described in qualitative research with broader autistic populations, including the role of environmental demands and social context (MacLennan, O'Brien, et al., 2022; Taels et al., 2023). Additionally, relationships were described as both a source of support and an additional source of difficulty. Participants described how understanding from others could facilitate preferred communication methods and accommodation of sensory needs, whereas dismissal or misunderstanding could compound the impact of difficult auditory experiences. These relational experiences were closely connected with wellbeing.

Autonomy was also closely connected with participants' accounts of relationships, wellbeing, and participation. Participants described actively shaping their environments and routines through preparation, environmental modification, auditory supports, choices about communication, and decisions about when to engage or withdraw. Conversely, limited choice over location, communication, or auditory conditions could restrict participants' capacity to respond to their sensory needs. These findings align with broader conceptualizations of autonomy as important to autistic wellbeing and with approaches emphasizing the relationship between autistic people and their environments (Pellicano et al., 2022; Pellicano & Heyworth, 2023; Poulsen et al., 2026; Wohofsky et al., 2023). The present findings suggest that for the intermittently-/non-speaking participants in this study, autonomy over auditory environments was intertwined with whether sensory and communication needs could be expressed, understood, and accommodated.

Quiet Environments

Intermittently-/non-speaking participants reported greater difficulty in quiet auditory environments compared to speaking participants within this sample, while no group differences were observed in noisy contexts. This finding challenges a common assumption that quieter environments are inherently more supportive or less demanding for autistic people (Rosas-Pérez et al., 2025). Much of the existing literature has focused on reducing overall sound levels or exposure to high-intensity noise, often treating quiet as a proxy for accessibility (Brueijs et al., 2024; Khalfa et al., 2004). The present findings suggest that this assumption may be overly simplistic. One plausible interpretation is that, rather than necessarily providing reduced sensory demand, quiet environments may introduce different types of challenges, including greater salience or unpredictability of individual sounds within the environment (Ashburner et al., 2013; Price & Romualdez, 2025; Taels et al., 2023). This reframes quiet not as inherently accessible, but as a context in which sensory salience and unpredictability might still interact. Importantly, this pattern has not been previously described in the literature, which has largely focused on responses to sensory sensitivity or loudness within an environment, and most often in broader autistic or child populations (Dwyer et al., 2020; Takach et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2021).

The absence of a significant group difference in noisy environments is also important. Competing background noise can increase listening and attentional demands, and previous research has demonstrated particular difficulties for autistic people in environments containing competing auditory information. Consistent with this broader literature, the null difference observed between both groups in the present study suggests that both groups likely experience noisy environments as challenging. One possible explanation for the absence of a between-group difference is therefore that noisy environments present substantial challenges for autistic adults across communication profiles. This may also reflect self-regulatory responses to difficult auditory input, with previous research suggesting that autistic people may use strategies to reduce or disengage from intense or painful sensory experiences (Failla et al., 2018; Nicolardi et al., 2025; Williams, Suzman, et al., 2021a). Such regulation may also involve anticipating difficulties in noisy environments and employing strategies such as environmental modification, hearing protection, or avoidance to reduce their impact (Poulsen et al., 2025). It is also possible that regulatory responses differ according to the intensity and nature of the auditory environment, with more overwhelming or painful input prompting greater disengagement from, or blocking of, auditory input (Bogdanova et al., 2022; Nicolardi et al., 2025; Williams, Suzman, et al., 2021a). Importantly, the absence of a group difference in noisy environments reflects a comparison between two autistic groups and should not be interpreted as indicating that competing background noise is unimportant to autistic auditory experience.

Relatedly, qualitative accounts from intermittently-/non-speaking participants described avoiding or withdrawing from environments they anticipated would be overwhelming, while support and opportunities to modify auditory input could make otherwise difficult environments more manageable. Choosing not to enter, or choosing to leave, an overwhelming environment could therefore form part of actively managing sensory needs and preserving capacity for participation elsewhere, consistent with broader accounts of autistic adults actively adapting to sensory environments (Harrop et al., 2018; Neville et al., 2026; Poulsen et al., 2024 Poulsen et al., , 2025)). Rather than positioning auditory challenges solely as an individual response to sound, participants' accounts highlight the importance of the surrounding environmental and relational context, including the opportunities available to respond to sensory needs.

Notably, although greater difficulty in quiet environments was an unexpected finding, the qualitative accounts reported here predominantly described challenging experiences involving loud or intense sounds and noisy environments. This apparent divergence may reflect differences in how these experiences were elicited. The quantitative items explicitly prompted participants to consider their experiences across matched quiet and noisy contexts, whereas the open-text questions did not specifically ask about either context. More intense or immediately distressing auditory experiences may therefore have been more salient when participants spontaneously described the impact of sound on their quality of life, consistent with literature documenting the substantial affective and physical impacts of decreased sound tolerance in autistic people (Williams et al., 2021). Difficulties experienced in quiet environments, by contrast, may have been more readily identified when directly prompted.

Auditory Pain

Across all MIST-A subscales, both communication groups showed elevated sound sensitivity relative to validated norms, consistent with existing literature documenting increased prevalence of auditory conditions in autistic populations (e.g., hyperacusis, misophonia) (Williams et al., 2025). Although pain hyperacusis differed between groups, no significant differences were observed for hyperacusis, misophonia or phonophobia, suggesting that the between-group difference was specific to pain-related responses rather than reflecting consistently greater sound sensitivity across auditory conditions in intermittently-/non-speaking participants. Pain hyperacusis is distinct from loudness hyperacusis: rather than sounds being experienced as excessively loud or uncomfortable, sounds can evoke physical pain. Although prior research has identified elevated rates of hyperacusis in autistic populations, much of this work has focused on loudness sensitivity or general sound intolerance, with relatively limited attention to pain-related auditory responses as a distinct subtype (Bang & Igelström, 2022; Brueijs et al., 2024).

The specificity of this group difference to pain hyperacusis may also be relevant to the broader literature on pain in autism. Autistic pain experiences are increasingly understood as heterogeneous, with differences in pain perception, expression, and communication challenging longstanding assumptions of reduced pain sensitivity (Bogdanova et al., 2022). Pain hyperacusis may therefore be particularly important to distinguish from loudness intolerance in intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults, for whom communicating or having pain recognised may present additional challenges (Bang & Igelström, 2022; Brueijs et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2021). The present findings extend this literature by identifying heightened pain-related auditory responses specifically within the intermittently-/non-speaking adult sample (Bogdanova et al., 2022).

Qualitative findings contextualize this pattern. Participants described sound-related physical discomfort and pain, often alongside emotional and physiological distress, disruption to rest and recovery, and the need for prolonged recovery following difficult auditory environments, reflecting the wellbeing impact of these experiences. Accounts highlighted how particular sound qualities, such as pitch or sudden onset, could trigger strong responses. These accounts are consistent with previous research linking sensory experiences with stress, overload, and mental wellbeing in autistic adults (Y. Chen et al., 2024; Green et al., 2018; Strömberg et al., 2022), while extending this literature through first-hand accounts from intermittently-/non-speaking adults, whose experiences have rarely been directly represented in auditory research. Participants also described challenges in accessing appropriate support, including instances where clinical or environmental interventions were ineffective or exacerbated discomfort, reflecting both autonomy and relational impacts. Having appropriate support, especially to reduce or remove auditory-related pain is imperative, through properly identified supports and autonomy to customize auditory environments (Beauchamp et al., 2025; Heyworth et al., 2022).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the intermittently-/non-speaking group was substantially smaller than the speaking group, comprising 36 of 419 participants (8.6%). This proportion is lower than estimates suggesting that approximately 25-30% of autistic people are intermittently-/nonspeaking or have limited spoken language (Bottema-Beutel et al., 2025; Kebede et al., 2026; Koegel et al., 2020). Although appropriate non-parametric analyses were used, some subtle effects may not have been detected due to the difference in the number of intermittently-/non-speaking participants. This underrepresentation may also reflect the online survey design, which may not have been accessible to all intermittently-/non-speaking autistic people. Future research could incorporate more flexible and individualized approaches to participation and data collection to better support a broader range of communication and access needs.

Second, while the approach used to identify participants' communication profile was informed by consultation with intermittently-/non-speaking autistic people, including community members and co-authors to minimize participant burden and maximize accessibility, it was nevertheless derived from a single self-report item, which may not fully capture the heterogeneity of communication patterns (e.g., context-dependent speech, AAC use, or supported communication). Furthermore, response modality (e.g., AAC, assisted vs. unassisted completion) was not systematically recorded, and future work using multi-item communication profiles and explicit response-process measures would strengthen classification and interpretation of group differences.

Third, internal consistency of the Quiet and Noisy item groupings was modest (α = .60 and .65, respectively), indicating that the items capture heterogeneous aspects of experience within these auditory environments rather than a single underlying construct. The groupings were defined a priori according to environmental context rather than derived as psychometric scales; nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted with this measurement limitation in mind. The open-text questions also did not specifically prompt participants to reflect on quiet versus noisy environments. Consequently, the qualitative findings cannot be interpreted as directly corroborating the quantitative comparison between Quiet and Noisy scores, and the relative prominence of noisy auditory experiences in participants' spontaneous accounts should be interpreted in light of how these data were elicited.

Fourth, measurement invariance across communication groups was not formally assessed for the QoL measure, MIST-A subscales, or AE items. As such, observed group differences may partly reflect differences in item interpretation as well as underlying experience. Multiple comparisons were conducted across subscales, and individual findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In addition, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences about relationships between auditory conditions, auditory environments, and quality of life. Despite these limitations, the integration of quantitative patterns and qualitative accounts provides complementary insight into participants' auditory experiences.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed examination of auditory environments and auditory condition profiles among intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults. By integrating structured quantitative measures with open-text reflections, the findings demonstrate that communication profile was associated with differences in auditory experience -despite comparable autistic trait profiles.

Intermittently-/non-speaking adults reported greater sound-related impacts on quality of life, greater difficulty in quiet environments, and higher levels of pain hyperacusis compared with speaking autistic adults. The qualitative accounts provide insight into these experiences, showing how sound interacts with emotional and physical wellbeing, relational dynamics, and opportunities for participation. Together, these findings underscore that auditory accessibility cannot be understood solely in terms of sound level or sensory thresholds; instead, it reflects the interaction of environmental conditions, communication demands, and the degree of autonomy individuals have in shaping their sensory worlds. By integrating first-person accounts with real-world contextual measures, the present study provides novel insight into how auditory experiences are organised and experienced in daily life for this underrepresented population. Our results highlight the importance of centring intermittently-/non-speaking autistic adults in sensory research and emphasise the need for approaches that consider both auditory environments and communication access as fundamental components of sensory and overall wellbeing.
