Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by a range of developmental differences following the exposure of alcohol in utero. Although not a stand-alone diagnostic criterion for FASD, sensory processing deficits are commonly observed in individuals with FASD. The purpose of this scoping review was to review the existing evidence regarding the sensory processing differences in individuals with FASD compared to neurotypical individuals. A scoping review of the literature was conducted investigating studies of human participants with FASD and neurotypical participants. Studies were included if they reported on sensory processing differences between FASD and neurotypical participants. Any measures of sensory processing and in-utero exposure to alcohol were included. APA Psycinfo, Embase, Medline, Scopus, and CINAHL were searched for relevant studies. A total of 1192 studies were screened for inclusion, of which 42 were extracted. Studies investigated sensory processing differences in unisensory visual, auditory, olfactory, and somatosensory processing, multisensory integration, and sensorimotor processing. Measures of sensory processing included imaging measures (fMRI, MRI), clinical interviews, questionnaires, behavioural tasks, psychometric tests, and electrophysiological measures (EEG, MEG, DTI). Results indicate that differences in sensory processing exist in individuals with FASD when compared to neurotypical controls; however, these differences may vary depending on the age of participants, the type of sensory processing being measured, and other individual participant factors.

1. Introduction

Sensory processing is defined as brain processes involved in detecting and integrating sensory inputs from the environment and generating an appropriate response (Al Qutub et al., 2024; Harrison & Hare, 2004). Typical sensitive periods for normal sensory processing development occur from prenatal periods to early childhood (Azañón et al., 2018; Maurer & Lewis, 2013). Inadequate and unvaried sensory experiences during these periods can result in atypical sensory processing, which can drastically impact daily functioning, including an individual's ability in work or school environments, to participate in family or leisure activities, and even overall quality of life (Kinnealey et al., 2011; Schaaf et al., 2011). Atypical sensory processing can also impact other developmental milestones, given that higher-order milestones such as motor, cognitive, language, and social-emotional development rely heavily on sensory input to achieve these skills (Bahrick et al., 2004; Barmak & Baş, 2024; Cabral et al., 2015; Kail, 2002). Atypical early sensory development has been demonstrated in individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders (Gigliotti et al., 2024) which are a cluster of disorders that onset in prenatal or early childhood periods. These disorders are characterized by developmental differences in brain processes that impact personal, social, academic, or occupational functioning (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). As a result of atypical sensory development, sensory processing differences have been well studied across a number of these disorders, including Autism Spectrum Disorders (ASD), Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), specific learning disorders, and intellectual disorders (Gigliotti et al., 2024; Goswami, 2015; Patil & Kaple, 2023; Stein, 2019; Thye et al., 2018). These differences occur along the sensory processing pathway and are evident in early sensory processing stages. Neurodivergent people demonstrate differences in sensory gating, which is the process of filtering out redundant or unnecessary sensory stimuli from the environment (Schulz et al., 2023; Seif et al., 2026b Seif et al., , 2026a;; Steiner & Tseng, 2016). Autistic individuals, for example, demonstrate impaired habituation to auditory, tactile, and visual stimuli when compared to neurotypical individuals (Edwards et al., 2017; S. A. Green et al., 2019; Seif et al., 2026a), suggesting impairments in the early processing of these stimuli. Autistic people also demonstrate impairment in the suppression of neural responses to redundant sensory stimuli when compared to neurotypical people (LaGasse et al., 2019). Similar results are found in individuals with ADHD, who demonstrate reduced habituation and impaired neural suppression when compared to neurotypical individuals (Hutchison et al., 2013; Spring et al., 1974). Further, individuals with child-onset fluency disorder show differences in the suppression of neural responses but not in their habituation towards sensory stimuli (Özcan et al., 2009; Selman & Gregg, 2020). These findings demonstrate early sensory processing impairment in neurodiverse individuals that impact their abilities to filter sensory stimuli.

Neurodivergent people also demonstrate differences in processing unisensory stimuli across sensory modalities, such as in sensory sensitivity, which is defined as the ability to perceive a stimulus as either increased (hypersensitivity) or decreased (hyposensitivity) in intensity (Schulz & Stevenson, 2020 , 2022). Autistic individuals also demonstrate differences in auditory pure tone and complex pitch discrimination (Bhatara et al., 2013; Bonnel et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2009; Lepistö et al., 2005), patterns of hypersensitivity to visual (Ashwin et al., 2009; Bertone et al., 2005; Clery, Andersson, et al., 2013; Clery, Roux, et al., 2013; S. A. Green et al., 2013) and to tactile detection thresholds (Blakemore et al., 2006; Mikkelsen et al., 2018; Puts et al., 2014), and vast heterogeneity in their sensitivity to olfactory (Larsson et al., 2017) and gustatory stimuli (Bennetto et al., 2007; Boudjarane et al., 2017; Damiano et al., 2014; Loucks & Doty, 2004; Tavassoli & Baron-Cohen, 2012). People with ADHD often demonstrate hypersensitivity and hyposensitivity to various stimuli (Bijlenga et al., 2017; Bolhuis, 2024; P. P. P. Cheung & Siu, 2009), difficulties with processing tactile stimuli (Ghanizadeh, 2011), and increased visual sensitivity when compared to neurotypical individuals (Dellapiazza et al., 2021; Little et al., 2018; Panagiotidi et al., 2018). Further, children with learning disorders demonstrate increased incidence of sensory processing deficits when compared to neurotypical children but are less severe relative to individuals with other neurodevelopmental disorders (ASD, ADHD) (Armstrong-Gallegos & Nicolson, 2025; Catts et al., 2002 Catts et al., , 2005;; McArthur et al., 2000). Similar differences in neurodivergent people are found in their processing of multisensory stimuli, also known as multisensory integration (the ability to integrate sensory information from multiple modalities into a unified precept (Stevenson, Ghose, et al., 2014)).

Autistic individuals have demonstrated a decrease in their ability to integrate information across audiovisual, audio-tactile, visuo-haptic, and visual-vestibular interactions (Ainsworth et al., 2021; Bebko et al., 2014; Brandwein et al., 2013; Feldman et al., 2018; Ostrolenk et al., 2019; Russo et al., 2010; Segers et al., 2020; Stevenson et al., 2017; Stevenson, Ghose, et al., 2014; Stevenson, Siemann, Schneider, et al., 2014; Stevenson, Siemann, Woynaroski, et al., 2014; Woynaroski et al., 2013). Further, issues in multisensory integration in Autistic individuals have been demonstrated to covary with sensory responsiveness (Feldman et al., 2019) and to have cascading effects on cognitive processes that rely on sensory processing (Siemann et al., 2013; Stevenson et al., 2017 Stevenson et al., , 2018;; Wallace et al., 2020; Wallace & Stevenson, 2014). Similarly, individuals with ADHD have demonstrated differences in multisensory integration when compared to neurotypical individuals, with studies reporting people with ADHD show impaired multisensory integration of complex audiovisual input such as speech (Hare et al., 2026; Michalek et al., 2014). Finally, people with dyslexia (a specific type of learning disorder characterized by difficulties with reading and language) demonstrate difficulties with audiovisual integration, specifically with regards to speech perception, audio-tactile, and visuo-tactile stimuli (Laasonen et al., 2000; Stein, 2001; Tallal et al., 1993).

These various deficits in sensory processing seen in neurodiverse individuals demonstrate the impacts that atypical sensory development has on sensory processing. These deficits have cascading effects on higher-level cognitive functioning, given that impairments in sensory processing are linked to impairments in the brain's ability to execute motor, cognitive, language, and social-emotional tasks (Barmak & Baş, 2024; T. Brown et al., 2021; Hilton et al., 2007; Pastor-Cerezuela et al., 2020). In fact, neurodiverse individuals have impaired fine and gross motor, early reading and mathematics, theory of mind, emotion discrimination, and language skills (Armstrong-Gallegos & Nicolson, 2025; W. C. Cheung et al., 2021; Luyster et al., 2011).

Taken together, previous literature on neurodevelopmental disorders suggests that impaired early sensory development impacts sensory processing in individuals with ASD, ADHD, learning disorders, and childhood-onset fluency disorders. These impacts in sensory processing are related to deficits in higher-order cognitive functioning in individuals with these disorders.

Sensory processing challenges have been less well documented in Fetal Alcohol

Spectrum Disorder (FASD), a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by a range of physical, cognitive, behavioural, and emotional challenges stemming from prenatal alcohol exposure (J. Brown, 2018). Diagnosis of FASD is typically based on a specific pattern of birth defects following prenatal alcohol exposure including prenatal and/or postnatal growth retardation, facial dysmorphology, neurobehavioural disabilities, and central nervous system dysfunction (Denny et al., 2017), though different diagnostic codes have been developed over time (Astley & Clarren, 2000; Chudley et al., 2005; Stratton et al., 1996; more details provided in S1) The timing and amount of alcohol exposure contribute to variation in the symptoms seen in individuals with FASD.

Prenatal alcohol exposure has drastic impacts on early sensitive periods. Research from animal models of FASD indicates that prenatal alcohol exposure can disrupt cell migration and neural plate formation, resulting in structural brain malformations including in areas responsible for sensory processing (Alsakran & Kudoh, 2021; Clark B.J. et al., 2025). Therefore, just as with other neurodevelopmental disorders, individuals with FASD experience disruptions in the development of early sensory areas, that may impact their sensory processing. Individuals with FASD also demonstrate impaired fine motor skills, neurosensory hearing loss, poor tandem gait, and poor hand-eye coordination (Chudley et al., 2005). These impairments in higher-order cognitive, sensory, and motor processes suggest that there may also be deficits in lower-order sensory processing, just as has been demonstrated with individuals with ASD, ADHD, childhood onset fluency disorder, and learning disorders (Armstrong-Gallegos & Nicolson, 2025; W. C. Cheung et al., 2021; Luyster et al., 2011). Based on this, it is reasonable to believe that just as in other neurodevelopmental disorders, atypical sensitive periods and deficits in higher-order processes in FASD may be related to deficits in sensory processing. The purpose of this review, the first of its kind, is to investigate the state of the literature and provide an overview of which areas of sensory processing are impacted by FASD, and how that compares to neurotypical individuals.

2. Methods

This scoping review follows the methodology described by the JBI Guidance for Scoping Reviews (Peters et al., 2020) and the PRISMA guidelines for scoping reviews (Tricco et al., 2018). The protocol was drafted by X.X. and was revised by the research team members. This project was preregistered with the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/bvdp7/overview) on September 21, 2025, and the final protocol was registered prospectively on October 23, 2025.

2.1 Eligibility Criteria

Included studies were original, peer-reviewed research works that investigated sensory processing in human participants of any age with FASD (including fetal alcohol syndrome, partial fetal alcohol syndrome, alcohol related neurodevelopmental disorder, and prenatal exposure to alcohol/ethanol) compared to neurotypical participants (healthy controls with no reported neurodevelopmental disorders). Sensory processing was purposefully kept broad to provide a general overview of sensory processing differences in FASD. Any study investigating the perception of a stimulus (uinsensory or multisensory processing, sensory sensitivity or reactivity, sensory gating, and/or sensorimotor skills) was included. Any measure of sensory processing was included, including behavioural, neural, questionnaire, and qualitative studies.

Studies were excluded if they investigated differences in sensory organs without assessing the response to a sensory stimulus. Case studies, dissertations, reviews, conference abstracts, books, and book chapters were excluded. Studies were also excluded if they did not specify whether the control group was made up of neurotypical/typically developing individuals.

2.2 Information Sources and Search

The searches were conducted across five databases on October 23, 2025: APA Psycinfo, Embase, Medline, Scopus and CINAHL. A sample search strategy can be found in Table 1, with all search studies attached in S1. Search results were imported to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2025) to be independently screened. Appropriate subject headings for each database (not applicable for SCOPUS) and all keywords synonymous with "sensory processing", "sensory perception", "fetal alcohol spectrum disorder", "prenatal exposure to alcohol", and "prenatal exposure to ethanol" were included in the searches.

2.3 Selection of Sources of Evidence and Data Extraction

A total of 2085 studies were identified in the database search and uploaded to Covidence by X.X. 876 duplicates were identified by Covidence and another 17 duplicates were identified manually. This left 1192 studies for screening. Titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by X.X. and X.X. or X.X. for inclusion eligibility using Covidence. Discrepancies between the three reviewers were discussed and a decision was made based on whether each study investigated some aspect of sensory processing and had a typically developing control group.

Studies where this was not clear from the abstract were included in the full-text review.

Following the title and abstract review, 128 studies were included for full text review, which was completed by X.X., and X.X. or X.X. Following the full text review, 86 studies were excluded because they were not peer reviewed articles, did not include a typically-developing control group, or did not measure or compare sensory processing between groups. The final analysis included 42 studies. Details of the review process are summarized in Figure 1. The following data was extracted from full text: year of publication, author(s), study aims, study population and sample size, definition of FASD, comparison group, sensory processing definition and measure, study design and methodology, and main findings and conclusion. The data-charting form was developed by X.X., and she independently charted the data and updated the data-charting form.

3.1 General Sensory Processing Differences

3.1.1 Questionnaire Measures

The Sensory Profile is a commonly used measure of sensory processing behaviours in daily experiences (C. Brown & Dunn, 2002; Dunn, 2014). These behaviours are grouped into four quadrants: low registration (length/intensity of stimuli needed to respond), sensation seeking (level of interest and pleasure associated with sensory stimuli), sensory sensitivity (ability to notice sensory stimuli), and sensation avoiding (need to control amount/intensity of sensory stimuli). In preschool aged children with confirmed low-moderate prenatal alcohol exposure, no differences on any of the Infant/Toddler Sensory Profile quadrants exist when compared to neurotypical children (Bakhireva et al., 2018). However, children's scores on the Sensory Profile seem to differ depending on the amount and timing of the alcohol exposure, with children whose mothers initially binge drank and proceeded to drink at low levels throughout pregnancy demonstrating greater odds of sensation avoidance compared to children whose mothers did not drink during pregnancy (Halliday et al., 2017). Further, children with FASD demonstrated significantly greater sensory processing abnormalities compared to typically developing children, specifically in tactile, visual, and auditory sensitivity, under-responsiveness, and auditory filtering (Hansen & Jirikowic, 2013; T. Jirikowic et al., 2008; Wengel et al., 2011).

Another commonly used measure of general sensory processing is The Sensory Processing Measure (SPM), which is a parent questionnaire that examines a child's behavioural responses to touch, movement, visual, and auditory input (Parham et al., 2021). Children with FASD score significantly higher on the SPM compared to neurotypical children, suggesting more behaviours indicative of poor sensory processing (Hansen & Jirikowic, 2013; T. L. Jirikowic et al., 2013).

Taken together, these findings suggest that general sensory processing differences seem more pronounced in children with more severe prenatal alcohol exposure compared to children with low-moderate prenatal exposure of alcohol.

3.1.2 Brain Imaging Differences

Research suggests differences in sensory neural pathways between individuals with FASD and neurotypical individuals. During resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), children with FASD demonstrate reductions in connectivity between the medial prefrontal cortex and anterior supramarginal gyrus when compared to neurotypical children, suggesting potential disruptions in sensory-motor integration (Alsameen et al., 2025). Further differences were also found in the frontal parietal network, with altered connectivity between the posterior temporal fusiform cortex, temporal occipital fusiform cortex, and parahippocampal cortex, suggesting dysregulation in networks associated with visuomotor control. Findings using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) also suggest lower white matter integrity in the right lateral temporal lobe and bilateral splenium in adolescents and young adults with FASD compared to neurotypical controls, suggesting white matter deficits in brain areas associated with auditory, visual, and somatosensory processing in FASD (Pinner et al., 2020; Sowell et al., 2008).

Together, these findings suggest that individuals with FASD have dysregulated networks associated with sensory processing, specifically, motor and sensory integration networks, and white matter tracts associated with auditory, somatosensory, and visual processing.

3.2 Visual Processing

The most commonly investigated topic in this review was visual sensory processing (investigated in 26 studies). Adolescents and children with FASD were reported to demonstrate pronounced differences in visual processing compared to neurotypical adolescents and children.

Alcohol-exposed adolescents and children demonstrate lower accuracy rates of detecting basic visual stimuli (such as shapes or letters) compared to neurotypical adolescents and children, suggesting impaired responsivity (Coles et al., 2002; Mattson et al., 2006; Paolozza A. et al., 2013 Paolozza A. et al., , 2014 Paolozza A. et al., , 2014;; Paolozza et al., 2015) and contrast sensitivity (Vernescu et al., 2012) to visual stimuli. Children and adolescents with FASD also react and initiate eye movements to visual stimuli slower than neurotypical controls, suggesting slower processing of these visual stimuli (Coles et al., 2002; C. R. Green et al., 2007 C. R. Green et al., , 2009;; Mattson et al., 2006; Paolozza et al., 2015; Pervin et al., 2022), with some studies suggesting that male children exhibit greater variability in their reaction times compared to females. Taken together, these findings suggest that children with FASD may have lower visual sensitivity and reactivity compared to neurotypical children.

In magnetoencephalography (MEG) studies of visual processing, adolescents and young adults with FASD demonstrate delayed M100 amplitude and latency compared to neurotypical controls (Coffman et al., 2013; Pervin et al., 2022). The M100 component indicates early cortical processing of sensory input. Delayed M100 amplitude and latency in response to visual stimuli in these studies suggests less accurate and efficient processing of visual stimuli. These delays seem to be more pronounced in peripheral vision in individuals with FASD when compared to healthy controls. M100 latency is also delayed in adolescents with FASD compared to neurotypical adolescents according to MEG measures during saccadic eye movement tasks, suggesting delayed early visual responses in FASD compared to neurotypical adolescents (Stephen et al., 2013). Further, adolescents with FASD demonstrate significantly lower gammaband power in frontal, right parietal, and left temporal/occipital cortex in response to visual stimuli compared to neurotypical adolescents (Stephen et al., 2013). Reduced gamma power in these areas suggests impaired feature binding across stimulus parameters and perception of visual stimuli. Further, children with FASD demonstrate greater magnitude and lower speed of cardiac orienting responses to visual stimuli than neurotypical children, indicating impaired processing of visual stimuli (Kable et al., 2021).

Using fMRI, differences in visual networks have been observed between children with FASD and neurotypical children. DTI results indicate that children with prenatal alcohol exposure have lower fractional anisotropy and higher mean diffusivity in multiple white matter tracts related to visual processing compared to neurotypical children on eye movement tasks (Paolozza et al., 2017). This finding suggests slower and less efficient transmission of visual information between visual processing areas.

Interestingly, some studies do not report differences in reaction time or accuracy between children with FASD and neurotypical children in response to visual stimuli (Paolozza et al., 2016; Stephen et al., 2013). However, children with FASD demonstrate altered neural functioning during task performance when compared to neurotypical children even in the absence of behavioural differences, suggesting children with FASD may use increased gamma activity as a compensatory mechanism to maintain performance. (Stephen et al., 2013) These findings suggest that while behavioural responses to visual stimuli may not always differ significantly in FASD compared to neurotypicals, significant differences in neural activity still exist.

One study investigated visual processing using qualitative interviews. Children with FASD more frequently reported visual perception problems compared to neurotypical children, specifically in orientation, depth perception, and recognition (Ayoub et al., 2023). More research on visual sensory processing differences in adults in FASD are needed to confirm whether differences reported in childhood and adolescence persist in adulthood.

3.3 Auditory Processing

Auditory processing has also been commonly investigated in people with FASD, suggesting differences in auditory perception when compared to neurotypical people. Children and adolescents with FASD demonstrate slower reaction times in response to auditory stimuli when compared to neurotypical children, suggesting deficits in sound perception (Coles et al., 2002). Some studies suggest that while their reaction times to auditory stimuli do not differ from neurotypical children, children with FASD are less accurate at detecting auditory stimuli (Mattson et al., 2006).

Children and adolescents with FASD also demonstrate differences in the electrophysiological processing of auditory information in cortical pathways according to electrophysiological (EEG) measures., Children with FASD show differences in N1, N2, and P50 amplitudes compared to neurotypical children during auditory oddball paradigms. Delayed amplitudes in these components suggest weaker early filtering, encoding, and processing of early sensory input. Therefore, these findings suggest that early stages of auditory processing may be impacted by fetal alcohol exposure (Kabella et al., 2018; Stephen et al., 2012).

Evidence from MEG recordings during auditory stimuli presentation are somewhat mixed. Some studies indicate small, delayed latencies in M100 and M200 in children with FASD compared to neurotypical children (Stephen et al., 2012), while other studies suggest no group differences (Coffman et al., 2020; Kabella et al., 2018). Adolescents with FASD do however demonstrate decreased gamma oscillatory power in response to auditory stimuli compared to neurotypical controls (Bolaños et al., 2017). Decreased gamma oscillatory power is indicative of less neural synchrony in response to stimuli, suggesting impairments in the early sensory processing of auditory stimuli.

In neurophysiological paradigms, children with FASD demonstrate deficits in auditory processing when compared to typically developing controls; however, these differences were not statistically significant (Kable et al., 2021). Researchers note that deficits in visual processing were significant in the FASD group and suggest therefore that the visual modality may be more vulnerable to the impact of prenatal alcohol exposure than the auditory modality is. Additional research using such paradigms is needed to better compare the auditory processing differences between individuals with FASD and typically developing controls, and whether these differences extend across other modalities.

Adults with FASD display deficits in auditory attention when compared to neurotypical controls. They have been found to perform significantly lower on psychometric tests of auditory attention when compared to neurotypical control, and these deficits in auditory perception are stronger than deficits in visual perception (Connor et al., 1999). Interestingly, MEG studies report shorter latencies of P50 and N1 components over auditory cortex in adolescents and young adults with FASD compared to neurotypicals (Tesche et al., 2015). Shorter latencies of the P50 component suggest a slower ability to filter out irrelevant stimuli while shorter latencies of the N1 component suggest a decrease in the efficiency of early attention. Adolescents and young adults with FASD therefore demonstrate decreased rates of filtering out irrelevant auditory information and efficiently attending to relevant auditory information. Taken together, these findings suggest that while behavioural and neurophysiological deficits in auditory processing may persist in adulthood, additional research is needed with adult populations to confirm these findings.

3.3.1 Speech processing

Adolescents with prenatal alcohol exposure do not demonstrate differences in speech perception compared to unexposed adolescents (Simões et al., 2023). However, children with FASD demonstrate difficulties in perceiving and repeating words during dichotic listening tasks when compared to neurotypical controls. Dichotic listening paradigms assess hemispheric asymmetry for speech and language processing and presume that dichotic listening asymmetry relies on the functional status of the corpus callosum (Westerhausen & Kompus, 2018). Using this paradigm, typically developing participants with left-hemispheric dominance report the stimuli presented in their right-ear with higher probability than their left ear. In children with FASD, however, this pattern of perception to the auditory processing of words was not seen, suggesting reduced auditory lateralization in children with FASD (Domellöf et al., 2009).

Further, children with FASD also seem to perform worse on psychometric tasks of auditory word and non-word discrimination, suggesting potential deficits in auditory speech processing in FASD (Terband et al., 2018). These findings suggest that complex auditory perception may only be impacted in young age, though further research is needed to support these findings.

3.4 Olfactory Processing

Only one study investigated olfactory processing differences in people with FASD compared to neurotypical people. Using an odor identification task, children and adolescents with prenatal alcohol exposure were significantly worse at identifying odors when compared to neurotypical controls (Bower et al., 2013). Further study of differences in olfactory perception between people with FASD and neurotypical controls is needed to better understand these findings.

3.5 Somatosensory Processing

MEG recordings during somatosensory stimulus presentation indicate decreased gamma oscillatory power in adolescents with FASD compared to neurotypical controls, suggesting impairments in the efficiency of processing somatosensory stimuli (Bolaños et al., 2017).

Further, FASD adolescents and young adults demonstrate delayed M200 response latency to somatosensory stimuli when compared to neurotypical controls (Coffman et al., 2020); however, somatosensory M100 response latency do not differ between groups. These findings seem to suggest intact early somatosensory encoding, with potentially delayed higher-level cognitive processing. Interestingly FASD adolescents and young adults do demonstrate reduced amplitude of M100 and M200 responses; however, these seem to be limited to the left hemisphere only and may therefore not be indicative of overall peripheral deficits in FASD (Coffman et al., 2020)..

Taken together, these findings suggest potential deficits in processing somatosensory information in individuals with FASD. Future research is needed in this area to replicate these findings.

3.6 Multisensory Integration

In addition to differences in unisensory perception, differences in the perception of multisensory stimuli have also been investigated between individuals with FASD and neurotypicals. MEG studies have identified reduced gamma power in response to audio-tactile stimuli in FASD adolescents compared to neurotypical adolescents (Bolaños et al., 2017). These findings suggest impaired integration of audio-tactile stimuli, suggesting restricted abilities to process multisensory stimuli in daily life in adolescents with FASD. Interestingly, some research has found no significant differences in M100 response latency to audio-tactile stimuli when compared to neurotypical controls, suggesting no differences in multisensory integration between the two groups according to MEG results (Coffman et al., 2020). However, the paradigms used in these studies do not account for sensitivity differences in participants.

Considering participants' unisensory detection thresholds is important when assessing multisensory integration as it ensures that all stimuli are presented at perception matched levels for each participant, thus controlling for participants' unisensory accuracy of detecting the sensory stimuli used in studies. Thus, future research should investigate multisensory integration responses after first controlling for participants' sensitivity levels, to ensure any gains in multisensory integration are in fact as a result of the multiple stimuli presented.

3.7 Sensorimotor Processing

Individuals with FASD may also demonstrate differences in sensorimotor development, processing, and behaviour., Children and adolescents with FASD and prenatal alcohol exposure perform significantly lower than neurotypical children and adolescents on clinical and behavioural measures of sensorimotor outcomes, including on measures of visual-motor development, gait, balance, motor proficiency, and overall static and dynamic movement (T. Jirikowic et al., 2008; T. L. Jirikowic et al., 2013; Korkman et al., 1994; Lidstone, Miah, Poston, Beasley, & Dufek, 2020a; Williams et al., 2014).

3.7.1 Visual-motor integration

Visual-motor integration has been frequently examined in individuals with FASD, specifically with regards to their scores on the Beery-Buktenica Developmental Test of Visual-Motor Integration (Beery & Beery, 2016). However, evidence is inconsistent on whether FASD is associated with differences in visual-motor integration relative to typically developing participants. Significant group differences in visual-motor integration scores have been found in children with FASD when compared with neurotypical controls (Janzen, 1995; Sowell et al., 2008), though children and adolescents with FASD do not differ from neurotypical children in their motor response to dynamic and static visual stimuli (Lidstone, Miah, Poston, Beasley, & Dufek, 2020b; Lidstone, Miah, Poston, Beasley, Mostofsky, et al., 2020), in their response times to visual-motor stimuli (Simmons et al., 2006), or their use of visual feedback in drawing tasks (Simmons et al., 2023). These findings suggest behavioural impairments in visual-motor integration may not be as apparent in children with FASD when compared to neurotypical controls.

4. Discussion

While not considered a core diagnostic criterion, sensory processing differences have been reported in people with FASD. Thus, this review sought to determine what sensory processing differences exist between people with FASD and neurotypical controls. This review of 60 papers documents reported differences in unisensory perception of visual, auditory, olfactory, and somatosensory stimuli, multisensory integration, and sensorimotor processing.

Studies investigated these differences using various measures, ranging from questionnaire to neuroimaging studies.

Certain areas of sensory processing in FASD seem to have more pronounced deficits when compared to neurotypical individuals. Specifically, children and adolescents with FASD demonstrated deficits in somatosensory processing (Bolaños et al., 2017; Coffman et al., 2020), olfactory perception (Bower et al., 2013), It is important to note, however, that fewer studies investigated these areas of sensory processing than areas of sensory processing that demonstrated greater variability in results. Thus, future research in the processing of somatosensory, and olfactory stimuli and motion is needed to understand whether these findings replicate. People with FASD show greater variability in sensory processing of other sensory stimuli, including static visual, auditory, sensorimotor, and multisensory stimuli. Findings suggest that the degree to which people with FASD differ in their processing of these stimuli varies depending on the age of participants and the measures used to assess the processing. Levels of prenatal alcohol exposure and other participant factors may also contribute to the reported differences in sensory processing.

4.1 Age of participants

Results in sensory processing may vary depending on the age of participants.

Specifically, children and adolescents seemed to demonstrate differences in various areas of sensory processing; however, fewer studies investigated differences in sensory processing in adults. While young adults with FASD seem to have results consistent with children and adolescents (Coffman et al., 2020; Pinner et al., 2020; Sowell et al., 2008; Tesche et al., 2015), differences in sensory processing in older adults are less clear (Connor et al., 1999). Thus, age may play a crucial role in sensory processing differences in people with FASD, with potential evidence suggesting impairments in early sensory processing may be compensated for overtime.

This would be consistent with research in other neurodevelopmental disorders, which suggests that with time, people with neurodevelopmental disorders may catch-up to typically developing peers, resulting in fewer differences seen between groups (Foxe et al., 2015). However, additional research is needed to confirm these findings, perhaps employing longitudinal study designs to examine long-term effects of FASD on sensory processing.

4.2 Measures of sensory processing

Various measures were used to assess sensory processing differences in FASD, and results varied depending on the measures used. For example, despite a lack of differences in task accuracy and reaction time, children and adolescents with FASD still demonstrate differences in neural pathways that support sensory perception when compared to neurotypical children (Stephen et al., 2013). These findings are consistent with studies of behavioural and neural sensory differences in other neurodevelopmental disorders, where studies report differences in sensory processing based on neurophysiological and electrophysiological findings despite behavioural accuracy and reaction times remaining unaffected (Choi et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2025; Hames et al., 2016; Pierce et al., 2021). These results suggest that individuals with FASD may demonstrate altered neural pathways and responses, but that these differences may not always translate to behavioural differences when compared to neurotypical individuals.

However, even using similar measures, heterogeneity is seen in sensory processing in those with FASD. For example, greater variability in Sensory Profile scores is reported in people with FASD when compared to neurotypical controls, whereas scores on the Sensory Processing Measure suggest deficits in sensory processing in people with FASD when compared to controls.

Similarly, findings from behavioural studies are mixed depending on the paradigm used and whether low-or high-level sensory processing is being assessed. Finally, variations in results were found when using different types of electrophysiological techniques (EEG vs. MEG vs.

cardiac orienting responses). These findings are consistent with studies of sensory processing in other neurodevelopmental disorders, which have suggested that different measures may capture different areas of sensory processing, thus potentially resulting in differing findings across studies (DuBois et al., 2017; Schulz & Stevenson, 2020 , 2022). It is the case that different areas of sensory processing were studied in the following review, which may contribute to the different findings reported. Further, these differences may also speak to the vast heterogeneity of FASD symptoms, which may be captured differently based on the measures used. Future research may seek to investigate how FASD impacts specific areas of sensory processing, to better understand the impact of prenatal alcohol exposure on these areas.

4.3 Level of alcohol exposure

Results suggested that the level of prenatal alcohol exposure may contribute to differences in sensory processing. Some studies reported no differences between individuals with low-to-moderate levels of prenatal alcohol exposure when compared to neurotypical individuals (Bakhireva et al., 2018; Kable et al., 2021), suggesting that low levels of prenatal alcohol exposure may not result in impairments in sensory processing. Further, the amount and timing of prenatal alcohol exposure may impact sensory processing. Results indicated that binge drinking in early pregnancy with continued drinking at low levels throughout pregnancy is associated with sensory processing deficits compared to no prenatal exposure (Halliday et al., 2017). However, not all studies reported the amount and timing of the prenatal alcohol exposure. It is important to consider differences in the level of prenatal alcohol exposure given that some areas of sensory processing are more sensitive to the developmental impacts of alcohol exposure. As a result, areas such as olfaction and audition are impacted by very low levels of alcohol exposure due to use of and reliance on delicate cell receptors, likely contributing to distinct group differences in these areas (Imamura, 2024; Römer et al., 2020). Further, research suggests that greater amounts of prenatal alcohol exposure is associated with greater impairments in sensory processing, with some studiese suggesting these impacts are only present in the visual and auditory domain, but others suggesting these are more widespread processing differences (Flak et al., 2014; T. L. Jirikowic et al., 2020). Variability in FASD symptoms is also impacted by factors such as maternal and fetal genetics (Dodge et al., 2014; Fish et al., 2017), timing of exposure (Sulik, 2014), dosage and patterning of exposure (Bandoli et al., 2019; Feldman et al., 2019), and nutritional factors (Keen et al., 2010). Any of these factors may contribute to differences in fetal development that can impact sensory processing. Future research may seek to understand how these factors impact sensory processing differences between those with FASD and neurotypical controls, and how sensory processing differs within those diagnosed with FASD based on these factors.

4.4 Other participant factors

Not all studies provided information regarding the co-occurring diagnoses that participants had. Thus, while all individuals included in this review had FASD/prenatal alcohol exposure, some may have also had other co-occurring disorders that may have been contributing to their sensory processing deficits.

For example, FASD commonly co-occurs with other neurodevelopmental disorders, including ADHD (28.4%) and other intellectual and developmental disorders (19.4%) (Attell et al., 2025), which may be contributing to sensory processing differences on their own. Thus, in studies that did not specify whether children also had co-occurring disorders, it is hard to attribute the sensory processing differences found to FASD alone. Additionally, not all studies reported on whether mothers who had drunk alcohol during pregnancy also used additional substances. Some research has linked prenatal cocaine and marijuana usage to deficits in sensory and information processing (Bailey & Osborne, 2023; Chiriboga et al., 2009), which may also be attributing to differences in sensory processing reported in the studies in this review. Future research may be needed to investigate sensory processing differences that are unique to children with FASD. However, it is important to note that this may not be representative of the general FASD population, given the high prevalence rates of co-occurring psychopathologies in this population (Attell et al., 2025).

4.5 Limitations

There are a number of limitations to consider in the following study. The broadness of the review was purposeful to allow for large inclusion of sensory processing differences in FASD, however, made it difficult to draw conclusions on overarching differences in sensory processing in FASD given the vast differences in measures used to assess sensory processing. Further, given only studies with a neurotypical/typically developing control group were included, some studies of sensory processing in FASD were excluded which did not include a clear neurotypical or typically developing control group, as they did not allow for comparisons between groups. Thus, there may be additional research findings that may clarify some of the discrepancies between results reviewed in the present study that were not included in this review.

4.6 Conclusion

The following review investigated the sensory processing differences between people with FASD and those with typical development. Findings from 42, peer-reviewed studies indicate that FASD seems to be associated with differences in the unisensory perception of visual, auditory, olfactory, and somatosensory stimuli, multisensory processing, and sensorimotor processing when compared to typical development. It is important to note the vast heterogeneity in results across studies, which may be due to differences in ages of participants, measures of sensory processing used, level of prenatal alcohol exposure, and other individual participant factors. Future research should continue to investigate sensory processing differences in those with FASD, to confirm whether differences are consistent across individuals with FASD when compared to those with typical development, while taking into account participant comorbidities and other factors contributing to heterogeneity in FASD symptoms.
