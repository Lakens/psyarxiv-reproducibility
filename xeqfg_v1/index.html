Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Educational technologies (EdTech) are increasingly used to support neurodivergent learners in primary classrooms, yet their educational value remains difficult to establish across diverse learners, technologies, and contexts. We address this challenge through a scoping review of EdTech for neuro-inclusive primary classrooms, screening 3,340 records across six research databases and including 93 studies. The findings reveal an uneven evidence base, concentrated on autistic learners, individualised academic and cognitive support, and emerging technologies such as virtual reality, robotics, and artificial intelligence, while social, communicative, and peer-interaction outcomes received less attention. Design and evaluation processes were frequently researcher-led or unreported, with limited participation from children and other stakeholders. Intended goals and evaluated outcomes were often misaligned, and educational value was predominantly validated by researchers rather than by those using the technologies in classrooms. Building on these findings, we argue that educational value is constructed across three interconnected dimensions: conceptual (what counts as valuable), operational (how value is measured), and endorsed (whose judgements validate it). When these dimensions are disconnected, claims of educational value can become detached from the priorities and experiences of those the technologies are intended to serve. In response, we propose two complementary frameworks: one to guide future EdTech research and development, and another to help practitioners assess the educational value and relevance of EdTech for their classrooms.

1. Introduction

An estimated 10-20% of the school-age population is neurodivergent (Department of Education, 2026). Neurodivergence refers to variations in neurocognitive functioning, including cognition, perception, behaviour and sensory processing, that differ from dominant societal expectations (City & Guilds Foundation, 2025; Walker & Raymaker, 2021; Xia et al., 2024). It encompasses conditions such as autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), dyslexia and developmental language disorder (Botha et al., 2024; Hobson et al., 2024). The neurodiversity paradigm frames these differences as natural forms of human variation rather than deficits, locating difficulties partly in mismatches between children's needs and environments organised around normative assumptions about learning, communication and participation (Kapp et al., 2013; Pritchard-Rowe & Gibson, 2024). From this perspective, neuro-inclusive classrooms enable children of all neurotypes to learn and participate fully (Nowack et al., 2026).

However, primary classrooms remain largely organised around a "typical" learner (Davis, R., O'Neill, C., & Fletcher-Watson, S. (Eds.), 2025). Although teachers may support inclusion in principle, they often lack the resources to enact it in practice (Cook, 2024; Starks & Reich, 2023). This mismatch has consequences beyond the classroom, as early educational experiences shape later academic engagement, social participation and wellbeing (Costa et al., 2024; Javornik & Klemenčič Mirazchiyski, 2023; Salvo-Garrido et al., 2026). Consequently, unmet educational needs have been associated with school attendance difficulties and heightened distress among neurodivergent children (Fielding et al., 2025 (Fielding et al., , 2026)).

Recent advances in educational technologies (EdTech) have attracted growing research, practice, and policy attention for their potential to support neurodivergent learners (Abbasi et al., 2025; Axelsson et al., 2019). These technologies span a broad range of types, including virtual reality, robots, conversational agents, assistive technologies, and more recently, generative artificial intelligence (AI) (Kucirkova et al., 2026; Lorenzo et al., 2016; Palioura & Sapounidis, 2025; Yeung et al., 2026). Features such as personalised instruction, adaptive feedback, and multimodal engagement may be particularly suited to neuro-inclusive learning in classrooms (Barry et al., 2026; Borup et al., 2026; Radianti et al., 2020).

Research in this area, however, is highly heterogeneous and fragmented (Grynszpan et al., 2014). Studies differ considerably in the technologies and neurodevelopmental profiles they address, conceptions of inclusion, and approaches to design and classroom adoption (Göransson & Nilholm, 2014). Technologies also embed different educational goals, from supporting individual academic tasks, such as reading for children with dyslexia, to facilitating autistic children's participation in shared classroom activities (Bauminger-Zviely et al., 2013; Fage et al., 2019). Evaluation approaches are similarly diverse, ranging from questionnaires and pre-post tests of learning and motivation to behavioural and biometric measures (Debets et al., 2025; Lai et al., 2022; Lai & Bower, 2019). This diversity makes findings difficult to compare and obscures what educational value different technologies offer, for whom, and under what conditions (Kucirkova et al., 2025; Lai et al., 2022).

For practitioners, this fragmented evidence base makes it difficult to judge whether a technology suits a particular child or classroom, especially as the same technology may be experienced differently by different neurodivergent children (Livingstone & Pothong, 2025; Sheejamol et al., 2025). Rapid technological change, particularly the emergence of generative AI, further complicates decisions, requiring practitioners to weigh uncertain evidence against diverse learner needs, educational values and practical constraints (Kanders et al., 2024; Kucirkova et al., 2025).

Existing reviews have examined specific technologies, neurodevelopmental conditions, or skill domains, often across K-12 or clinical settings (Section 2.2), limiting their relevance for primary practitioners. To our knowledge, no previous review has synthesised evidence across technologies and neurodivergent learner profiles specifically for primary classrooms while examining how educational value is defined and evaluated. The literature therefore remains conceptually, methodologically and empirically fragmented, making it difficult to establish what is known, where evidence converges or diverges, and which questions remain unresolved.

To address these gaps, we conducted a scoping review of EdTech for neuro-inclusive primary classrooms. Rather than evaluating the effectiveness of individual technologies, we examine what counts as success, how it is established, and for whom. The review has two aims: (1) to map how EdTech is conceptualised, designed, adopted and evaluated in neuro-inclusive primary classrooms across learner profiles and educational contexts, identifying key gaps; and (2) to develop two complementary frameworks, one to guide future research and development and another to help practitioners assess EdTech's relevance and readiness for specific classroom contexts. Four research questions guide the review: RQ1. Which neurodivergent learner profiles, educational contexts, technology types and intended goals are represented in the literature on EdTech for neuro-inclusive primary classrooms?

RQ2. How is EdTech designed and developed according to the literature, and to what extent are neurodivergent children and other stakeholders involved?

RQ3.

How is EdTech adopted in primary classroom practice according to the literature?

RQ4.

How are the intended goals of EdTech operationalised and validated, and to what extent are neurodivergent children and other stakeholders reported to be involved in the evaluation?

2. Conceptual background and existing reviews

>Conceptual background and existing reviews

2.1 Children's rights and participation in EdTech

This review adopts a child-rights perspective, recognising children as rights-holders whose views should be heard in matters affecting them, including the design and evaluation of technologies (Livingstone & Third, 2017; United Nations, 1989; Wilson et al., 2025). These ethical principles are reflected in emerging policy frameworks (Livingstone & Pothong, 2025; UN Committee on the Rights of the Child., 2021; UNICEF Innocenti, 2025) and in Child-Computer Interaction (CCI) research, an established field within Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) (Alper et al., 2025; Kurian, 2025; Markopoulos, P. et al., 2008).

CCI has long emphasised children's participation in technology design (Giannakos et al., 2020; Qi & Yu, 2025; Read & Markopoulos, 2013). Druin's influential taxonomy distinguishes four roles: user, tester, informant and design partner (Druin, 1999; Fails et al., 2013). This framework underpins subsequent participatory design research and helps examine not only whether children participate, but also their roles and influence over design decisions (Bratteteig & Wagner, 2014; Iversen et al., 2017). The same perspective applies to evaluation, where children's involvement can shape how a technology's usefulness, effectiveness and suitability are assessed (Atabey et al., 2025; Bossen et al., 2016; Kucirkova et al., 2025; Parsons et al., 2020).

Examining participation across design and evaluation therefore reveals whose perspectives shape educational goals, how success is defined, and whether intended outcomes are realised. This is particularly important for neuro-inclusive EdTech, where deficit-oriented perspectives and adult-led decision-making have historically marginalised neurodivergent children's voices (Pritchard-Rowe & Gibson, 2024; Schmidt & Glaser, 2026; Spiel et al., 2019; Starks & Reich, 2023).

3. Methodology

We conducted a scoping review following (Peters et al., 2015). Its guidelines provide a standardised methodology for conducting scoping reviews, which is useful for mapping literature in new fields with emerging evidence.

3.1 Database and search strategy

The literature search was conducted across six major academic databases: ACM Digital Library, ERIC (Educational Resources Information Center), PsycINFO, IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers), Scopus, and WOS (Web of Science), selected for their complementary coverage of this interdisciplinary topic. The latest search was conducted on 9 August 2026 for studies written in English published from 2015 onwards.

3.2 Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were required to address all three elements of the

Joanna Briggs Institute's PCC (Population, Concept, Context) scoping review framework (Joanna Briggs Institute, 2015): Population: Neurodivergent primary school children, encompassing a range of developmental conditions and experiences, based on classifications outlined in the (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders: DSM-5, 2013) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) by (World HealthOrganization (WHO), 2019).

Concept: Educational technologies, in full-text, peer-reviewed journal articles or conference proceedings written in English and published from 2015.

Context: Educational technologies that are designed, adopted, or evaluated for primary school classroom settings, including home-schooling. Studies of technologies not clearly associated with classroom practice, or of technology broadly (e.g. ICT), were excluded.

Unpublished data or grey literature, reviews, protocols, and non-English articles were excluded. The full search strategy, alongside extended inclusion and exclusion descriptions, is outlined in the supplementary material (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

3.3 Study selection

The initial search identified 3,340 articles across the six databases. After removing 815 duplicates, 2,525 records underwent title and abstract screening. Although independent duplicate screening is not required for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2015), we incorporated it to enhance methodological rigour and assess consistency in study-selection decisions.

The first author screened the titles and abstracts of all 2,525 records, retaining 272 articles for full-text review. The first and second authors then independently assessed a random 10% sample of these, achieving 89.2% interrater agreement on inclusion decisions. Given this high agreement (McHugh, 2012), the first author screened the remaining full texts, flagging uncertainties for discussion with the second author. This process resulted in 179 exclusions, yielding a final corpus of 93.

Learner profiles were adapted from the DSM-5 criteria. 2) Design and Development: This dimension captured how technologies were designed, including their guiding principles and the involvement of intended users. 4. Results

The PRISMA diagram (Figure . 1) shows the flow of the records through to inclusion for this review. Of the 3,340 records identified, 272 reached full-text screening, and 93 met the eligibility requirements (see Supplementary Table S4 for a complete list of included records). Autism (ASD) was the most studied profile (n=37, 39.8%), followed by intellectual/cognitive disabilities (n=22, 23.7%), ADHD (n=16, 17.2%), and dyslexia (n=15, 16.1%). A further 14 (15.1%) articles focused on general learning disabilities/difficulties without specifying a particular profile. Language disorder/delay was represented in only six articles (6.5%), and other profiles, including dysgraphia, Down syndrome, developmental delay, and visual or auditory processing difficulties, appeared in one to three studies each (1.1-3.2%).

4.1.2 Educational contexts

Regarding the educational context, most studies took place in mainstream schools (n = 63, 67.7%), followed by Special Educational Needs (SEN) schools (n = 16, 17.2%) and homeschooling (n = 7, 7.5%). Seven studies (7.5%) did not report a setting. At classroom level, inclusive classes (n=36, 38.7%) and special classes (n=33, 35.5%) were similarly common. The most frequent school-classroom combination were inclusive classes in mainstream schools (n = 33, 35.5%), followed by special classes in mainstream schools (n = 20, 21.5%) and special classes in SEN schools (n = 13, 14.0%). Resource rooms, home settings and mixed special/inclusive classes were rare. Fifteen studies (16.1%) did not report classroom type (Figure . 3).

4.1.3 Technology types

Extended reality (XR: VR, AR, MR) was the most studied technology category (n = 26, 28.0%), followed by robotics and embodied technologies (n = 14, 15.1%), AI-driven systems (n = 12, 12.9%) and mobile/tablet applications (n = 10, 10.8%). Game-based learning and multimedia/content-based tools accounted for nine studies each (9.7%). The remaining 13 studies spanned five categories, each under 5%: platforms and digital environments, assistive and accessibility technologies, classroom interaction tools, sensor-based systems, and multimodal integrated systems (Figure 4).

4.1.4 Intended goals

Many technologies targeted multiple goals. Academic or domain-specific goals were the most common target, reported in over half of the studies (n=69, 53.9%), including efforts to deliver personalised instruction (e.g. (Alsolami, 2025)). Cognitive goals were the second most frequent (n=23, 18.0%), such as technologies designed to support listening skills and attention (e.g. (Erhan, 2016)). Socio-emotional goals were the third most frequent (n = 11, 8.6%), often paired with other aims, for example robots promoting self-esteem and motivation in collaborative learning (e.g. (Jimenez et al., 2017) ). Communicative and peer interaction goals were each reported in eight studies (n = 8, 6.2% each), for example supporting communication and participation (Wilson et al., 2018). Behavioural goals appeared in seven studies (n = 7, 5.5%), such as AI-powered wearable vests, providing feedback on hyperactive behaviour in classrooms (Qiu et al., 2026). Affective goals and teacher support appeared once each (n = 1, 0.8% each). These were enjoyment of physical activity enjoyment of physical activity (Koceska et al., 2026) and a system supporting special education teachers in interpreting autistic children's behaviour and communication with parents (Wu et al., 2026) (Figure 5).

4.1.5 Intended goals by technology and learner profile

Academic goals dominated in every technology category, especially Extended Reality (XR) (n = 23), AI-driven systems (n = 9) and mobile/tablet applications (n = 9). XR was rarely used for other outcomes: cognitive (n = 6), behavioural (n = 2), and socio-communicative and communicative (n = 1 each). Cognitive outcomes were most often linked to robotics and embodied technologies (n = 6). Socio-emotional and peer interaction goals were comparatively concentrated in robotics and game-based learning, whereas AI-driven systems were the only category linked to teacher support, and assistive/accessibility technologies were exclusively tied to academic/domain goals (Figure 6, left).

Across neurodivergent (ND) profiles, ASD and intellectual/cognitive disabilities had the widest range of outcomes. Interestingly, most technologies associated with socio-emotional, behavioural and communicative outcomes were targeted almost exclusively for autistic children. Dyslexia (n = 13) and dyscalculia (n = 9) were almost entirely linked to academic outcomes. Dysgraphia and visual and auditory processing challenges were confined to one or two goal types each. Overall, the cross-tabulation showed that the range of intended outcomes varied across neurodivergent profiles (Figure 6, right).

4.4.2 Evaluation measures

Questionnaires were the most widely used measure (n = 43, 46.2%), followed by observations (n = 36, 38.7%) and interviews (n = 30, 32.3%), with many studies triangulating multiple data sources within a single evaluation. 4.4.4 Evaluation roles by stakeholder group

Turning to which stakeholder groups occupied these roles in practice, the reported roles varied substantially across groups. Children took part in evaluation for most of the studies (n=83, 89%). They were most often positioned as subjects of measurement (n = 48, 52%) or respondents providing their own views via researcher-designed instruments (n = 35, 38%). No studies reported children as evaluation consultants or partners involved in shaping the evaluation process.

Teachers were as likely to be absent (n = 45, 48.4%) as to be respondents (n = 44, 47.3%). A few studies consulted teachers on evaluation design, for example to validate pre/post motivation and mathematics measures (J. Alghamdi & Alshahrani, 2025). One study made a teacher an evaluation partner across two design-based research cycles (Lan et al., 2018). Domain experts and parents were absent from most evaluations (n = 86, 92% and n = 82, 88%, respectively). Where involved, domain experts were mainly respondents (n = 6, 6%), with one consultant (Cascales-Martínez et al., 2016). Parents were respondents in 12% of studies (n = 11) but never as consultants or evaluation partners (Figure 15).

4.4.5 Stakeholder continuity from design to evaluation

Stakeholders who were users during design most often became evaluation subjects (n = 40, 41%) or respondents (n = 38, 39%). Only two studies (2%) reported a consultant role during evaluation. Design partner, one of the most influential design roles, showed a different pattern. Among reported design partners, 12 (52%) became respondents and 9 (39%) did not appear in the evaluation. These included domain experts and parents who co-designed a technology but were not involved in evaluating it (e.g. (Torrado et al., 2019; Wilson et al., 2025)). Only one stakeholder, a teacher (Lan et al., 2018) continued from design partner to evaluation partner.

5. Discussion

The results reveal substantial technological and methodological diversity but offer limited clarity about what constitutes meaningful educational value in neuro-inclusive educational technologies (EdTech) and how it should be validated. Here, we discuss the findings through two questions: a) what does the existing evidence show across each research question, including the research gaps and their implications for establishing evidence of educational value and b) what this implies for future research and practice. From these, we derive two complementary frameworks: one to guide future research and development (Section 5.2.5), and one to support practitioners in appraising EdTech for their classrooms (Section 6).

5.1 Answers to research questions and identified research gaps

5.1.1 Learner profiles, contexts, and intended goals (RQ1)

The evidence base provides an uneven representation of neurodevelopmental profiles, technologies, educational contexts, and intended goals. Research concentrated on particular learner profiles, especially ASD, and emerging technologies such as VR, robotics, and AI, while other learner profiles and technology types received comparatively less attention. Studies were similarly concentrated in mainstream and inclusive classrooms, with less evidence from SEN schools and home settings. Intended goals were predominantly academic and cognitive, with considerably less attention to socio-emotional, communicative, peerinteraction, and affective outcomes. These goal patterns also varied across learner profiles, with dyslexia and dyscalculia primarily associated with academic goals, while socioemotional goals were most explicitly associated with ASD learners (Section 4.1).

The evidence therefore provides uneven coverage of the diversity of neurodivergent learners, their educational needs, or the range of technologies that could support them. This pattern may reflect intersecting research priorities and assumptions across educational, technological, clinical and diagnostic domains (Kucirkova et al., 2025), although the underlying reasons for these concentrations cannot be established from the present evidence. This interpretation is consistent with earlier reviews that have similarly concentrated around particular populations, technologies, or educational goals. For example, robotics research on autism has emphasised social-emotional learning (Kewalramani et al., 2024) or technology-enhanced interventions for students with dyslexia (Skočić Mihić et al., 2022).

5.1.2 Design and stakeholder participation (RQ2)

Evidence of child-centred design in neuro-inclusive EdTech is limited. Design processes were frequently unreported or researcher-led, and participatory approaches involving teachers and children remained a minority. Although children were the primary users in most studies, they were identified as design partners in only one. Teachers, by contrast, were less often represented as users, yet occupied a broader range of design roles than children. The resulting technologies were predominantly built around multimodal, individualised interaction, with less attention to social, collaborative, and shared classroom experiences (Section 4.2).

Taken together, the design evidence in this field thus remains opaque and strongly adultdefined. This may reflect long-standing challenges in designing with children (Druin, 1999; Frauenberger et al., 2015) and the historical exclusion of neurodivergent learners from decisions concerning their educational experiences. The emphasis on sensory-rich, individualised technological interactions may likewise represent researchers' and educators' assumptions about what constitutes meaningful technological value for neurodivergent learners, rather than evidence grounded in neurodivergent children's experiences of neuroinclusive classrooms (Schmidt & Glaser, 2026; Spiel et al., 2019).

5.1.3 Classroom adoption (RQ3)

Classroom adoption of neuro-inclusive EdTech followed a relatively narrow model. Individual child-technology interaction dominated, with the whole-class and small-group adoption a small minority. Teachers mostly facilitated or scaffolded these individual interactions as needed, rarely using the technology themselves or integrating it into wider classroom activity (Section 4.3).

Neuro-inclusive EdTech therefore appears to function mainly as support for individual children within existing classroom practice, rather than as a means of changing collective practice. One possible explanation, which the review cannot directly establish, is that individualised adoption aligns more readily with conventional classroom models and standardised assessment, particularly where educational success is framed in individual academic terms. We do not argue that EdTech should necessarily be designed for whole-class use, but question how the current individualised technologies support children's participation in the wider classroom, including opportunities for shared activity, peer interaction, and classroom membership (Bauminger-Zviely et al., 2013; Fage et al., 2019).

5.1.4 Outcome operationalisation and evaluation (RQ4)

The findings reveal a mismatch between conceptual, operational, and endorsed educational value. Intended goals did not always correspond to the outcomes evaluated, and some outcomes not stated as intended goals became prominent in evaluation. The operational educational value may thus diverge from the conceptual one, introducing criteria that serve as indicators of value alongside, or in place of, the goals the technology was intended to achieve. The same asymmetry extends to the endorsement, that is, whose judgments are treated as legitimate in determining whether educational value has been achieved. Children took part in most evaluations, but largely as subjects of assessment or as respondents to researcher-defined dimensions; teachers typically responded to researcher-designed questionnaires, and domain experts and parents were mostly absent. Evaluation therefore remained largely researcher-led, with children and teachers having little influence over what counted as educational value (Section 4.4).

The evidence gap therefore concerns not only the quantity of evidence, but also what is conceptualised as value, how it is operationalised, and who endorses it as educational success. We interpret this as a potential epistemic limitation, and the evidence offers a narrow account of educational value because the perspectives and priorities shaping its definition and evaluation are themselves unevenly distributed. The divergence between conceptual and operational value may also reflect wider EdTech decision-making, in which procurement teams and policymakers weigh features such as usability alongside educational impact. When researchers predominantly authorise evaluation, the resulting evidence may privilege one perspective and provide a more limited account of how value is experienced by those who use the technology in practice. Our findings therefore extend existing concerns about the limited evidence base for EdTech by showing that the problem is not only a lack of evidence, but a lack of diverse perspectives in producing it (Kucirkova et al., 2025 (Kucirkova et al., , 2026;; Lai et al., 2022). More evidence built on researcher-defined outcomes may continue to reinforce those outcomes, leaving dimensions of value identified by children and teachers less visible.

5.1.5 Stakeholder continuity across the technology lifecycle

This review also examined whether stakeholder participation continued across the conceptual, operational, and endorsed dimensions of educational value. Stakeholders who helped define priorities during design were rarely reported as retaining similarly influential roles during evaluation (Section 4.5). This discontinuity of social participation compounds the concern about what educational value the evidence ultimately reflects. Stakeholders may help define priorities during design but play no part in deciding how the resulting value is endorsed, leaving little opportunity to check whether their priorities carry through to evaluation. As a result, the evidence used to establish educational value may disproportionately reflect researcher-defined priorities. Importantly, this does not mean that stakeholder participation must be identical at every stage. Rather, it suggests that stakeholder-defined priorities can be lost as technologies move from design to measurement and evaluation. This is particularly problematic given the heterogeneity of neurodivergent learners, who may experience and value different aspects of a technology, as may the teachers, schools, and families who support them (Sheejamol et al., 2025).

5.2 Conceptualising educational value: implications for research

Drawing the findings together from Section 5.1, the evidence of educational value of neuroinclusive EdTech reflects a narrow set of outcomes and voices. Conceptually, educational value was oriented towards individual, academic benefit: technologies were typically designed to adapt content or task demands to individual learners rather than to the classroom as a whole. Operationally, this value was measured mainly through academic, cognitive, behavioural, or perceptual outcomes. Finally, it was endorsed largely by researchers, with children and teachers rarely shaping what counted as success.

Here, conceptualisation refers to what counts as valuable, operationalisation to how value is translated into outcomes and measures, and endorsement to whose judgements determine whether value has been achieved. These dimensions are analytically distinct but interconnected and shape which forms of educational value becomes visible in the evidence.

5.2.1 Learner profiles and educational purpose

Research focuses disproportionately on autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and intellectual or cognitive disabilities, possibly reflecting historical research priorities and the prominence of these diagnostic categories in educational research. Their association with particular educational purposes raises the possibility that diagnoses, rather than needs that cut across diagnostic categories, shape how educational needs are conceptualised. This should be understood as a possibility suggested by the observed pattern rather than an established causal explanation. Future research should broaden representation, particularly of underrepresented profiles such as developmental language disorder and co-occurring conditions, and examine whether links between diagnoses and educational purposes reflect children's needs or prevailing assumptions. Needs-based approaches that prioritise children's experiences and support needs over diagnostic labels offer one way forward (Kapp et al., 2013; Pritchard-Rowe & Gibson, 2024).

5.2.2 Technology and design

Neuro-inclusive EdTech appears to be shaped primarily by researcher-led design and the affordances of emerging technologies, with limited evidence of development driven by needs identified by children and teachers. Research concentrates around VR, robotics, and AI, with multimodality, feedback, and personalisation among the most common design principles. This raises the question of whether design responds to identified educational needs or is also driven by technology research trends, particularly given the rarity of children as design partners. This is particularly consequential for neurodivergent children, who may experience the same technology differently depending on their sensory, attentional, communicative, and cognitive needs. Future research should prioritise participatory approaches that enable children and other stakeholders with diverse communication and participation profiles to shape both technology design and how educational value is defined (Iversen et al., 2017; Nowack et al., 2026).

5.2.3 Classroom use

The individualised orientation persists in classroom use and may position neuro-inclusion primarily as individual accommodation rather than classroom-level inclusion. Individual accommodation asks how technology can help a learner engage with an existing task. Classroom inclusion asks how technology reshapes classroom environment and practice to support diverse participation (Bauminger-Zviely et al., 2013; Fage et al., 2019). These two approaches are not mutually exclusive and neuro-inclusive EdTech may require both individual responsiveness and classroom responsiveness (Göransson & Nilholm, 2014). Future research should examine the relational consequences of individualised technology use within classroom dynamics, as well as small-group and whole-class models. Longitudinal studies in authentic classrooms would be particularly valuable to help bridge the gap between EdTech advancements and practical implementation in schools.

5.2.4 Evaluation

A key gap concerns the construction of educational value. Operational value often diverged from conceptual, and stakeholder influence was not consistently sustained across the conceptual, operational, and endorsed dimensions. Participation appeared to function as a discrete step rather than a sustained process through which stakeholders iteratively shape how a technology's value is defined, measured, and endorsed. The selection of outcomes, measures, and evaluators is consequential, as it shapes which forms of success become visible in the evidence and which forms of educational value remain unmeasured.

At a minimum, future research should align operational value with the conceptual. Studies should report explicitly how each intended goal was operationalised and measured, and justify any new outcomes introduced at evaluation. Beyond alignment, the field needs methods for situating these measures within stakeholders' priorities. Stakeholders hold different interests, goals, and definitions of value, which shape which outcomes are prioritised and which strategies are adopted. Building on this finding, one possible methodological direction is to adapt social multi-criteria evaluation for neuro-inclusive EdTech (Kucirkova et al., 2026; Livingstone & Pothong, 2025). Criteria developed with relevant stakeholders, children, families, teachers, and researchers, could complement technical with social and educational perspectives, so that measured performance aligns with experienced value. Standardised academic and behavioural measures remain valuable, but they should be complemented by, rather than replaced with, outcomes defined by learners and educators.

5.2.5 A framework for future research

Advancing the field requires moving beyond whether an EdTech is effective to how its educational value is conceptually and operationally defined, for which contexts, and under whose authority. Our first framework structures this shift across three levels (Figure 17).

At the conceptual level: future research should define neuro-inclusive EdTech for primary classrooms together with multiple stakeholders through participatory methods. It should bridge the divide between individual accommodation and collective inclusion and address cross-cutting learner needs beyond narrow diagnostic labels.

At the operational level: researchers should ensure that operational value of educational success align with the conceptual, so that value measured in classrooms reflects intended value.

At the endorsement level: children, teachers, and families should participate in and influence evaluation, so that endorsed educational value reflects the priorities of those affected by the technology.

Stakeholder participation should also be continuous across all three levels, because priorities articulated during design can be reinterpreted, narrowed, or lost when researchers alone translate them into measures and judge whether they have been met. Alongside such participatory approaches, broader evaluation infrastructure, such as common reporting standards and open repositories of outcome measures, would improve the transparency and comparability of evidence across studies. This would strengthen its usefulness for decisionmaking by schools, procurement teams, and policymakers.

5.3 Contributions to the literature

This review makes three main contributions to the literature on neuro-inclusive EdTech. First, rather than focusing on a single technology, diagnostic population, or educational outcome, it integrates learner profiles, design processes, classroom adoption, and evaluation within a single analytic framework for primary school classrooms, revealing patterns across the technology lifecycle that remain invisible in narrower reviews. Second, it proposes two complementary frameworks. The research framework (Section 5.2.5) conceptualises educational value in terms of its conceptualisation, operationalisation, and endorsement dimensions, demonstrates that these can diverge, and introduces stakeholder continuity as a dimension of analysis. The practitioner framework (Section 6) translates these dimensions into criteria for appraising technologies in practice. Third, the review extends existing concerns about limited empirical evidence in this field (Lai et al., 2022; Lai & Bower, 2019; Ronksley-Pavia et al., 2025) by identifying a potential epistemic gap: increasing the volume of evidence alone may not resolve whose perspectives and definitions of educational value that evidence represents. The contribution is therefore not simply to show that more research is needed, but that what counts as meaningful educational value in neuro-inclusive primary classrooms depends on what evidence is produced and whose priorities define and evaluate it.

6. Practical implications

We now turn to the implications of this framing of the educational value of neuro-inclusive EdTech for practice, in particular how it can help bridge the substantial gap between rapid advances in EdTech and its adoption in primary school classrooms (Kanders et al., 2024; Kucirkova et al., 2026).

To support practitioners, we provide two complementary resources. The first, our second conceptual framework, is a practitioner appraisal guide (Figure 18). It translates the research framework into questions practitioners can ask when selecting and adopting a technology, paired with the practical implications of each answer. It is accompanied by the https://neurotechcompass.github.io/ , a free interactive dashboard, with an optional AI chatbot, through which practitioners can access and interpret the studies included in this review. Both resources can be used together or independently, including beyond this review, to support evidenceinformed decision-making in education and related fields.

7. Strengths, limitations, and directions for future reviews

This review provides a broad map of the neuro-inclusive EdTech literature for primary school classrooms across learner profiles, technologies, design processes, classroom adoption, and evaluation (see Section 5.3 for its principal contributions).

Despite these contributions, as with any study, this review also has limitations. It was restricted to English-language studies from six databases, which may have excluded relevant work published elsewhere or in other languages. Restricting inclusion to peer-reviewed publications excluded grey literature, such as white papers and reports. The themes of interest were also broadly specified. This was deliberate, as one of our primary aims was to map the landscape of EdTech for neuro-inclusive primary school classrooms, which is the purpose of a scoping review. Future research could consider systematic reviews and, where sufficient methodological and outcome homogeneity exists, meta-analysis of specific technologyoutcome relationships.

8. Conclusion

This scoping review of 93 studies published between 2015 and 2026 reveals a narrow and fragmented understanding of educational value in EdTech for neuro-inclusive primary classrooms. Existing research focuses predominantly on autistic learners and individualised academic and cognitive support, while social, communicative, peer-interaction and teachersupport outcomes receive less attention. Design and evaluation processes remain largely researcher-led, the intended goals do not always align with those evaluated, and children's and educators' participation rarely extends from design to evaluation. Educational value thus depends not only on which outcomes are prioritised, but also on how success is evaluated and whose perspectives inform these judgements. To address these challenges, we propose two complementary conceptual frameworks: one to guide future research and development, and another to help practitioners assess the relevance and readiness of EdTech for specific classroom contexts. Together, the frameworks connect the development and evaluation of EdTech with the practical judgements required for their use in classrooms, and provide a basis for integrating children's and educators' perspectives more systematically. The contribution of this review thus extends beyond mapping the evidence on whether EdTech works by broadening the question to include what counts as educational value, how it is established, and for whom, offering a basis for more participatory, contextually grounded and educationally meaningful approaches to designing, adopting and evaluating EdTech for neuro-inclusive classrooms.
