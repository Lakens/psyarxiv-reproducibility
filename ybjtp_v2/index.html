Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Treatment manuals and protocols (TM/Ps) are increasingly used to standardise clinical music therapy interventions, yet they often neglect clients' motivational needs, which are essential for engagement and recovery. Therapists may adapt or deviate from established TM/Ps to accommodate individual preferences or motivational factors. This study examined music therapists' attitudes, practices, and reasons for deviation regarding TM/P use across diverse clinical settings. Method Qualified music therapists (N=93) from 23 countries completed an online survey, available in five languages, capturing demographic data, theoretical orientation, views on TM/Ps, and reasons for deviation. Descriptive statistics summarised the sample, while crosstabulations with χ² tests and ordinal logistic regression were used to examine relationships among variables.

Therapists held mixed views on TM/Ps, acknowledging their benefits for structure while expressing concerns about diminished personalisation, spontaneity, and motivation. Although many perceived rigid protocols as detrimental to engagement, these attitudes did not significantly predict TM/P usage. Usage varied by context (lower in educational settings) and theoretical approach (lower in anthroposophic and attachment-oriented, higher in integrative frameworks). Beliefs about TM/P importance, rather than experience or age, appeared as stronger determinants of use.

While exploratory and statistically modest, the findings highlight persistent tensions between standardisation and individualisation in music therapy, and that these concerns are shared by many practitioners. The results suggest a need for TM/Ps that support flexible client-centred practice, while retaining sufficient structure for clinical and research purposes.

Introduction

In clinical sciences, treatment manuals and protocols (TM/Ps) have been reported to be one of the most common ways interventions are disseminated to practising clinicians (Becker et al., 2013). Protocolised clinical work can offer various advantages, such as establishing standardised interventions, facilitating training for less experienced practitioners, and enabling the collection of empirical data for evidence-based practice (Morrison, 2017). Yet, despite these potential advantages, the adoption and perceived value of TM/Ps in practice can vary significantly across disciplines. Since their inception, treatment manuals have been surrounded by controversy, particularly in fields like psychotherapy and the creative arts therapies, where individualised, relational, and improvisational elements are central to the therapeutic process (Carroll & Nuro, 2002). In music therapy, these challenges are especially pronounced, as they arise not only from its highly individualised nature and reliance on a therapeutic relationship, but also from the significant difficulty of protocolising the musical aspect, which, in general terms, serves a similar function to words in verbal psychotherapy (Bunt & Stige, 2014).

The present study is informed by the authors' positioning as neuroscience-informed music therapists and/or researchers. In this context, neuroscience-informed music therapy refers to the integration of relevant knowledge about neural, cognitive, sensorimotor, affective, developmental, and social processes into the understanding, selection, adaptation, and evaluation of music therapy interventions. This perspective recognises that music therapy is a relational and context-sensitive practice and cannot be attributed to neurobiological processes alone (De L' Etoile & LaGasse, 2013; Moore & LaGasse, 2018; Magee & Stewart, 2015). It also includes neurological approaches to music therapy and rehabilitation, including Neurologic Music Therapy® (Thaut & Hömberg, 2025). The lead author's clinical practice is additionally informed by psychotherapeutic principles, in which the therapeutic relationship-grounded in empathy, attunement, and collaboration-is regarded as a central vehicle for therapeutic change. These perspectives inform how we understand music therapy: music is not only an active therapeutic medium, but its therapeutic potential emerges through the relationship and interaction between therapist and client.

It is precisely this particular musical aspect that characterises music therapy due to its deeply interactive nature. A core aspect of music therapy is the intersubjective musical interaction, in which music therapists shape and respond to clients' expressions to foster attunement and shared meaning, processes that directly support client motivation and engagement (Ansdell & Pavlicevic, 2004; Bruscia, 2014). Drawing on developmental psychology, musical mirroring and attunement in early pre-verbal communication provide a foundation for using music to build therapeutic relationships (Malloch and Trevarthen, 2009). Co-creation and improvisation further emphasise how meaning emerges from shared musical narratives, allowing for non-verbal expression and agency (Mossler et al., 2022). This interactive and often spontaneous nature distinguishes music therapy from (music-based) protocols used by other disciplines, in which musical play and spontaneity are not features of the intervention. Motivation appears to be a key concept in this balancing act. However, little is known about how music therapists decide whether to adhere to, adapt, or deviate from TM/Ps when client needs, engagement, or clinical circumstances change during practice. These decisions may be closely related to the motivation of both clients and therapists and raise important questions about the gap between the standardisation promoted by TM/Ps and the realities of sustaining motivation and engagement in clinical practice.

Discussion

The present study set out to clarify how music therapists view and use TM/Ps, particularly regarding deviation from these TM/Ps and their perceived impact on motivation in clinical practice. An international sample of music therapists was surveyed, and crosstabulation and regression analyses were conducted to examine how attitudes, therapeutic frameworks, experience, and demographic variables relate to TM/P use and perception. The findings suggest that protocol use may be shaped by therapists' theoretical orientations, work context, and attitudes, rather than by age or length of clinical experience.

The first hypothesis proposed that protocol use would vary according to therapist approach, experience, setting, age, and attitudes. This was only partially supported. Contrary to expectations, neither work experience nor age significantly predicted TM/P use, suggesting that TM/P adoption is not simply a matter of career stage or generational cohort. One possible explanation is that awareness of TM/Ps has grown across the profession, so both newer and more experienced therapists are exposed to TM/Ps and use them when they fit their work setting and training background. Clinical setting emerged as a significant negative predictor of TM/P use, but only for educational environments. A relatively high proportion of therapists working in educational settings reported less frequent TM/P use despite the structured nature of these contexts. This apparent paradox may be explained by institutional demands, such as the Individual Education Plan (IEP), which, especially in countries like the UK or the USA, may supplant all other protocols, even clinical ones. Music therapists in schools are indeed often required to work within a hybrid role that blends therapy with education and consultation (Robertson, 2000).

Regression analyses indicated that respondents' theoretical approach predicted TM/P use in some cases. Specifically, therapists endorsing anthroposophic and attachment-oriented approaches reported less frequent TM/P use, whereas those identifying with an integrative approach reported more frequent use. One interpretation is that anthroposophic music therapy relies on individualised, holistic care and favours 'diagnostic flexibility' over standardised procedures (Intveen & Edwards, 2013). Similarly, attachment-oriented therapists may prioritise the need for adaptive, relationship-based work with clients whose insecure attachments require flexibility and relational attunement (Slade, 2019). Perhaps unsurprisingly, therapists' attitudes towards TM/Ps themselves were most strongly related to self-reported frequency of use. Three specific attitudes, namely TM/Ps' perceived importance for practice, contribution to therapists' confidence, and concerns about ΤΜ/Ps hindering personalised approaches, were all significantly associated with increased usage frequency. Therapists who felt that TM/Ps impede their ability to personalise care reported less use in practice, whereas those who did not share this concern reported more regular protocol use. As might be expected, a clear majority who strongly agreed that TM/Ps are important for practice reported 'always' using them. In contrast, none of those who disagreed that TM/Ps are important reported 'always' using them. Heightened concerns over TM/Ps as barriers to individualised care were associated with decreased use, with most of those expressing this concern reporting infrequent TM/P use. Spontaneity and creativity also emerged as salient factors: therapists viewing TM/Ps as restrictive in these areas were considerably less likely to adopt them, underscoring the discipline's commitment to musical/clinical improvisation and creativity. This highlights the need for protocols designed to carefully balance structure with creative flexibility to gain therapist acceptance.

Importantly but also unsurprisingly, therapists indicated that protocol use should never take precedence over client needs. Across items, therapists consistently prioritised individualised care and clinical judgement, also amongst those with generally positive views of standardisation. Many also reported that, in their experience, adherence to TM/Ps does not on its own determine clinical outcomes. Rather than being viewed as directives to be followed rigidly, TM/Ps may offer a shared reference point for treatment planning, documenting intervention components, and communicating about treatment with other professionals when they are adapted to the client and clinical context. The observed associations between positive attitudes towards TM/Ps-for example, perceiving them as important for practice or confidence-enhancing-and more frequent TM/P use are consistent with this interpretation.

Regarding the second hypothesis, which focused on reasons for deviation from TM/Ps, the findings provide partial support for the proposed role of perceived restrictions on spontaneity and play. In the exploratory analyses, agreement that TM/Ps restrict spontaneity and play was associated with more frequent self-reported deviation. However, this was not the only attitude associated with deviation frequency. Positive associations were also observed for attitudes concerning the importance of TM/Ps for practice, the contribution to professional communication, and their perceived influence on therapy accessibility. More broadly, the most highly agreed with reasons for deviating (loss of interest, functional limitations, a client's wish to stop, and the emergence of alternative therapeutic options) suggest that deviation may often occur in response to immediate clinical circumstances and perceived clinical needs. The third hypothesis proposed that TM/Ps might conflict with personalised approaches, potentially dampening motivation for both clients and therapists. This notion was supported at the level of reported attitudes (many therapists believed that TM/Ps that do not sufficiently reflect client goals can negatively affect motivation), but these concerns did not translate into clear differences in reported TM/P use. This discrepancy suggests that motivational reservations alone may not be enough to drive non-use or rejection of TM/Ps. It is possible that therapists weigh perceived structural or pragmatic benefits (e.g., clarity, documentation, interdisciplinary communication) against motivational concerns and choose to use TM/Ps while adapting them in practice. In this study, we focused primarily on music therapists' perceptions of how TM/Ps may affect the maintenance of client motivation and engagement, and secondarily on therapists' own motivation to use TM/Ps. Future research could examine more explicitly whether and how motivational processes mediate associations between protocol characteristics and therapeutic outcomes.

Overall, our findings suggest that TM/Ps occupy an ambivalent but potentially productive position in music therapy practice. On one hand, many therapists in this sample associate TM/Ps with inflexibility and procedural burden, and a substantial proportion report using them rarely. On the other hand, respondents also recognise important advantages, particularly for research, communication with stakeholders, and providing a degree of structural support. TM/Ps appear to function best when they are adaptable scaffolds that support, rather than replace, clinical judgement, relational work, and musical spontaneity. For TM/Ps to be widely adopted, they will likely need to offer clear benefits (e.g. for interdisciplinary communication or documentation) while explicitly allowing room for improvisation, client preference, and therapist-led adaptation (Flückiger et al., 2018).

Limitations and recommendations for future research

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively modest (N = 93) and recruited via online networks, which may have introduced self-selection bias. It is unclear whether the sample appears skewed towards therapists who rarely or only occasionally use TM/Ps, or whether it actually represents the general population of therapists or not, which would limit the generalisability of the findings and may amplify particular views on standardisation and flexibility. Future studies should aim for larger and more diverse samples, perhaps specifically targeting therapists who regularly work with manualised interventions across different regions and professional settings.

Furthermore, although the survey was translated into five languages, most of the responses were in Dutch and English, with only a few responses in German, Greek, and Spanish. This imbalance precluded formal comparisons between language groups and may have obscured potential cultural or contextual differences in TM/P use and attitudes. Third, the survey instrument was newly developed and, although content-validated by experienced therapists and native speakers, it has not undergone formal psychometric validation. These design choices reflect concerns commonly reported in practice and our intention to include both positive and negative statements as a method to reduce response biases, but they may also constrain responses and reflect our own assumptions and biases about TM/Ps. We consider these issues important to add refinement in future survey development. Finally, an important limitation on interpreting the statistical outcomes is that the results were not corrected for multiple tests, and as the effects were modest in size, they would not have survived this correction. The choice to do this was based on the exploratory nature of the investigation, deeming it useful even with this caveat to report the patterns found in the data. However, it does mean these results should be interpreted cautiously, and replication in larger samples is needed. As such, while these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, rather than confirmatory, we believe that they will be valuable to future, adequately powered studies.

Future research could integrate qualitative methods to examine in more depth how therapists across different approaches and settings understand TM/Ps, how they make decisions to adhere or deviate in specific clinical situations, and how they negotiate motivation when working with more or less structured interventions. Mixed-methods designs could also help clarify how therapist attitudes, contextual factors, and specific features of TM/Ps interact to shape both motivation and clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

In this sample, music therapists tended to prioritise individualised work over strict adherence to TM/Ps, often modifying protocols to maintain musical spontaneity and engagement. At the same time, respondents noted that having some structure can support consistency and collaboration with other professionals when it is applied flexibly rather than rigidly. Overall, the findings suggest that TM/Ps are most helpful when they offer direction without overly narrowing therapists' choices in how they work with clients or attend to both client and therapist motivation.
