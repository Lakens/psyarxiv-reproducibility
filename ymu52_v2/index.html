Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Research into cognitive control has flourished over the last three decades across many areas of the psychological and neural sciences, but problems have recently emerged that raise foundational questions about what cognitive control is, how individual differences should be measured, and the validity of cognitive control as an explanation for variation in behavioral traits and clinical conditions. Here, we outline a novel perspective that is rooted in evidence accumulation models, formal mathematical models that explain how individuals make choices across many contexts. We review model-based work that distinguishes conflict-specific processes, which are selectively engaged to address goal-conflicting information, from task-general processes that facilitate goal-relevant responding irrespective of the presence of conflict. This review reveals an emerging consensus that task-general, but not conflict-specific, processes show validity as coherent individual difference dimensions. We then highlight the computationally-derived measure we call efficiency of evidence accumulation (EEA) that reflects the amplification of goal-relevant information across many tasks and contexts.

EEA shows strong validity as a trait-like individual difference dimension, explains individual differences in performance in cognitive control tasks, and shows clear relevance to behavioral traits and clinical conditions in which cognitive control, as traditionally defined, is thought to be impaired. These findings help address multiple theoretical, methodological, and empirical challenges in the study of control processes in cognition, indicate that characterizing processes that enhance goal-relevant responding across contexts is a promising way forward, and suggest important ways in which theories of control's role in behavioral outcomes could be revised.

Introduction

Cognitive control is a foundational construct in the psychological and neural sciences that is typically characterized as a person's ability to pursue goal-relevant behaviors in the face of habitual or immediately compelling alternatives (Cohen, 2017).

In a seminal account, Miller and Cohen proposed that cognitive control is realized through neural systems, primarily located in the prefrontal cortex, that represent goals and facilitate their achievement through the use of top-down biasing signals that selectively activate goal-relevant neural pathways (Miller, 2000; Miller & Cohen, 2001).

By influencing distributed neural systems involved in sensory perception, information integration, and motor responding, these biasing signals are thought to selectively amplify processing relevant to an individual's current goal over goal-irrelevant information (Figure 1). In the two decades since this model was proposed, clarifying the neural basis of cognitive control has become a central focus of contemporary neuroscience (Friedman & Robbins, 2022; Menon & D'Esposito, 2022a; Uddin, 2021).

Figure 1. Graphical representation of the account of cognitive control proposed by Miller and Cohen (2001), in which cognitive control selectively activates goal-relevant neural systems involved in processing sensory information and response sets. The first two examples reflect Miller & Cohen's classic description of the Stroop task. The stimulus evokes activation in sensory systems involved in processing information about word reading and color naming, but cognitive control's biasing function (represented here by the color blue) ensures that the sensory system and response sets most relevant to the current goal are activated to a greater degree, increasing the likelihood of a goal-relevant response. As detailed in the main text, we argue that cognitive control is also relevant in the third example, in which the stimulus does not evoke any conflicting sensory dimensions or response sets. In this case, cognitive control must still facilitate the selection of the goal-relevant response by selectively activating sensory systems involved in the processing of stimulus orientation and the appropriate response sets, shifting the larger cognitive system away from task-irrelevant processes.

In personality, clinical, and developmental psychology, cognitive control is widely conceptualized as a trait-like individual difference dimension reflecting a person's ability to control their cognition in the service of goals. It is hypothesized to develop over the course of childhood and adolescence (Somerville & Casey, 2010), and is thought to be reduced in psychiatric conditions such as Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) (Barkley, 1997) and substance use disorders (Bechara, 2005). Moreover, it is widely assumed that individual differences in cognitive control contribute to people's behavior in real-life contexts that demand "self-control", typically thought of as a person's ability to act in accordance with one's goals, especially long-term goals, for example avoiding unhealthy foods, pursuing long-term career goals, or saving money for a "rainy day" (Moffitt et al., 2011; Robinson et al., 2010; Wilkowski & Robinson, 2016).

In experimental psychology, cognitive control has typically been conceptualized more narrowly as a process that suppresses or inhibits responses that are activated by task stimuli but that conflict with task goals (MacLeod, 2007; Ridderinkhof et al., 2004).

As a result, cognitive control has been primarily studied experimentally through tasks that require people to suppress or disregard conflicting stimulus information in one or more experimental conditions, such as the Stroop (Stroop, 1935 (Stroop, , 1938) ) and flanker (Eriksen & Eriksen, 1974) tasks (Figure 2A). Stimuli from commonly used perceptual "conflict tasks" and an example of the n-back "conflict recognition" task. (A) All perceptual conflict tasks include "congruent" conditions in which the goal-relevant and goal-irrelevant stimulus features are aligned and "incongruent" conditions in which these features are in conflict with one another. In the Stroop task, participants respond based on the color of the word while disregarding irrelevant information about the meaning of the word. In the Flanker task, participants respond based on the direction of a central arrow while disregarding irrelevant information from the peripheral arrows. In the Simon task, participants respond based on the direction of the arrow while disregarding irrelevant information about the spatial location of the arrow. "Non-target" stimuli that have not been presented recently are easily rejected. "Lure" stimuli that were presented recently, but not the specified number of spaces back, are more difficult to reject because goal-irrelevant evidence arising from stimulus familiarity encourages participants to incorrectly accept these stimuli as targets.

The definition of cognitive control embedded in conflict paradigms, as a process involved in suppressing stimulus information that conflicts with task goals, allows for a high degree of terminological and experimental precision, which likely explains the popularity of these tasks in experimental psychology. Yet, there is also a tension between this narrow definition and the broader conceptualizations of cognitive control used in other subfields, as a process that amplifies goal-relevant information (e.g., Miller & Cohen, 2001) or as people's trait-like ability to engage in cognitive operations relevant to their goals (e.g., in clinical and developmental psychology). Processes that amplify goal-relevant information may theoretically operate even in the absence of goalconflicting stimuli (Figure 1), indicating that the narrow form of cognitive control probed in conflict tasks need not represent the only cognitive process that can be classified as a "control" process. Indeed, in other scientific fields, such as control systems engineering (Lamnabhi-Lagarrigue et al., 2017; Murray et al., 2003; Rivera et al., 2007), and in lay discourse, a "control" process is broadly understood to be a process that modulates the operations of other processes in the service of a goal. Hence, in this review, we focus on conflict tasks as the predominant paradigm used to experimentally investigate cognitive control, but also conceptualize "control" processes along the lines of this broader, commonly accepted definition as any process that modulates cognitive operations in the service of a goal. As will become clear, the tension between these narrow and broad conceptualizations of cognitive control has important implications for theory.

Although research into cognitive control has flourished over the last three decades, serious problems have recently come to light that call for a careful reassessment. The aim of this review is to characterize these problems and argue for a principled way forward. Section 1 reviews critical problems with conflict tasks, a dominant method used to experimentally investigate cognitive control. We argue that these problems raise foundational questions about what cognitive control is and how it should be measured.

Against the backdrop of these questions, the following sections chart a way forward. Conflict tasks are members of a broader class of forced-choice tasks, and evidence accumulation models (EAMs) have emerged as the leading computational models of the mechanisms engaged in forced-choice tasks. We identify two classes of processes operative in EAMs that are relevant to cognitive control: conflict-specific processes that are engaged by information that actively conflicts with goals, and taskgeneral processes that facilitate goal-congruent responding irrespective of the presence of conflict. Although theorists have mostly focused on conflict-specific processes, we make a systematic case for the importance of task-general processes to the construct of cognitive control. More specifically, we review a growing literature on efficiency of evidence accumulation (EEA), a parametric measure derived from EAMs that indexes the efficacy of task-general processes that facilitate the selective gathering of goalrelevant information over goal-irrelevant information when making choices. We show that, unlike parameters that index conflict-specific forms of control, EEA constitutes a reliable, trait-like individual difference dimension, explains a large portion of the variance in individuals' performance on task-based measures of cognitive control, and shows clear relevance to traits (e.g., self-reported self-control) and psychiatric conditions (e.g., ADHD and substance use) in which control has traditionally been thought to be impaired.

We argue that these findings provide at least two important insights for how cognitive control is understood. First, studying basic processes that enhance EEA across many contexts could reveal mechanisms of "cognitive control", in the sense that they enhance goal-relevant processes across tasks. Second, because EEA relates to many individual difference variables that have previously been associated with the construct of cognitive control, EEA is useful explanatory construct for revising theories in fields that have relied on the construct of cognitive control to understand individual differences in behavior. We conclude with a discussion of theoretical and methodological implications of these emerging insights and consider future directions for research.

1. Conflict Tasks, Subtraction Metrics, and the Selective

>Conflict Tasks, Subtraction Metrics, and the Selective

Engagement Assumption

Conflict-Based Measures of Cognitive Control Conflict tasks (Eriksen & Eriksen, 1974; Stroop, 1935 Stroop, , 1938) ) (Figure 2A) are the leading method for experimentally investigating cognitive control. The "incongruent" condition in conflict tasks involves presentation of irrelevant information that directly contradicts the task goal (e.g., an overlearned verbal cue in the Stroop; peripheral visual information in the Flanker), while in the "congruent" condition the irrelevant information is instead aligned with the goal-relevant response. In experimental psychology research, cognitive control is assumed to be selectively or preferentially engaged to resolve conflicting stimulus properties in the incongruent condition and to be minimally engaged in the congruent condition.

Another class of "conflict recognition" paradigms, including the frequently used nback task (Figure 2B), seek to illuminate cognitive control in the context of recognition memory. The goal in these paradigms is to evaluate whether memory-probe stimuli meet specific "target" criteria (e.g., in the 2-back task: is the current probe the same as that presented two trials back?). "Novel" probe stimuli that have not been presented recently are easily rejected as non-targets due to their unfamiliarity. In contrast, "lure" stimuli that were presented recently but do not meet criteria for being a target (e.g., a stimulus presented one trial back in the 2-back task) require individuals to resolve conflict between goal-relevant evidence that the stimulus fails to meet the target criterion and contradictory evidence from stimulus familiarity (Oberauer, 2005).

Rejection of lures is, therefore, thought to require selective or preferential engagement of cognitive control.

Task-switching paradigms, in which people are cued to switch from one set of stimulus/response mappings to another on a given trial, are also commonly used to study cognitive control (Monsell, 2003; Wylie & Allport, 2000). Although not typically considered "conflict" tasks, these paradigms similarly assume that cognitive control is selectively or preferentially engaged to resolve conflict between the obsolete task set and the new goal-relevant task set on "switch" trials and to only be minimally engaged on trials that allow the prior task set to be carried forward. Therefore, despite considerable methodological diversity in task-based measures of cognitive control, these tasks share the fundamental assumption that cognitive control is selectively engaged in situations in which stimulus features or mental processes (e.g., obsolete task sets, task-irrelevant peripheral visual information) that directly contradict task goals are highly influential and must be suppressed or ignored.

Subtraction Methodology: Rationale and Recent Critiques

Cognitive control is typically indexed in conflict tasks using subtraction methods that contrast behavioral performance metrics in conditions in which cognitive control is thought to be minimally engaged (e.g., congruent trials) versus strongly engaged (e.g., incongruent trials). Analogous measures of "switch costs" that compare performance on trials immediately following a task switch to performance on non-switch trials are similarly used to index the integrity of control in task-switching paradigms (Monsell, 2003). There is understandable appeal to these subtraction-based methods in that they appear to disentangle contributions of cognitive control from myriad other processes that might be engaged during task performance, thus providing a relatively "process pure" measure of an individuals' control ability (Draheim et al., 2019; Rouder et al., 2023).

In recent years, however, multiple lines of evidence have suggested that subtraction-based metrics face serious problems, especially in the context of individual differences research. First, large, multi-task studies show that conflict task subtraction metrics generally have poor test-retest reliability (Draheim et al., 2019; Enkavi et al., 2019; Hedge et al., 2018). Second, interrelations among subtraction metrics drawn from different conflict tasks that are similarly assumed to index cognitive control are relatively weak (Frischkorn et al., 2019; Karr et al., 2018). Third, well-powered analyses find that subtraction metrics of cognitive control display weak to negligible relationships with criterion measures that they should theoretically be related to, such as self-report indices of self-control (Eisenberg et al., 2019; Saunders et al., 2018).

Problems with subtraction metrics have been ascribed to the increase in measurement error that is common to all difference scores (Draheim et al., 2019). Yet, interrelations between subtraction metrics from different conflict tasks remain weak even when novel methods are used to enhance their reliability (Rey-Mermet et al., 2019 , 2025; Rouder et al., 2023; Rouder & Haaf, 2019). An alternative explanation for the failure of subtraction metrics is that the "selective engagement" assumption underpinning them applies only to task-specific types of control (e.g., focusing spatial attention in the flanker task; the suppression of familiarity evidence on the n-back). If there are, in addition, more general control mechanisms that are involved in selectively enhancing goal-relevant processes across tasks and conditions (which fall under the broader conceptualization of "control" outlined in the introduction) such mechanisms would not be measured by subtracting performance across incongruent and congruent conditions-subtraction would instead remove the signal of interest. But what could these more general mechanisms be, and how could they be disentangled from other processes? 2. Computational Models of Choice and Conflict-Specific Versus Task-General Processes Evidence Accumulation Models: A Brief Overview and Recent Developments Conflict tasks are instances of a broader class of tasks that involve speeded forced choices. In parallel to the growing literature on cognitive control, the last several decades have seen substantial advances in computational modeling frameworks for these tasks that posit formal mathematical explanations for how individuals make decisions, providing quantitative predictions about task performance that have been extensively supported (Busemeyer & Johnson, 2004; Heathcote & Matzke, 2022).

Although this field has generated a diverse set of approaches, we focus on evidence accumulation models (EAMs), which have become one of the most well-validated and widely applied frameworks for understanding a broad array of choice tasks across the cognitive and neural sciences (Gold & Shadlen, 2007; Heathcote & Matzke, 2022; Ratcliff et al., 2016; Shadlen & Kiani, 2013; Smith & Ratcliff, 2004). (Ratcliff et al., 2016) frames two-choice decisions as resulting from a noisy evidence gathering process in which a single relative evidence total drifts between two boundaries representing each possible choice, set at 0 and parameter a. The process starts at parameter z and a choice is made when it reaches one of the boundaries. The drift rate (v) parameter represents the average rate at which the decision process moves toward the boundary matching the correct/adaptive response. Ter accounts for "nondecision time" spent on peripheral perceptual and motor processes. (B) The RDM frames decisions as a race between noisy accumulators that represent each possible choice toward an upper boundary (b). Mean drift rates for the accumulators matching and mismatching the correct/adaptive response are set by vmatch and vmismatch and nondecision time is accounted for by t0. (C) The LBA (Brown & Heathcote, 2008) has a similar accumulator-based structure to the RDM but assumes that evidence gathering within a single trial is not noisy, and is instead linear and ballistic. Noise in accumulation rates is assumed to occur at the between-trial level (drift rates for individual trials are represented by the thin red and blue lines) and the LBA also includes a parameter (A) for between-trial noise in accumulator start points, which is also sometimes included in the other models.

EAMs assume that choices and response times are determined by a process in which people gradually gather noisy evidence from environmental stimuli for each possible choice until an evidence threshold for one choice is first reached. There are two general EAM architectures. Diffusion models (Figure 3A) assume that evidence for each choice is collapsed into a relative evidence total that drifts between two boundaries representing each of two response options until a choice is selected when the process terminates at one of the boundaries (Ratcliff et al., 2016). Racingaccumulator models (Figure 3B-C) assume that evidence for each choice is gathered by a separate processes (allowing easy extension to situations with greater than two choices) that race until one process reaches its evidence threshold (Brown & Heathcote, 2008; Heathcote & Matzke, 2022; Miletić et al., 2021). Parameters from these models show a high level of biological plausibility in neurophysiological research (Cassey et al., 2016; Gold & Shadlen, 2007; Shadlen & Kiani, 2013) and provide mechanistic interpretations of humans' behavioral performance.

Racing-accumulator models capture the dynamics of evidence accumulation with parameters that quantify the rate of accumulation for each choice (vmatch and vmismatch in Figure 3B-C). These parameters allow the quantity of evidence accumulation, indexed as the mean of rates for all accumulators, to be dissociated from quality of evidence accumulation, indexed by the difference between rates of accumulators matching the goal-relevant choice (vmatch) and accumulators for goal-irrelevant choices (vmismatch).

Higher quantity produces faster overall accumulation of evidence, regardless of its goal-relevance, whereas higher quality produces greater accumulation of goal-relevant evidence relative to goal-irrelevant evidence.

Diffusion models instead characterize evidence accumulation using a single parameter (v in Figure 3A) that indexes the average rate at which the decision process drifts toward the boundary for the goal-relevant response. This single parameter prima facie clearly maps to evidence quality in racing-accumulator models, given that a stronger rate of drift toward the goal-relevant response boundary requires the selective accumulation of goal-relevant over goal-irrelevant evidence. Several lines of empirical evidence also directly support the view that diffusion model drift-rate parameters primarily reflect evidence-accumulation quality rather than quantity (Donkin et al., 2011; Stevenson et al., 2024; Weigard et al., 2026), a point we return to below in Section 5.

We therefore use efficiency of evidence accumulation (EEA) as a general term that captures evidence quality in racing accumulator models and v in diffusion models, both of which functionally denote the efficiency with which goal-relevant evidence accumulates relative to goal-irrelevant evidence.

Recent years have seen the development of specialized "conflict EAMs" designed to provide mechanistic explanations of conflict tasks, and at least two conflict EAM frameworks have been proposed. Conflict diffusion models (Hübner et al., 2010; Ulrich et al., 2015; White et al., 2011) (Figure 4A) assume that the salient goal-irrelevant evidence presented in traditional conflict tasks is more influential early in the decision process, leading to an initial deflection of the relative evidence total toward the goalirrelevant choice boundary in incongruent conditions. The influence of goal-irrelevant evidence wanes until the goal-relevant evidence causes the total to bend back toward the goal-relevant choice boundary. This transient early influence of goal-irrelevant evidence explains empirical features of conflict task performance that traditional diffusion models cannot (e.g., very fast error response times) (White et al., 2011). In conflict accumulator models (Figure 4B), the early deflection of the evidence accumulation process toward the goal-irrelevant choice is accounted for by priming of the evidence total for the goal-irrelevant accumulator, essentially giving it a "head start" in the race (Molloy et al., 2026; Weigard, Huang-Pollock, Heathcote, et al., 2018; Weigard et al., 2019). broader category of conflict diffusion models (White et al., 2011) that assume goalirrelevant automatic processes primarily impact evidence accumulation early in a trial, causing an early deflection of the evidence accumulation process toward to incorrect (lower) response boundary. The observed drift rate in DMC is a combination of the timelimited influence of this automatic process, which is described by a gamma function, and the enduring influence of a controlled process that accumulates goal-relevant information towards the adaptive response boundary, which is described by a standard diffusion model v parameter. The shape of the combined evidence accumulation process leads to the characteristic pattern of error response times that are faster, on average, than correct response times in incongruent task conditions, as the early influence of the automatic process causes many errors to occur early in the trial. (E) The CLBA (Molloy et al., 2026; Weigard, Huang-Pollock, Heathcote, et al., 2018) is a variant of the LBA designed for conflict tasks that quantifies the early deflection toward the response favored by goal-irrelevant automatic information as a priming process. The priming process is implemented by allowing the favored response accumulator to have a reduced distance-to-boundary, giving it a "head start" in the race.

In sum, two classes of processes within the EAM framework stand out as being potentially relevant to understanding individual differences in performance on cognitive control tasks. First is a set of "conflict-specific" processes that suppress the early influence of goal-irrelevant evidence and that are reflected in the parameters that describe the early deflection of the decision process in conflict diffusion models and parameters that describe the automatic priming effect in conflict accumulator models.

Second are "task-general" processes that are operative across both the congruent and incongruent conditions in conflict tasks, and likely across many non-conflict tasks as well (a point we elaborate below). Several EAM parameters offer insights into the potential nature of these task-general processes. Threshold separation in diffusion models (a) and the average height of the threshold (b) in accumulator models both index an individual's level of caution in responding as higher values of each allow the decision maker to gather a greater amount of evidence before making a choice (Heathcote & Matzke, 2022; Hedge et al., 2019; Ratcliff et al., 2016). Nondecision time parameters (Ter / t0) are thought to quantify the time individuals spend on encoding perceptual information before the choice process begins and on carrying out motor responses, although the selectivity of this parameter's mapping to these processes has been challenged (Bompas et al., 2024). Finally, as described above, EEA parameters quantify how well an individual can selectively accumulate goal-relevant, as opposed to goal-irrelevant, information when making choices. In the following sections we examine the prospects of each of these conflict-specific and task-general processes to illuminate the determinants of performance on cognitive control tasks.

3. Selectively Engaged Conflict Suppression Mechanisms Fail to

Generalize Across Conflict Tasks Despite the theoretical and computational precision afforded by conflict EAMs, recent attempts to use them to search for selectively engaged cognitive control mechanisms that generalize across conflict tasks have come up empty handed. Hedge and colleagues (Hedge et al., 2022) fit a conflict diffusion model to multiple data sets and found that parameters indexing conflict-selective processing were moderately reliable, but nonetheless showed little evidence of correlations across conflict tasks. These findings were recently replicated (Molloy et al., 2026) in a racingaccumulator model framework using hierarchical Bayesian joint modeling to optimize measurement of model parameters and their covariance (cf. Rouder et al., 2023; Rouder & Haaf, 2019). This analysis showed that that both task-general parameters (EEA, response thresholds, nondecision times) and the parameters describing individuals' suppression of goal-irrelevant evidence (threshold priming) showed evidence of test-retest reliability, but that only the task-general parameters were credibly correlated across tasks (Molloy et al., 2026).

Another recent line of work identified parallel findings involving conflict recognition conditions in the n-back task (Figure 2B). Consistent with prior studies (Oberauer, 2005), analyses indicated that that individual differences in youths' n-back performance was best explained by a model in which individuals weigh a combination of goal-relevant evidence for whether a stimulus is a target, which improves performance on all trial types, and goal-irrelevant evidence of stimulus familiarity, which hinders performance on lure trials because evidence of lures' familiarity conflicts with the evidence that they fail to meet target criteria. This pattern was then described with a novel model of evidence integration in the diffusion model that explicitly dissociates individuals' goal-relevant EEA from their ability to suppress familiarity evidence, a conflict-specific mechanism (Weigard, Suzuki, et al., 2024). Parameters for EEA and familiarity suppression both showed evidence of test-retest reliability, but only the EEA parameter showed evidence of criterion validity in its relations with other task-based measures of cognitive control and with parent-and teacher-reports of youths' ADHD symptoms.

These findings across multiple task paradigms and modeling frameworks converge on a single conclusion: selectively engaged mechanisms that allow individuals to suppress goal-irrelevant evidence in conditions of conflicting information appear to be measurable in multiple conflict tasks and are somewhat stable across time, but the mechanisms measured in these models do not reflect a common construct across tasks.

This raises two possibilities. The first is that current conflict models are simply inadequate for capturing mechanisms that resolve conflict across diverse tasks. It is possible that future developments in modeling may provide better evidence for such mechanisms. The second possibility, which is bolstered by consistent findings that conflict-specific mechanisms show little cross-task validity even under conditions of reliable measurement (Hedge et al., 2022; Molloy et al., 2026; Weigard, Suzuki, et al., 2024), is that these mechanisms reflect real cognitive traits but these traits only have relevance within narrow and idiosyncratic task contexts (e.g., suppressing peripheral visual information in a flanker task or disregarding evidence of stimulus familiarity in an n-back). Although further work is necessary to litigate between these possibilities, the common upshot is that the hope of identifying a cognitive control mechanism involved in conflict suppression across many tasks appears unlikely to be realized in the near term. Numerous studies using a wide variety of cognitive task batteries have consistently found that EEA parameters form a strong trait-like general factor that drives performance across diverse cognitive tasks and conditions (Lerche et al., 2020; Lerche & Voss, 2017; Löffler et al., 2024; Schmiedek et al., 2007; Schmitz & Wilhelm, 2016; Schubert et al., 2016; Schubert & Frischkorn, 2020; Stevenson et al., 2024; Tomlinson et al., 2025; Weigard, Clark, et al., 2021; Weigard & Sripada, 2021). One particularly relevant example is a study that attempted to replicate the foundational results supporting the influential theory that individual differences in cognitive control involve three distinct sub-components of control (Miyake et al., 2000). Löffler and colleagues (2024) applied a diffusion model analysis to data from individuals who completed a battery of tasks that represented each of these hypothesized subcomponents (inhibition, task-switching, and working memory). Their diffusion model analysis indicated a complete lack of evidence for the existence of distinct control domains and instead provided strong evidence that a single task-general latent factor for EEA drove performance across the entire task battery. Similarly, Weigard, Clark, et al. (2021) conducted an EAM analysis of seven cognitive control tasks in a large sample (Eisenberg et al., 2019) to more explicitly compare models that assume conflict-specific processes to those that assume task-general EEA. This study replicated prior findings that subtraction metrics fail to form cohesive or reliable factors but found that a taskgeneral EEA factor, measured from these same tasks, explained a sizable portion of the variance in performance across all tasks. Critically, this general EEA factor had a similarly strong influence on behavior across the congruent and incongruent conditions of conflict tasks, as well as across the switch and non-switch trials of two task-switching paradigms. Therefore, EEA appears to be equally important for driving performance across conditions that both have, and have not, been traditionally assumed to engage cognitive control. Moreover, in contrast to subtraction metrics, this EEA factor exhibited good test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.78-0.81). Taken together, this growing body of work indicates that task-general EEA is a trait-like individual difference dimension that is critically important for driving performance across cognitive control tasks, as well as across other tasks and conditions not traditionally associated with cognitive control.

EEA is Consistently Related to Traits and Conditions with Which Cognitive Control Is Theoretically Linked

A growing array of studies document relationships between EEA and criterion variables that have been linked to cognitive control. As described above, cognitive control is closely theoretically connected with the construct of self-control (Cohen, 2017), thought of as the broader ability to act in accordance with one's goals in the face of competing cravings, emotions, and other such complex motivational states (see discussion in Section 7). Three recent studies have found that EEA, as opposed to traditional subtraction-based measures of cognitive control processes, is robustly positively related to self-report measures of self-control (Sripada & Weigard, 2021; Tomlinson et al., 2025; Weigard, Clark, et al., 2021).

Furthermore, there is now a large and growing literature demonstrating that EEA is lower in clinical conditions that have been theoretically linked with deficits in cognitive control and with real-life difficulties in self-control. Conditions with the most evidence for associations with EEA are detailed in Table 1. These conditions show a clear pattern when considered in the context of modern dimensional models of psychopathology, such as the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) (Achenbach, 2020; Kotov et al., 2022; Krueger et al., 2021; Ruggero et al., 2019). ADHD and substance use are both closely associated with the "externalizing" dimension of psychopathology, which is characterized by impulsive and disruptive behaviors and for which impaired self-control has been posited to be a core feature (Krueger et al., 1996 (Krueger et al., , 2021)). Recent work on sub-components of the externalizing spectrum has suggested that reduced EEA may be selectively related to the antagonistic, rather than disinhibited, subcomponent of externalizing (Hall et al., 2021). Psychosis and Bipolar disorder are both associated with the "thought disorder" dimension of psychopathology, characterized by symptoms of disorganized thinking, distortion of reality, and atypical perceptual experiences, which are theorized to be attributable, in part, to problems with cognitive control (Cohen et al., 1996; Lesh et al., 2011; Quraishi & Frangou, 2002; Walshaw et al., 2010).

It is worth noting that not all studies of these conditions find lower EEA. A study of an online (Mechanical Turk) sample reported paradoxical positive associations between psychotic symptoms and EEA (Scaramozzino et al., 2025) and another study of an online sample (Prolific) reported a null association between EEA and problematic gambling (Oxtoby et al., 2025), which is thought to fall under the externalizing spectrum.

Null associations of EEA with ADHD or ADHD symptoms have also been reported in online (Yusuf et al., 2025) and college student samples (Merkt et al., 2013; Shahar et al., 2016), although these samples may be higher-functioning and therefore may not reflect the full range of cognitive impairments in the ADHD population. Nonetheless a clear majority of studies in this area, as well as evidence from formal meta-analyses of ADHD (Huang-Pollock et al., 2012; Karalunas et al., 2014; Ziegler et al., 2016), suggest that EEA is consistently lower in clinical conditions associated with the externalizing and thought disorder dimensions of psychopathology.

Research is more mixed on EEA's relations with another major psychopathology dimension, internalizing, which is less associated with self-control problems and reflects symptoms of depression and anxiety (Achenbach, 2020; Kotov et al., 2022; Ruggero et al., 2019). Multiple studies have found reduced EEA in major depressive disorder (Dillon et al., 2024; Lawlor et al., 2020; Pitliya et al., 2022; Vallesi et al., 2015), but EEA was found to be unrelated to depression symptoms in a recent analysis of a large community sample of youth (Barry et al., 2026). Studies of individuals with anxiety symptoms have suggested null associations with EEA (White et al., 2016) or even suggested positive associations of EEA with trait anxiety (Liu et al., 2026) and with social anxiety disorder (Dillon et al., 2022). Similarly, a study of EEA in a community sample of youth found evidence of a significant positive association between EEA and a broadband measure of internalizing symptoms (Tomlinson et al., 2025). These discrepant findings could suggest that EEA has specific associations with the "distress" sub-component of the internalizing spectrum, for which major depressive disorder is representative, but null or even positive associations with the "fear" sub-component, which is specific to anxiety disorders (Kotov et al., 2022). It could also indicate that individuals with substantial depression symptoms, who are likely to show impaired cognition (Pan et al., 2019), have reduced EEA, but that individuals who show a general predisposition to internalizing but do experience significantly impairing symptoms of depression or other disorders, do not. Indeed, recent work using bifactor modeling of psychopathology in youth found that, after accounting for the general "p"-factor of psychopathology, which represents overall severity of symptoms and is negatively related to cognitive abilities, the internalizing dimension shows positively associations with cognitive abilities (Brislin et al., 2022). This suggests the intriguing possibility that better cognitive abilities, potentially mediated by better EEA, convey risk for anxiety and internalizing symptoms at the population level, but that lower cognitive abilities or EEA are more likely to be found in the subset of individuals with severe expressions of psychopathology symptoms and who meet formal criteria for depressive disorders.

In sum, EEA shows positive relations with self-reported self-control and generally shows robust negative relations with clinical conditions that have been closely associated with impairments in cognitive-and self-control (externalizing and thought disorders). EEA's relations with internalizing conditions, which are less closely associated with cognitive-and self-control, appear to be more nuanced, but observations of null, or even positive, links of EEA with anxiety symptoms provides evidence for discriminant validity. First, individual differences in response threshold parameters, which determine the amount of evidence necessary to select a response, form a latent factor that is thought to index an individual's general level of caution in responding. Although response threshold parameters have been theoretically linked to the construct of impulsivity (Hedge et al., 2019; Metin et al., 2013), studies of response threshold and nondecision time parameters' criterion associations suggest that neither task-general factor has a straightforward relation with the construct of cognitive control.

Neither the response threshold nor nondecision time factors showed evidence of associations with self-reported self-control in a well-powered sample of adults (Weigard, Clark, et al., 2021). Similarly, two studies by Hedge and colleagues found that neither individuals' overall response thresholds settings, nor their strategic adjustments of thresholds in response to task demands, were systematically related to self-reported impulsivity (Hedge et al., 2019 (Hedge et al., , 2020)). Associations of response threshold and nondecision time parameters with clinical conditions associated with cognitive-and selfcontrol difficulties appear to be nuanced. Although some studies report relations of lower threshold settings (i.e., lower caution) with cigarette smoking (Gao et al., 2022), cigarette and cannabis use (Weng et al., 2026), and gambling disorder symptoms (Oxtoby et al., 2025), threshold settings are higher in people with schizophrenia (Heathcote et al., 2015) and meta-analyses indicate a lack of clear evidence for any systematic threshold differences in ADHD (Karalunas et al., 2014; Ziegler et al., 2016), the clinical condition that has been studied most extensively with EAMs thus far.

However, the same body of work suggests that individuals with ADHD tend to exhibit shorter nondecision times (Karalunas et al., 2014; Ziegler et al., 2016), an effect that is smaller than that of group differences in EEA but is fairly consistent across studies.

As parameters in EAMs can often trade off with one another (Donzallaz et al., 2026; Lüken et al., 2025), such nondecision time findings may reflect trade-offs with EEA parameters. Perhaps more troublingly, the reverse possibility, that EEA findings in the clinical and individual differences literature may reflect trade-offs with poorly estimated nondecision time parameters, is also plausible. However, we note that several of the studies reviewed above that link EEA to self-control and relevant clinical disorders in large samples used the EZ diffusion model estimation method (Huang-Pollock et al., 2012; Sripada & Weigard, 2021; Weigard, Clark, et al., 2021), suggesting that EEA results are likely to be robust to the quality of nondecision time estimation. Hence, although we return the question of how other EAM parameters, and their potential trade-offs with EEA, may shed light on mechanisms of control in Section 7, EEA currently stands out as the taskgeneral parameter with the clearest and most robust empirical links to criterion measures traditionally associated with cognitive control.

5. The Common Interpretation of EEA as "Processing Speed" is Problematic

A core challenge faced by computational research programs in cognitive science is that model parameters often have ambiguous mappings to traditional cognitive constructs that originated outside of formal modeling. Many theorists understand EEA parameters, particularly v in diffusion models, as representing the legacy construct of "processing speed" (Hedge et al., 2022; Lerche et al., 2020; Löffler et al., 2024; Oberauer, 2024; Schubert & Frischkorn, 2020). Processing speed has traditionally been theorized to reflect the general speed with which individuals can process information across all cognitive operations and this theoretical construct has typically been defined empirically as the mean latency of response time (mean RT) on simple choice tasks (Fry & Hale, 1996 , 2000; Kail & Salthouse, 1994; Salthouse, 1996). For example, in the study reviewed above that established that the task-general EEA factor explains most of the variance in performance across diverse cognitive control tasks (Löffler et al., 2024), the authors drew the conclusion that these tasks measure "nothing more than speed of information uptake". We believe, however, that characterizing the v parameter, or EEA more broadly, as reflecting "processing speed" is likely to create more confusion than clarity.

As introduced in Section 2, although the diffusion model uses a single parameter (v) to capture evidence accumulation dynamics, racing-accumulator models have parameterizations that allow the average quantity of accumulated evidence to be dissociated from the quality of that evidence. These features of evidence accumulation can vary independently of one another (Figure 5A). For example, an individual who can process information very quickly, but who fails to base their decision on goal-relevant information, would make inaccurate responses. In a racing-accumulator model, this individual would have relatively large quantity of evidence accumulation, reflecting their ability to rapidly accumulate putative (i.e., both correct and misleading) evidence during a trial. However, the quality of their evidence accumulation would be low, reflecting their failure to base decisions on goal-relevant information. Of these two distinct features of evidence accumulation, quantity has the most straightforward conceptual link with the traditional notion of "processing speed", as it indexes the speed with which individuals can take up information regardless of the accuracy of that information. In contrast, evidence quality has little conceptual overlap with, and might vary completely independently of, an individual's "speed of information uptake". Further, simulations show that evidence quantity has a selective relationship with the most common traditional measure of "processing speed", mean choice RT, while evidence quality instead primarily affects choice accuracy (Weigard et al., 2026). In the clinical and individual differences studies reviewed above that utilize racing-accumulator models rather than diffusion models (Molloy et al., 2026; Stevenson et al., 2024; Weigard, Huang-Pollock, Brown, et al., 2018), EEA is reflected in measures of evidence quality rather than quantity, and EEA therefore has little conceptual relation to the speed with which information is processed. People responding on the basis of goal-relevant evidence would have high evidence quality, resulting in faster rates for the accumulator that matches the correct response on a given trial than the accumulator that mismatches the correct response. People who are ineffective at gathering goal-relevant evidence would have poor evidence quality, as indicated by similar rates across the matching and mismatching accumulators. People who accumulate information at a higher speed would have faster rates of both matching and mismatching accumulators, but the difference between the rates can vary independently of the average speed, as shown by the four quadrants in the figure.

Hence, evidence quantity (processing speed) is completely orthogonal to evidence quality (efficiency of evidence accumulation). Although diffusion models, which are far better represented in the literature reviewed above than racing-accumulator models, do not explicitly dissociate quantity versus quality of evidence accumulation, several lines of inquiry indicate that the v parameter is primarily a measure of evidence quality. First, early simulations using accumulator model parameterizations in which evidence quantity was held constant showed a close one-to-one relationship between v and EEA (Donkin et al., 2011).

Second, simulations shown in Figure 5B demonstrate that this relationship continues to hold when estimating diffusion model v parameters from data that are simulated with a racing-accumulator model (Brown & Heathcote, 2008) while randomly sampling values of both evidence quality and quantity from a uniform distribution between 0 and 4. The v estimates have a relatively weak association with quantity but a strong, systematic relationship with quality, even within this context of widely varying quantity. Finally, a recent empirical study sought to dissociate v's relations with evidence quantity versus quality in empirical data by fitting both diffusion and accumulator models to data from three tasks across three large, diverse data sets (Weigard et al., 2026). Empirical correlations between v and quality were consistently strong (r ~ 0.59-0.89) while correlations between v and quantity were systematically weaker (r ~ 0.14-0.35).

Further, evidence quality displayed clear relations with criterion measures of general cognitive ability and cognitive control task performance while quantity displayed no significant relations with any of these measures.

Taken together, these lines of evidence make a strong case against viewing the task-general EEA factor, as measured by v parameters from the diffusion model and evidence quality parameters from racing-accumulator models, as reflecting the traditional construct of "processing speed". Rather, this factor reflects how efficiently individuals, across diverse cognitive tasks, can selectively accumulate information relevant to task goals and avoid accumulating information irrelevant to task goals.

6. What Is EEA's Mechanistic Basis and Ontological Role in Science?

We Posit that Evidence Quality Parameters Reflect, in Part, Mechanisms that Amplify Goal-Relevant Processes Through Top-Down Selection If the conceptualization of the task-general EEA factor as "processing speed" is not warranted, what is the mechanistic basis for this factor? More specifically, what mechanistic processes could possibly underlie a task-general factor that drives evidence quality parameters by enhancing the selective accumulation of goal-relevant over goal-irrelevant evidence across many different cognitive tasks and domains? In our conceptualization, an individual's observed rate of goal-relevant evidence accumulation in a given task or condition (v in diffusion models, evidence quality in racing accumulator models) is the product of at least three factors.

The first factor consists of the set of stimulus-specific properties that determine the discriminability of choice options in that task or condition (Stafford & Gurney, 2004; Van Ravenzwaaij et al., 2020), with less discriminable options leading to lower goalrelevant evidence accumulation rates. We note that, as EAMs were initially conceived as a method of characterizing experimental effects rather than individual differences, this factor is equivalent to the original interpretation of evidence accumulation parameters (e.g., v as a property of the stimulus). Experimental manipulations of perceptual quality or other stimulus factors that impact task difficulty have welldocumented selective effects on evidence quality parameters (Brown & Heathcote, 2008; Dutilh et al., 2019; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Ratcliff & Smith, 2004).

However, EAMs have also proven to be a uniquely attractive framework for dissociating the influence of stimulus properties on performance from the influence of individual difference factors. As evidence quality parameters provide an effective summary measure of behavioral performance, they can be integrated with itemresponse theory, a psychometric framework that explicitly separates stimulus effects ("difficulty" parameters) from individual differences (Kang et al., 2022; Tuerlinckx & De Boeck, 2005; Van Der Maas et al., 2011). More recent methodological developments now allow for the explicit estimation of evidence quality parameters, effects of stimulus difficulty/discriminability on evidence quality, and latent factors that describe individual differences in both (Stevenson et al., 2024 (Stevenson et al., , 2025;; Strickland et al., 2026). These methods consistently identify latent factors for task-general EEA that drive evidence quality parameters across tasks and that can be measured separately from the effect of stimulus difficulty on evidence quality and from individual differences in these stimulus quality effects (Molloy et al., 2026; Stevenson et al., 2024). Hence, EAMs allow the effects of stimulus properties on observed evidence quality to be effectively dissociated from individual differences variance.

The second factor that impacts observed evidence quality parameters on a given task is individual differences in domain-specific abilities that are relevant to the task at hand. These domain-specific abilities may relate to the integrity of modular neural systems that are involved in specialized forms of information processing, for example the striate cortex for tasks that involve visual frequency or other low-level visual information (Tootell et al., 1988) or the fusiform face area for tasks that require the discrimination or memory of faces (Kanwisher & Yovel, 2006). Domain-specific individual difference factors in evidence quality parameters have been convincingly demonstrated by Lerche and colleagues in a latent factor modeling study (Lerche et al., 2020). These authors found that evidence quality parameters from a battery spanning three cognitive domains (verbal, numeric, figural) were described by both a general factor (the task-general EEA factor we have introduced) as well as domain-specific factors for each of the cognitive domains. The domain-specific factors were clearly psychometrically distinct from one another and showed dissociable relations with performance on more complex tasks from the different cognitive domains. Therefore, this work clearly supports the presence of domain-specific individual differences factors that influence evidence quality parameters and likely reflect the integrity of specialized neural systems underlying processing within a given cognitive domain.

The third factor that influences observed evidence quality parameters on a given task, separately from stimulus properties and domain-specific abilities, is the domaingeneral EEA factor that has been identified both in the Lerche et al. (2020) study and the larger body of work reviewed above. As outlined in the previous section, the interpretation of the domain-general component -which we call task-general EEA -as "processing speed" is problematic. Yet, following directly from the core assumptions of EAMs, it stands to reason that any process that contributes to task-general EEA must influence evidence quality parameters by enhancing the selective accumulation of goalrelevant, as opposed to goal-irrelevant, evidence across tasks. As has been recently highlighted by Oberauer (2024), plausible mechanisms that could enhance evidence accumulation, amongst others, include the selective activation of a task's specific stimulus-response mappings (i.e., task set), the attentional gating of goal-relevant perceptual information, and the selective retrieval of goal-relevant memory representations. Although the respective roles of these possible mechanisms, their relative importance for EEA, and their differential engagement across individual tasks and conditions are not currently clear, a key commonality across them is that they all involve the selective amplification of goal-relevant information.

Thus, we posit that individual differences in task-general EEA reflect, at least in part, the integrity of a set of mechanisms that are involved in the top-down selection of goal-relevant information or of goal-relevant cognitive and neural processes. We assume that these top-down selection mechanisms are domain-general, in that the processes they support (e.g., task-set activation, perceptual gating, activation of goalrelevant memory representations) would be broadly relevant to improving goal-directed behavior across diverse tasks and contexts, regardless of the specific cognitive domain involved. Even though the set of possible mechanisms that may be involved in top-down selection is not currently well-characterized, we believe that the goal of better clarifying these mechanisms offers a productive focus for the future scientific investigation of goal-directed behavior and has important implications for how the construct of cognitive control is understood.

EEA and the Construct of Cognitive Control Weigard, Clark, et al. (2021) and Weigard and Sripada (2021) have previously argued that EEA is relevant to the construct of cognitive control and may predict individual differences in the broader construct of self-control. This proposal has been met with some controversy, in part because it has been inferred that EEA was being proposed as a direct measure of, or replacement for, these traditional constructs (Oberauer, 2024). In this section, we clarify that, although we agree that task-general EEA should not be viewed as equivalent to the traditional construct of cognitive control (let alone self-control), findings on EEA have important and specific implications for scientific fields that have relied on this construct.

As a starting point, consider that the task-general ability indexed by EEA bears a striking connection with the foundational conceptualization of cognitive control referenced at the beginning of this article: the ability to use top-down bias signals to selectively amplify the processing of information relevant to an individual's current goals (Miller, 2000; Miller & Cohen, 2001) (Figure 1). If such a top-down process, or a set of multiple top-down processes, was successful at amplifying goal-relevant information, this amplification would necessarily be directly reflected in EEA estimates given that EEA is an explicit measure of the degree to which individuals selectively accumulate goal-relevant information. In experimental, developmental, and clinical research, cognitive control has been conceptualized as a trait-like cognitive individual difference dimension that explains individual differences in performance in conflict tasks and that contributes to an array of broader behavioral traits (e.g., self-control) and clinical conditions (e.g., ADHD, substance use). The evidence reviewed earlier shows that the abilities indexed by EEA do in fact form a trait-like individual difference dimension, that this dimension explains individual differences across diverse laboratory tasks, including those traditionally assumed to measure cognitive control, and that EEA shows clear connections with control-relevant behavioral traits and clinical conditions. Thus, EEA appears to fill several of the theoretical and functional roles closely associated with the legacy construct of cognitive control. Further, there are several additional connections between EEA and common conceptualizations of cognitive control.

Traditionally, cognitive control has been hypothesized to suppress or inhibit inappropriate automatic or habitual responses, thus leading to the widespread use of conflict tasks to study control. Interestingly, the processes indexed by EEA do, in fact, play a functional role in reducing the influence of automatic processes that conflict with task goals. This is most clearly brought out in conflict EAMs, in which two kinds of independent processes compete for influence on incongruent trials (Figure 4).

Processes underpinning automaticity exert early influences that favor the incorrect response, while processes underpinning EEA exert later-onset, sustained influences that favor the correct response. These influences combine additively, and since on incongruent trials they have opposite signs, EEA does in fact oppose automatic processes and reduces their influence on the eventual choice. Importantly, we do not claim that processes indexed by EEA play any specific role in inhibiting evidence driven by conflicting features. As we have emphasized throughout, the processes indexed by EEA are task-general, and they amplify goal-relevant information irrespective of the presence of conflicting automatic influences. Hence, our claim is entirely consistent with the assumption of current conflict EAMs that behavior on conflict tasks is determined by two distinct categories of processes (conflict-specific and task-general) that are conceptually independent of one another but have opposing influences on choices made on incongruent trials.

Another notable connection between EEA and cognitive control pertains to the neural correlates of EEA. Decades of theoretical and empirical research have localized cognitive control functions to brain regions in the prefrontal cortex and the broader frontoparietal network (Friedman & Robbins, 2022; Menon & D'Esposito, 2022b; Miller, 2000; Miller & Cohen, 2001). Experimental (M. Mulder et al., 2014) and individual differences (Brosnan et al., 2020; Weigard, Angstadt, et al., 2024) studies have similarly identified neural correlates of evidence accumulation processes within the frontoparietal network. Recent work in large neuroimaging samples demonstrates that the frontoparietal network's flexible changes in response to cognitive demands are strongly predictive of individual differences in EEA (Weigard, Angstadt, et al., 2024). This relationship was replicated across two diverse samples of youth and young adults. The association was also similarly strong regardless of whether EEA was obtained from a task typically assumed to measure cognitive control (2-back) or a task that has not traditionally been assumed to measure cognitive control (a "0-back", or simple recognition memory, task), underscoring the task-general nature of EEA and its neural correlates. Therefore, task-general EEA not only plays similar functional roles to the construct of cognitive control in explaining individual differences in behavior but also shows important associations with neural features that have long been associated with the construct.

To summarize, the task-general EEA factor has important links with foundational conceptualizations of cognitive control; hypothesized control processes that amplify goal-relevant information would be expected to be reflected in evidence quality parameters and task-general EEA, which largely drives individual variation in evidence quality parameters, relates to behavioral individual differences and brain features that have previously been linked to cognitive control. Despite these connections, we caution that viewing EEA as a measure of, or substitute for, the construct of cognitive control, is not appropriate for several reasons.

First, although EEA displays several conceptual links with the construct of cognitive control, its task-general nature is in direct opposition to popular conceptualizations of cognitive control as being selectively engaged in conditions of explicit conflict between relevant and irrelevant information, a disconnect we address in the next subsection. Second, although mechanisms involved in top-down selection of goal-relevant information or other "control" mechanisms that amplify goal-relevant processes would be expected to be reflected in EEA, alternative processes that have little to do with selection or control may also impact task-general EEA. Indeed, as EEA is almost certainly multidetermined (Weigard & Sripada, 2021), it is not at all safe to assume that EEA reflects any one specific process alone. Finally, the problems described above with the tendency to equate EEA with the legacy construct of "processing speed" show the perils of trying to map formal cognitive model parameters on to older psychological constructs that were derived from verbal theories (Weigard et al., 2026). Hence, attempting to equate, or replace, the traditional construct of cognitive control with the EEA factor is inappropriate and would likely lead to more confusion than clarity.

Nonetheless, we argue that the growing literature on task-general EEA has at least two broad implications for the study of cognitive control. First, the fact that a large portion of the variance in evidence quality parameters across diverse tasks appears to be explained by task-general EEA suggests there are likely mechanistic processes that are involved in selectively amplifying goal-relevant information across many tasks and cognitive domains. The study of such processes has the potential to reveal novel mechanisms of "control", in that such processes modulate the operations of other cognitive processes in service of individuals' goals.

The second implication is that, because EEA relates to many behavioral outcomes and neuroimaging variables that have previously been associated with the construct of cognitive control, EEA is useful explanatory construct for revising theories in fields that have relied on the construct of cognitive control to understand individual differences in behavior (e.g., clinical and personality psychology, psychiatric neuroscience). We outline below, at the end of Section 6, our proposal that EEA can operate as a valuable "fulcrum construct" linking the study of basic mechanisms that enhance goal-relevant processes across cognitive tasks to the study of individual differences in goal-directed behavior. But first, we address the important caveat that our view of task-general EEA shows a fundamental disconnect with the common assumption that, for a process to be considered a "cognitive control" process, it must be selectively engaged in conditions of conflict.

EEA and the Selective Engagement Assumption

We have contrasted the task-general processes indexed by EEA with the conflictselective processes that are also identifiable in EAMs and argued that the former are likely to be relevant to the construct of cognitive control while the latter are unlikely to represent a coherent cross-task factor or to be related to individual differences in goaldirected behavior. Yet this assertion displays a key contradiction with the way that cognitive control has been conceived by many theorists in the field. Cognitive control, has often (though not universally) been thought to be selectively or preferentially engaged by conflict (Oberauer, 2024). Task-general processes indexed by EEA, however, are instead active ubiquitously across a wide variety of tasks and conditions even beyond those that involve conflict (Löffler et al., 2024; Weigard, Clark, et al., 2021), a point we elaborate on in detail in Section 7. Thus, the processes indexed by EEA deviate in a key way from this widely adopted conception of cognitive control.

As discussed in the Introduction, the view that cognitive control must be selectively engaged in conditions of conflict is a core assumption of research on conflict tasks and is particularly influential in experimental psychology due to the terminological and experimental precision that this assumption affords. However, this narrow experimental definition of cognitive control is not widely shared across other areas in the behavioral and brain sciences, which describe control as a more general process that amplifies goal-relevant information (e.g., Miller & Cohen, 2001) or as individual differences in people's ability to engage in cognitive operations that are relevant to their goals (e.g., in personality or clinical psychology). In fact, in other scientific fields, such as control systems engineering (Lamnabhi-Lagarrigue et al., 2017; Murray et al., 2003; Rivera et al., 2007), and in lay discourse, a "control" process is broadly understood to be a process that modulates the operations of other processes in the service of a goal.

Hence, if some of the processes reflected in EEA facilitate the amplification of goalrelevant information across many tasks, such processes would be highly relevant to both this more general definition of "control" and to the foundational conceptualization of cognitive control articulated by Miller and Cohen (2001), even if it they are clearly not relevant to the narrow conceptualization of cognitive control adopted in current research on conflict tasks.

How might the field resolve these terminological ambiguities? Constructs posited by scientists frequently undergo revision and refinement over time as a better understanding of the target system emerges, and it is typical to allow some flexibility to the meanings of scientific terms as mechanistic knowledge grows, as has been discussed extensively in the philosophy of science (Kitcher, 1993). For example, Aristotle thought "stars" are holes in the firmament that encases the universe, allowing rays of light to enter. As scientific knowledge grew, astronomy did not reject the idea that there are stars, but it instead recognized that early Aristotelian claims about them turned out to be false. Similarly, if the task-general EEA factor reflects, in part, processes that selectively modulate other cognitive operations in a top-down manner to amplify goalrelevant information, we think it would be appropriate infer that "cognitive control" processes exist, but that the common assumption that such processes must be selectively engaged is false. The alternative is to conclude that, because experimental conflict task research has failed to identify selectively engaged cognitive control processes, "cognitive control" does not exist and should therefore be forever banished from the scientific lexicon. Although such a conclusion may maintain terminological precision within subfields of experimental psychology, we believe that any benefit would be overshadowed by the problematic effect it would have on broader scientific discourse by implying that "control" processes, defined as those that modulate other processes in top-down manner in the interest of goals, do not exist.

In the end, however, we believe that this disagreement is somewhat semantic.

The deeper underlying substantive point is that the task-general processes indexed by EEA play important theoretical and empirical roles: they amplify goal-relevant information, they form a trait-like individual difference dimension, they are related to traits and conditions of significant practical and clinical importance, they are underpinned by distinctive neural circuitry, and so on. These underlying points are unaffected by whether we consider the task-general processes indexed by EEA to be forms of "cognitive control" or not. Putting these semantic considerations aside, we argue in the following section that the study of EEA has great potential for establishing novel connections between research programs focused on the mechanistic processes underlying goal-directed cognition and broader research programs on individual differences in self-control and associated clinical conditions.

EEA Represents a Valuable "Fulcrum Construct" Linking Lower-Level Neurocognitive Mechanisms with Higher-Level Goal-Directed Behaviors

In order for the study of EEA to be beneficial to scientific research programs, the ontological status of EEA requires some clarification. One important question concerns whether EEA should be viewed as reflecting a mechanism that explains goal-directed behavior or a parameter (or a computational variable) that simply describes patterns in data but does not in itself have mechanistic meaning. A related question is whether EEA is a unitary "thing" or is a composite of many things. Our answers to both questions are nuanced: it depends on the level of analysis at which a given theorist or research program is working.

To illustrate how a scientific construct can simultaneously operate as a descriptive parameter and an explanatory mechanism at different levels of analysis, we first consider an illustrative example from a different area of science: cold fronts, a construct studied in atmospheric physics and meteorological forecasting. Empirically, a cold front is defined as the leading edge of a mass of cool and dense air that advances to replace a warmer air mass. Cold fronts are formed by the combination of multiple interacting causal factors, including wind patterns in the upper atmosphere, differences in solar heating of air at different latitudes, low pressure systems that sweep cooler air southward, and the tendency of cold dense air to slide underneath warm air. In this way, an atmospheric physicist studying the lower-order physical mechanisms that govern shifts in air masses would view the cold front as a descriptive construct; it is a description of a higher-level phenomenon that reflects the aggregate effect of interactions between lower-level physical mechanisms and it is impossible to attribute a given cold front to any one of these specific mechanisms alone. However, for a scientist focused on meteorological forecasting, a research area that operates at a higher level of analysis than basic atmospheric physics, the cold front has proven to be an extremely valuable explanatory construct. Cold fronts can be easily identified and modeled and they provide clear, causal explanations for why weather patterns (e.g., rain, thunderstorms) occur at specific places and times. We posit that EEA similarly operates as a descriptive construct for researchers working at the lower level of understanding basic neurocognitive processes that support the accumulation of goal-relevant information but as an explanatory construct for researchers who seek to provide causal explanations for individual differences in clinical and behavioral outcomes.

Figure 6 illustrates, in greater detail, the roles that EEA plays in research programs that operate at different levels of analysis. Although EEA, as a measure of the shared variance in EAM parameter estimates across diverse tasks, indexes a relatively unitary high-level process ("enhancement of goal-relevant evidence relative to goalirrelevant evidence"), we expect that its underlying determinants are not unitary. As detailed above and in other work (Oberauer, 2024; Weigard & Sripada, 2021), EEA is very likely to be supported by multiple lower-level mechanisms involved in the top-down selection of goal-relevant perceptual information, for example, activating taskappropriate task sets, retrieving task-appropriate memory representations, and other processes necessary for adaptive task performance. Research programs that operate at the level of understanding how basic neurocognitive processes support goal-directed cognition (bottom box in Figure 6) would be concerned with characterizing and dissociating these lower-level mechanisms that contribute to EEA, but it would not consider EEA to be a mechanism in itself. Rather, these research programs would consider EEA to be a higher-level descriptive construct in the same way that atmospheric physicists studying lower-order physical mechanisms would consider a cold front a descriptive construct.

We turn now to research programs that focus on gaining a higher-level understanding of individual difference factors that contribute to successful long-term goal pursuit and related life outcomes (top box in Figure 6). Here, we believe that it is indeed appropriate to view EEA, or at least the high-level, multidetermined process that it indexes, as both a unitary mechanism and as an explanatory construct that can, in the same way that cold fronts provide causal explanations for weather patterns, explain these behavioral outcomes. Since EEA is a well-characterized summary measure of how well an individual is able to base their choices on information that is relevant to their current goals, it provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for why a given individual may be more or less consistent in their ability to make choices that serve their goals in real-world situations.

In this way, we argue that EEA serves as a valuable fulcrum construct that can be used to bridge the gap between basic research programs focused on the lower-level mechanisms of cognitive task performance and higher-level research programs focused on understanding differences in behavioral outcomes across development, across individuals, and across clinical disorders. EAMs' clear mathematical specification and well-characterized relations with neurophysiological processes (Gold & Shadlen, 2007; Heathcote & Matzke, 2022; Ratcliff et al., 2016; Shadlen & Kiani, 2013; Smith & Ratcliff, 2004) can likely be leveraged to provide greater mechanistic clarity about the top-down selection processes that support individuals' EEA. In turn, EEA's parsimonious definition and excellent psychometric properties would allow these lower-level mechanistic processes to be linked to substantive differences in real-world human behavior and clinical conditions. Hence, although the precise mechanisms that support task-general EEA have yet to be clarified, EEA nonetheless provides the field with a promising framework for linking basic neurocognitive mechanisms to real-world manifestations of goal-directed behaviors.

7. Key Implications and Future Directions

>Key Implications and Future Directions

Summary of Our Thesis and its Implications

In the preceding sections, we have presented evidence from a growing body of EAM research that addresses several of the key theoretical and empirical puzzles in the study of cognitive control. This work distinguishes two distinct categories of processes that are potentially relevant to the construct of cognitive control: 1) conflict-specific processes, which suppress information that directly conflicts with task goals, and 2) task-general processes that facilitate the selective accumulation of goal-relevant evidence irrespective of the presence of conflict and that would be expected to be reflected in the task-general EEA factor. We have detailed evidence that conflict-specific processes appear to be idiosyncratic to specific conflict tasks, do not form a cohesive individual difference dimension, and appear to be at best weakly related to behavioral traits and clinical conditions in which control is thought to be impaired. These lines of evidence all converge on the conclusion that the field's conceptualization of cognitive control as a unitary trait that is selectively or preferentially engaged in conditions of explicit conflict is not supported by evidence and is unlikely to be a useful direction for understanding goal-directed behavior unless these critical problems are resolved.

In contrast, we highlight the burgeoning literature on the task-general EEA factor as being relevant to construct of cognitive control. We assembled evidence that EEA is a unitary trait-like construct that drives evidence quality parameters across diverse cognitive tasks irrespective of their conflict resolution demands and that it shows robust relations with behavioral traits and clinical conditions in which control is thought to be impaired. We provide evidence that EEA should not be understood as "processing speed" and instead represents an individual's general ability to accumulate goalrelevant, as opposed to goal-irrelevant, information across tasks. As cognitive control processes have traditionally been conceptualized as processes that selectively amplify goal-relevant information, it follows that, if such processes exist, they will impact evidence quality parameters. If processes that selectively amplify goal-relevant information were to operate across diverse tasks, their integrity should, therefore, be reflected in the general EEA factor. Hence, we posit that an individual's EEA likely reflects, at least in part, the integrity of underlying mechanistic processes that selectively amplify goal-relevant information and that may be thought of as forms of cognitive control if they are better characterized in future work. Finally, we proposed that EEA represents a useful fulcrum construct for linking basic research programs focused on the mechanistic neurocognitive processes that facilitate goal-directed cognition to higher-level research programs focused on individual differences in self-control and the etiology of clinical conditions in which control is thought to be impaired (e.g., ADHD and substance use).

Beyond highlighting the promise of EEA as a valuable fulcrum construct for the field, our argument has several other key implications for the study of cognitive control.

We now detail these implications and identify critical directions for future work in this area, below.

The Need for Top-Down Selection Is Ubiquitous, Urging a Rethink of How to Study Processes Relevant to Successful Goal Pursuit

The dominant view in cognitive science has been that processes involved in goal-directed behavior must be selectively or preferentially engaged in situations in which stimuli or mental processes that directly conflict with one's goals are active and must therefore be overcome (e.g., incongruent conditions of conflict tasks). As detailed above, this "incongruent selectivity" assumption is reflected in the subtraction methodology widely used to analyze conflict tasks. However, if EEA represents one of the key cognitive individual difference dimensions that supports goal-directed behavior, as is suggested by a growing body of clinical and individual differences research, it is notable that EEA does not appear to be preferentially or selectively engaged by conflict. Instead, when EEA factors are formed from task batteries consisting of diverse conflict tasks, similar loadings for EEA are observed for incongruent conditions that involve stimulus conflict and congruent conditions in which stimulus conflict is absent (Weigard, Clark, et al., 2021). Additionally, when EEA factors are formed in task batteries that span conflict tasks and simple perceptual choice tasks, loadings for EEA are nearly identical across both types of tasks (Weigard, Clark, et al., 2021). Finally, when EEA factors are formed separately from simple perceptual choice tasks and more complex tasks thought to require cognitive control, the resulting EEA factors are nearly perfectly correlated (Löffler et al., 2024).

The preceding observations suggest that if we understand EEA as reflecting the integrity of mechanisms involved in the top-down selection of goal-relevant information, then these mechanisms operate ubiquitously: they appear to be important for performance on any task in which the subject has a specific task goal and must accumulate evidence from stimuli that is relevant to achieving that goal, a category that arguably subsumes almost all experimental choice tasks used in cognitive science, however simple.

The generality of processes indexed by EEA raises the need for a key distinction between conflict and task relevance. In conflict, for example the incongruent condition of the Stroop task, there is goal-irrelevant information that opposes the selection of the goal-congruent response. Neutral or goal-irrelevant information, in contrast, merely weakens the impact of goal-relevant information on response selection. If EEA reflects the functioning of mechanisms that promote task relevance, boosting activation of goalrelevant information over goal-irrelevant information, this could explain why such mechanisms are ubiquitously engaged. For example, the need to focus on task relevant information is present even in the simplest perceptual discrimination tasks that have no explicit experimental manipulations of conflicting information. To successfully complete such tasks, individuals must preferentially gather evidence from the goalrelevant stimulus dimension as opposed to the many other irrelevant stimulus dimensions that are available, preferentially activate the appropriate stimulus/response mappings from many other possible mappings, and preferentially activate memory representations relevant to the goals of the current task as opposed to representations relevant to other potentially salient goals (e.g., making dinner after finishing the study).

This point can be sharpened by recognizing that information that is goalirrelevant but does not explicitly bias choice towards the incorrect response can still negatively impact choice behavior and must be discounted relative to goal-relevant information. To see how, consider the first formal description of binary choice in humans as an evidence accumulation process (Stone, 1960) based on Wald's (Wald, 2004) optimal sequential probability-ratio test. For a stream of samples from a stimulus xi, the ideal evidence consists of a pair of probabilities that each option is the correct response, p1(xi) and p2(xi). A single process accumulates the difference in evidence (where evidence for each option is defined as the logarithm of corresponding probabilities), or equivalently the logarithm of the ratio of probabilities:

log [p1(xi)] -log [p2(xi)] = log [p1(xi)/ p2(xi)]

A negative difference favors the first option and positive the second. This account faces limitations in terms of the fact that decision makers are unlikely to know the exact form of these probability distributions, and because, contrary to empirical findings, it predicts equal response time distributions for correct and error responses (Stone, 1960).

Subsequent elaborations of EAMs that have been able to account for the relative speeds of correct and error responses have recognized that evidence accumulation, at best, approximates a likelihood ratio (e.g., (Brown & Heathcote, 2008; Ratcliff & Rouder, 1998)). Importantly, a key implication of (approximate) likelihood ratio-based evidence accumulation is that including "neutral" evidence that equally favors all options reduces the accuracy of decisions. For example, compare a simple case where 4 units of evidence favor one option and 2 units favor the other, so that the first option is favored by a 2:1 ratio. If we add 2 units of neutral evidence equally to each option, their ratio becomes 1.5, and so the first option is now less strongly favored. This illustrates why goal-irrelevant information, even if it is neutral with respect to the response it favors, degrades choice performance. Hence, prioritizing goal-relevant over goal-irrelevant information, even if the irrelevant information is "neutral", is critical for goal-congruent responding. Importantly, this ability enhances goal-congruent responding across a wide range of tasks, and not just in incongruent conditions of conflict tasks in which the goalirrelevant information actively opposes the goal-congruent response.

In sum, it has been widely assumed that processes relevant to goal-directed behavior are selectively or preferentially engaged in the incongruent condition of conflict tasks. We have, however, distinguished the notion of conflict between stimuli from a separate notion of the task relevance of stimuli, and argued that EEA reflects, at least in part, processes that promote task relevance by prioritizing goal-relevant information over goal-irrelevant information. Since task relevance is a factor in all choice tasks used in cognitive science, from complex conflict tasks to simple perceptual discrimination tasks, this helps explain why the processes indexed by EEA show a ubiquitous influence on behavior.

Acknowledgement of the ubiquitous importance of task relevance and the functional role of processes that promote task relevance for furthering goal-directed behavior has broad implications for developmental, clinical, and individual differences research programs that are currently strongly influenced by selective influence assumptions. Despite the widespread tendency to ascribe individuals' goal-directed behavior to processes that selectively influence behavior in conditions of conflict, there is now solid evidence that these processes do not form a cohesive individual difference dimension and that they are not linked to behavioral traits and clinical conditions in which goal-directed behavior is thought impaired. Therefore, a continued focus on conflict-specific processes in these individual differences fields appears untenable. On the other hand, if processes reflected in the general EEA factor that broadly enhance the processing of task-relevant information do represent important individual difference dimensions relevant to cognitive performance, behavioral outcomes, and clinical conditions, the selective influence assumption should be explicitly avoided for measuring these processes. Since the general EEA factor drives performance similarly across both the incongruent and congruent conditions of conflict tasks, the selectivity assumption violated and subtraction methods would lead variance relevant to goaldirected behavior to be removed. Overall, we argue that, for the study of goal-directed behavior, a focus on cognitive and neural mechanisms that amplify the accumulation of goal-relevant information across many tasks and contexts is likely to provide more insights than on a continued focus on processes that are thought to be selectively engaged in a narrow set of cognitive tasks or experimental conditions.

EEA's Place in Models of Psychopathology and Clinical Research

As outlined above, EEA's relations with psychopathology have been gradually coming into focus. EEA shows clear negative relations with externalizing disorders such as ADHD and substance use, fairly consistent negative relations with thought disorders and major depressive disorder diagnoses, but weaker evidence of relations with depression symptoms at the population level and even some evidence for positive associations with internalizing and anxiety symptoms. Given these associations, how should EEA be conceptualized within dimensional models of psychopathology and adaptive functioning?

We argue that EEA is a cognitive dimension that represents an important common denominator of risk for disorders that are characterized problems with selfcontrol, goal-directed cognition, and decision-making, which include the externalizing and thought disorder diagnoses with which EEA is convincingly linked. As a transdiagnostic mechanism, EEA alone clearly cannot possibly explain what makes each of these separate clinical conditions unique. However, this is highly consistent with the multi-determined nature of most clinical disorders that is now generally acknowledged, such as in the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework (Cuthbert & Insel, 2013; Michelini et al., 2021; Morris et al., 2022) and other modern dimensional psychopathology models. EEA is likely just one cognitive factor that interacts with many other trait dimensions of functioning (e.g., RDoC dimensions including positive and negative valence systems, reward processes, social processes, etc.) and environmental circumstances to lead to the emergence of diverse clinical phenotypes. This doesn't necessarily take away from EEA's importance as a risk factor, but it instead indicates that the field needs to better understand how EEA's interactions with other dimensions of functioning convey risk for specific clinical conditions. Furthermore, given that EEA itself is likely multidetermined, as we have highlighted above, better understanding the lower-level processes that enhance processing of goal-relevant information across diverse tasks may highlight different causes of EEA impairments in different disorders.

Another important implication of the general EEA factor is that many of the different cognitive measures that are currently used in psychopathology research, such as those aimed at dissociating specific "executive functions" (Friedman & Robbins, 2022), may predominantly reflect EEA rather than the wider array of specific dimensions that they were originally designed to measure. As has been previously pointed out (Weigard & Sripada, 2021), the "fractionation" paradigm in clinical neuroscience that aimed to distinguish psychiatric disorders on the basis of cognitive task performance alone has largely failed; cognitive impairments in disorders are typically found across many domains rather than being selectively linked to the specific domains that are hypothesized to be impaired (e.g., disinhibition in ADHD). The general EEA factor can explain these findings of broad impairments, and suggests that, although lab-based cognitive tasks clearly contain information that is relevant to self-control and psychopathology, the relevant processes appear to be largely task-general rather than localized to the specific functions that have been the focus of the field (e.g., conflict suppression, working memory, cognitive flexibility).

This raises what might appear to be an important paradox to researchers studying the cognitive basis of psychopathology. As mentioned above, the single dimension of EEA clearly cannot, on its own, explain the emergence of each of the unique clinical conditions that it is linked to (e.g., psychosis versus ADHD). Yet, thus far, many of the tasks the field has designed to measure dissociable dimensions of functioning, at least in the cognitive domain, seem to predominantly reflect EEA. We argue that this paradox can be explained by the fact that many of the other relevant dimensions that may interact with EEA to lead to diverse clinical outcomes are simply outside of the cognitive domain (e.g., positive and negative valence systems, reward processes) or are difficult to measure in the context of controlled lab-based cognitive tasks. Put another way, even if individual differences in laboratory task-based measures that are currently classified as part of the cognitive domain of RDoC can largely be attributed to the single dimension of EEA, we should not forget about the wide array of dimensions from the five other domains of RDoC, or as-of-yet undiscovered domains, that could interact with cognition to explain clinical outcomes.

It is also certainly possible that cognitive paradigms can be improved to better dissociate more specific dimensions of cognitive functioning that are relevant to psychopathology. Social cognition is one particularly promising area, as recent computational modeling work with EAMs (C. Lasagna et al., 2025) and analyses of cognitive task data in large samples (Blain et al., 2025) has converged to suggest that performance on social cognition tasks reflects a dimension that is dissociable from taskgeneral EEA and has separate clinical correlates. The development of more naturalistic experimental paradigms and models that can be fit to naturalistic data (e.g., smartphone and social media use data, passive sensing) may also reveal novel dimensions of cognitive functioning that can be reliably measured and that explain additional variance in different clinical conditions (Gillan & Rutledge, 2021; Mirea et al., 2025; Whelan et al., 2022).

Although these areas show great promise for dissociating specific cognitive functions beyond EEA, clinical and individual differences researchers seeking to index EEA as a general cognitive dimension relevant to self-control and psychopathology may be able to simplify their current approaches to measuring cognition. Data collection for studies in this area often features long batteries of tasks that are hypothesized to selectively engage cognitive control or different "executive" functions. However, as EEA appears to be a strong task-general dimension that is measurable via even relatively simple choice tasks, the careful curation of tasks traditionally thought to be involved in different cognitive control processes is likely unnecessary. Instead, researchers can likely obtain a valid measure of the task-general EEA factor through the inclusion of 3-4 relatively simple and efficient choice tasks from different domains. In archival data sets, a very broad range of forced choice tasks, from simple perceptual tasks to complex conflict tasks, can also be used to measure EEA. Put another way, if most current laboratory tasks designed to measure cognitive functions relevant to goal-directed behavior primarily relate to relevant behavioral outcomes through the single dimension of EEA, we may as well rely on a smaller set of simple and efficient tasks.

The Need to Dissociate Top-Down Selection Processes That Impact EEA and Potentially Other EAM Parameters As we note above, although we believe it is reasonable to assume that EEA likely reflects multiple underlying mechanisms involved in the top-down selection of goalrelevant perceptual information, task sets, or memory representations, more needs to be known about these potential mechanisms and how they should be characterized and measured. The apparent ubiquity of task-general EEA across many paradigms and experimental conditions significantly adds to this challenge, because using experiments to dissociate processes is a central tool for understanding such basic mechanisms. In this way, we think further refinement of EAMs and other formal mathematical models would be helpful for positing potential top-down selection mechanisms and making specific predictions about their manifestations in empirical data, including in new experimental manipulations. The merging of EAMs with latent variable models shows particular promise in this regard (Stevenson et al., 2024, in press), as this joint modeling framework can simultaneously model mechanistic processes the underlie task performance and determine whether such processes represent robust individual differences constructs.

Another promising method for clarifying mechanistic determinants of EEA connects with artificial neural networks as model systems for processing in the human brain. A recent study demonstrated that evidence accumulation dynamics arises naturally in Bayesian neural networks (Rafiei et al., 2024). This approach understands individual differences in EEA in terms of core parameters of the Bayesian network (such as the width of probability distributions of activation weights), opening up new avenues for modeling the normative and mechanistic basis of interindividual differences in EEA.

Another important area of inquiry concerns the potential influence of top-down selection processes or "control" processes (as broadly defined outside of the field of experimental conflict task research) that may impact other EAM parameters. As noted in Section 4, parameters representing response caution and non-decision times show a similar task-general influence to EEA, but the literature currently indicates that these parameters have more ambiguous relations with relevant criterion measures. That said, it is possible that top-down cognitive processes that are highly relevant to goal-directed behavior may impact these parameters. Individuals' ability to adaptively shift response thresholds in response to changing task conditions has been proposed as a form of cognitive control in prospective memory tasks (Strickland et al., 2018) and in ADHD (M. J. Mulder et al., 2010; Weigard & Huang-Pollock, 2014), although the relevance of this form of control to broader individual differences in self-control is not clear (Hedge et al., 2019). Similarly, hints of shorter non-decision times in ADHD (Karalunas et al., 2014; Ziegler et al., 2016) suggest that further mechanistic characterization of non-decision processes (Bompas et al., 2024) is crucial for understanding relations between EAM parameters, including EEA, and self-control. This is especially important considering that EAM parameters can trade off with one another during estimation (Lüken et al., 2025). For example, findings of shorter non-decision time in ADHD may reflect a trade-off with EEA parameters, as described enacting these strategies requires engaging in extended sequences of basic decision operations-for example, operations involving perception, attention, memory, and preferential choice-that involve weighing multiple goal-relevant attributes. An individual with lower EEA will be less able to consistently perform these operations in goalcongruent ways, and the results of these operations will instead be more likely to reflect automatic or habitual processing. Thus, lower EEA can result in incorrect execution of key steps of self-control strategies and the individual would commensurably be less likely to act in goal-congruent ways and instead select a tempting option (Sripada, 2022).

A similar "componential" approach connects EEA with general cognitive ability (i.e., general intelligence), especially fluid intelligence which relates to online problemsolving abilities. Tests of fluid intelligence, such as Raven's progressive matrices, require the test taker to perform numerous basic decision operations regarding how to approach the problem, how to execute a search for solutions, when to deviate from the current strategy or backtrack, and so forth. Individuals with lower EEA are less likely to execute these steps in goal-congruent ways, resulting in poorer performance on the overall task. These issues are not specific to Raven's matrices but rather arise for any complex cognitive task, thus explaining the reliable connection between EEA and general cognitive ability (for a related view see Oberauer et al., 2025).

Testing elements of these componential explanations for EEA's relations with self-control and intelligence is a critical focus of future research. Furthermore, these connections raise a more general question of whether task-general EEA is functionally equivalent to the construct of general intelligence. In addition to noting the previously discussed perils of equating parameters from formal modeling with traditional psychometric constructs, we highlight important distinctions between EEA and intelligence. Whereas intelligence is primarily a descriptive construct based on the observation that performance on many different tasks is correlated, EEA is drawn from a formal process model that has specific assumptions that can be quantitatively related to mechanisms determining choice behavior, several of which have been linked to specific neurobiological processes. Overall, then, EEA is a more specific, parsimoniously defined, statistically estimable, and biologically translatable construct than intelligence.

It thus has greater promise for providing mechanistic clarity about processes that support cognitive performance across tasks (e.g., top-down selection).

Conclusion

This review has detailed pressing problems with the current literature on the construct of cognitive control and has presented a novel perspective on these problems that is rooted in the burgeoning literature on computational models of choice. It suggests that there are two distinct categories of processes that may be relevant to implementing control during experimental tasks: conflict-specific processes that are engaged to oppose goal-inconsistent information and task-general processes that facilitate the processing of goal-relevant information irrespective of the presence of conflict. Although conflict-specific processes have garnered the bulk of attention, we highlight the importance of task-general processes that impact evidence quality parameters in EAMs and that are reflected in the general EEA factor. In contrast to conflict-specific processes, the task-general EEA factor represents a cohesive individual difference dimension, explains individual differences in performance across diverse cognitive tasks, including those thought to reflect control, and is linked to relevant behavioral traits and clinical conditions. These findings impact the field of cognitive control in two major ways. First, they highlight the benefits of understanding mechanistic processes involved in the selective amplification of goal-relevant information across tasks as an important direction for understanding goal-directed behavior. Second, they suggest that EEA is a useful explanatory construct for revising theories in fields that have relied on the traditional construct of cognitive control to understand individual differences in self-control and related clinical conditions and behavioral outcomes.

Moving forward, computational models of choice are likely to provide a valuable general framework for research on cognitive control (defined broadly as cognitive processes that modulate other processes in the service of goals) and related constructs (such as selfcontrol and general intelligence), allowing theorists to take advantage of the large and growing body of theory and empirical findings surrounding these mathematically specified and biologically plausible approaches to understanding mechanisms of adaptive behavior.

Characterizing EEA's Links with Self-Control and General Intelligence.

EEA measured in the highly controlled context of laboratory tasks is correlated with more complex psychological constructs including self-control (Sripada & Weigard, 2021; Tomlinson et al., 2025; Weigard, Clark, et al., 2021) and general cognitive ability (Lerche et al., 2020; Schmiedek et al., 2007; Schmitz & Wilhelm, 2016; Schubert & Frischkorn, 2020), raising fundamental questions about how to explain these relationships. We propose a "componential" model.

Computational models of choice describe basic decisions that unfold over several hundred milliseconds, and these decision operations are ubiquitous in perception, memory, attention, and preferential choice. EEA is related to these basic decision operations because it indexes processes that amplify goal-relevant information.

Individuals with lower EEA are thus more likely, on aggregate, to select options during speeded decisions that are less goal-congruent.
