Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Broad traits predict consequential life outcomes but are ill-suited to causal inference, whereas single narrow, behaviorally measured traits are more interpretable causally but predict weakly.

The space of narrow traits is also so large that choosing among them is challenging. I propose the Power of Narrow Traits (PNT) framework to address both challenges. In a specific person, the causal effect of a narrow trait (e.g., talkativeness) on a specific outcome (e.g., social capital) accumulates across the situations in which the trait can be expressed as a state (e.g., talking) and in which that state influences the outcome, with situations described by their features. In its basic form, four factors multiply at each point of the feature space: (a) how often per year the person encounters such situations, (b) over how many years, (c) how strongly the trait translates into the state there, and (d) how strongly that state causally affects the outcome. These contributions are then summed over the situations the person encounters. The decomposition clarifies when narrow traits should be consequential, which traits to measure for a given outcome, why the same trait level leads to an outcome for some people but not for others, and how interventions can be tailored to each person's trait and situational profile. I formalize the framework, illustrate it with worked examples, relate it to existing theories, and derive six empirical predictions. PNT thus provides a unifying structure for future work on stable individual differences and life outcomes.

The Power of Narrow Traits (PNT) Framework: How Behavior Accumulates Across Situations into Life Outcomes

Personality traits and cognitive abilities are among the most robust correlates of consequential life outcomes. For example, personality traits are associated with subjective wellbeing (Anglim et al., 2020; Soto, 2019), mental and physical health (Jokela et al., 2013; McGeehan et al., 2026), educational attainment (Duckworth et al., 2019; Poropat, 2009), income (Denissen et al., 2018), and interpersonal outcomes such as divorce (Roberts et al., 2007) and social status (Grosz, van Aert, & Back, 2024). Similar evidence exists for cognitive abilities (e.g., Brown et al., 2021; Calvin et al., 2017; Grosz, van Aert, & Back, 2024). These associations might carry substantial societal stakes. For example, Cuijpers and colleagues (2010) estimated that the excess societal costs associated with the highest quartile of neuroticism amount to approximately $1.39 billion per million inhabitants per year, roughly 2.5 times the combined excess costs of all common mental disorders in the same population. Such findings, together with the observation that many people want to change their own personality (Hudson & Roberts, 2014), motivate an active literature on personality change interventions intended to improve lives through targeted modification of broad personality traits (e.g., Bleidorn et al., 2019; Haehner et al., 2024; Stieger et al., 2021).

However, research on the consequences of individual differences confronts two profound methodological challenges that limit its ability to move from association to causal inference. The first challenge concerns a trade-off between predictive validity and causal interpretability.

Although broad measures of personality and intelligence predict life outcomes reasonably well, they are composite variables rather than unidimensional traits (e.g., Grosz, 2026; Revelle, 2024; van der Maas et al., 2014). Thus, the consistency assumption of causal inference, which requires that all underlying versions of a given treatment level yield the same potential outcome, is often violated (e.g., Cole & Frangakis, 2009; Junker & Grosz, 2026; VanderWeele, 2009). The reason is that the same composite score can reflect different configurations of lower-order components with potentially distinct causal effects. For example, the same conscientiousness score can reflect greater Organization or greater Productiveness, which may have different health effects (Junker & Grosz, 2026). The opacity of broad measures not only violates the consistency assumption but also makes it difficult to identify appropriate confounders, mediators, and colliders in observational studies, further complicating causal estimation (Grosz, 2026; Wysocki et al., 2022). When one shifts to single narrower or behavioral measures, above all single-occasion task measures, predictive validity typically drops substantially (Bagaini et al., 2025; Boon-Falleur et al., 2022; Eisenberg et al., 2019; Epstein, 1979; Paap et al., 2024; Wennerhold & Friese, 2020).

The drop concerns single measures: a single narrow trait rarely out-predicts a single broad domain, even though a regression on many narrow items out-predicts a regression on the few domain scores they compose (Mõttus et al., 2017; Seeboth & Mõttus, 2018; Stewart et al., 2022).

Narrower and behavioral measures are nevertheless better suited to causal inference in two respects. They satisfy the consistency assumption, and they are more amenable to manipulation than trait levels because they are closer to the concrete behavior. The dilemma is that the broad measures with the strongest predictive power are the least suitable for causal inference and intervention, whereas the narrow, behaviorally anchored measures of single narrow traits that are better suited have weaker associations with outcomes.

The second challenge concerns the proliferation of narrow traits. Because life circumstances vary widely and interact with genetic, biological, and developmental differences to produce innumerable specific behavioral tendencies, there are essentially countless narrow traits one could assess (e.g., Condon et al., 2020; Mõttus et al., 2017; Revelle et al., 2011).

Public-domain item banks already hold more than 3,000 personality items, and their counterparts for cognitive abilities have grown from 60 to roughly 1,000 items within a decade (Condon & Revelle, 2014; Dworak et al., 2021; Goldberg et al., 2006), while new constructs and measures accumulate at a rate of thousands per year (Anvari et al., 2025). The most systematic proposal to date is the six-step procedure of Condon et al. (2020), which screens candidate items on content and item statistics, retest reliability, cross-rater agreement, and generalizability, and ends with post hoc evaluations of predictive utility. Such screening yields curated, non-redundant pools.

For example, the 100 Nuances of Personality comprises 198 items (Henry & Mõttus, 2025).

However, such screening cannot tell a researcher which of the surviving traits are likely to have a substantial causal effect on a given outcome. The prospect of an unguided choice among thousands of candidates deters some researchers from studying narrow traits at all. Researchers who nevertheless study narrow traits often select them by intuition or convention. This practice risks under-sampling of the tendencies that matter most for the outcome at hand.

To address both challenges, I propose the Power of Narrow Traits (PNT) framework. Its central claim is that, in a specific person, the causal effect of a narrow trait (e.g., talkativeness) on a specific outcome (e.g., well-being, longevity, or income) accumulates across the situations in which the trait can be expressed as a state and in which that state influences the outcome. The state can be a behavior (e.g., talking), but also an affect (e.g., anxiety), a cognition (e.g., ruminating), or a desire (e.g., desire for social status). In the framework's basic form, four factors are multiplied at each configuration of situation features: (a) how often per year the person encounters situations with those features, (b) over how many years the trait is expressed in them, (c) how strongly the trait translates into the state there (trait expressivity), and (d) the average causal effect of the expressed state on the outcome there. These contributions are then summed over all the situations the person encounters. Extensions of this basic form cover diminishing returns, outcomes that return to a set point, interactions among behaviors and among narrow traits, and trait-driven situation selection (see Extensions).

The contribution of the PNT framework is to formalize a multiplicative decomposition that (a) reconciles the predictive-causal dilemma, (b) provides an operational criterion for choosing which narrow traits to measure for a given outcome and population, (c) yields tractable chains of causal effects that differ from the predictions of broad-trait and interactionist accounts, and (d) motivates tailored, situation-aware interventions. The PNT framework is deliberately a coarse-grained model in the sense of Smaldino (2020): it reproduces qualitative patterns (orderings, contrasts, and the conditions under which trait-outcome effects appear or vanish) rather than exact effect sizes. The values in the examples that follow are therefore order-ofmagnitude calibrations rather than parameter estimates. That said, the framework's main purpose is to serve as a template from which fine-grained, empirically calibrated models (instances) can be built for a specific narrow trait, outcome, and population by estimating their components.

The Power of Narrow Traits (PNT) Framework The Idea in One Example

Consider two employees, Nora and Ben, who are equally good at quantitative reasoning: both score one standard deviation above the mean. Nora is a portfolio analyst. In a typical year she makes about 1,700 decisions that involve quantities, with about $6,000 at stake in the average decision. Ben is a communications manager. He makes about as many quantitative decisions, 1,500 a year, but with about $700 at stake in the average one. In every such decision, better quantitative reasoning improves the result a little. Suppose that one standard deviation of the ability is worth 0.1% of the amount at stake, through sounder valuations, avoided errors, and the evaluations and raises that follow. Over 10 years, the arithmetic is simple. Nora earns 10 years × 1,700 decisions × $6,000 × 0.1% ≈ $102,000 more than a colleague of average ability in her position would earn. Ben earns 10 × 1,500 × $700 × 0.1% ≈ $10,500 more. The same trait, expressed equally in every decision and with the same effect per dollar at stake, is ten times as consequential for Nora as for Ben, and the entire difference lies in the situations the two encounter.

Four quantities did all the work: how many relevant situations the person encounters per year (1,700 or 1,500 decisions), over how many years (10), how strongly the trait shows in what the person does in each situation (here, equally in every decision), and what one instance of that behavior does to the outcome (here, 0.1% of the stakes, so that it depends on a feature of the situation). Multiplying the four gives the trait's effect on the outcome for that person. The PNT framework is the general statement of this arithmetic. Because the four factors multiply and three of them depend on the situations the person encounters, two consequences follow. First, the same trait can be powerful for one person and inert for another, so that population averages can conceal large effects. Second, the narrow traits worth measuring for a given outcome can be found by starting from the situations that produce the outcome, rather than chosen by intuition.

Narrow Trait

A narrow trait, denoted n, is a specific individual-difference characteristic with high temporal stability. Narrow personality traits are often referred to as personality nuances that are more specific than facets and much more specific than domains in standard personality inventories (Condon et al., 2020; Mõttus et al., 2017). That said, "narrow trait" is not limited to personality nuances. It also includes specific cognitive abilities and other temporally stable individual differences.

Examples of narrow traits include talkativeness as a nuance within the extraversion domain, reactive aggression as a nuance within the agreeableness domain, quantitative reasoning as a specific cognitive ability (often classified under fluid intelligence depending on the model), exercise adherence as a nuance within the conscientiousness domain, and rejection sensitivity as a nuance within the neuroticism domain. These examples deliberately span a range of cultural generality, and PNT applies uniformly across this range. Basic tendencies such as talkativeness, reactive aggression, and rejection sensitivity are expressed in situations that occur in most cultural contexts. Characteristic adaptations such as quantitative reasoning and exercise adherence are expressed in situations that exist only where technology or institutions create them (e.g., McCrae & Costa, 2008). Culture thus enters PNT through the situations it makes available and how often they occur (see Prediction 1), not as a situation feature itself.

A person p has a score on narrow trait n. For convenience, I denote the person's standardized score as z(p), representing the number of standard deviations p deviates from the sample mean. The index n is omitted because the equations treat one narrow trait at a time.

Where several traits enter jointly, the trait is indexed.

Situation Features and Exposure

Each narrow trait n has an associated region X(n, Y) of the space of situation features in which n can be expressed as a state and that state has a non-negligible causal effect on the outcome Y. A situation is described by a vector of features, denoted x, that determine the affordances, demands, and constraints it presents (Gibson, 1977; Rauthmann et al., 2015).

Features can be continuous or ordered (how many people are present, how much money is at stake, how strongly a setting discourages conversation) or present versus absent (whether a decision involves quantities at all). The PNT framework does not commit to any particular taxonomy of situation features. It does require that the features be scored on the situation itself rather than on how the focal person perceives it, and that they be as narrow as the trait, as explained below.

Researchers may draw on existing taxonomies such as DIAMONDS (Rauthmann et al., 2014) or CAPTION (Parrigon et al., 2017), domain-specific feature sets tailored to the trait of interest, or bottom-up feature sets derived empirically for the trait-outcome link at hand. Which features matter, and over which range, depends on the narrow trait, the state through which it is expressed, and the outcomes under investigation. For talkativeness, for example, the region X(n, Y) comprises the situations in which social interaction is possible and verbal behavior can unfold. Within this region, narrower features such as the number of people present, how familiar they are, and how far the activity permits conversation plausibly shift both how strongly talkativeness translates into the state and how strongly that state causes the outcome of interest. Breakfast with family members, commuting on public transit alongside strangers, and waiting with peers for a university lecture to start are points in this feature space: they differ in the familiarity of those present, their number, and the openness of the activity to conversation. Which part of the space belongs to X(n, Y) depends on the outcome. For subjective well-being, all three plausibly lie inside it. For the number of new friendships formed, situations in which everyone present is already a close tie, such as breakfast with family members, contribute nothing, because no new tie can result.

The region is thus indexed by trait and outcome jointly. This is also what keeps the problem tractable: parts of the feature space can often be excluded on theoretical grounds before any parameter is estimated.

Situation features are scored by criteria that do not depend on the focal person's construal, so that exposure tracks situation contact rather than situation perception. Two kinds of criteria qualify. The first are observable cues: who is present, where, and doing what (Johns, 2006; Rauthmann et al., 2015; Saucier et al., 2007), which can be counted and coded. The second are psychological characteristics rated by observers or by consensus rather than by the focal person alone, for example how much a situation offers an opportunity to gain status (cf. Rauthmann et al., 2014). The existing dimensional taxonomies were developed as measures of situation perception. Used as features here, their dimensions must be scored by others or from coded cues, because self-scored features would confound exposure with construal. Either way, the features must be as narrow as the trait. A narrow trait is expressed in response to specific affordances, and a broad situation dimension averages over affordances that differ in whether they make the state possible and consequential, which blurs trait expressivity and the causal effect of the expressed state on the outcome in the same way that a broad trait blurs the effects of its components. Narrowness is required on both sides.

For each person p, there is a long-run exposure density f(x | p): integrated over any range of feature values, it gives the expected number of objectively scorable instances per year that person p encounters with features in that range. Integrating over all of X(n, Y) gives N(p), the number of relevant situations the person encounters per year, and f(x | p)/N(p) is the distribution of the person's situations over the features. A representative sample of the person's situations, each scored on the features, estimates the distribution, and the number of instances per year scales it (see Measurement of Expressivity and Exposure).

Life Outcome

A life outcome Y is, formally, a person-level variable, realized over a time horizon T, on which the expressed state has a well-defined causal effect and which is not itself an aggregate of that state. Whether such a variable counts as a life outcome involves a further judgment about which end states matter. That judgment is normative rather than formal (Wessels et al., 2016).

Outcomes span the individual, interpersonal, and institutional domains (Ozer & Benet-Martínez, 2006; Wilmot et al., 2025), from daily well-being and survival to age 80 through relationship satisfaction to annual income, and can be continuous, binary, or time-to-event. They can be more or less distal from the expressed state: ruminating affects daily well-being within a single step, whereas exercising affects mortality only through mediators such as cardiorespiratory fitness and cardiovascular risk (Wessels et al., 2016). Outcomes can also differ in how the causal effect is distributed across instances: accruing through countless small contributions or turning on a handful of consequential ones.

Effect on the Expressed State

In a situation with features x, the state through which narrow trait n is expressed by person p is modeled as where α(x) is the average state level in situations with features x, β(n, x) is the trait expressivity at x (how strongly individual differences in the narrow trait are translated into differences in the expressed state in situations with those features), and ε captures residual variation from unmodeled factors.

In words, a person's state in a situation is the state an average person shows there, plus a deviation that grows with the person's trait level at a rate set by how much the situation lets the trait show, plus noise. The term "state" encompasses behavior (e.g., speaking, exercising) as well as affect, cognitions, and desires (e.g., ruminating, experiencing pride, suppressing an impulse, wanting to impress). Behavior is the paradigmatic case. Where a trait is a capacity (e.g., quantitative reasoning) rather than a tendency (e.g., talkativeness), the state is the momentary performance in which the capacity is exercised (e.g., the quality of a quantitative decision, as in Box 1), and S is its level on the dimension that is causally relevant to Y.

Trait expressivity β(n, x) is trait-and situation-specific: it varies across the feature space, within and beyond X(n, Y). It is zero in two cases: where the state cannot occur at all, because the situation does not afford it, and where the situation fixes the state at the same level for everyone, so that the state occurs but does not vary with the trait. In the second case α can be substantial even though β is zero. Note that situations outside X(n, Y) may nonetheless have substantial β, if the state is expressed there but has no consequence for Y. Situational strength is itself a feature, or a combination of features, along which β is expected to decline: classical and recent accounts suggest that β is smaller in strong situations than in weak situations, because strong situations constrain decision-making freedom and limit the range of individually differentiated behavior (Judge & Zapata, 2015; Meyer et al., 2010; Mischel, 1977). The evidence is less clear-cut than the claim's currency suggests (Cooper & Withey, 2009), although a rigorous recent test supports it for cooperative behavior (Li et al., 2025; see Text S5). The framework is agnostic on the precise form of β and treats it as an empirically estimated function. Estimating it requires pooling instances with similar features, either by assuming a functional form (β linear in a feature, say, estimated as a trait × feature interaction) or by smoothing across neighboring feature values. How much β varies across the feature space is itself an empirical question. In everyday experience-sampling data, situational differences in trait-state slopes have been modest relative to individual differences in situation-state contingencies (Kuper et al., 2024).

Where β is roughly constant over X(n, Y), variation in PNT contributions across the feature space is carried by f and γ, as in Box 1.

For Nora, ΔY ≈ 10 × 1.0 × 0.001 × 1,700 × $6,000 ×1 ≈ $102,000.

For Ben, ΔY ≈ 10 × 1.0 × 0.001 × 1,500 × $700 ×1 ≈ $10,500.

Like the other person-specific parameters in Table 1, η is defined counterfactually, as the amount by which person p's state in situations with features x would change per SD of the trait, over and above the population slope. Because each person has a single trait value, η cannot be estimated from that person's data alone. Individual differences in situation-state contingencies (Bauditz et al., 2026; Kuper et al., 2022 Kuper et al., , 2024) ) constrain it without identifying it for any single person, and between subgroups it can be estimated directly as differences in β (e.g., across countries; Kuper et al., 2026). For readability, the equations below use the population slope β(n, x); substituting β(n, x, p) yields the fully person-specific form.

Equations 2 and 3 below use only the slope β but not the average state level α, because α is trait-independent: under their assumptions it is shared by a person and their mean-level (z = 0) counterfactual and cancels from the trait-attributable deviation. It re-enters once exposure itself depends on the trait, as in the selection extension, or once the outcome depends nonlinearly on cumulated state, as under the diminishing returns extension. I return to both extensions below.

Effect on Outcome

In a situation with features x, the expressed state S(n, x, p) produces an effect on the outcome Y. Let γ(x, Y | p) denote the person-specific average causal effect of a unit increase in S on Y in one instance of a situation with features x. The effect need not materialize within the situation. It includes consequences that unfold later, through mediators (exercising affects mortality through fitness; see Life Outcome), through others' memories and reactions (Back et al., 2011), and through the person's own later reflection on the episode. The qualifier withinsituation therefore refers to where the state is expressed, not to when its consequences occur. In some settings γ will be homogeneous across persons. In many settings γ varies across persons for biological, contextual, or structural reasons (e.g., Bryan et al., 2021; Denissen et al., 2018; Wenzel et al., 2025). Because γ is the effect of a single instance of the expressed state, it is typically very small in absolute terms (see Box 1) and cannot be read off any one instance.

Estimating it requires aggregating the state-outcome relationship across many instances or many persons, as discussed below under Estimating the Within-Situation Causal Efficacy, and, like β, pooling instances with similar features. The talkativeness example introduced earlier shows why β and γ must be carried as separate functions of the features. Talkativeness is expressed most fully among familiar others, as at breakfast with family members, less fully among waiting peers, and least among commuting strangers, where norms discourage unsolicited conversation: β declines as the people present become less familiar. The per-instance effect on forming a new friendship plausibly runs in the opposite direction: γ rises as they become less familiar, because only a new tie can become a new friendship. Box 1 presents a complementary case, in which β is roughly constant across the feature space and the entire contrast is carried by γ.

The same arithmetic explains the appeal of broad traits: a domain pools narrow traits that are expressed across many regions of the feature space, so its summed exposure is large even when no single component is powerful.

Extensions

The basic PNT equations (Equations 2 and 3) assume additive cumulation of withinsituation effects, linear expression of narrow traits in the state, and situation exposure f given exogenously as an input. None of these assumptions is strictly necessary.

Four extensions are worth noting briefly. These extensions complicate the equations but not the substantive claims: relaxing additivity or exogenous exposure alters the magnitude and shape of trait-outcome effects, not the four factors that govern them.

Outcome Trajectory Classes

The baseline form cumulates within-situation effects additively across the time window T, which fits monotonically nondecreasing outcomes most directly. Wessels et al.'s (2016) remaining trajectory classes call for different aggregation operators over time: single-switch binary outcomes such as mortality for a hazard-rate form, reversible binary outcomes such as marital status for a state-transition form, and freely varying outcomes such as well-being for a time-weighted kernel that down-weights more distant behavior (the device already introduced in Footnote 1 for recency-dependent outcomes). In each case the four ingredients (f, T, β, γ) remain the only quantities required, and only the rule by which within-situation effects are summed over time changes. These reformulations are compatible with the decomposition but each adds estimation burden.

Interactions Among Situations and Traits

The basic equation is additive in two respects. First, it integrates within-situation effects across the situations in which a trait is expressed. Second, when several narrow traits bear on an outcome, it implicitly sums their separate contributions. For some outcomes neither additivity holds, because the effect of a behavior depends on what else the person does, whether in another situation or as the expression of another trait. Across situations, joint analyses of sleep, physical activity, and nutrition suggest that the three behaviors interact synergistically in their association with all-cause mortality, with combinations producing risk reductions that exceed what any single behavior achieves in isolation (Stamatakis et al., 2025; see also Han et al., 2026). Across traits, behavioral tendencies can compensate for or facilitate one another: a high level on one narrow trait can substitute for a low level on another (sub-additive), or two tendencies can be jointly more consequential than their separate effects would imply (super-additive). For instance, conscientiousness and interest seem to partly compensate in producing academic effort and achievement (e.g., Bareis et al., 2024; Trautwein et al., 2015). In both cases the contribution of one behavior is conditional on another, and both are absorbed by letting γ(x, Y | p) depend on the joint behavioral configuration rather than on a single behavior in isolation. When the interacting behaviors occur in situations with different features, γ generalizes to γ(x, x′, Y | p). When they are driven by different narrow traits, γ generalizes to an analogous cross-trait term. The corresponding aggregation then turns from additive to bilinear.

Situation Selection

The basic equation takes situation exposure f as an exogenous input. In reality, however, exposure is itself partly a consequence of narrow traits, because individuals actively shape the distribution of situations they encounter (e.g., Kuper, Breitmoser, et al., 2025; Rauthmann et al., 2015; Trappes et al., 2022; Wagerman & Funder, 2009; Xi & Jackson, 2026). For example, the effect of the narrow trait talkativeness on accumulated social capital might be driven by both within-situation trait expression and trait-driven differential exposure. Trappes et al. (2022) define three mechanisms by which individuals shape their fit with their environments (niche choice, niche conformance, and niche construction), and Kuper, Breitmoser, and colleagues (2025) map these mechanisms across disciplines. Two of them bear on exposure. Niche choice refers to the individual selecting into environments that fit their tendencies (e.g., a more talkative student joining more student clubs). Niche construction refers to the individual changing the properties of environments they already inhabit (e.g., a more talkative student turning a quiet study group into a lively one). Choice changes which situations the person enters. It typically raises f in trait-congruent regions of the feature space and lowers it elsewhere. Trait-congruent regions are those in which the trait is readily expressed (higher β) or its expression pays (higher γ). Construction changes the features of the situations the person already occupies, which moves them to another point of the feature space (the study group made lively moves from the quiet to the lively part), so construction also shifts f, typically toward traitcongruent regions.

A dynamic extension of PNT accommodates both mechanisms by treating the situation exposure density f as a function of the narrow-trait level z rather than as a fixed input: where f₀(x | p) is the person's baseline exposure density that would obtain in the absence of trait-driven choice and construction, and λ(x) is a composite trait-to-exposure slope that aggregates over both.

In words, each SD of the trait adds λ(x) situations per year at feature configuration x, or removes them where λ is negative, and exposure cannot fall below zero. λ is positive where situation selection is trait-congruent (e.g., talkative people selecting situations in which they can talk), but can also be negative where it is trait-incongruent. For example, people high in self-control avoid tempting situations (Ent et al., 2015; see Text S4 and Figure S1).

Implications of the PNT Framework

Empirical Predictions

As a template, the PNT framework is not refuted by any single observation, because it is close to definitional that a trait's effect is carried by its manifestations in situations. In addition, departures from the basic form, such as non-additive aggregation, diminishing returns, and traitdependent exposure, are absorbed by the proposed extensions or, where none fits, by new ones Prediction 1: Exposure-dependent group differences. The causal effect of n on Y differs across groups (occupations, sexes, cultures) that meet the outcome-relevant situations at different rates, in proportion to T × ∫ₓ (f × β × γ) dx within each group. Even where β and γ are comparable, PNT differs when the outcome-relevant situations are rare in one group and frequent in another. At the extreme, exposure approaches zero, as for quantitative reasoning where languages lack exact-number systems (Frank et al., 2008) or for exercise adherence where exertion is embedded in subsistence labor rather than scheduled as a distinct activity. The relevance of a narrow trait for an outcome thus depends on the group's situational ecology.

Differences in r(n, Y) track differences in the causal effect only if confounding and scaling are comparable across groups. The alternative is the interactionist account (Judge & Zapata, 2015; Tett & Burnett, 2003), which ties validity to situational strength and trait relevance and has no term for how often situations occur. The discriminating implication is that groups meeting the same kinds of situations (comparable β and γ) at different rates still differ, especially in regions of the feature space where β × γ is large (Box 1). Suggestive evidence exists: in a community sample in which men engaged in more of the risk behaviors sampled (gambling, risky driving, risky investments), and thus met the decision situations that generate negative outcomes more often, heuristics-and-biases performance predicted those outcomes more strongly in men (r = -.32) than in women (r = -.10; Toplak et al., 2017). Demographic categories only proxy exposure, however (Beck & Jackson, 2022; Text S3).

Prediction 2: PNT weights are person-and group-specific. How much a trait counts depends on the person's situation exposure: a trait's weight in a composite of narrow traits that predicts Y is its PNT coefficient. Thus, composites with person-specific PNT weights out-predict fixed-weight composites of the same traits in samples with heterogeneous exposure. Groups differ as well: people who differ in parenthood, occupation, health, relationship status, or cultural context can differ systematically in f, β, and γ (e.g., for occupations, Denissen et al., 2018; for β across countries, Kuper et al., 2026; on the group-specificity of principles more generally, Kuper, Breitmoser, et al., 2025). Because the three factors multiply, their group differences can reinforce or offset one another, so that a narrow trait with large PNT for one group can have near-zero PNT for another, and weights estimated in one group transfer to another only after re-weighting by the target group's f (and, where β and γ differ as well, re-estimating them). The alternative is a purely statistical account that estimates item-or nuance-level weights by penalized regression, with no structural interpretation and no mechanism for re-weighting across persons or groups.

The discriminating implication is that PNT names what must be re-estimated (at a minimum f) and predicts a gain from person-specific weighting and from f-re-weighting that grows with the heterogeneity of f. The test is an out-of-sample comparison of person-specific PNT weights, fixed weights, and broad-trait scores in heterogeneous samples, and a cross-group validation of transferred versus f-re-weighted weights.

Prediction 3: Narrow-trait change drives outcome change. Changing a narrow trait changes the outcome in proportion to the trait's PNT coefficient. A broad trait is a composite of narrow traits (Grosz, 2026; Revelle, 2024; van der Maas et al., 2014), so broad-trait change moves the outcome by the PNT-weighted sum of the changes in its components. The alternative is the common-cause account, on which the broad trait causes both its components and the outcome: outcome change then follows change in the broad trait regardless of which components moved, and change in a single component with the broad trait held constant leaves the outcome unchanged.

Prediction 4: Profile-tailored interventions outperform. An intervention that selects its

targets from each participant's exposure (f) and efficacy (γ) profile produces larger gains than a content-matched intervention that delivers the same targets to everyone. The alternative is generic personalization: a tailored intervention engages the person more and therefore works better, whatever the basis of the tailoring, such as change goals (Stieger et al., 2021) or characteristics that predict adherence (Haehner et al., 2026). Two examples follow, and in both cases the nonlinearity lies outside the trait: First, under diminishing returns (Equation 6), the outcome depends on the cumulative dose of the state, ∫ₓ f × (α + βz) dx, through a function that flattens as the dose grows, so the effect flattens at lower z for persons with higher exposure (cf.

Box 2), and no flattening in z remains once the cumulative dose is held constant. Second, for outcomes that return to a set point, such as well-being (Luhmann et al., 2012), only recent behavior matters, so the effect stops growing once T exceeds the span over which behavior still counts (Footnote 1). For cumulative outcomes such as earnings it keeps growing (see Outcome Trajectory Classes). Whether an effect keeps growing with T thus depends on the outcome, not on the trait. The alternative is a trait-level dose-response account on which the effect flattens or reverses beyond a particular value of z, the same for everyone whatever their exposure (Brown et al., 2021; Le et al., 2011). The discriminating test requires the cumulative dose to be measured separately from z (e.g., device-measured physical activity over years) and effects compared across horizons within persons (Text S3).

Measurement of Expressivity and Exposure

The PNT framework implies that trait-outcome studies should assess not only the trait and the outcome but also the frequency of relevant situations, trait expressivity, and the effect of the expressed state on the outcome to clarify when and why a trait leads to an outcome.

Concretely, the framework proposes a three-step measurement pipeline. First, specify the outcome Y, the target population, and the relevant time window T. Second, enumerate potentially relevant candidate narrow traits and their associated situation regions X(n, Y). This can be done using taxonomic and behavioral resources such as trait taxonomies (e.g., Ashton et al., 2004; Bainbridge et al., 2022; Henry & Mõttus, 2025), situation taxonomies (Parrigon et al., 2017; Rauthmann et al., 2014), and occupational task databases (e.g., O*NET, 2024). Third, for each candidate narrow trait, measure and estimate the PNT components: population-average situation exposure (e.g., from diary or census data), trait expressivity (e.g., from experience sampling), and within-situation efficacy (e.g., from longitudinal, quasi-experimental, or ideally experimental designs). Because assessment over the full horizon T is rarely feasible, the parameters can be measured in a random sample of time intervals and extrapolated over T (Wessels et al., 2016). Candidate traits are then ranked by their expected PNT, and components whose variation does not materially change the implied effect can be fixed at default values, concentrating measurement effort where it matters.

Implementing this third step in practice involves measuring both trait expression and situation frequency at the level of specific situation features. Narrow-trait inventories typically present items detached from situational anchors ("I am talkative"). One way to assess trait expressivity might be to explicitly situate items (cf. the frame-of-reference tradition; Holtrop et al., 2014), with each item identifying a recurring configuration of situation features and asking about typical behavior in situations of that kind ("In informal conversations with friends or family, I tend to speak frequently and at length"). To capture not only expression but also exposure, each expressivity item should be paired with a brief situation-frequency item ("How often do you have informal conversations with friends or family in a typical week?"). The expressivity item provides an estimate of β, the frequency item provides an estimate of f, and together they yield the f × β contribution of situations with those features to person-specific PNT at the item-pair level. This conditioning logic descends from the if-then-signature tradition (Mischel & Shoda, 1995; Mischel et al., 2002) and from behavior-anchored measurement (Buss & Craik, 1983; Chapman & Goldberg, 2017; Jackson et al., 2010). PNT extends it by aggregating across situation features and frequencies.

Whereas situated items provide a static, retrospective route to β and f, experience sampling provides a dynamic, momentary one. Multi-week smartphone-based protocols (Harari & Gosling, 2023; Mestdagh et al., 2023) combined with brief situation-coding items can generate the joint distribution of f and S. Multilevel dynamic structural equation models provide statistical tools for this route (Asparouhov et al., 2018). From a single experience-sampling protocol, they estimate person-specific average levels of situational experience and behavior, person-specific situation-state contingencies as random slopes (which constrain η), and the state-to-subsequentsituation paths that inform the selection extension (for an application across 25 trait-situation pairs, see Xi & Jackson, 2026).

An important caveat applies to both static situated items and momentary experience sampling: self-reports remain self-reports and inherit response-style and social-desirability biases (e.g., Baird et al., 2017; Leising et al., 2025). Furthermore, participants' self-reports of which situations they encounter reflect not only the situations they actually faced, but also how they interpreted those situations (e.g., Wessels et al., 2016). Because personality shapes interpretation (Rauthmann et al., 2015), self-reported exposure to situations with given features and the psychological experience of those situations are systematically entangled, with evidence that the construal component may be the larger of the two (Bauditz et al., 2026; Kuper et al., 2026).

PNT-oriented designs should therefore, wherever feasible, supplement self-reports with otherreports from interaction partners or with mobile-sensed behavioral and situational indicators. The converse caveat applies to observer-based measures: where a trait is expressed largely in affect or cognition (e.g., ruminating), other-reports and behavioral coding might register little of the state and underestimate β. For the estimation of f in particular, objective situational indicators (location or audio data, calendar records, or observer-coded experience-sampling annotations) are preferable, as they permit situation contact and situation construal to be disentangled.

Estimating the Within-Situation Causal Efficacy

Estimating γ poses a distinctive challenge. By construction, γ is the effect of a single instance of the expressed state, which is typically a small fraction of an outcome event or of a standard deviation of the outcome (Boxes 1-3) and too small to reliably detect in any single instance. It becomes estimable only after the within-situation state-outcome relationship has been aggregated, across the many instances of situations with similar features that one person encounters over time or across many persons who differ in how much trait-relevant state expression they produce. The target of estimation is the average per-instance slope of the outcome on the state, which, multiplied by expressivity and scaled by exposure and time, reproduces the causal effect of n on Y (Equation 3).

Confounding is the central obstacle to estimating γ. The quantity of the state a person expresses in situations with given features is not randomly assigned: it covaries with other states, with stable dispositions, and with features of the situations the person occupies, each of which can affect Y through paths that bypass the focal state. Regressing the outcome on the aggregated state therefore conflates γ with the effects of these correlated causes. The cleanest estimates of γ accordingly come from designs that sever this dependence: experimental manipulation of the state in situations with given features (e.g., Schmiedek & Neubauer, 2020), or natural and quasiexperiments that supply exogenous variation in the state or at least in situational exposure (Grosz, Ayaita, et al., 2024). Purely observational and longitudinal designs identify γ only under strong assumptions about measured confounding (see Scope, Limitations, and Open Questions).

Intervention Design and Strategy

The consistency problem recurs on the intervention side. A broad-trait intervention moves a mix of narrow traits (Olaru et al., 2024), and outcome change is the PNT-weighted sum of their changes, so identical broad-trait interventions can yield different outcome change (Junker & Grosz, 2026; Prediction 3). The case for broad-trait targets, their breadth of consequences (Bleidorn et al., 2019), holds only when many outcomes are to be moved at once and the components' effects point the same way. Even then, a trial identifies the effect of the intervention package, not of the trait: because the mix of narrow traits moved is unknown, the effect can neither be attributed to a component nor be expected to transfer to another intervention on the same broad trait. The PNT framework therefore directs intervention programs to the narrow traits with the largest PNT for the outcome and population at stake.

The PNT framework distinguishes three levers. Trait-level interventions shift z and thus the state wherever the trait is expressed. Each SD of change moves the expected outcome by the PNT coefficient (Equation 2), plus the selection channel where λ ≠ 0 (Equation 9). They are favored when f, β, and γ are high across a wide region of the feature space, as for aspects of anxiety or impulsivity that recur across life domains and bear on well-being or longevity, respectively. Situated behavioral interventions change the state in situations with one or a few targeted feature configurations, holding z and exposure constant. Each unit of state change moves the expected outcome by T × f × γ, regardless of β. They are favored when f × γ is concentrated in a small region of the feature space and the targeted behavior is specifiable and cue-triggered, as when implementation intentions target medication adherence (Gollwitzer, 1999). Situation-selection interventions change f directly rather than through z, helping people seek out situations in which each instance's contribution to the outcome, γ × S, is positive (social engagement for loneliness, niche-picking in career development; cf.

Box 1) and avoid or reshape those in which it is negative (stimulus control in addiction treatment). They are favored when this per-instance contribution varies sharply across the feature space, people can control their exposure, and changing the trait or the within-situation state is impractical.

Which lever yields the largest gain thus depends on estimable parameters that vary across traits, outcomes, and persons, and levers can be combined when none suffices alone. In particular, because f and γ vary across persons, a lever can pay off for one participant but not for another, a difference that sample averages conceal.

Smartphone-based personality change interventions typically tailor content to participants' change goals, not to measured exposure and efficacy (e.g., Stieger et al., 2021). The most fine-grained study to date shows that the situational context of a prompt predicts adherence and momentary affect (Haehner et al., 2026). For example, participants completed tasks more often when at home and not moving. Furthermore, the best situation differed between strategies; for example, emotional-support tasks worked even under stress, whereas skill-building tasks were disliked then.

Relationship to Existing Frameworks and Theories

The ideas that went into the PNT framework are not new. Psychologists have long noted that small, situation-bound behavioral effects can accumulate into substantial long-run consequences (e.g., Abelson, 1985; Funder & Ozer, 2019). Decision-theoretic work in personnel psychology has recognized early that the effects of individual differences depend on contextual conditions (Brogden, 1949; Cronbach & Gleser, 1965). Interactionist work has developed the concepts of situational strength and situational affordances (e.g., Gibson, 1977; Jones, 2003; Judge & Zapata, 2015; Li et al., 2025) and systematic characterizations of situations (e.g., Parrigon et al., 2017; Rauthmann et al., 2014). And the "heterogeneity revolution" in behavioral science has emphasized that effects are person-and context-specific (Beck et al., in press; Bryan et al., 2021).

Closest to the present proposal is the neo-Lewinian analysis of Liou et al. ( 2023 S3). Even under ideal measurement, however, a narrow trait's PNT accounts only for the part of an outcome that runs through its states. Total predictability also depends on causes that bypass those states and is capped by exogenous intervening events.

Beyond this neo-Lewinian framing, PNT connects to several more specific traditions, each of which it absorbs as a component rather than competes with: The SORKC model (Wessels et al., 2016) supplies the response-consequence contingency and proximal/distal structure of γ. PERSOC (Back et al., 2011) supplies the account of how γ arises for interpersonal outcomes, where the state affects the outcome through interaction partners' perceptions and reactions. The cognitive-affective personality system (Mischel & Shoda, 1995), the Dynamics of Personality Approach (Quirin et al., 2020 (Quirin et al., , 2023)), and functional-field and expectancy × value accounts (Feather, 1982; Wood, 2021; Wood et al., 2025; Wood et al., 2015) supply candidate generative models of β. Finally, the situational-strength (Judge & Zapata, 2015; Li et al., 2025; Mischel, 1977) and situational-affordance (Gibson, 1977; Jones, 2003) literatures specify what β represents and when it is large. Text S5 develops each of these comparisons. The PNT framework combines these traditions and formalizes a step they describe only verbally: the rule by which within-episode consequences aggregate over situations and time into long-run outcomes.

Scope, Limitations, and Open Questions

The PNT framework is bounded by several assumptions and leaves several questions open. First, it assumes that the situations in which the state is expressed can be meaningfully described by features. For many states this is straightforward. For diffuse affective states or generalized mood it is not, and extending PNT to non-situational domains will require further work, perhaps through integration with distinct-emotions research (Mercadante et al., 2025). A related open problem is how finely β and γ should be allowed to vary with the features. A functional form that is too rigid hides variation that matters, whereas one that is too flexible cannot be estimated from the situations a person encounters.

Second, narrow-trait levels are treated as stable over T. For outcomes that cumulate over decades, within-person change can be substantial (Bleidorn et al., 2022), including change that feeds back from a person's own states through reflection (Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). That said, in the pooled estimates of the largest test to date, Big Five change never predicted outcomes more strongly than the paired static level (Wright & Jackson, 2023; see Prediction 3). Integrating PNT × z(t) over time is straightforward in principle but requires longitudinal estimation of z trajectories.

Third, γ is the framework's hardest component to estimate (see Estimating the Within-Situation Causal Efficacy). Experiments remedy confounding but face two further difficulties.

First, manipulating one behavior typically perturbs other behaviors, attention, and contextual cues (fat-handedness; Eronen, 2020). Second, self-reported outcomes are susceptible to demand effects (Corneille & Lush, 2023). The PNT framework does not dissolve these problems, but it localizes them to within-situation state-outcome links of narrow traits, which are more amenable to manipulation than trait-outcome links of broad traits. Its γ tests are likely most informative for extrapsychological outcomes (income, academic achievement, social status, objectively measured health).

Finally, the framework is silent on the origins of individual differences in its components (why persons differ in f, β, or γ), which require theories about genetic and environmental influences, development, socialization, and selection. Of the features, sources, and outcomes of individuality distinguished by Kuper, Breitmoser, et al. (2025), PNT addresses the outcomes and takes the rest as given, which is also a programmatic commitment to start from outcomes rather than traits. The guiding question thus becomes "why do people differ on this outcome, and which narrow traits are most powerful in producing it?" rather than "what does this trait predict?" This shift directly counters the effect-size magnification that Gandhi et al. (2024) document when researchers fix on a predictor rather than an outcome (Watts, 2017). Starting from an outcome and working backward through the decomposition specifies, in order, which situations are most consequential, which narrow traits are expressed in them, and which within-situation states carry the largest causal effects, then guides measurement and intervention accordingly.

Conclusion

The PNT framework formalizes a rule by which within-situation effects aggregate over Second, a trait shapes an outcome through the states it produces in the situations a person encounters. Therefore, the leverage for causal inference and intervention lies in those states and situations rather than in the trait in the abstract. The PNT framework thereby turns a set of longrecognized but loosely connected intuitions into a structure that is precise enough to explain trait-outcome links once its components are estimated, to test in its calibrated instances, and to intervene on.
