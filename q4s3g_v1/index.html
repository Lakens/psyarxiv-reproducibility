Participants were recruited using opportunity sampling via university email circulars, online noticeboards, and printed posters distributed across the University of St Andrews campus.

Exclusion criteria were specified as follows and participant data were excluded if any of the criteria applied: (a) failure of the comprehension-check following the task instructions; (b) total task completion time falling more than 50% below or above the sample mean, indicating either rushed non-engagement or interrupted responding, following duration-based screening practice for computer-administered tasks (Meade & Craig, 2012); (c) identical responses on ≥ 50% of consecutive trustworthiness ratings, indicating straight-lining (see Maniaci & Rogge, 2014; Meade & Craig, 2012); (d) failure on two or more of the three embedded attention-check items; or (e) a non-response rate exceeding 30% of trustworthiness trials, which would reduce the percondition trial count below the 10-trial minimum specified in the a priori power analysis (see above).

Each participant completed one of three Gorilla spreadsheets (i.e., trial structures), which intermixed the trust, filler, and catch trials, while the three attention checks were held at fixed positions across all versions to maintain an even spread throughout the experiment.

Following the tutorial, participants completed a short comprehension check confirming that they understood the task instructions and the use of the slider; failure to answer all items correctly met exclusion criterion (a).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Introduction Priming, Objects, and Social Meaning

The proposition that incidental cues can influence social judgements and behaviour has a longstanding presence in psychological research (Bargh & Chartrand, 1999; Loersch & Payne, 2011). In an early demonstration, Berkowitz and LePage (1967) found that previously provoked participants behaved more aggressively towards a confederate when a weapon was visible in the testing room, an effect attributed to the aggressive associations the weapon carried into an otherwise unrelated situation. However, the reliability of such object-based effects soon came under debate, as replications often produced weaker and less consistent findings than originally reported (e.g., Page & Scheidt, 1971; Turner & Simons, 1974). Partly for this reason, subsequent work came to favour word-based primes, which offered greater control over the concepts activated, particularly for less direct associations where a single object could evoke several meanings (Bargh & Chartrand, 2000; Higgins, 1996).

Research using word-based primes later demonstrated how this activation could bias interpersonal judgements. For example, Higgins et al. (1977) exposed participants to trait words such as reckless or adventurous as part of an ostensibly unrelated perceptual task, before they read information about a target person whose behaviour was open to either interpretation. Participants later judged the same ambiguous behaviour as reckless or as adventurous depending on which words they had previously encountered. Srull and Wyer (1979) showed that this assimilation grew stronger the more frequently and recently the trait category had been activated, tying the effect to the momentary accessibility of the primed concept rather than to any independently motivated inferences about the target. Together, these findings show that incidentally activated concepts can bias interpersonal judgement; the key question is whether object-derived concepts can do the same.

Although psychology has given comparatively little attention to whether discrete objects can act as primes, the broader social sciences, particularly consumer research, have long regarded objects and everyday physical settings as important influences on how people are perceived and judged (Solway et al., 2016). Within this literature, Kotler's (1973) foundational theory of atmospherics proposed that environmental features such as lighting, sound, and room layout communicate socially relevant information about a setting, including the behavioural norms associated with it. Subsequent research supported this proposition, showing that atmospheric cues can systematically influence behaviour and evaluative judgements, including impulse purchasing (Mohan et al., 2013) and perceptions of service quality (Baker et al., 1994; also see Bitner, 1992; Turley & Milliman, 2000). Crucially, this logic extends from whole settings to individual objects: Schuldt et al. (2012), for example, demonstrated that manipulating an object's visual angle alone, such as presenting an SUV head-on rather than in profile, was sufficient to alter observers' ratings of a target owner on status-and power-related traits. Collectively, these findings suggest that object-based cues can influence interpersonal evaluations, but only when the object's meaning maps fairly directly onto the trait being judged. Therefore, the current question is whether an object's category membership can carry meaning across into a judgement it has no direct bearing on.

One account of how objects come to hold such meaning comes from research in material culture, an interdisciplinary field spanning anthropology, sociology, and archaeology. This research has consistently shown that particular objects are associated with specific social settings and can convey the normative expectations of those environments, rather than any meaning intrinsic to the objects themselves (Csikszentmihalyi & Rochberg-Halton, 1981; Dant, 1999). From this perspective, objects may function as cues that activate the broader social schemas associated with their usual environments. For example, home-related objects are frequently associated with intimacy, familiarity, and care (Csikszentmihalyi & Rochberg-Halton, 1981; Rochberg-Halton, 1984), characteristics that closely align with trust (Dupuis & Thorns, 1998; Miller, 1997). By contrast, work-or office-related objects are more frequently associated with hierarchy, formality, and instrumental purpose (Fiske, 1991; Hodder, 2012; Nippert-Eng, 1996), characteristics of the formal, contractual interactions associated with reduced trust (Malhotra & Murnighan, 2002; Sitkin & Roth, 1993). These contrasting associations suggest that home-and work-related objects may have the potential to differentially influence trust-related judgements, providing a useful test of whether objectderived social meaning influences social perceptions. Yet whether such objects systematically influence trustworthiness judgements remains largely untested.

Establishing whether these associations shift trust judgements first requires specifying the mechanism by which an object might activate them. Within psychology, the most established framework for understanding how associated concepts become accessible is semantic priming, whereby a stimulus is easier to process when it follows a meaningfully related cue, as when bread is preceded by butter rather than an unrelated word (Meyer & Schvaneveldt, 1971). Such facilitation does not require two concepts to be learned through association or co-occurrence (e.g., nurse and doctor); it can also arise when a prime and a target share meaning, features, or category membership irrespective of a learned grouping (e.g., whale and dolphin; Hutchison, 2003; McRae & Boisvert, 1998). This distinction is relevant to the present study, as the objects used here (e.g., an ironing board or a teapot) are linked less by habitual co-occurrence than by their shared membership to a broader, potentially unlearned environmental category (i.e., the home). It is this category-based form of semantic relatedness and the social meaning attached to the category that the present manipulation depends on.

Classical network models of semantic memory explain how such relationships influence subsequent processing. These models represent concepts as nodes within a network connected by weighted links, so that activating one concept simultaneously raises the accessibility of those related to it (Collins & Loftus, 1975). This heightened accessibility, termed constructaccessibility, biases the interpretation of ambiguous targets towards the activated construct when that construct is relevant to the judgement being made (Higgins et al., 1977; Srull & Wyer, 1979). Such effects are strongest when information about the target is limited, so that the accessible construct, rather than the target itself, guides interpretation. Their direction, however, depends on the strength of the prime: moderate primes are assimilated into the judgement, whereas highly salient or extreme primes serve as a comparison standard, producing contrast and thereby shifting the judgement away from the primed construct (Herr, 1986). Therefore, because the everyday objects (home and work) used here are moderate rather than extreme primes, assimilation is predicted.

The conditions under which such accessibility affects subsequent judgements are specified by the situated inference model, which proposes that priming only arises when accessible content is misattributed to the perceiver's own response to the target and incorporated into its interpretation. On this account, the effect is strongest when the content is readily accessible and consistent with the perceiver's existing attitudes towards the primed category, and weaker when the prime is sufficiently salient to be recognised as an external cue rather than as part of the perceiver's own reaction (Loersch & Payne, 2011). The same object-based prime may therefore affect one perceiver and not another, contingent on each perceiver's context-specific association with it. Together, these two accounts explain how the social meaning of an object category could shift judgements of an ambiguous target and why that shift may vary from person to person.

A further question is whether these semantic processes are specific to words or extend to object images. Neurophysiological evidence indicates that they are not confined to words: the N400, an event-related potential that indexes the ease of integrating a stimulus into a semantic context, is elicited comparably by words and object images (Kutas & Federmeier, 2011; Lau et al., 2008; Salisbury, 2004; Willems et al., 2008). This cross-format equivalence is consistent with a partially overlapping, transmodal semantic system, in which concepts are represented across modalities rather than tied to a single verbal format (Lambon Ralph et al., 2017), supporting the extension of predominantly word-based priming accounts to object-based primes.

The timing of a prime also matters. Although no single theory captures the full range of semantic priming effects (Neely, 1991), a prominent account, two-process theory, distinguishes between two mechanisms that operate over different temporal intervals (Neely, 1977a (Neely, , 1991)). The first, spreading activation, is a fast, automatic, bottom-up process. It briefly raises the accessibility of closely related or over-learned concepts before decaying within a few hundred milliseconds. It is therefore most readily observed at short prime-to-target intervals (Neely, 1977b; Neely et al., 2010). The second, controlled processing, is a slower, strategic, top-down process in which a prime is used to generate expectations about a subsequent target. This process can apply semantic relationships more flexibly, extending activation to less closely associated concepts when sufficient time is available to redirect attention (Gawronski & Bodenhausen, 2005; Neely, 1977b; Posner & Snyder, 1975). This distinction is particularly relevant to the present study, as the proposed association between an object and a social category is cross-domain and therefore less likely to arise through automatic spreading activation. Consequently, the present design favours the controlled-processing account (see Design Rationale). With the mechanism specified, the question becomes whether object categories have been shown to activate social concepts in this way.

Evidence that object categories can produce cross-domain social activation comes from Kay et al. (2004). Across five experiments, participants were exposed to business-related objects (e.g., briefcases) during an ostensibly unrelated task. This exposure increased the accessibility of competition-related concepts, biased ambiguous social interactions towards more competitive interpretations, and shifted offers in an economic bargaining game towards greater self-interest, even though the objects bore no relation to the subsequent tasks. These findings suggest that an object's membership within a coherent environmental category can activate associated social concepts, which can influence subsequent social judgements and behaviour. Importantly, their fifth experiment showed that this effect emerged only when the situation was ambiguous, indicating that the activated concept shaped how an underspecified situation was interpreted rather than producing a fixed response. Neuberg (1988) reported a related pattern using subliminal competition-related word primes, wherein competitively predisposed participants played more competitively towards a partner, but only once the partner had made a move that could be interpreted, suggesting that the prime biased how participants construed their partner's behaviour rather than altering their behaviour directly. Together, these studies show that primed social concepts can bias the reading of ambiguous interpersonal information.

Whether trustworthiness is open to this same kind of priming first requires distinguishing it from interpersonal trust, which most of the existing priming literature has targeted (Todorov et al., 2015). Interpersonal trust is the willingness to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations about another person's intentions (Mayer et al., 1995; Rousseau et al., 1998). It is commonly measured with economic trust games, in which a participant entrusts a sum of money to another person; the sum is multiplied, the recipient returns however much they choose, and the amount initially entrusted serves as the behavioural index of trust (Berg et al., 1995; Johnson & Mislin, 2011). Trustworthiness, by contrast, is the property attributed to a person that gives rise to that willingness (Mayer et al., 1995). The present study targets this latter attribution rather than the behavioural decisions related to trust, so the following evidence is considered with this distinction in mind. Posten et al. (2014) provide a particularly direct test of whether trustworthiness itself can be primed. Using a subliminal priming manipulation, they exposed participants to the words trust or distrust and found that unfamiliar faces were subsequently judged more trustworthy following the trust prime than the distrust prime. The same primes also increased the amount participants sent in a subsequent economic trust game, indicating that the effect extended beyond perceived trustworthiness to trusting behaviour. Verosky and Todorov (2010) similarly showed that minimal behavioural information can alter trustworthiness judgements: after participants learned to associate unfamiliar faces with a single positive, neutral, or negative statement about the person's behaviour, ratings shifted accordingly, and the effect generalised to novel faces that merely resembled the learned ones. Together, these findings show that trustworthiness judgements can be altered by information external to the face itself. However, both demonstrations rely on relatively direct routes, either providing explicit evaluative information about the person or transferring affect to the face. It therefore remains unclear whether a more indirect prime, whose social meaning derives from an everyday environment, can produce a similar shift in trustworthiness.

The study that comes closest to addressing this possibility is Huang and Murnighan (2010). Across two experiments, participants first rated the names of people and, in the second experiment, objects as either liked or disliked. One such name or object was then presented subliminally before participants acted as the sender in an economic trust game. Participants primed with a liked name sent more money than those primed with either the disliked name or the control name (nonsense syllable). In contrast, liked and disliked object primes produced no significant difference in the amount sent to the target. Because the name and object primes were matched in valence using each participant's liked/disliked ratings, the authors argued that the effect depended on the primes' relation to the person rather than on the general pleasantness of the objects or their broader social associations. They speculated that certain objects may carry particular social connotations that could introduce uncontrolled effects and called for future research to use coherent, socially meaningful object categories to determine whether objectbased primes could influence trust.

Taken together, the literature reviewed above shows that trustworthiness can be shifted by external information. Still, it leaves unclear whether an everyday object category can do so through its broader social meaning, rather than the idiosyncratic preferences tested previously. This represents the specific research gap addressed by the present study and has both practical and theoretical significance. If incidental object cues can bias trustworthiness judgements when information about a person is sparse, they may represent a subtle source of context-dependent bias in social perception. Such a finding would also provide behavioural evidence that objectderived meaning, like verbal meaning, can influence social perception, potentially through the shared multimodal semantic system outlined earlier.

The Present Study: Design Rationale and Predictions

The present study tests this possibility by comparing three prime categories: home-and workrelated objects, with their contrasting trust-relevant associations, and novel objects with no preexisting trust-relevant meaning. The Novel category provides a baseline that controls for object exposure and processing, while allowing differences in the Home and Work conditions to be attributed more specifically to social-category content. Having established trustworthiness as the target judgement, the next choice concerns how to operationalise it: first the form in which it is elicited, and then the instrument used to record it. The present study elicits trustworthiness judgements from unfamiliar faces. Such impressions form rapidly and are highly consensual across perceivers (Willis & Todorov, 2006), making them a quick and reliable target. Despite this stability, they remain open to information beyond the face itself. As noted above, subliminal trust primes and single items of behavioural information can both shift them (Posten et al., 2014; Verosky & Todorov, 2010), and the surrounding social context matters too: for example, faces presented in threatening contexts are rated less trustworthy than the same faces in neutral ones (Brambilla et al., 2018; see also Hooker et al., 2011; Cai et al., 2020; Todorov et al., 2015). This combination of a fast, consensual judgement that is nonetheless sensitive to context makes facial trustworthiness a suitable outcome for testing whether object primes influence social perception.

Trustworthiness has more often been measured in other ways. As mentioned, economic trust games (Berg et al., 1995) index the behavioural decision to trust, while dispositional scales such as Rotter's (1967) 25-item Interpersonal Trust Scale measure generalised expectations of others' reliability. These capture behavioural trust or a stable disposition rather than the targetspecific impression of interest here (Evans & Revelle, 2008). The present study therefore records facial trustworthiness on a continuous 0 to 100 slider adapted from Pehlivanoglu et al. (2023), which preserves sensitivity to the small, bidirectional shifts the priming manipulation is expected to produce (Reips & Funke, 2008). A single-item rating keeps each judgement brief and unobtrusive, and this format yields ratings comparable to those from longer instruments (Bowling, 2005).

A remaining design choice concerns the onset interval between the prime and target, or stimulus-onset asynchrony (SOA). As set out above, a cross-domain inference from an object to a social category is expected to rely on controlled rather than automatic processing. Automatic spreading activation is most readily captured at short SOAs of around 250 ms or less (Neely, 1977b; Neely et al., 2010), whereas controlled processing requires a longer interval, typically 400 ms or more, for attention to be redirected (Gawronski & Bodenhausen, 2005; Posner & Snyder, 1975). The present design therefore uses a 1,000-ms SOA to favour controlled, sustained accessibility over automatic activation (Bargh et al., 1988). Because this form of processing requires the prime to be consciously perceived, the objects are presented explicitly, following Kay et al. (2004), rather than the subliminal primes used by Posten et al. (2014) and Huang and Murnighan (2010). Conscious presentation nevertheless introduces a potential demand characteristic: participants may notice the manipulation (the change in object categories) and infer the experiment's purpose, a replicability issue that was proposed to have contributed to the inflated weapon-priming effect discussed earlier (Page & Scheidt, 1971; Turner & Simons, 1974). The procedure was therefore designed to minimise this possibility (see Procedure).

Finally, because the situated inference model predicts stronger priming when the primed category is more accessible and consistent with the perceiver's dispositions (consistent with the disposition dependence reported by Kay et al., 2004; Neuberg, 1988), exploratory analyses examined whether participants' affective relationships with home and workplace environments and their degree of workplace experience moderated the effects. Affective relationships indexed disposition towards each category, whereas workplace experience indexed the availability of work-related representations. These analyses were underpowered given the present sample size and were not part of the primary test of object-based priming.

Two directional predictions follow. First, if domestically affiliated objects activate trustrelevant concepts through their association with the home (Collins & Loftus, 1975; Dant, 1999), trustworthiness ratings following Home-object primes will be higher than those following Novel-object primes (H 1 ). Second, if Work-affiliated objects activate competition-and self-interest-related concepts, as suggested by Kay et al. (2004), trustworthiness ratings following Work-object primes will be lower than those following Novel-object primes (H 2 ). Exploratory analyses will also examine whether individual differences in participants' affective relationships with the home and work environments, and if their workplace experience, moderate these effects.

Methods

Participants Participant Characteristics

Thirty participants were recruited using opportunity sampling from the University of St Andrews community. The recruited sample (24 from Scotland, 3 from England, 2 from the USA, and 1 from Italy; 26 female, 4 male) had a mean age of 24.8 years (SD = 4.9, range 19 to 42). Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 16 years, fluency in English, normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and having been raised in a Western country between the ages of 7 and 15, to ensure shared cultural associations with the object categories (Soley, 2019). These criteria were stated prior to booking and confirmed on arrival via a yes/no screening questionnaire covering childhood upbringing, vision, age, fluency, and willingness to participate; answering "no" to any item prevented continuation. Two participants were subsequently excluded during data screening, leaving a final analysed sample of N = 28 (see Data Handling and Exclusions); because data were anonymised at collection, the demographic characteristics reported here describe the full recruited sample. The subsections below describe the a priori power analysis, recruitment procedure, exclusion criteria, and ethical considerations.

A Priori Power Analysis

A simulation-based a priori power analysis was conducted using the simr package (Version 1.0.9; Green & MacLeod, 2016) in R (Version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026), following recommendations for linear mixed models (LMM; Kumle et al., 2021; Meteyard & Davies, 2020). A dataset was simulated to reflect a one-way within-subjects design with three condition categories (Home, Work, Novel), each providing 10 trials per condition (840 total observations) for a presumed sample of 28 participants. The final design included 15 trials per condition; the lower simulated trial count of 10 therefore provided a conservative estimate that accounted for potential trial losses, as participants could skip trials during the experiment. A small-to-medium effect size was specified (β = 3; f = 0.15), below Kay et al.'s (2004) reported effects (partial η² = 0.13 to 0.32; f = 0.39 to 0.69). This conservative estimate is motivated by two considerations: the shift in the dependent variable from competition to trustworthiness, which may attenuate effects (Cesario, 2014), and broader cautions against overestimating reported priming effects (McCarthy et al., 2018; Open Science Collaboration, 2015). Random intercepts were specified for participants (ICC = .220)foot_0 , with a residual standard deviation of σ = 13 and a grand mean of 48 assumed. Based on 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations of the specified model parameters (seed = 2025 for reproducibility, powerSim, α = 0.05), the analysis returned 81.90% power (95% CI [79.37, 84.24]) at 28 participants. The power curve indicated that 80% was reached between 25 and 28 participants. Therefore, a recruitment target of 30 participants was set to increase power further and allow for potential participant exclusions.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited using opportunity sampling via university email circulars, online noticeboards, and printed posters distributed across the University of St Andrews campus. All recruitment materials were deliberately general and did not disclose the study aims (e.g., the role of the object stimuli or the hypothesised direction of effects), to minimise demand characteristics (Nichols & Maner, 2008) and preserve the integrity of the manipulation (Doyen et al., 2012). Individuals who registered interest were shown the participant information sheet (PIS) to verify eligibility and, if eligible, were invited to book an in-person session. Participation was entirely voluntary, and no individuals were approached directly. Recruitment took place between May and July 2026.

Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion criteria were specified as follows and participant data were excluded if any of the criteria applied: (a) failure of the comprehension-check following the task instructions; (b) total task completion time falling more than 50% below or above the sample mean, indicating either rushed non-engagement or interrupted responding, following duration-based screening practice for computer-administered tasks (Meade & Craig, 2012); (c) identical responses on ≥ 50% of consecutive trustworthiness ratings, indicating straight-lining (see Maniaci & Rogge, 2014; Meade & Craig, 2012); (d) failure on two or more of the three embedded attention-check items; or (e) a non-response rate exceeding 30% of trustworthiness trials, which would reduce the percondition trial count below the 10-trial minimum specified in the a priori power analysis (see above).

Ethics and Participant Welfare

The study was assessed as minimal risk and approved by the University of St Andrews Teaching and Research Ethics Committee (UTREC; approval reference PS-1485-2292-2026). Full hypothesis disclosure was withheld at the outset to prevent demand characteristics; this incomplete disclosure was judged proportionate to the minimal risk of the study and mitigated by disclosure within the debrief. Potential discomfort from home or workplace content was addressed through a generalised PIS, freedom to withdraw without penalty, and signposting to student support services at debrief. Compensation was fixed at £10, non-contingent on performance or completion, consistent with University guidance and the current UK National Living Wage (effective April 1 st 2026; GOV.UK, 2026). Sessions lasted ~30 minutes. Data collection took place between May and July 2026. Data were anonymised at collection and stored on the University's encrypted OneDrive system. The researcher did not collect payment details; they were handled by the School Finance Administrator, stored in the University's encrypted OneDrive system, separate from the research data, and deleted once payment processing was complete.

Materials Apparatus

The task was administered via the Gorilla experiment platform, accessed through a web browser running on macOS 26.6.1. The experimental interface and stimuli were displayed on an external 24-inch (16:9) LCD monitor (1920 × 1080 pixels, 60 Hz) at a viewing distance of ~80 cm, maintained with a forehead-and-chin rest. Responses were recorded via a standard QWERTY keyboard and mouse.

Priming Object Stimuli

Object images were drawn from the IMAGINE database (Cooper et al., 2023), which provides standardised photographs of familiar objects and computer-generated images of "Novel objects" presented in a common photographic style against a uniform white background. Images were normalised for low-level visual attributes including size, contrast, colourfulness, luminance and resolution. From the familiar object set, 48 images were selected and recategorised into two sub-categories of 24 images each: home objects (the experimental primes) and work objects (the contrast primes). A further 24 images were drawn at random from the novel objects set to serve as control primes, yielding 72 total prime stimuli across three categories, each of equal size (Figure 1). Note. All images are normalised for size, contrast, luminance, and colourfulness and presented against a uniform white background.

Home objects (experimental primes): Items strongly associated with domestic spaces (e.g., an ironing board and teapot). The selection of canonical household items was guided by the material-culture literature as identified above (Csikszentmihalyi & Rochberg-Halton, 1981; Dant, 1999), and for their broad recognisability across Western domestic contexts.

Work objects (contrast primes): Items strongly associated with workplace environments (e.g., manila files, filing cabinet, executive pen). The category was guided by the material-culture literature (Fiske, 1991; Hodder, 2012; Nippert-Eng, 1996) and defined in accordance with Kay et al. (2004).

Novel objects (control primes): AI-generated images that present as objects but lack identifiable real-world referents, randomly drawn from the Novel-object subset of Cooper et al. (2023). These provide a baseline that indexes responses to a visual stimulus in the absence of meaningful contextual or semantic associations.

Face Stimuli

Face stimuli were drawn from the Oosterhof and Todorov (2008) database, consisting of computer-generated faces varying parametrically along trustworthiness and dominance dimensions, generated in FaceGen Modeller 3.2. All faces were male, bald, Caucasian and front-facing, with neutral expressions and a direct gaze (Figure 2). To isolate trustworthiness, dominance was held constant at its midpoint (dominance = 0). The trustworthiness dimension was sampled at seven equally spaced levels, from very untrustworthy (-3) to very trustworthy (+3). The samples were gathered through the "490 Faces (2 orthogonal models)" database by extracting items 3, 10, 17, 24, 31, 38 and 45 from each identity file 1 to 10.

Trustworthiness Rating Item

Trustworthiness was rated using a single-item visual analogue scale (VAS), adapted from Pehlivanoglu et al. (2023). For analysed trials, each participant was asked, "How trustworthy is this face?" and responded by moving a slider along a continuous horizontal line, anchored at 0 ("Not at all trustworthy") to 100 ("Extremely trustworthy"). This continuous format was selected to avoid the response compression inherent to discrete Likert scales (Reips & Funke, 2008) and to allow participants to directly map their immediate, pre-reflective gut response onto a spatial continuum -bypassing the discrete categorisation that would disrupt the rapid first impressions of trustworthiness targeted by the manipulation (Bowling, 2005; Castle et al., 2012; Evans & Revelle, 2008; Willis & Todorov, 2006).

Affiliation Probes

Three self-reported items were administered at the end of the session, after debriefing, to index participants' evaluation of home environments, their evaluation of workplace environments, and their degree of workplace exposure, for use in the exploratory analyses. Each exploratory item appeared on separate sliders and used a continuous 0 to 100 VAS, consistent with the VAS measures used in the experimental phase (Bowling, 2005). Administering these items after the debriefing prevented participants from recognising the Home/Work prime categories; however, it also means that the affiliation ratings index a state measured after exposure to the primes, which may affect their accuracy (Howard et al., 1979). The three items were: (i) "How positive is your overall relationship with the home environments you have lived in?", anchored at0 = "extremely negative" and100 = "extremely positive"; (ii) "How positive is your overall relationship with the workplace or office environments you have experienced?", anchored at0 = "extremely negative" and100 = "extremely positive"; (iii) "How much personal experience do you have with traditional workplace or office environments (for example, paid work in an office)?", anchored at0 = "no experience at all" and100 = "a great deal of experience".

Research Setting

Sessions were run individually, under the supervision of the experimenter, in the University of St Andrews School of Psychology and Neuroscience 3D Vision lab. The forehead-and-chin rest used in the study held viewing distance and visual angle constant across participants and restricted the participant's field of view to the screen. The latter precaution was important as the laboratory itself contains some workplace-affiliated objects (e.g., filing cabinets, office furniture) potentially capable of raising work-related associations independent of the experimental primes (Kay et al., 2004). Ambient lighting was held consistent across sessions and external noise was minimised.

Design

The study employed a within-subjects priming design with the three prime categories (Home, Work, and Novel). Participants rated face trustworthiness (0 to 100 VAS) across 45 trials split evenly across prime categories, yielding repeated measures at both participant and item (face) levels. Each participant completed one of three Gorilla spreadsheets (i.e., trial structures), which intermixed the trust, filler, and catch trials, while the three attention checks were held at fixed positions across all versions to maintain an even spread throughout the experiment. Within each spreadsheet, prime frequencies and face-trustworthiness levels were balanced every ~10 trials (across 7 blocks), to prevent consistent prime-face pairings. The design also included the three (post-debrief) affiliation probe items, which assessed participants' individual category preferences. This follows established priming paradigms (Bargh et al., 1996; Kay et al., 2004) while adhering to replicability recommendations regarding power and counterbalancing (Doyen et al., 2012; Munafò et al., 2017).

Procedure

On arrival, participants viewed the PIS on screen and ticked confirmation boxes to confirm they had read each section, met eligibility, and provided consent; Gorilla prevented progression to the experimental phase unless all boxes were ticked. Participants then completed a brief tutorial (one demonstrated trial and three practice trials, all using the 0 to 100 VAS) involving fillerstyle perceptual judgements (e.g., age) of faces and displayed random images from the Cooper et al. (2023) database, unrelated to the experimental prime categories. This familiarised participants with the interface without directing attention to trust or the Home/Work/Novel categories. Following the tutorial, participants completed a short comprehension check confirming that they understood the task instructions and the use of the slider; failure to answer all items correctly met exclusion criterion (a).

This was then followed by the experimental phase, which comprised 73 trials in total: 45 main trustworthiness trials, 18 filler trials, 7 catch trials, and 3 attention checks, distributed across the 7 blocks, with three self-paced breaks. The 45 main trustworthiness trials each proceeded as follows: central fixation cross (2,000 ms), object prime (1,000 ms), second fixation cross (1,000 ms), face stimulus (1,000 ms), self-paced trustworthiness rating (0 to 100 VAS; "How trustworthy is this face?"), and a 2,000 ms inter-trial interval. Filler trials (×18) were scattered throughout to interrupt the sequence of trustworthiness questions and reduce participants' ability to infer the hypothesis; these used the main trial structure but asked about a different facial attribute on the same 0 to 100 VAS (age, dominance, approachability, attractiveness, intelligence, or health; e.g., "How healthy does this face look?"). Catch trials (×7) required a response about the object prime rather than the face, to sustain participants' attention to the primes (e.g., "How clearly do you remember the object you just saw?" from 0 "not at all" to 100 "extremely well"). Attention checks (×3) were fixed at trials 16, 38, and 57 across the three spreadsheets; these presented explicit instructions (e.g., "Please move the slider all the way to one hundred, to show you are reading") to comply with exclusion criterion (d). Three self-paced breaks were positioned at trials 22, 44, and 66, each carrying the message: "Take a break; take as long as you need and press continue when ready". Participants then received a written debrief describing the study's rationale, which also provided the researcher's contact details. After the debrief, they completed the two Home/Work affiliation items alongside the work experience item (described above).

Data Analysis Data Handling and Exclusions

Two participants were excluded for non-response exceeding 30% of trustworthiness trials (31.1% and 64.4% missing respectively) as per exclusion criteria (e); no other exclusion criteria (a-d) were met, leaving a final analytic sample of N = 28 and 1,217 valid trustworthiness-rating trials (Novel n = 410, Home n = 403, and Work n = 404) across all participants. Anonymisation prevented participant characteristic linkage post-exclusion. Among the retained participants only 3.4% of trials were missing (43 of 1,260); these did not contribute to the model, which was fitted to all trials with complete data. Missingness was unrelated to prime condition and related only to face-trust level, a variable already in the model (see Appendix B), so the data can plausibly be treated as missing at random, under which restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation (the method used to fit the models below), remains unbiased. Filler and catch trials were excluded prior to model fitting.

General Analytic Procedures

The analysis is a within-participant comparison: it asks whether trustworthiness ratings differ across the three prime conditions (Home, Work, Novel). What complicates a simple comparison is the repeated-measures design, as each participant rated every face identity and level, so trials were correlated both within participants and within face identities (Baayen et al., 2008); a linear regression would treat all 1,217 trials as independent and understate the standard errors (Barr et al., 2013). Therefore, a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) is the appropriate tool here, running the same comparison of prime conditions while allowing each participant and each face their own baseline rating, so that repeated ratings from the same person or face are not mistaken for effects of the primes (Baayen et al., 2008; see also Brown, 2021). Consequently, crossed random intercepts were specified for participant and face identity (28 participants, 9 face identities), modelling both sources directly (Baayen et al., 2008; Judd et al., 2012). The model was estimated by REML, which is the recommended estimation method when interpreting random effects in a single fitted model (Bates et al., 2015), with Satterthwaite (1946) approximate degrees of freedom used to evaluate fixed effects given that exact degrees of freedom cannot be determined analytically in mixed models. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = .05. All statistical analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 31; macOS). All figures were produced in R (Version 4.6.1, using the ggplot2, ggeffects, lme4 and haven packages; macOS).

Model assumptions were assessed; a full list of the assumptions and the procedures used are reported in Appendix A. Face identity was treated as a random effect throughout, its intercept retained because the repeated-measures design requires it. Random slopes for prime were not fitted; the rationale, and the safeguard applied to any significant prime effect, are described in Appendix A.

Primary Analysis: Trial-Level Mixed-Effects Model

A trial-level LMM was fitted to the trustworthiness ratings, estimated on 1,217 valid trials from the final analytic sample (N = 28). This main-effects model (Model 1) included two dummycoded prime variables (prime_home and prime_work) and a continuous covariate for the normative trustworthiness level of each face (face_trust_level) based on Oosterhof and Todorov's (2008) original rankings. A significant prime term would indicate that the preceding object category (Home and/or Work) shifted how trustworthy the face appeared relative to the Novel baseline and each face's own normative trustworthiness, which the covariate holds constant.

Two further models were then fitted. Model 2 added prime × face-trust level interaction terms, testing whether any priming effect varied across the trustworthiness dimension (i.e., whether the primes mattered more for some faces than others). Model 3 replaced these with prime × ambiguity interactions, comparing ambiguous faces (normative levels -1 to +1) with unambiguous faces (±3). A significant prime × ambiguity interaction would indicate that the prime effect differed by the face's ambiguity, consistent with the expectation that primes exert more influence when the target is ambiguous. Together, these two models tested whether any prime effects were concentrated among particular faces rather than spread evenly across the trustworthiness dimension.

The two prime contrasts (Home, Work) and face-trust level all vary within participants and within face identities and are therefore trial-level (level-1) predictors; the model includes no predictor that varies between participants or between faces (i.e., no level-2 predictor); whether effects differed between participants is taken up separately in the following exploratory analyses. The model is specified in full in Appendix A.

Exploratory Analysis: Within-Participant Profile Groups

The first exploratory analysis asks whether the primes worked differently for different people.

It was motivated a priori by the situated inference model (Loersch & Payne, 2011), which holds that a prime exerts a stronger influence when the primed category fits the perceiver's own dispositions. If so, participants who feel more positively about home than work environments, or the reverse, might respond differently to Home and Work primes, an effect that could be masked in the sample as a whole. Participants were grouped by their differences in Home and Work affiliation ratings, classifying participants as Home-leaning (Home > Work by more than 20 points), Work-leaning (Work > Home by more than 20 points), or Balanced (all remaining participants). Group membership was dummy-coded with Balanced as the reference category, yielding two dummy variables (Home-leaning vs Balanced; Work-leaning vs Balanced); these were entered into the trial-level model alongside all four resulting Prime Category × Group interactions. The random structure and estimation method were as described above, the mixed-effects framework being retained as the same two crossed sources of non-independence apply and because it estimates the group contrasts at the participant level while retaining all trials. The assumption checks described for the primary analysis (Appendix A) were repeated for this model, because residuals are model-specific (Brown, 2021; Field, 2024; Meteyard & Davies, 2020).

Two features of this analysis limit how far its results can be interpreted. First, four Prime Category × Group interactions are tested simultaneously, so the probability that at least one reaches p < .05 by chance alone is ~19% rather than 5%. The significance threshold for this family of tests was therefore Bonferroni-corrected to α = .0125 (.05 / 4; Field, 2024). Therefore, any interaction reaching the conventional threshold (α = .05) but not the corrected threshold, was treated as tentative at most and followed up with a focused sensitivity analysis, in which that term was tested in isolation against all other participants to assess its robustness to the grouping specification. Second, and more fundamentally, affiliation group is a characteristic of the participant, not of the trial. Its interactions with the primes are therefore estimated between participants (a level-2 comparison across only 28 individuals) rather than across the 1,217 trials. These estimates draw their power from the number of participants per group, not the trial count, which makes them far less precise than the primary effects. The results of this analysis are accordingly treated as exploratory and should be interpreted with caution (Field, 2024).

Exploratory Analysis: Work Experience as a Continuous Moderator

The final analysis follows the same accessibility logic as the profile group analysis. Applied to the full analytic sample, it tested whether individual differences in workplace exposure moderated priming effects. Planned a priori from theory, it was motivated by the situated inference model (Loersch & Payne, 2011), which holds that a prime influences judgement only when the primed representation is accessible, so participants with greater workplace exposure, and thus more accessible work-related representations, should show a stronger Work prime effect. The prediction therefore concerns the Work prime specifically: a significant Work prime × experience interaction would indicate that the Work prime shifted trustworthiness ratings more strongly in participants with more workplace exposure. The Home prime interaction was included for symmetry but has no corresponding theoretical basis. Self-reported work experience (range 0 to 100, M = 40.36, SD = 36.68) was mean-centred and entered as a fixed effect together with its interactions with each prime contrast. All other modelling choices, including the crossed random-intercept structure, estimation method and assumption checks, were as described for the primary analysis; because residuals are model-specific, they were reapplied to this model (see Appendix A). As with the profile groups, work experience is a between-participant (level-2) predictor and its prime interactions are therefore, cross-level interactions estimated across only 28 participants. This is the lower bound for such tests, leaving the moderation underpowered by construction (Hox et al., 2018); its results should therefore be read with caution and as hypothesis-generating only.

They did not: the small prime effects were relatively constant across face-trust levels (Home × FTL, b = 0.41, 95% CI [-0.75, 1.56], p = .488; Work × FTL, b = 0.24, 95% CI [-0.92, 1.41], p = .685; Figure 3). A further model tested whether priming effects differed between ambiguous (-1 to +1) and unambiguous (±3) faces, given the greater potential susceptibility of ambiguous faces to priming (Higgins et al., 1977; Kay et al., 2004). Neither interaction was significant (Home × ambiguity, b = 2.18, 95% CI [-3.25, 7.60], p = .431; Work × ambiguity, b = 1.32, 95% CI [-4.19, 6.82], p = .638), and the ambiguity main effect was also non-significant (b = 1.20, 95% CI [-2.62, 5.01], p = .538). There was therefore no evidence that the effects of the primes varied according to face-trust level or face ambiguity.

One trial had a large standardised residual (3.40, exceeding the conventional |z| > 3.29 threshold; see Appendix B). Since this outlier was a Novel prime condition, removing it could shift the Novel baseline that both prime contrasts are measured against, so it was retained in the primary model and its influence checked by re-fitting the model without it. When refitted, the direction of all effects was unchanged, however, the Home-versus-Novel contrast now reached conventional significance (b = 2.40, 95% CI [0.08, 4.72], p = .043), while the Work-versus-Novel contrast remained just short of significance (p = .053). Therefore, the evidence for a priming effect was somewhat sensitive to this single trial. However, because both primeversus-Novel contrasts increased when the trial was removed, the change appears to reflect, at least in part, a change in the estimated Novel reference level rather than a uniquely increased Home effect. Full estimates are reported in Appendix B.

Within-Participant Profile Groups

The first exploratory analysis tested whether participants' relative affiliation with Home versus Work moderated the effects of the object primes on trustworthiness ratings, comparing Homeleaning and Work-leaning participants with the Balanced group. Applying the Home-minus-Work difference rule yielded three groups: Home-leaning (n = 5; ≤225 trials), Work-leaning (n = 4; ≤180 trials), and Balanced (n = 19; ≤855 trials). The model was fitted to the same 1,217 trials from N = 28 participants and 9 face identities as the primary analysis. As in the primary model, random intercepts were specified for participants and face identities, and model assumptions were checked and were satisfactory (see Appendix C). The estimated variance components and ICCs were also similar to those of the primary model (see Appendix C).

Overall, there was little evidence that affiliation systematically altered the prime effects. Face baseline trustworthiness remained the strongest predictor and was essentially unchanged from the primary model (b = 4.61, 95% CI [4.15, 5.07], p < .001). Within the Balanced reference group, neither prime reliably shifted ratings (Home, b = 1.26, 95% CI [-1.53, 4.05], p = .375; Work, b = 1.81, 95% CI [-0.97, 4.60], p = .201). Nonetheless, one interaction reached the conventional threshold (α = .05), but not the Bonferroni-corrected one (α = .0125): the Homeversus-Novel contrast was ~6.6 points larger among Home-leaning than Balanced participants (b = 6.59, 95% CI [0.52, 12.66], p = .033). However, this interaction is difficult to interpret reliably as a Home-specific priming effect, since Home-leaning participants also tended to rate Novel-primed faces lower than the Balanced group (b = -6.87, 95% CI [-14.00, 0.26], p = .058; Figure 5). This is important because the Home prime effect is measured relative to that Novel baseline, and this lower baseline can inflate the Home-versus-Novel contrast, so the interaction may reflect this depressed baseline rather than a specific Home prime effect (this within-group pattern is shown in Appendix C; Figure 6). The interaction's robustness to a by-participant random-slope structure could not be established, as that model did not converge (Appendix C). The other three prime × group interactions were non-significant (Appendix C, Table 3).

Additionally, the observed interaction remained similar when the outlier trial was removed (b = 6.39, p = .038; see Appendix C), indicating that the interaction was not substantially altered by that single Novel trial. Work Experience as a Continuous Moderator

The final analysis tested whether work experience moderated the effects of the object primes, on the reasoning that greater exposure to workplace environments might make work-related associations more accessible and therefore increase sensitivity to the primes (Loersch & Payne, 2011). All assumptions were checked and were satisfactory, and random-intercept specifications were equivalent to those of the primary model; the variance components and model diagnostics differed only slightly and were non-consequential (see Appendix D).

The analysis provided no evidence of moderation. As in the primary analysis, face baseline trustworthiness strongly predicted ratings, b = 4.61, 95% CI [4.15, 5.07], p < .001, while the Home-versus-Novel contrast (b = 2.23, p = .061) and the Work-versus-Novel contrast (b = 2.08, p = .075) remained small and non-significant. Thus, the pattern of the primary prime effects was unchanged when work experience was included in the model. Work experience itself was not associated with overall trustworthiness ratings, b = -0.01, 95% CI [-0.09, 0.07], p = .796, indicating no evidence that participants with more work experience rated the faces differently overall. More importantly, neither prime × work experience interaction was significant: the Home prime × work experience interaction was b = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.10], p = .217, and the Work prime × work experience interaction was b = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.09], p = .491. These results provide no evidence that work experience altered the effect of either object category on trustworthiness ratings. Furthermore, these conclusions were unchanged when the outlier trial was removed (see Appendix D).

Purpose

The purpose of this study was to test whether everyday objects carry socially relevant meanings capable of influencing how people judge others. Specifically, it asked whether an object's membership of a broad social category, the home or the workplace, could shift the trustworthiness attributed to an unfamiliar face, following the call by Huang and Murnighan (2010) for research using coherent, socially meaningful object categories. Two directional hypotheses were set out in a data analysis plan before the data were analysed: trustworthiness ratings following Home-object primes would be higher than those following Novel-object primes (H₁), and ratings following Work-object primes would be lower than those following Novel-object primes (H₂). Exploratory analyses would then examine whether participants' affective relationships with the home and work environments, and their workplace experience, moderated these effects.

Neither hypothesis was supported. Home and Work primes instead produced small, similar, non-significant increases relative to Novel objects, so the study did not produce the categoryspecific divergence it was designed to detect. Because no manipulation check was included, the design cannot separate a failure of the objects to activate their intended constructs from a failure of those constructs to bear on the judgement (see Limitations and Future Directions).

Discussion

This study examined whether everyday objects carry socially relevant meanings capable of influencing how people judge others, specifically whether brief exposure to home-related, work-related, or novel objects would alter the trustworthiness attributed to unfamiliar faces. This question has practical significance because incidental environmental cues that influence trustworthiness judgements could represent a subtle and largely unrecognised source of context-dependent bias in everyday social perception. Participants therefore rated the trustworthiness of unfamiliar faces following brief exposure to an object prime. The data were examined in three ways: a primary test of whether prime category influenced trustworthiness judgements, an exploratory test of whether participants' affiliation with home or work environments moderated this effect, and a further test of whether workplace experience did so. Across all three analyses, object primes produced no reliable effect on trustworthiness, and the exploratory affiliation effect was not robust to further sensitivity analysis. Each analysis and its implications are considered in turn below.

Primary Analysis

The trial-level LMM provided no clear evidence that prime category influenced trustworthiness ratings. Neither directional hypothesis was supported: Home primes did not significantly increase ratings (H 1 ), and Work primes did not significantly decrease them (H 2 ). Instead, both contrasts were positive and similar in magnitude, rather than opposing in direction as predicted (Home, b = 2.23, p = .061; Work, b = 2.08, p = .075). Although small effects cannot be ruled out entirely, these near-threshold values should be read with caution. The model omitted a perparticipant random slope for prime (a deliberate simplification; see Appendix A), and omitting such slopes inflates the risk of false positives (Barr et al., 2013). Any prime effect is therefore unlikely to be stronger than the estimates reported here.

There was also no evidence that any prime effect was concentrated among face-types. Neither prime interacted with normative face-trustworthiness level, nor face ambiguity, where the lack of contextual information was expected to exert greater influence on the judgement (Cesario, 2014; Loersch & Payne, 2011). At the same time, normative facial trustworthiness strongly predicted participants' trust ratings (b = 4.61, p < .001), indicating that the measure was sensitive to the judgement being made. This, together with evidence that trustworthiness judgements of such faces are susceptible to task-relevant priming (Posten et al., 2014), provides little indication that the null finding resulted from an insensitive measure or a paradigm inherently incapable of priming. The study was also powered to detect effects smaller than those typically reported within the incidental priming literature (e.g., DeCoster & Claypool, 2004; Kay et al., 2004; Weingarten et al., 2016), although smaller effects remain possible.

The sensitivity analysis likewise did not alter the substantive interpretation. Removing the single extreme Novel-condition trial increased both prime contrasts by a similar amount, raising the Home effect across the conventional significance threshold, while leaving the Work effect close to it. However, since the removed observation contributed to the shared Novel baseline, rather than uniquely affecting the Home condition, this shift does not provide evidence for a Home-specific effect. The precise size of the estimates is thus somewhat sensitive to this one trial, but the substantive pattern of two near-identical, same-direction contrasts remains unchanged.

This shared pattern is important because it does not match the category-specific divergence predicted by the semantic-affiliation account: Home and Work primes produced almost identical positive shifts despite the opposing directions predicted by H 1 and H 2 . However, the present analysis alone cannot establish why the two familiar categories moved in the same direction. The exploratory analyses therefore examined whether this pattern varied according to participants' pre-existing affiliations with home and work environments.

Within-Participant Profile Groups

This analysis examined whether participants' relative affiliation with Home and Work environments moderated the effects of the object primes. One interaction was in the predicted direction: the Home-versus-Novel effect was larger among Home-leaning than Balanced participants (b = 6.59, p = .033). However, the evidence for affiliation-based moderation was limited. The interaction was estimated from only five Home-leaning participants, so it was imprecise (95% CI [0.52, 12.66]). It was also sensitive to how affiliation was operationalised.

Comparing Home-leaning participants with all other participants reduced the interaction to non-significance (b = 4.93, p = .062), whereas treating affiliation as a continuous score retained the effect (p = .046). Most importantly, because the four interactions were tested as a family, significance was judged against a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (α = .0125; Field, 2024), which this interaction (p = .033) did not meet.

More tellingly, the interaction may reflect lower ratings following Novel primes among Homeleaning participants rather than greater responsiveness to the Home primes. Home-leaning participants tended to rate Novel-primed faces lower than Balanced participants (b = -6.87, p = .058); because the Home effect is measured relative to the Novel condition, this lower baseline likely inflated the apparent interaction. As Figure 6 shows, Novel-prime ratings generally fell below those following both Home and Work primes across the facetrustworthiness range, a pattern more consistent with a depressed Novel baseline than with a Home-specific increase, which further limits the support the interaction provides for an affiliation-based interpretation.

The source of this lower baseline is itself uncertain. One possibility the design cannot rule out is that the affiliation measures capture broader dispositional differences rather than the categoryspecific valences they were intended to assess. Pre-existing dispositions can influence how priming is expressed (Neuberg, 1988), and Home-and Work-leaning individuals may differ in related traits such as introversion and extraversion, which have been linked to variation in trustworthiness judgements (Evans & Revelle, 2008; Freitag & Bauer, 2016). For example, a more work-oriented, socially exposed (extraverted) disposition might be associated with more uniform trust judgements, whereas a more Home-oriented (introverted) disposition might be associated with lower baseline trust, which a Home prime may briefly offset. This could explain why Home-leaning participants rated Novel-primed faces as less trustworthy than Work-leaning participants did, and why the Home primes briefly offset that difference. No personality measure was collected, however, so this explanation remains speculative.

Taken together, the Home × Home-leaning interaction is best regarded as suggestive rather than robust evidence of affiliation-based moderation. The data cannot distinguish a genuine but underpowered moderation from a baseline difference within the small Home-leaning subgroup. This uncertainty motivated the final exploratory analysis, which examined whether a more direct index of exposure to work environments (self-reported workplace experience) similarly moderated the prime effects.

Work Experience as a Moderator

Work experience did not predict trustworthiness ratings or moderate either prime effect, providing no evidence in support for the accessibility prediction drawn from the situated inference model (Loersch & Payne, 2011), a finding that was unchanged in the sensitivity analysis. This null should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously: because work experience varies only between participants, moderation is estimated from just 28 individuals rather than the full trial set, and the confidence interval for the Work prime interaction (b = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.09]) reflects this imprecision. The interval is consistent with effects ranging from a small negative to a small positive association; across the full 0 to 100 work experience scale, its upper bound corresponds to a ~9-point difference in trustworthiness ratings, while the point estimate corresponds to ~2 points. The predicted interaction, whereby greater work exposure would strengthen the Work prime effect, was also the weaker and non-significant of the two, providing no support for this specific prediction of the accessibility account. The primary interpretation therefore remains unchanged. With the three analyses now considered individually, their combined implications can be drawn together.

Interpretation and Implications

Across the three analyses, the central interpretive challenge is that Home and Work primes produced similarly sized increases in trustworthiness ratings, rather than the opposing effects predicted by H 1 and H 2 . The affiliation subgroup showed only limited, non-robust moderation, and work experience added none. The pattern is therefore best described as two weak, samedirection effects of familiar object categories, not the category-specific divergence the semantic-affiliation account predicts.

One explanation is that the effect reflects familiarity rather than category-specific meaning. Home and Work objects were familiar whereas Novel objects were not, and the processing fluency of familiarity can raise evaluations independently of a stimulus's specific content (Reber et al., 2004; Zajonc, 1968); on this account Home and Work resemble each other because both were familiar, not because they activated distinct trust-related representations. Equally, because the Novel objects lacked real-world referents, the same pattern could reflect these unfamiliar objects mildly depressing trust rather than the familiar categories raising it; either reading locates the effect in familiarity rather than social-category content. A second possibility is that Home and Work objects are less psychologically opposed than originally assumed. Both may evoke trust-relevant associations, or broader meanings acquired through their connections with people, activities, and social contexts (Csikszentmihalyi & Rochberg-Halton, 1981; Dant, 1999; Nippert-Eng, 1996). A third possibility is that object-evoked meanings were not readily applied to the trustworthiness judgement. Priming effects on social judgement are generally modest and depend on conditions that allow activated information to bear on the target (Cesario, 2014; DeCoster & Claypool, 2004; Loersch & Payne, 2011), and here participants judged the face after only incidental exposure to an object, with multiple prime categories presented per participant further limiting how far any one meaning remained accessible. The face itself also provided a strong cue to trustworthiness: normative facial trustworthiness shifted ratings across much of the response range, whereas the prime effects were only around two points. The relatively small size of the prime effects therefore suggests that any object-evoked meaning may have had little scope to shift judgements that were already strongly determined by the facial cue.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive. Familiarity may have conferred a general evaluative advantage on both familiar categories, while any category-specific meanings overlapped or transferred only weakly to the face. Given that prime activation was not independently verified, the findings offer limited support for the semantic-affiliation account and cannot determine whether the absence of divergence reflects a general familiarity effect, overlapping category meanings, or a failure of object-evoked meaning to influence the target judgement. On the practical question raised at the outset, the present data therefore give no reason to treat incidental object cues as a meaningful source of bias in everyday trustworthiness judgements, although they do not exclude effects under conditions in which object meaning is more directly relevant to the judgement being made.

Limitations and Future Directions

These competing interpretations cannot be conclusively separated because several features of the design were not independently established. The most consequential is the absence of a manipulation check confirming that the object categories activated their intended constructs; as a result, the null divergence is compatible with both successful activation that did not influence judgement and a failure of activation at the outset. However, embedding such a check in the main experiment would be problematic, since revealing the Home-Work distinction risks disclosing the manipulation's purpose and thereby increasing demand characteristics, to which priming effects are sensitive (Bargh & Chartrand, 2000). A stronger approach would validate the prime categories in a separate sample, using measures such as lexical decision or word completion, so that failures of construct activation can be distinguished from failures to apply an activated construct to the target judgement.

A second limitation concerns how the object primes were integrated into the task. Familiarity was confounded with category, since Home and Work objects were familiar, whereas Novel objects were not. Consequently, higher ratings following the familiar categories cannot be unambiguously attributed to category-specific meaning rather than familiarity. More importantly, the objects were presented incidentally and were not integrated into the trustworthiness judgement in a task-relevant way. This may have limited the extent to which any object-evoked meaning was brought to bear on the subsequent social judgement. Materialpriming studies reporting robust effects, such as Kay et al. (2004), instead embed objects within the context of the decision itself, increasing their relevance to the judgement being made. Future research should therefore include a familiar but trust-irrelevant control category to separate familiarity from category meaning and should examine whether integrating object primes more directly into the judgement context produces stronger transfer to the social judgement (Loersch & Payne, 2011).

A third limitation is the study's limited treatment of individual differences relevant to trustworthiness judgement. The exploratory affiliation analysis suggests that participant characteristics may contribute to variation in these judgements. However, the measured affiliations may themselves index broader dispositions, such as introversion and extraversion. Relatedly, a small number of participants reported to the experimenter that they were autistic and found everyday trustworthiness judgements difficult. This represents potentially relevant unmodelled variation, given that autism has been associated with atypical facial trustworthiness evaluation (Adolphs et al., 2001; Forgeot d'Arc et al., 2016). These participants were nevertheless retained, as autism was not an exclusion criterion. Furthermore, since the data were anonymised at collection, these verbal reports could not be linked to individual data records, and their influence on the results therefore cannot be assessed. Future research should therefore measure theoretically relevant individual differences directly, allowing variation in social-cognitive processing to be modelled rather than inferred from subgroup patterns or addressed retrospectively.

Finally, the exploratory moderation analyses were limited by small subgroup sizes and low statistical power; therefore, the observed interactions should be considered tentative rather than robust evidence of individual-difference moderation. A larger, preregistered replication specifically designed to detect interactions would provide a more rigorous test. Where supported by the design, participant-level random slopes should be incorporated to model variation across individuals and conditions (Barr et al., 2013). Additionally, employing a broader stimulus set and recruiting a more diverse sample would help determine whether the observed pattern generalises beyond the current objects, faces, and predominantly young participants.

Conclusion

This study found no reliable evidence that incidental exposure to Home or Work objects altered trustworthiness judgements of unfamiliar faces under the conditions tested. Although both familiar categories produced small, same-direction increases relative to Novel objects, neither effect was reliably distinguishable from zero, and the exploratory affiliation effect did not provide robust evidence of individual-difference moderation. The findings therefore do not support the predicted category-specific divergence between Home and Work primes, but they also do not establish that everyday objects cannot influence social perception. Instead, they indicate that any influence of object-related meaning on social judgement may be sensitive to how that meaning is activated and brought to bear on the target. Further research should therefore establish whether the intended prime constructs are activated, separate familiarity from category meaning, and test object cues under conditions more conducive to transfer to interpersonal judgement.

Appendix A: Data Analysis Plan Assumption

Assumptions were checked as follows. Residual normality was assessed by skewness and kurtosis (|value| < 1) and visual inspections of histograms and Q-Q plots, with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (Lilliefors corrected) as supporting evidence only, as significance tests of normality detect trivial departures at this number of trials (Field, 2024). Multicollinearity was assessed from the correlation matrix of the fixedeffect estimates (|r| > .80; the two prime dummies are correlated by construction). The adequacy of the data at each level was evaluated from the number of levels and observations per level.

Random-Effects Structure and Model Fit

Trustworthiness ratings varied both across participants and across face identities, so the model included random intercepts for both (Table 1). Between-participant variance was significant (30.16, p = .002), indicating that participants differed in their overall trustworthiness ratings. There was also some variation in ratings across face identities (estimated variance = 21.86), although this did not reach conventional significance (p = .068). The face-identity random intercept was nevertheless retained because observations were nested within face identities, and Wald tests of variance components can be unreliable when the true variance is near the zero boundary (Molenberghs & Verbeke, 2007), as was the case (Wald z = 1.83). Affiliation as a Continuous Moderator

The moderation observed in the profile-group model was re-examined with the Home-minus-Work affiliation difference entered as a continuous predictor interacting with the prime contrasts, avoiding the information loss associated with dividing participants into three groups using a fixed cut-off (all 1,217 trials; N = 28). The Home prime × affiliation interaction was positive and reached conventional significance, b = 0.095, 95% CI [0.002, 0.189], p = .046, indicating that the Home-versus-Novel prime effect increased by ~0.10 rating points for each one-point increase in Home-leaning affiliation. The Work prime × affiliation interaction was not significant, b = 0.063, 95% CI [-0.029, 0.156], p = .181, providing no evidence that Home-Work affiliation altered the effect of the Work prime. The affiliation main effect was nonsignificant, b = -0.08, 95% CI [-0.19, 0.03], p = .134, as were both prime contrasts at a balanced affiliation score (Home, b = 1.78, 95% CI [-0.53, 4.08], p = .130; Work, b = 1.53, 95% CI [-0.74, 3.81], p = .186); thus, there was no evidence of a main effect of affiliation or of either prime contrasts. This model did not converge; SPSS reported that iteration terminated without achieving convergence and that the validity of the fit was therefore uncertain. Consistent with this, the Work-prime slope variance was estimated at the zero boundary (0.00), and the Homeprime slope variance was effectively unidentified, its 95% CI spanning from near zero to over 1.6 million (variance = 1.12, SE = 8.10, 95% CI [7.75, 1624165.05], p = .890). The data therefore could not support by-participant random slopes for prime, consistent with the small number of trials per condition per participant and empirically supporting the random-intercept structure used throughout (Matuschek et al., 2017). Since the model did not converge its estimates cannot be interpreted; although the interaction remained nominally close to the threshold (b = 6.68, p = .039), no weight can be placed on this value. The interaction's robustness to a random-slope specification therefore could not be established, reinforcing the conclusion that it is best treated as a fragile, exploratory result.
