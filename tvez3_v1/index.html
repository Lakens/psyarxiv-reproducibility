No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and sleep-related out-of-body experiences (OBEs) are usually defined by a characteristic feature, although considerable phenomenological variability exists within each phenomenon. We explored how different experiential features were organized across participant-level profiles within these phenomena. Data came from an online survey completed by 389 adults who reported at least one of the three experiences. Separate multivariate analyses were conducted within each phenomenon using multiple correspondence analysis followed by hierarchical clustering on principal components. We examined volitional regulation, predominant affective category, and, for OBEs, embodiment-related features. In lucid dreams and OBEs, voluntary induction, agency, and environmental control did not always occur together. Consistent voluntary induction and consistent environmental control characterized different profiles in both phenomena. In OBEs, predominantly positive emotions characterized profiles with different patterns of induction and agency, whereas predominantly negative emotions characterized a profile with low volition. In sleep paralysis, predominantly negative emotions occurred across several phenomenological profiles, whereas predominantly positive emotions characterized a profile associated with vestibular-motor experiences and out-of-body feelings (OBFs). Sleep-related OBEs also showed marked variability in bodily experience, including profiles characterized by consistent body ownership, variable embodiment, or no perceived body. These findings provide an exploratory description of how phenomenological features are organized across participant-level profiles within lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and OBEs. The resulting profiles should not be considered fixed subtypes, but rather patterns to be tested in independent samples using prospective or episode-level assessments.

1. Introduction

Lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and sleep-related out-of-body experiences (OBEs) are usually treated as distinct phenomena, each identified by a characteristic feature. In lucid dreaming, the dreamer becomes aware that they are dreaming while the dream continues (LaBerge et al., 1981; Voss et al., 2013). Sleep paralysis occurs at the transition between sleep and wakefulness and is characterized by a temporary inability to perform voluntary movements despite preserved awareness (Cheyne et al., 1999). OBEs are defined by an altered self-location, in which the self is experienced outside the physical body, typically together with an extracorporeal visuospatial perspective (Blanke et al., 2004; Blanke & Arzy, 2005). These defining features, however, say relatively little about the rest of the experience. Lucid dreams vary in the degree of agency and control that accompanies lucidity (Voss et al., 2013; Stumbrys et al., 2014), sleep paralysis can include very different perceptual and emotional experiences (Cheyne et al., 1999), and OBEs show substantial variation in their circumstances of occurrence and phenomenology (Twemlow et al., 1982).

The boundaries between these experiences are also not strict. Lucid dreaming and OBEs have been found to occur in the same individuals (Blackmore, 1982). This is also the case for lucid dreaming and sleep paralysis, and their frequency is positively related, particularly when sleep paralysis includes vestibular-motor experiences (Denis & Poerio, 2017). During sleep paralysis, participants may report floating, flying, illusory movement, or experiences of being located outside the physical body (Cheyne et al., 1999; Girard & Cheyne, 2004). OBEs have also been reported during sleep paralysis and in relation to lucid dreaming (Campillo-Ferrer et al., 2024; Moix et al., 2025).

These overlaps show that some experiential features are shared across lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and OBEs despite the different criteria used to define each phenomenon.

In lucid dreams, awareness of dreaming and control over the dream are not equivalent. In the Lucidity and Consciousness in Dreams scale, insight and control emerged as separate dimensions of lucid-dream experience (Voss et al., 2013), and surveys of lucid dreamers show considerable variability in active participation and the ability to influence dream events (Stumbrys et al., 2014).

Voluntary induction, intentional action during the experience, and control over the experienced environment also refer to different aspects of volition. OBEs may occur spontaneously or be deliberately induced (Herrero et al., 2023; Moix et al., 2025), and individuals differ in the degree of agency and control reported during the experience (Twemlow et al., 1982; Weiler et al., 2025). Initiating an episode, acting intentionally within it, and modifying the experienced environment refer to different aspects of volition, and how these aspects combine within lucid dreams and OBEs remains unclear.

Sleep paralysis provides a particularly clear example of internal phenomenological heterogeneity. Cheyne and colleagues identified three broad groups of associated experiences: Intruder phenomena, including sensed presence and visual or auditory hallucinations; Incubus phenomena, involving bodily pressure, breathing difficulties, suffocation, or choking; and vestibular-motor phenomena, which are centered on one's own body and encompass anomalous experiences of bodily movement and out-of-body phenomenology (Cheyne et al., 1999; Cheyne & Girard, 2009). Within this broader vestibular-motor domain, movement-related experiences include sensations such as floating, flying, falling, spinning, or illusory bodily movement. Cheyne and Girard (2009) further distinguished the bodily sensation of separation from, or leaving, the physical body-termed an out-of-body feeling (OBF)-from the visual experience of perceiving one's own body from an external vantage point. These components are closely related but need not occur together, indicating that OBFs capture a specific disembodiment component of out-of-body phenomenology rather than the broader phenomenological configuration typically encompassed by an OBE. These distinctions are also relevant to the emotional character of sleep paralysis. Intruder and Incubus phenomena are strongly associated with fear and distress, often involving a threatening external presence or bodily threat, whereas vestibular-motor phenomena can include more positively valenced experiences (Cheyne et al., 1999; Cheyne & Girard, 2009; O'Hanlon et al., 2011; Sharpless & Kliková, 2019). Emotional variability is also observed in lucid dreams, where affective valence is distinguishable from insight and control (Voss et al., 2013), and in OBEs, which can vary substantially in emotional tone (Herrero et al., 2023; Gallo et al., 2025). How predominant emotional character is organized together with other phenomenological features within each of these experiences, however, remains less well understood.

Extracorporeal self-location can likewise be accompanied by different forms of bodily experience (Blanke & Arzy, 2005; Bünning & Blanke, 2005).

Neurological studies of OBEs and related autoscopic phenomena have described alterations in perceived body position and movement, body completeness, and the size or shape of body parts (Blanke et al., 2004). Heautoscopy represents a related disturbance of bodily self-consciousness in which self-location may become ambiguous or divided between the physical body and an experienced double (Blanke & Mohr, 2005). More recent analyses further indicate that body ownership can shift dynamically during heautoscopic experiences (Szczotka & Wierzchoń, 2023). Variability also extends to the termination of OBEs: some reports describe a distinct perceived return to the physical body, whereas others end through awakening or without a consciously perceived return (Alvarado, 1997; Alvarado & Zingrone, 1998 -1999).

Previous studies have characterized individual features of lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and OBEs, and some have compared selected features across these phenomena (Cheyne et al., 1999; Voss et al., 2013; Denis & Poerio, 2017; Gallo et al., 2023; Weiler et al., 2025). Much less is known about how several features are organized together within each phenomenon. We asked whether distinct participant-level phenomenological profiles could be identified within each of these phenomena.

Here, we examined this question using multivariate analyses conducted separately within lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and sleep-related OBEs. We focused on three phenomenological domains. Volitional regulation was examined in lucid dreams and OBEs using voluntary induction, agency, and environmental control. Predominant affective category was examined in lucid dreams and OBEs together with voluntary induction and agency, whereas in sleep paralysis it was examined together with intruder experiences, incubus experiences, vestibular-motor experiences, and OBFs. Embodiment-related features were examined exclusively in OBEs. Analyses were conducted separately for each phenomenon because the available variables and their theoretical relevance differed across phenomena. Because the study was exploratory, the number of clusters was not specified in advance. Cluster interpretation was descriptive, with the aim of identifying recurrent patterns in the reported experiences.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This study is a secondary cross-sectional analysis of data collected as part of a broader online survey on sleep-related experiences. Part of the sleep-paralysis and OBE data analyzed in the present study were collected as part of the same broader online survey previously reported by Herrero et al. (2023), which addressed different research questions concerning emotions, OBE induction, and sensory experiences preceding sleep paralysis and OBEs. The multivariate analyses and phenomenological profiles reported here have not been previously published. Spanish-speaking adults aged 18 years or older were recruited internationally through social media. Recruitment posts were mainly directed at people with frequent dream recall or an interest in unusual sleep experiences.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Alberto C. Taquini Institute for Translational Medicine Research, University of Buenos Aires. All participants provided electronic informed consent before completing the survey.

Identifying information collected for study administration was removed before analysis, and the analytic datasets were stored and analyzed in de-identified form.

A total of 389 unique participants completed at least one of the three modules assessing lucid dreams (n = 389), sleep paralysis (n = 319), and sleep-related out-of-body experiences (OBEs; n = 216). Because participants could complete multiple modules if they reported experiencing more than one phenomenon, these sub-samples partially overlapped (e.g., n = 187 participants completed all three modules). Each phenomenon was analyzed separately.

The questionnaire had a modular structure, and some items were incorporated during data collection. For each analysis, only participants with complete data for all variables included in that specific model were retained (missing responses were not imputed). Consequently, final analytic sample sizes differed depending on the specific variables modeled: for sleep paralysis, N = 215 (affective analysis); for lucid dreams, N = 147 (volitional-regulation analysis) and N = 134 (affective analysis); and for OBEs, N = 170 (volitional-regulation analysis), N = 165 (bodily-self analysis), and N = 153 (affective analysis).

2.2. Survey procedure and identification of experiences

The survey was administered in Spanish. After completing general questions, participants were presented with separate modules on lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and sleep-related OBEs. Each module began with a brief definition of the experience followed by a yes/no screening question. Participants who answered affirmatively continued with the corresponding module.

Lucid dreams were described as dreams in which the person becomes aware that they are dreaming while the dream continues. Sleep paralysis was described as an episode occurring while falling asleep or awakening in which the person feels unable to move their physical body voluntarily. Sleep-related OBEs were described as experiences involving the sensation of being located outside the physical body and perceiving the surroundings from an external perspective.

At the end of each module, participants were invited to describe their most memorable experience of that phenomenon in their own words. These reports were used to assess whether participants had understood the screening definition and whether the described experience was phenomenologically compatible with the corresponding category. No participants were excluded on the basis of these descriptions.

2.3. Phenomenological domains and coding

The questionnaire items included in the present analyses were organized into three phenomenological domains: volitional regulation, predominant affective category, and embodiment-related features. These groupings were used to structure the multivariate analyses and were not treated as psychometric scales; no composite scores were calculated.

The domains were examined only within the phenomena for which the relevant variables were available and theoretically meaningful. Volitional regulation was analyzed in lucid dreams and sleep-related out-of-body experiences (OBEs). Predominant affective category was analyzed in all three phenomena, together with phenomenon-specific accompanying variables.

Embodiment-related features were analyzed exclusively in OBEs.

Unless otherwise specified, questionnaire items characterized participants' experiences across the corresponding phenomenon rather than a single index episode. Accordingly, the resulting variables are interpreted as participant-level phenomenological tendencies or experiential histories. Their joint representation within a profile does not necessarily imply that all component features co-occurred within the same episode.

Several questionnaire items originally used more detailed categorical response scales. Where applicable, semantically related response options were collapsed into broader analytic categories before the multivariate analyses.

These recodings were performed independently of the clustering results.

2.3.1. Volitional regulation

Volitional regulation was examined separately in lucid dreams and sleep-related OBEs using three categorical variables: voluntary induction, agency, and environmental control. These variables were retained as distinct dimensions because they captured different aspects of volitional regulation: the ability to intentionally initiate an experience, the ability to act intentionally once within it, and the ability to modify the experienced environment.

Voluntary induction referred to participants' self-reported ability to intentionally induce the experience at will. The original response options distinguished consistent successful induction, less consistent successful induction, unsuccessful induction attempts, and absence of previous induction attempts or intention to attempt induction. For analysis, responses were grouped into three levels: consistent voluntary induction, intermittent voluntary induction, and no reported voluntary induction. The latter category therefore did not necessarily indicate that the experience itself was spontaneous, because it also included participants who had not successfully induced the phenomenon, had never attempted to do so, or did not wish to attempt induction.

Agency was operationalized as participants' reported ability to act or move voluntarily within the experience. Original responses distinguished consistent voluntary action, two intermediate levels reflecting variable ability to act, and complete absence of voluntary action. The two intermediate response levels were combined for analysis, yielding three categories: consistent agency, variable agency, and absent agency.

Environmental control referred to participants' reported ability to intentionally modify aspects of the experienced surroundings, such as changing the scene or other features of the environment. Responses were similarly represented by three analytic categories reflecting consistent, variable, or absent environmental control.

The three variables were entered jointly into the volitional-regulation analyses for lucid dreams and OBEs.

2.3.2. Predominant affective category

The predominant emotional character of the experience was assessed in lucid dreams, sleep-related OBEs, and sleep paralysis. In the analytic datasets, this variable comprised three categories: predominantly positive emotions, predominantly negative emotions, and no emotions.

For lucid dreams and OBEs, predominant affective category was analyzed jointly with voluntary induction and agency. This analysis therefore examined whether different affective profiles were embedded within different patterns of intentional induction and action during the experience.

For sleep paralysis, predominant affective category was analyzed together with four binary phenomenological-history variables: intruder, incubus, vestibular-motor, and out-of-body feelings (OBFs). Intruder experiences referred to reports of a sensed presence, intruder, or unfamiliar agent in the immediate environment. Incubus experiences included perceived touch or bodily pressure and sensations of breathing difficulty, suffocation, or bodily oppression. Vestibular-motor experiences included sensations of movement, falling, flying, or floating. OBFs referred specifically to perceived separation from, or movement outside, the physical body.

Participants could endorse more than one sleep-paralysis feature. Each feature was coded as a separate binary variable indicating whether it had ever occurred during the participant's history of sleep paralysis. Consequently, these variables do not establish that the endorsed features occurred simultaneously or within the same sleep-paralysis episode. The coding of the four SP features was performed before clustering and corresponds to the phenomenological groupings used in the analytic dataset.

2.3.3. Embodiment-related features in OBEs

Embodiment-related phenomenology was examined only in sleep-related OBEs using three categorical variables: perceived embodiment, history of heautoscopic bilocation, and mode of OBE termination. Together, these variables characterized different aspects of how participants typically experienced their bodily self across their history of OBEs rather than the structure of a single target episode.

Perceived embodiment referred to whether participants generally experienced the extracorporeal or "other" body encountered during an OBE as their own. Responses were represented by three analytic categories: consistent body ownership, variable embodiment, and no perceived body. Consistent body ownership indicated that an experienced body was reliably perceived as one's own. Variable embodiment included reports in which bodily ownership or bodily presence was not consistent across experiences, as well as some atypical descriptions of embodiment such as experiencing oneself primarily as a form or point of consciousness. The no-perceived-body category comprised responses indicating that a body was generally or consistently not experienced. Thus, the intermediate category should not be interpreted as spatially partial ownership of specific body parts.

Heautoscopic bilocation indicated whether participants had ever experienced themselves as simultaneously located outside the physical body while also remaining in the body in bed. This item captured the bilocation component characteristic of heautoscopy, but did not require visual perception of an autoscopic double.

Mode of OBE termination described how participants reported their OBEs typically ending. Responses were coded as return to the physical body, awakening, or another mode of termination.

Because these variables summarized broader experiential tendencies or histories, their combination within the clustering analysis should be interpreted as a participant-level embodiment profile rather than as evidence that all three characteristics occurred together within an individual OBE.

2.4. Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1. Each phenomenological domain was analyzed separately within the corresponding phenomenon. Participants with missing data in any variable included in a given analysis were excluded from that analysis.

Because some participants completed more than one experiential module, the lucid-dream, sleep-paralysis, and OBE samples partially overlapped. No clustering analysis combined observations from different phenomena, and cluster solutions obtained for different phenomena were not compared statistically.

2.4.1. Multiple correspondence analysis

Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) was performed using the MCA() function from the FactoMineR package (Lê et al., 2008). Separate MCAs were conducted for each phenomenon and phenomenological domain.

MCA was used because all active variables were categorical. Participants were represented according to their response profiles in a multidimensional factorial space, while response categories were positioned according to their patterns of co-occurrence. Categories with an ordinal interpretation were treated as categorical modalities, without assuming equal distances between adjacent response levels.

For each analysis, MCA dimensions were retained until cumulative inertia reached at least 80%. Participants' coordinates on these retained dimensions were subsequently used for clustering. Category contributions and squared cosine values were inspected when interpreting the factorial dimensions.

2.4.2. Cluster analysis and selection of the number of clusters

Hierarchical clustering on principal components was performed on the retained MCA coordinates using the HCPC() function from the FactoMineR package.

Euclidean distances were computed across the reduced factorial space, followed by hierarchical agglomeration using Ward's minimum-variance criterion (ward.D2). The resulting partition was subsequently consolidated via k-means clustering initialized from the hierarchical cluster centroids, as implemented in HCPC. Cluster membership and all subsequent descriptions refer to this consolidated partition.

Candidate solutions containing between two and eight clusters were systematically evaluated. For each solution, average silhouette width, the Calinski-Harabasz index, and the Dunn index were computed. Because these validation metrics did not always converge on the same partition, the final number of clusters was determined by balancing the three indices against cluster sample sizes, model parsimony, and the conceptual interpretability of the response profiles. When adjacent solutions exhibited comparable statistical support, the more parsimonious solution with fewer clusters was retained.

In the resulting hierarchical trees, fusion heights reflect the cost of aggregation under Ward's criterion-namely, the increase in within-cluster inertia (variance) upon merging. Mergers occurring at relatively low heights denote closely aligned, highly homogeneous response profiles. Sub-branches consolidating only at intermediate tree levels indicate clusters with broader internal variability or differentiated sub-structures, whereas major branches joining near the top of the tree demonstrate that their amalgamation would introduce substantial heterogeneity, thus justifying their separation into distinct clusters.

2.4.3. Cluster characterization and stability

Clusters were characterized based on the distribution of response categories within each profile. Category-specific v-test statistics were computed using the catdes() function in FactoMineR.

Positive v-test values indicate overrepresentation of a response category within a cluster. Benjamini-Hochberg corrections were applied across category-level comparisons within each cluster.

Figures display only categories that were significantly overrepresented after correction (p_adj < .05).

Cluster stability was assessed using 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples of the retained MCA coordinates. For each bootstrap sample, Ward.D2 hierarchical clustering was reapplied at the selected number of clusters. The MCA solution itself was not re-estimated within bootstrap iterations; therefore, the resulting Jaccard coefficients quantify the stability of the hierarchical clustering conditional on the factorial representation obtained from the original sample. For descriptive interpretation, mean clusterwise Jaccard coefficients below .60 were considered indicative of weak cluster recovery (Hennig, 2007).

Because bootstrap stability was evaluated for the Ward hierarchical partition, whereas cluster membership and profile descriptions were based on the final k-means-consolidated HCPC partition, agreement between the hierarchical and consolidated solutions was assessed separately. Concordance was quantified using the adjusted Rand index (ARI), which measures agreement between two cluster partitions while correcting for agreement expected by chance.

In the figures, horizontal dendrograms illustrate the initial Ward hierarchical tree, whereas cluster membership, sample sizes, and profile descriptions correspond to the final k-means-consolidated partition. The trees are read from right to left, progressing from individual participants or closely aligned subgroups toward broader clusters. Merges occurring at lower linkage heights join more similar response profiles, whereas merges at higher linkage heights indicate greater dissimilarity within the hierarchical representation.

Finally, schematic illustrations were developed post hoc to visually summarize the predominant experiential features of each profile; these served strictly for presentation purposes, were not shown to participants, and were not incorporated into any statistical analysis.

3.2.2. Lucid dreams

A three-cluster solution was retained for the affective-volitional analysis of lucid dreams (n = 134; Figure 2). The final partition had an average silhouette width of .539, a Calinski-Harabasz index of 41.6, and a Dunn index of .686. Cluster 1, the negative/low-volition profile (n = 6, 4.5%), combined predominantly negative emotions with no reported voluntary induction and absent agency. Cluster 2, the variable-volition profile (n = 120, 89.6%), combined intermittent voluntary induction with variable agency. Cluster 3, the induction-dominant profile (n = 8, 6.0%), was characterized by consistent voluntary induction.

The variable-volition profile accounted for 89.6% of the analytic sample.

Predominantly positive emotions were frequent overall (70.9%) but were not significantly overrepresented in any cluster. Predominantly negative emotions were overrepresented only in the small negative/low-volition profile. Given the marked imbalance in cluster sizes and the limited bootstrap recovery, the affective-volitional organization observed in lucid dreams should be interpreted cautiously.

4. Discussion

The present study explored how phenomenological features were organized across participant-level profiles within sleep-related out-of-body experiences, lucid dreams, and sleep paralysis. Different profiles emerged within each phenomenon, showing that participants reporting the same type of experience could differ considerably in agency, control, affect, perceptual features, and bodily experience. These profiles are descriptive and specific to the present samples. They nevertheless provide a way of examining the internal variability of experiences that are usually identified through a single defining feature.

In lucid dreams and OBEs, voluntary induction, agency, and environmental control did not always occur together. This is consistent with previous work on lucid dreaming showing that insight and control represent distinguishable aspects of the experience and that becoming aware of dreaming does not guarantee an equivalent capacity to influence dream events (Voss et al., 2013; Stumbrys et al., 2014). Our results add voluntary induction to this picture.

Participants who reported being able to deliberately initiate the experience were not necessarily those who reported consistent control over the environment once the episode had begun. The same pattern was observed in OBEs, where induction and environmental control also characterized different profiles. This is relevant because induction, agency, and control are often discussed together when describing highly volitional sleep experiences, even though they refer to different moments or aspects of the episode. Deliberately entering an experience, acting intentionally within it, and modifying its contents therefore need not covary in a fixed manner across individuals. The present data do not establish whether these features rely on different cognitive mechanisms, but they show that treating them as interchangeable would miss part of the phenomenological variability observed in both lucid dreams and OBEs.

Predominant emotional character also appeared in different combinations with volitional features. In OBEs, predominantly positive emotions characterized profiles with different patterns of induction and agency. Predominantly negative emotions characterized a profile with absent agency and no reported voluntary induction. Previous studies have described marked variation in the emotional quality of OBEs and differences in emotional content and experiential control between OBEs and lucid dreams (Herrero et al., 2023; Gallo et al., 2025). The present analysis adds a within-phenomenon perspective, showing that similar predominant emotional character can occur in OBEs with different patterns of induction and agency.

The affective organization of lucid dreams was less differentiated.

Predominantly positive emotions were frequent overall but did not define a particular profile, and most participants were grouped in a large variable-volition profile. A small profile combined predominantly negative emotions with absent agency and no reported voluntary induction. Given its small size and the more modest bootstrap recovery obtained in this analysis, this configuration should be interpreted cautiously. Previous work has already shown that lucidity, affective valence, and dream control are not equivalent dimensions of lucid-dream experience (Voss et al., 2013; Stumbrys et al., 2014). The present findings are compatible with that variability, although they provide limited evidence for a specific organization between predominant emotional character and volition in lucid dreams.

Sleep paralysis showed a different relationship between affect and phenomenology. Predominantly negative emotions characterized several profiles, including profiles associated with intruder experiences and another with a broader combination of intruder, incubus, vestibular-motor experiences, and OBFs. Predominantly positive emotions characterized a profile associated with vestibular-motor experiences and OBFs. This organization has clear points of contact with the classic phenomenological description of sleep paralysis.

Cheyne and colleagues distinguished intruder, incubus, and vestibular-motor components, with intruder and incubus phenomena more strongly associated with fear and distress and vestibular-motor experiences encompassing phenomena such as floating, flying, illusory movement, and out-of-body experiences (Cheyne et al., 1999; Cheyne & Girard, 2009). Subsequent studies have similarly emphasized the strong negative affect associated with sensed presence and hallucinatory experiences during sleep paralysis (O'Hanlon et al., 2011; Sharpless & Kliková, 2019).

It is noteworthy that some of these previously described associations were recovered in an exploratory multivariate analysis. In particular, intruder experiences characterized negative profiles, whereas vestibular-motor experiences and OBFs characterized the positive profile. At the same time, predominantly negative emotions were not restricted to a single phenomenological profile, illustrating the diversity of experiential histories that can occur under the broad label of sleep paralysis.

The sleep-paralysis variables referred to participants' broader histories of these experiences. Features grouped within the same profile may therefore have occurred in different episodes. The positive vestibular-motor/OBF profile cannot be taken as evidence that these experiences are accompanied by positive emotions within the same episode. Episode-level reports would be needed to examine that association directly.

The bodily-self analysis of OBEs also showed substantial heterogeneity.

Extracorporeal self-location defines the experience, but participants differed markedly in how the bodily self was represented. The resulting profiles included consistent body ownership, variable embodiment, and no perceived body.

Similar variability has been described in studies of OBEs and related autoscopic phenomena, in which self-location, bodily identification, perceived body position, and first-person perspective can be altered in different combinations (Blanke et al., 2004; Blanke & Arzy, 2005; Blanke & Mohr, 2005). The present findings are consistent with this literature and suggest that extracorporeal self-location can coexist with substantially different forms of bodily experience during sleep-related OBEs.

More broadly, this variability may be informative beyond the phenomenology of OBEs themselves. Bodily self-consciousness can be conceptualized as involving partially dissociable components, including body ownership, self-location, and first-person perspective (Blanke, 2012; Serino et al., 2013). Dreaming provides a natural model for examining the flexibility of this configuration, as the dream self can remain embodied, assume an altered or unstable bodily form, or in some cases persist in the absence of a spatially extended body representation. Metzinger (2013), for example, draws a parallel between such "bodiless dreams" and asomatic OBEs, in which the experiencing subject is located outside the represented physical body without experiencing itself as possessing an extended bodily form. Sleep-related OBEs may nevertheless constitute a particularly informative case within this broader range of bodily-self features. Their defining alteration concerns self-location itself, which becomes spatially dissociated from the physical body, while the phenomenal body associated with that extracorporeal self-location may remain stable, become variable, or disappear altogether. From this perspective, the profiles observed here may capture different ways in which body ownership and self-location can be recombined once their usual alignment is disrupted. Rather than treating the OBE body as equivalent to the ordinary dream body, sleep-related OBEs may therefore extend the study of dream embodiment by providing a natural context in which the spatial relation between self and body is itself systematically altered. Profiles characterized by consistent body ownership also differed according to participants' history of heautoscopic bilocation and their typical mode of OBE termination. Two profiles shared consistent body ownership and a reported history of heautoscopic bilocation, but differed in how the experience typically ended, with one characterized by a perceived return to the physical body and the other by awakening. Episode termination has received considerably less attention than self-location and visuospatial perspective in descriptions of OBEs, yet these findings suggest that it may contribute an additional dimension of phenomenological variability. Heautoscopic bilocation itself requires cautious interpretation. The item captured simultaneous self-location inside and outside the physical body, a core component of heautoscopic phenomenology, but did not require visual perception of an autoscopic double. It therefore should not be interpreted as a measure of the full heautoscopic syndrome. Nevertheless, the occurrence of bilocation-like experiences among individuals reporting sleep-related OBEs is theoretically relevant because heautoscopic phenomena involve disturbances in the normally unified relations among self-location, bodily identification, and perspective (Blanke & Mohr, 2005; Szczotka & Wierzchoń, 2023). Future episode-level studies that assess these components separately could determine more precisely how bilocation, body ownership, self-location, and visual autoscopy relate to one another during sleep-related OBEs.

Taken together, the analyses show that the defining characteristic of each phenomenon leaves considerable room for phenomenological variation. Lucid dreams differed in the way volitional features were combined, sleep paralysis included several combinations of affective and perceptual phenomena, and OBEs varied in volitional, affective, and bodily characteristics. Similarities across analyses were also apparent, particularly in the separation between voluntary induction and environmental control in lucid dreams and OBEs. However, the analyses were conducted independently, using partly different variables and participant subsets, and therefore do not establish a common phenomenological structure across phenomena.

Several limitations are important when interpreting these findings.

Participants were recruited through an online survey primarily directed toward individuals with frequent dream recall or an interest in unusual sleep experiences. The samples cannot be considered representative of the general population, and the frequency of the clusters should not be interpreted as the prevalence of particular experiential profiles. The data were retrospective and, for several variables, summarized participants' experiences across multiple episodes. Predominant affective category likewise reflected an overall characterization rather than the emotional course of an individual event.

The questionnaire was not originally designed as a psychometric instrument for identifying latent phenomenological subtypes. Several original response options were collapsed into broader analytic categories on semantic grounds before clustering, and the resulting solutions necessarily depend on the variables and coding decisions used. Because some questionnaire items were introduced after data collection had begun, analysis-specific samples differed substantially in size and may not be directly comparable. Although cluster stability was generally good and was high in several analyses, this does not establish that the same profiles will emerge in independent samples. The lucid-dream affective analysis deserves particular caution because most participants belonged to a single profile, the remaining profiles were small, and cluster recovery was lower than in the other analyses. Lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and OBEs were analyzed separately, and similarities between profiles across phenomena were not tested statistically. Direct comparisons would require common measures administered across phenomena within a design developed specifically for that purpose.

The present study provides an exploratory description of participant-level phenomenological profiles within lucid dreams, sleep paralysis, and sleep-related OBEs. The profiles observed here capture part of the variability that exists within each experiential category and identify combinations of features that can be examined more directly in future studies. Their reproducibility remains to be established in independent samples, ideally using prospective or episode-level assessments and common measures across phenomena.
