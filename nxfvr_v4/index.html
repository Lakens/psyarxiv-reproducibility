How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Humans use language to communicate psychological experiences. Advancements in Natural Language Processing have enabled researchers to quantitatively study and assess psychological states and behaviors through language. Insights from these language analyses are often best conveyed through visualizations. Data-driven visualizations of how language statistically relates to psychological dimensions can help interpret assessment scores, uncover meaningful patterns, and understand the intricate relationships between language use and mental states. Visualizations can be used to differentiate between related constructs (e.g., How does language related to depression versus anxiety differ?), understand the validity of assessment tools (e.g., Does the language related to high depression severity match with depression theory?), and explain language-based assessments (e.g., Which language is indicative of higher depression severity in a language-based assessment?). This tutorial demonstrates how to transform language data into visual insights using the text and topics packages in R. We provide practical guidance on creating four types of visualizations based on different language analysis techniques, examining how language use patterns are statistically associated with psychological constructs. We visualize 1) individual words and phrases (n-grams), 2) topics (word clusters), 3) words in the word embedding space, and 4) relevant language examples. The different visualizations offer complementary perspectives, visualizing linguistic elements from individual words to overarching topics and text examples. The underlying methods range from correlations between individual words to more advanced techniques, including topic modeling and Large Language Models. By making these methods accessible, this tutorial aims to empower researchers to use language visualizations for meaningful, data-driven insights.

Introduction

Language plays an important role in organising emotions and thoughts, as well as in sharing them with others and allowing us to comprehend the perspectives of others. Through language, we make sense of past experiences, plan for the future, and navigate relationships by cooperating, learning, and teaching (Pennebaker et al., 2003; Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010). These diverse functions make language an integral part of human life. Analyzing language use patterns can provide insights into how people express their states and traits and navigate their psychological experiences in diverse contexts (Boyd & Schwartz, 2021; Eichstaedt et al., 2021; Mihalcea et al., 2024).

Visualizations of Human Language

Analyzing language use patterns-how people talk about emotions, thoughts, or experiences-can provide valuable insights into psychological constructs and behaviors. With numerous language visualization options available, this tutorial aims to streamline quantitative techniques for a broader audience in a user-friendly manner through the R programming language. Our aim is to support social and behavioural scientists in creating language visualizations and to advance the use of language visualizations for meaningful data-driven insights.

Advancements in Quantitative Language Analysis

Visualizations of language can rely on different language analysis techniques. There are different approaches to quantitative language analysis in psychology, which can roughly be divided into theorydriven and data-driven approaches (also known as closed-vocabulary and open-vocabulary approaches, respectively; Eichstaedt et al., 2021). A widely used theory-driven approach is the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC), which calculates the relative frequencies of predefined, psychologically relevant word dictionaries (e.g., negative emotion words, social words) across texts. This enables the analysis of the relationships between word-dictionary frequencies and psychological states (Francis & Booth, 1993; Pennebaker et al., 2001). However, data-driven approaches allow researchers to move beyond predefined categories to discover patterns directly from the data. This often begins with ngrams, which are individual words or phrases (Schwartz et al., 2013). By identifying which specific words or phrases are statistically associated with a variable, researchers can capture linguistic markers that dictionaries might overlook. To manage the vast variety of individual words, topic modeling (e.g., Latent Dirichlet Allocation; Blei et al., 2003) groups co-occurring words into clusters, or "topics." These word clusters can capture broader semantic themes that are often more stable and interpretable than individual words.

Over the past decade, advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Machine Learning (Devlin et al., 2019; Vaswani et al., 2017) have further transformed the field. These technologies have made it possible to transform language into psychologically meaningful numerical representations called word embeddings, which can capture the complexities of language-such as context and word order-in a structured, quantified manner (Demszky et al., 2023; O. Kjell et al., 2024; Mihalcea et al., 2024). These advancements have improved the accuracy of language-based assessments of psychological constructs (O. Kjell et al., 2022), which have demonstrated high measurement validity in assessing personality traits (e.g., Eichstaedt et al., 2021), mental health problems (e.g., K. Kjell et al., 2021), and well-being (e.g., Sametoglu et al., 2022). As these techniques grow more sophisticated, the role of visualization becomes critical, providing a necessary bridge between numerical outputs and interpretable psychological insights.

Applications of Language Visualizations

Beyond quantitatively analyzing language to produce assessment scores (e.g., depression severity scores based on language) or behavioural predictions (e.g., sick-leave predictions derived from text), language visualizations have emerged as a valuable complement to explore and elucidate individuals' experiences. There are numerous reasons for using language visualizations, such as to better understand language data itself or to describe the measured constructs in more detail. Visualizations can support the interpretation of assessment scores, reveal meaningful patterns, and clarify how language relates to psychological states and behaviours. They offer intuitive ways to explore and communicate the output of language-based assessments, complementing quantitative scores with richer, more interpretable insights. By visualizing language use based on statistically significant relationships, researchers can better understand individuals' psychological experiences and evaluate the validity of the tools used to assess them. Previous research provided comprehensive comparisons between the outputs and applications of different language analysis techniques. Schwartz and Ungar (2015) illustrated the utility of data-driven techniques in analysing large-scale social media data, revealing how linguistic patterns reflect psychological traits, emotions, and demographics. Eichstaedt et al. (2021) provided a detailed review of theory-driven and data-driven approaches. They compared predefined word-dictionary analyses (e.g., LIWC) with data-driven methods based on topic modeling or Large Language Models and advocate for hybrid techniques to balance interpretability and discovery. Egger and Yu (2022) extended the discourse by evaluating the practical implications of diverse topic modelling methods, ranging from traditional dictionary-based approaches to modern machine learning models, with a focus on their accuracy, scalability, and suitability for various constructs.

While these theoretical and empirical comparisons have identified the diverse applications and complementary nature of these techniques, practical guidance for their implementation remains limited.

In particular, while existing literature highlights opportunities for visualization, researchers often lack the technical guidance to create them. This tutorial aims to fill this gap by providing practical guidance on different quantitative language analysis techniques, specifically tailored for making visualizations. The tutorial uses R, specifically the topics package (Ackermann et al., 2024) and the text package (O. Kjell et al., 2023), and provides step-by-step guidance for making a range of data-driven language visualizations. Drawing on the aforementioned literature, we offer user-friendly tools, sensible defaults, and practical "rules of thumb" intended as helpful starting points rather than strict prescriptions. This guidance is designed to support researchers in making informed decisions and thoughtful applications without requiring deep technical expertise.

Tutorial

In this tutorial, we demonstrate how to make four types of data-driven visualizations of linguistic elements, ranging from individual words to overarching topics (i.e., word clusters) and complete text examples. The underlying techniques all involve Differential Language Analysis (Schwartz et al., 2013; 2017), examining how language use patterns are statistically associated with a psychological construct or behaviour. Specifically, we visualize 1) individual words, 2) topics (word clusters), 3) words based on their numerical representations in the word embedding space, and 4) relevant text examples highlighting the qualitative aspects of language as opposed to quantitative assessments (Table 2). The visualizations complement each other, providing unique insights while highlighting common patterns.

Strong effects, such as differences in emotional valence (e.g., low versus high positivity), are typically evident across all methods. However, each technique also reveals distinct aspects of the language data, enriching the overall analysis.

We employ methods ranging from correlations between word frequencies to more advanced techniques based on topic modeling and Large Language Models. Each visualization is accompanied by a technical description of the functions used in R, including their outputs and parameters (for which rules-of-thumb are provided). At the end of the tutorial, we discuss aspects to consider when interpreting language visualizations, including ethical considerations.

Visualizations of words and phrases (n-grams)

Here, we visualize individual words and phrases (also called n-grams) in relation to a variable of interest. Specifically, we visualize individual words (from the depression and worry responses) positively and negatively associated with depression severity (PHQ-9 ratings) through two differential word clouds. The analyses underlying this visualization involve structuring meaningful n-grams based on word frequency and co-occurrence, and testing the relationship between the n-gram frequencies and PHQ-9 ratings using regression. The visualization is created with three functions of the topics package, including 1) topicsGrams(), which structures meaningful n-grams; 2) topicsTest(), which significance tests the n-grams in relation to the variable of interest; and last, 3) topicsPlot(), which visualizes the significant n-grams. Table 3 at the end of the tutorial provides rules of thumb and recommendations for setting the parameters across functions.

Structure meaningful n-grams

Participants' responses are first split into individual words and phrases (n-grams) using the topicsGrams() function. N-grams are sequences of words: One-grams are single words, and 2-or 3grams are sequences of words combined with underscores (e.g., "happy_birthday" instead of "happy" and "birthday"). The size of n-grams is set with the ngram_window parameter (here, set to sequences of 1 to 3 words).

Rather than including all possible 2-and 3-grams, it is possible to only retain phrases from which the individual words are more likely to occur together than to occur independently. This is achieved using the pmi_threshold parameter (set to 5 here), based on the Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI; Church & Hanks, 1990; Lin, 1998; Schwartz et al., 2013). The PMI quantifies the association between two events -in the context of n-grams, these events correspond to words that co-occur. When two words co-occur more often than expected by chance, the PMI of the phrase will be larger than 0. When the two words occur together purely by chance (i.e., they are independent), the PMI of the phrase will be 0. Phrases with a PMI greater than 1 occur at least twice as likely as expected by chance. Using the PMI is a common method for removing weak or less meaningful associations and limiting the correction for multiple comparisons in subsequent statistical tests (Lin, 1998; Schwartz et al., 2013; see Table 3 for rules of thumb).

To further ensure focus on meaningful n-grams and limit the corrections for multiple comparisons, it is possible to restrict the analyses to words and phrases used by a minimum proportion of the participant sample (e.g., see Atkins et al., 2012; Eichstaedt et al., 2021) using the occurance_rate parameter (here, set to 0.01, which limits the analysis to n-grams that appear in at least 1% of individuals' responses; see Table 3 for rules of thumb). The n-grams are filtered based on their frequency of occurrence to include only those n-grams that appear in a certain proportion of the responses, calculated by dividing the number of responses that contain a specific n-gram by the total number of responses.

Additionally, it is possible to remove words using the stopwords parameter. Here, you can specify a list of common words (such as "the", "and", "is") that you want to exclude from the analysis, because they are not expected to be meaningful in relation to the variable of interest (e.g., depression severity). The default option is the predefined list of 175 stopwords from the snowball lexicon (Porter, 2001). By removing stopwords, the function focuses on more meaningful words and phrases, enhancing the clarity and relevance of the resulting visualizations.

Before the responses are structured into n-grams, the responses are preprocessed according to parameter settings (which defaults to TRUE), including lower (texts are lowercased), remove_punctuation (punctuation is removed), and remove_numbers (numbers are removed). The output of topicsGrams() is a list of the most frequently occurring n-grams across the dataset, including their frequency, prevalence, and the number of documents. The frequency indicates the number of times an n-gram appears in the dataset; the prevalence indicates the frequency of an n-gram relative to the total number of n-grams in the dataset; and the number of documents indicates the number of distinct responses in which an n-gram appears.

Visualizations of topics (word clusters)

Here, we visualize topics (word clusters) in relation to a variable of interest. Topic visualizations cluster words into semantically meaningful themes, offering a higher-level summary of language patterns that may not be apparent from individual words alone. Specifically, we visualize topics (in the depression and worry responses) positively and negatively associated with depression severity (PHQ-9 ratings) through multiple topic word clouds.

The analyses underlying these visualizations involve extracting underlying topics from the responses using topic modelling and testing the relationship between these topics and the PHQ-9 ratings using regression. There exist different topic modelling techniques, such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA; Blei et al., 2003), Structural Topic Modelling (STM; Roberts et al., 2014), and BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022). They share the same aim of automatically identifying themes in text. LDA is a probabilistic model that can be used to uncover underlying topics within text collections by modelling texts as mixtures of topics and topics as distributions over words (Blei et al., 2003; Griffiths et al., 2007). STM is similar to LDA but extends the probabilistic modelling by allowing text-level metadata (e.g., time, authorship, or demographics) to influence the topic prevalence and content (Roberts et al., 2014; 2019), requiring more decisions and setup. BERTopic, in contrast, is a more recent method that instead of relying on word frequency and co-occurrence, leverages Large Language Models to generate topic representations. This method uses word embeddings-numerical representations of word meaning derived from Large Language Models. Topics are derived from clustering word embeddings to capture word meanings in a contextualized way (Grootendorst, 2022). This tutorial focuses on LDA and BERTopic because together they illustrate two complementary perspectives: a foundational, more interpretable probabilistic approach and a more modern embedding-based method. See Egger & Yu (2022) for a more detailed comparison between different topic modeling approaches, and see the stm package and tutorial (Roberts et al., 2019) for an introduction to STM.

Visualizations of topics using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)

LDA topics are clusters of co-occurring words within a collection of texts or responses, identified using the LDA algorithm. The identification and visualization of the LDA topics is conducted through five functions of the topics package: 1) topicsDtm(), which creates a matrix of the text data (here, the depression and worry responses), 2) topicsModel(), which employs the LDA algorithm to identify underlying topics within the matrix, 3) topicsAssess(), which applies the trained LDA model to assign relative frequencies of each topic on the specified language data, 4) topicsTest(), which significance tests the topic frequencies in relation to individuals' characteristics (here, depression severity), and 5) topicsPlot(), which visualizes the significant topics.

We use the topicsDtm() function to structure participants' responses into a Document Term Matrix (DTM). A DTM represents the frequencies of the n-grams across all responses and serves as the primary input for the LDA algorithm. By transforming language responses into a structured matrix, the DTM enables the efficient application of algorithms such as LDA to uncover underlying topics. The matrix is constructed by processing the text data, which includes removing stopwords (using the stopwords parameter), structuring n-grams (with the ngram_window parameter), and filtering n-grams based on specified thresholds using the pmi_threshold and occurance_rate parameters, as described in the previous visualization. Also, the same preprocessing parameters apply (e.g., lower, remove_punctuation, and remove_numbers).

Identify topics using the LDA algorithm

Once the DTM is established, we employ the LDA algorithm to identify underlying topics within the text data using the topicsModel() 1 function. LDA assumes that texts are mixtures of topics, and each topic is characterized by a distribution over words. Using techniques such as Gibbs sampling (Yao et al., 2009), an underlying topic structure is inferred by estimating 1) the topic prevalences for each response and 2) the word distributions for each topic. In essence, Gibbs sampling is a repetitive process that iteratively reassigns words to topics until the most likely overall topic structure emerges. Notably, LDA requires pre-specifying the number of topics, which can be challenging and may not always capture the actual underlying structure of the data (a rough rule of thumb is one topic per 50 responses; see Table 3). The DTM is submitted to the dtm parameter, and the LDA algorithm is applied to the DTM while optimising for a specific number of topics (with the num_topics parameter; here set to 20) and number of iterations (with the num_iterations parameter; default is 1000; see Table 3). Visualizations of topics using BERTopic

BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022) is a more recent topic modeling method that instead of relying on word frequency and co-occurrence like LDA, uses word embeddings from Large Language Models to group semantically similar texts into topics. The identification and visualization of the BERTopics is conducted through three functions of the text package: 1) textTopics(), which leverages a pretrained Large Language Models to generate topic representations of the text (here, the depression and worry responses), 2) textTopicsTest(), which significance tests the topic representations in relation to individuals' characteristics (here, depression severity), and 5) textTopicsWordcloud(), which visualizes the significant topics.

Generate topic representations by clustering embeddings

The textTopics() function generates topic representations of the depression and worry responses, including 1-to 3-grams (the default in n_gram_range). This function employs a pre-trained Large Language Model from the Huggingface library to convert the words into word embeddings. The language model (here, the default model "distilroberta-base"; Sanh et al., 2019) is submitted to the embedding_model parameter, and the text data is submitted to the variable_name parameter. A word embedding is a vector that numerically represents the meaning of a word. Since word embeddings comprise many numbers, a word is represented by many dimensions. By applying the pre-trained word embeddings from the Large Language Model, each response is converted into a high-dimensional embedding. Next, the embeddings are reduced in dimensionality, and the reduced embeddings are clustered by applying two different clustering algorithms: the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP; McInnes et al., 2018) for dimensionality reduction and the Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering (HBDSCAN; McInnes et al., 2017) for clustering. Then, for each cluster, the most characteristic words (i.e., words that distinguish that cluster from the other word clusters) are extracted using a procedure called class-based Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF; Grootendorst, 2022; Joachims, 1996) to produce interpretable topic representations. See Grootendorst (2022) for a more detailed description of the dimensionality reduction, embedding clustering, and keyword extraction. Unlike LDA, the function does not require prespecifying the number of topics, and it does not return topic prevalences because it is based on embeddings rather than word frequencies and co-occurrences. The output includes a list of the generated topics (here, 28), along with their "representations" (i.e., characteristic keywords). The number of top terms per topic representation is set with the num_top_words parameter (default is 10).

Significance test the topics

Then, the trained BERTopics model is submitted to the textTopicsTest() function to perform a significance test of the topic representations in relation to depression severity. The depression ratings are submitted to the x_variable parameter, and the method for testing the statistical relation is set with the test_method parameter (here, the default is "linear regression" since the target variable is continuous). Multiple regression is applied while controlling for age and gender (set with the controls parameter) and correcting for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995; set with the p_adjust_method parameter). It is also possible to test the topic representations in relation to two variables of interest and make two-dimensional visualizations by adding a second variable of interest to the y_variable parameter. The output of this regression are standardized beta coefficients (β), where the beta coefficients indicate the unique association between each topic and depression severity.

Visualize the significant topics

The BERTopics model and statistical test are then submitted to the textTopicsWordcloud() function to visualize the topics. Visualizations of words in the word embedding space

Here, we visualize words in the word embedding space in relation to a variable of interest. Specifically, we visualize individual words (from the depression and worry responses) positively and negatively associated with depression severity (PHQ-9 ratings) using the Supervised Dimension Projection method (Kjell et al., 2023). While visualizations of n-grams and LDA topics provide insights based on word frequency and co-occurrence, visualizing words in the word embedding space offers a more semantic perspective. Similarly to BERTopic, the analyses underlying this visualization make use of word embeddings. By projecting these embeddings onto a supervised dimension (e.g., low to high depression severity), we can visualize which words are most semantically aligned with higher or lower levels of the outcome of interest. This approach can capture patterns in word use that may not be evident through frequency-based methods alone and provides a complement to more traditional techniques like n-gram or topic analyses.

As discussed previously, a word embedding is a vector that numerically represents the meaning of a word. Since word embeddings comprise many numbers, a word is represented by many dimensions in the word embedding space. The numbers can be seen as coordinates in a high-dimensional space: The closer two words are in this space, the more similar their word embeddings are, and the more similar the words are expected to be in meaning. The Supervised Dimension Projection plot can be created with three functions of the text package, including 1) textEmbed(), which creates word embeddings of the participants' responses, 2) textProjection(), which preprocesses the data for plotting by creating supervised dimension projections, and 3) textProjectionPlot(), which visualizes the words in the word embedding space. The functions are described below; see the tutorial of the text-package for more detailed descriptions (O. Kjell et al., 2023).

Transform the data into word embeddings

We transformed the language responses into word embeddings using the textEmbed() function, which employs a pre-trained Large Language Model from the Huggingface library. While many models exist (the default is BERT; "bert-base-uncased"; Devlin et al., 2019), we here use the MixedBread model ("mixedbread-ai"; Li & Li, 2023), which has shown good performance in psychological assessment tasks (e.g., Bång et al., in revision). The name of the language model is submitted to the model parameter, and the language responses are submitted to the texts parameter. Before the responses are transformed into embeddings, the language is preprocessed by removing Non-ASCII characters (remove_non_ascii = TRUE), since most language models cannot handle these well. ASCII (American Standard Code for Information Interchange) characters include the English alphabet, numbers, and some basic punctuation.

The responses are first split into individual words, where every single word of each response gets its own embedding. The output of the function comprises three types of contextualised embeddings, which represent the meaning of the words in their context. These types of embeddings are 1) token embeddings, which represent the meaning of a single word or word stem (e.g., "someone" consists of the tokens "some" and "one") in a specific response, 2) text embeddings, which represent the meaning of a response by combining all token embeddings of the response, and 3) word type embeddings, which represent the meaning of a word type by combining all token embeddings of a word. A word type refers to a word in general (i.e., in all responses) as opposed to a token, which refers to a word in a specific response. The Supervised Dimension Projection requires two types of embeddings as input, namely the text and word type embeddings. The text embeddings are created by taking the mean of all token embeddings in a specific response (with the aggregation_from_tokens_to_texts parameter).

The word type embeddings are created by taking the mean of all token embeddings for a specific word (with the aggregation_from_tokens_to_word_types parameter).

To create an embedding that captures the distinction between high and low depression severity, the function divides the dataset into two groups based on a split (default is median) of the depression rating scale, resulting in a high and low-scoring group. The embeddings of the two groups are subtracted from each other to create an embedding that captures the distinction between high and low depression severity, also known as the supervised dimension in the word embedding space. Next, supervised dimension projections are created by projecting each single word embedding onto this supervised dimension. This is achieved by using the dot product, which mathematically measures how similar two embeddings are. To determine the statistical significance of the created supervised dimension projections, they are compared to a null distribution of supervised dimension projections while correcting for multiple comparisons to obtain a p-value.

Interpreting Language Visualizations

While a benefit of language visualizations is their intuitive communication, interpreting language visualizations warrants some considerations. A key difference between, for example, interpreting rating scale scores versus language lies in dimensionality (Ganesan et al., 2021; Stochl et al., 2022). Validated scales, such as the PHQ-9, are often one-dimensional (e.g., from low to high depression severity; Kroenke et al., 2001), whereas language visualizations comprise language features with multiple dimensions (e.g., calm has positive valence and low arousal). Language analyses involve a significantly higher number of units: They may examine tens, hundreds, or even thousands of words, phrases, or topics, including additional complexities such as polysemy (i.e., words may have multiple meanings depending on the context; Nerlich, 2003) and subtleties in language use (e.g., sarcasm, idioms, cultural expressions; Reyes et al., 2012). Because of these complexities, language visualizations require thoughtful interpretations to identify higher-order patterns and relationships. Below, we present factors to consider when interpreting language visualizations, followed by ethical considerations.

Employ multiple methods and triangulate.

We recommend considering different visualization methods collectively or complementing the language visualizations with other methodological approaches for a comprehensive understanding. Triangulation of methods can lead to more robust conclusions, ensuring that findings are not an artefact of any single technique (Lahat et al., 2015; Lawlor et al., 2016).

Visualizations of single words (e.g., words in the word embedding space) can benefit from visualizations of topics, because word clusters can provide more context. For example, the n-gram plot (Figure 1) visualizes the word "pointless" as related to higher depression severity, and we can see in the BERtopics plot (Figure 5) that it is part of the same cluster as "suicide", "alive_selfish" and "life_boring", which provides context to positive association with depression severity. Combing language visualizations can also provide more nuance. For example, the two-dimensional SDP plot (Figure 9) visualizes the word "future" as indicative of worry language and low depression severity, while the two-dimensional LDA topics plot (Figure 8) shows that "future" also co-occurs with the word "worried" in a cluster positively associated with depression severity. Similarly, visualizations of ngrams or topics can benefit from visualizing representative examples from the language data, as demonstrated by Figure 7 for the n-gram "lack" and the topic "feeling". In summary, triangulation enhances reliability and provides a richer picture of the relationship between language use patterns and psychological constructs (e.g., see Eichstaedt et al., 2021; Nilsson et al., 2024). Also, adding language visualizations to support and extend conventional statistical approaches can provide context and help uncover patterns that might otherwise go unnoticed (O. Kjell et al., 2024).

Anchor in context and theoretical frameworks. Contextualisation is essential for interpreting language visualizations. For example, instead of overinterpreting single words or topics, it is more suitable to find overarching themes and contextualise them with theoretical frameworks and other empirical findings. Contextualization can help identify higher-order patterns and avoid potential biases in interpretation. For example, the prominence of a word in a word cloud may suggest it is important, but its significance could be influenced by confounding factors that are not immediately apparent. For example, the word "depressed" can be part of one of the topics related to low depression severity, such as in combination with the use of a negation before. Similarly, the words "interest" or "motivation" might be visualized in association with high depression severity. Contextualisation might provide cooccurrences with words like "lack" or "loss", which together fit theoretical frameworks and diagnostic criteria of depression (e.g., anhedonia; American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013). It is crucial to carefully consider the thematic and contextual implications, rather than equating linguistic patterns directly with psychological states, to ensure that insights are meaningful and valid.

Consider the language source. To contextualize language visualizations, it is essential to consider where the language originated (e.g., social media, survey responses, interview transcripts), as the context in which it was generated can significantly impact its patterns. For example, responses to a question such as "Over the last 2 weeks, have you been depressed or not?" might have a higher chance of including words such as "depressed", "not", and "yes" or "no" than, for example, social media posts. Also, the length of the text entries can influence the results, as we elaborate on in the Supplementary Material, where we apply the analyses to essays instead of survey responses. Furthermore, it is essential to consider the authors' demographics and cultural background, as these factors significantly influence individuals' language patterns (Abitbol et al., 2018; Rai et al., 2024).

Specify exploratory versus confirmatory.

It is essential to consider that research aims and design influence how visualizations can be interpreted, such as whether they are created in the context of exploratory analyses (e.g., identifying patterns and generating hypotheses) or confirmatory analyses (e.g., testing specific hypotheses or relationships). It is important to state this explicitly, since language has many features, and some words or topics might almost always be significant. One approach is to compute topics and, before any further analysis, pre-register the topics hypothesized to relate to the outcome, then test them on a new dataset (e.g., see O. Kjell et al., 2024).

Take behavioral patterns into account.

The presented language visualizations in this tutorial are based on data-driven language patterns-seen as behavioral in nature -they reflect how individuals articulate their thoughts and feelings (Boyd & Markowitz, 2025). For instance, a patient using more sadnessrelated topics in their language does not necessarily mean the patient is more depressed clinically, as depression is a multifaceted construct with specific diagnostic criteria (APA, 2013), including loss of interest, sleep problems, and so on, and language patterns alone may only partially reflect these criteria. Therefore, interpreting the statistical significance or meaning of a topic differs from interpreting a rating scale score or the frequency of a theory-driven word dictionary from the LIWC, where the meaning has been predefined rather than retrieved from a data-driven approach.

Consider effect sizes carefully. Finally, interpreting effect sizes, for example, for topics requires some considerations. In general, effect sizes are often misinterpreted due to an overreliance on general effect size benchmarks that were not intended for broad application across various research fields (Glaser et al., 2025). A correlation or standardized beta coefficient should be interpreted as it is typically interpreted in a field (e.g., β = .30 as a threshold for a strong correlation in individual-differences research; Gignac & Szodorai, 2016). However, it is very rare that a topic has a correlation beyond β = .30. This is because the signal in language is spread out, and thus, any single word or topic usually has a small signal. These signals become stronger when considered together. Future research could focus on establishing data-driven guidelines and benchmarks for specific language analysis or visualisation techniques (e.g., see the tutorial on calculating field-specific effect size distributions; Glaser et al., 2025).

Ethical Considerations

Privacy and Data Sensitivity. Language data often contains personal and sensitive information. Even when anonymised, certain linguistic patterns can indirectly reveal identifying details. Therefore, it is essential to respect the privacy and sensitivity of the data, especially when the language relates to personal or clinical topics, and consider in each analysis which parts are identifiable (alone or in combination) and what can be saved or shared (and how). Researchers should ensure that data collection and analysis comply with established ethical guidelines, including obtaining informed consent and implementing secure data storage practices.

Bias in AI-based Models.

AI-based techniques and language-based assessments can inherit and amplify biases present in training data. For example, gender, racial, or socio-economic biases may manifest in word associations, leading to misleading conclusions (Giorgi et al., 2022). Researchers are recommended to critically examine their models for bias and consider using debiasing techniques when necessary, such as controlling for certain characteristics (e.g., age and gender) and visualizing these controls.

Generalisation and Stigmatisation.

Language visualizations summarise complex, high-dimensional data into intuitive formats, such as topics. This simplification makes results more accessible and generalisable but also risks obscuring critical nuances, especially if the visualization design introduces bias or fails to highlight key patterns or critical details (Egger & Yu, 2022). Overgeneralised interpretations may lead to misleading conclusions or stigmatisation. Researchers are advised to emphasize that visualization results represent data-driven behavioral patterns, which are probabilistic rather than deterministic, and can be best interpreted within the context of theoretical frameworks, other empirical findings, and complementary methods.

Transparency and Reproducibility.

Given the complexity of quantitative language analyses using Natural Language Processing and Large Language Models, ensuring transparency in methodological choices is important. Consider providing clear documentation on how data was processed, which parameters were used, and how visualizations were generated, which enhances reproducibility and allows for critical evaluation by other researchers.

Summary

This tutorial has introduced four complementary types of language visualizations, which can be created with the topics and text packages in R. Each method-word-level analysis, topic modelling, word embeddings, and identifying relevant text examples-offers unique insights into the relationship between language use patterns and psychological constructs. We recommend using different visualizations together, taking context and theory into account for interpretation. By integrating robust methodological practices with ethical responsibility, this tutorial aims to support social and behavioral scientists in creating language visualizations and advancing their use for meaningful, data-driven insights.
