Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Abstract

Cancellation tasks are widely used measures of attention but are conventionally scored by metrics such as mean accuracy or concentration scores, disregarding the temporal structure of responding. Here, we derive two reaction-time (RT) biomarkers from our tablet-based UCancellation task in 388 young adults: intra-individual RT variability (RTV), a marker of sustained attention, and switch cost, an index of cognitive flexibility from transitions between two different task's target categories. Because consecutive targets are separated by a variable number of scanned distractors, both are confounded by search effort, which we remove with a scanning-load (p) correction framework. RTV behaved as a scored individual-difference measure: correction improved reliability and convergent validity against TOVA low-frequency RTV (n = 373), and a cross-target composite was the most reliable index. Switch cost behaved as a within-task phenomenon whose sign reversed with load-large and positive when targets were adjacent, reversing as scanning load increased-consistent with inhibition of return. Together, these metrics show that RT structure within a brief cancellation task carries attentional information that standard metrics alone does not capture.

1. Introduction

Selective attention-focusing on relevant stimuli while suppressing distractors-and sustained attention-maintaining consistent focus over time-are foundational components of cognitive control (Cohen, 2014; Pashler et al., 2001; Sarter et al., 2001). Cancellation tasks, such as the d2 Test of Attention, in which participants scan a field of stimuli and mark targets while rejecting distractors, are among the most widely used measures of these abilities, indexing processing speed, concentration, and inhibitory control (Brickenkamp & Zillmer, 1998). Our UCancellation task (Pahor et al., 2022), adapts this paradigm to tablets and smartphones and extending its existing set of stimuli, correlating well with the standard paper-and-pencil d2 Test of Attention and providing a brief, engaging, self-administered measure of selective attention and concentration Cancellation performance is traditionally summarized by accuracy-based scores such as concentration performance (hits minus false alarms). Yet every response also carries a reaction time (RT), and the temporal structure of responding encodes information about attention and cognitive control that accuracy alone does not capture. Two RT-derived constructs are especially informative, and neither is used by conventional cancellation scoring.

The first is intra-individual reaction-time variability (RTV), the trial-to-trial fluctuation in response speed. RTV is among the most robust behavioral markers of sustained attention with greater variability reflecting momentary lapses in attentional control (Machida et al., 2019; Yamashita et al., 2021). Elevated RTV is a hallmark of conditions characterized by attentional instability, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (Adams et al., 2011; Castellanos et al., 2005; Kofler et al., 2013), schizophrenia (Kaiser et al., 2008), autism spectrum disorder (Schachar et al., 2023), and Alzheimer's disease and mild cognitive impairment (Duchek et al., 2009; Gorus et al., 2008; Tales et al., 2012). Lapses of this kind also relate to broader executive and fluid abilities (Unsworth et al., 2020), and RTV predicts real-world attentional and academic outcomes (Gallen et al., 2023; Ziegler et al., 2019). Extracting RTV from a brief cancellation task could therefore provide a sensitive, low-burden marker of attentional stability.

The second RT-derived biomarker is switch cost, which refers to the additional processing incurred when shifting between task or target sets-a classic index of cognitive flexibility (Wylie & Allport, 2000). Response times are typically slower on switch than on non-switch trials, reflecting the cost of reconfiguring the attended set (Draheim et al., 2019; Dykstra et al., 2022).

In the picture version of the UCancellation task, targets alternate between two categories (upright dogs and inverted monkeys), so consecutive target selections can be classified as switches or non-switches. This allows a switch cost to be estimated within the cancellation task itself, without administering a separate task-switching paradigm.

Deriving these metrics from cancellation data poses a measurement challenge not present in standard RT tasks. Specifically, consecutive targets are separated by a variable number of intervening distractors that must be scanned, so a response time reflects not only the cognitive process of interest but also search effort. This scanning load-indexed by the number of items processed since the previous target-confounds both biomarkers and must be removed before RTs can be compared. How responses evolve with scanning load may itself be informative, potentially reflecting the inhibitory dynamics of serial search, such as inhibition of return (Klein, 2000).

The present study derives and evaluates two reaction-time biomarkers-RTV and switch costfrom the UCancellation task, and introduces a correction framework that removes scanning-load variance from the RT data. We characterize how each biomarker varies with scanning load, and we assess the convergent validity of RTV against a standard continuous-performance measure of Tasks were administered on participants' personal tablets and computers through our custom application Recollect the Study (Collins et al., 2022; Pahor et al., 2022) and through Inquisit Web (Millisecond Software, version 6.1.0.0), each session lasting up to one hour, with a twominute break offered between tasks.

2.2. The UCancellation Task

UCancellation is a timed, tablet-based cancellation task adapted from the d2 Test of Attention (Brickenkamp & Zillmer, 1998) and previously validated as a mobile measure of selective attention, sustained attention, and inhibitory control (Pahor et al., 2022). We used the Pictures version, in which stimuli are cartoons of dogs and monkeys rather than letters, some rotated about the vertical axis or presented upside down.

2.2.1. Task structure and stimuli

Figure 1 presents a typical stimulus of the UCancellation task where each row displayed eight items in a single left-to-right sequence, containing three to five targets. In the design, every ten rows contained exactly forty targets. Rows were presented for six seconds each, separated by one second blank intervals. If a participant finished a row before the time limit, they could advance manually to the next trial. Targets were upright dogs (tail on the left) and upside-down monkeys (tail on the right); all other orientations were non-targets or distractors. Participants were instructed to select as many targets as possible while avoiding non-targets. The task consisted of three blocks-two single-target blocks (e.g. only dogs) and one mixed block (dogs and monkeys)-each lasting one minute and ten seconds (see Figure 1 for an illustration). The application logged, for every item, whether it was selected and the timestamp of each selection, together with hits, misses, false alarms, and correct rejections per row.

The present study uses only the mixed block, because it is the sole block in which both target types are present and therefore the only context in which switching between target types can occur. The traditional UCancellation outcome, Concentration Performance (hits minus false alarms), was retained for reference; our focus is on two novel RT-based biomarkers derived from the same block.

2.2.2. Target types

Within the mixed block we classified each correctly selected target into one of three types (Figure 1, bottom panel). The First target (FT) is the first target detected in a row. A Switched target (SW) is a target of a different type from the immediately preceding selected target (e.g., a monkey following a dog), requiring a shift of the attended target category. A Non-switched target (NonSW) is a target of the same type as the preceding selection, allowing the same category to be maintained. This distinction operationalizes task switching at the level of individual selections and defines the contrast underlying the switch-cost biomarker.

2.5. Correcting Reaction Time for Scanning Load

>Correcting Reaction Time for Scanning Load

2.5.1. The confounding role of p

RTs following two processed items are not comparable to RTs following four, because the latter entail more visual search; uncorrected, any measure aggregated over p confounds the cognitive effect of interest with search effort. Scanning load must therefore be controlled for both biomarkers, but the two require different mechanisms because of their structure. Switch cost, a difference at matched p, has its control built into the matched-p construction, in which the common load cancels; no further model correction is applied. RTV, a within-category dispersion metric, has no matching to cancel the load and so requires the p-driven trend to be removed explicitly by a correction model. The two models below are therefore the mechanism by which RTV is corrected.

RTV was corrected within each target type using that target type own load slope, because the three types differ in scanning dynamics, a single shared slope would over-correct types whose RTV grows more slowly and under-correct those whose RTV grows more steeply, leaving residual load in some tracks. Non-switched Targets in particular follow a distinct load function; estimating their slope separately both yields an appropriate correction for the NonSW RTV biomarker and permits a direct test of whether their per-item slope differs from that of the other types.

2.6. External Validation Measure -Test of Variables of Attention (TOVA)

To evaluate the validity of the UCancellation biomarkers, participants completed the Test of Variables of Attention (TOVA), administered through Inquisit Web; usable data were available for 373 participants. The TOVA is a continuous performance task and a standard benchmark for sustained attention (Forbes, 1998; Greenberg & Waldman, 1993), from which we took reactiontime variability as the convergent criterion. In the task, participants are instructed to fixate in the center of the screen while attending to the upper and lower parts of the display. If the stimulus (small square) appears in the upper part, it is a target and participants must press a key. Instead, if the stimulus appears in the lower part, it is a non-target, and participants must hold the key press. The TOVA includes low-frequency (rare-target) and high-frequency (frequent-target) blocks. We took the low-frequency blocks as the convergent criterion, because RTV is theorized as a marker of vigilance and sustained attention, and low-frequency blocks-with long waits between rare targets-tax vigilance, whereas high-frequency blocks primarily load prepotent responding.

2.7. Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted in MATLAB 2025b. Because many measures departed from normality, associations were quantified with Spearman correlations. Reliability and convergent validity were computed for the RTV biomarkers only; switch cost, a within-task phenomenon, is characterized by its profile across p (above). Where appropriate we conducted standard likelihood-ratio test and t-tests.

2.7.1. Reliability

Split-half reliability of each RTV biomarker (the three per-target-type scores and the composite) was estimated as the Spearman correlation between scores computed on the two trial halves (odd vs even trials), 𝜌 ℎℎ , and stepped up to the full test length by the Spearman-Brown formula, 𝜌 full = 2𝜌 ℎℎ /(1 + 𝜌 ℎℎ ). For the corrected scores, the fixed group slope estimated on the full sample was applied to each half as a constant, rather than refitting the model within each half, so that the reliability estimate reflects the stability of the score and not sampling variability in the slope. To enable a like-for-like comparison, the reliability of the TOVA low-frequency criterion (its two-block Spearman correlation) is also expressed on the same full-length Spearman-Brown basis, so that UCancellation reliabilities and the criterion's reliability are compared as full-length coefficients.

2.7.2. Convergent validity

The association between each RTV biomarker and TOVA low-frequency RTV was estimated as an observed Spearman correlation with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval (5,000 resamples), in the 373-participant subsample. TOVA is the appropriate convergent criterion for RTV because both index sustained-attention/response consistency. Observed correlations with their confidence intervals are the primary evidence.

2.7.3. Model comparison (RTV)

To test whether correcting for scanning load improves the biomarker, ME and PC were each compared against the uncorrected (raw) RTV score-the same collapse over p with no load correction-on split-half reliability and observed convergent validity, for every per-target-type biomarker and the composite. ME and PC were also compared with each other, and the composite was compared against the strongest single per-type track.

2.7.4. Reaction-time dynamics across target types

These analyses concern raw RT data. Per-item RT slopes were compared across target types in a single mixed-effects model with a p × target-type interaction, testing whether the NonSW slope exceeds the FT and SW slopes. The switch-cost profile across p (≤ 4) was tested for a sign change using per-p tests against zero, the significance of the switch-cost-on-p slope, and the estimated zero-crossing.

3. Results

3.1. Reaction time by target type and search load

Reaction times were summarized as each participant's mean RT within each target type and level of p, after outlier removal, and analyses were restricted to p ≤ 4. Table 1 reports the number of contributing participants and the across-participant mean RT for each target type and level of p. Two features of the profiles (Figure 1) serve as a frame for the analyses that follow. First, RT increased monotonically with p for all three target types, consistent with each additional intervening item adding search time. Second, the types differed in both, level and rate of increase. First Target types were slowest at every p and rose most steeply (from ~1081 ms at p = 1 to ~2157 ms at p = 4), consistent with an initial orienting response to each trial row. At p = 1, Switched target types were much slower than Non-switched targets (~610 vs 312 ms)-the expected cost when the attended category has just changed. However, the two trajectories meet by p = 2 (~782 vs 778 ms) and then diverged from p = 3 onward. Furthermore, Non-switched RTs exceeded Switched RTs (~1210 vs 1134 ms at p = 3; ~1602 vs 1522 ms at p = 4). This crossover-Non-switched responses beginning fastest but overtaking Switched responses as search load grows-is the basis for the switch-cost reversal and target-type slope differences quantified next. indistinguishable from zero at p = 2 (M = 11 ms, t(380) = 1.30, p = .19) and had reversed by p = 3, where Non-switched responses were slower than Switched (M = -99 ms, t(355) = -6.66, p < .001); the p = 4 estimate remained negative but did not differ from zero (M = -38 ms, t(132) = -0.95, p = .34; n = 133). Across p, switch cost declined significantly (mixed-effects slope = -140.7 ms per item, 95% CI [-157.6, -123.7]; t(1241) = -16.3, p < .001), with the crossover falling near p = 2. Because the effect changes in sign across p, a value collapsed over p is near zero and uninformative. Therefore, for the switch-cost readout we recommend the p = 1 switch cost (~248 ms), which isolates the category switch before the reversal emerges.

3.2.2. Non-switched RT slopes are steeper

The reversal reflects a difference in how quickly RT grows with search load across target types (Figure 1), tested in a mixed-effects model with a p × target-type interaction. Per-item RT slopes were steepest for Non-switched targets (447 ms per item, 95% CI [432, 462]), intermediate for First targets (364 [348, 380]), and shallowest for Switched targets (310 [296, 324]). The Nonswitched slope exceeded both the First target slope (Δ = 83 ms per item, SE = 9.7, 95% CI [64, 102]) and the Switched slope (Δ = 137 ms per item, SE = 8.7, 95% CI [120, 154]); both intervals exclude zero by a wide margin. The Non-switched RT growth across p showed no reliable acceleration (a quadratic term did not improve fit; if anything the trend was slightly decelerating), so the reversal arises from a steeper, approximately constant per-item RT rather than an accelerating one. Thus, Non-switched responses, though fastest when targets are next to each other (p = 1), integrates search-related delays most rapidly, surpassing Switched responses by p = 3 and producing the observed switch cost sign reversal. Possible mechanisms, including inhibition of return, are considered in the Discussion.

3.3. Reaction time variability (RTV)

>Reaction time variability (RTV)

3.3.1. RTV by target type, search load, and its functional form

RTV was computed within each participant, target type, and level of p. Table 2 reports the number of participants and the across-participant mean RTV for each cell. RTV increased with p for all three target types, but the three differed sharply in the shape of that increase (Figure 3). We assessed functional form within each target type by comparing a linear model of RTV on p against a quadratic model and against a saturated model treating p as categorical (mixed-effects fits, maximum likelihood; lack of fit evaluated as the linear-versussaturated likelihood-ratio test). For First targets (Figure 3, left), the linear model was adequate. That is, it did not significantly differ from the saturated model (LR = 2.0, df = 2, p = .37) and a quadratic term did not improve fit (p = .30). The FT slope was shallow (~34 ms per item), consistent with RTV changing little with search load for the initial orienting response.

For Switched targets (Figure 3, middle), RTV rose approximately linearly (slope ~48 ms per item), and a quadratic term did not reliably improve fit (p = .05). A well powered saturatedmodel comparison detected a small departure (LR = 26.0, df = 2, p < .001) mostly due to a nearflat p = 1 to p = 2 segment, but this local irregularity was negligible in magnitude, with residuals within ~30 ms, and the profile was not a smooth curvature (Figure 4). Thus, we treated SW as linear for the rest of the analysis.

For Non-switched targets (Figure 3, right), the linear model failed significantly (LR = 155.1, df = 2, p < .001) and the departure was large and concave (quadratic term strongly negative, p < .001). As seen in Figure 3, RTV jumped from p = 1 to p = 2 and then flattened, so a straight line badly overshoots at higher p (slope ~110 ms per item). This saturating profile is visible as an arch in the linear-model residuals-the mean deviation of observed RTV from the fitted line (intercept + slope × p) at each p (Figure 4). This is a robust descriptive feature of Non-switch variability. Importantly, not that this lack-of-fit residual is a diagnostic of the linear model and is distinct from the ME-corrected biomarker, which subtracts only the p-dependent term, β × p, and so preserves each participant's overall RTV magnitude rather than centering it at zero (Figure 5).

Thus the three target types differ in functional form: linear is adequate for FT, a practical approximation for SW despite a small departure from linearity, and clearly inadequate for NonSW. As shown next, however, a quadratic correction for NonSW does not improve the biomarker's psychometric properties, so we kept the linear correction for all three, and NonSW's concavity is reported here as a descriptive feature of its scanning dynamics rather than assumed into the correction model.

3.3.2. RTV scanning load correction and reliability

RTV biomarkers were formed by removing the fitted per-target-type scanning load (mixedeffects correction, ME), which subtracts only the p-dependent term and so preserves each participant's overall RTV magnitude, and collapsing over p (median). To note, the following analyses were conducted using the TOVA subsample (n = 373). Figure 5 shows the effect of this correction: for First and Switched targets the corrected profile is approximately flat across p, confirming that the linear term captures their load dependence, whereas for Non-switched targets the corrected profile remains curved and dips below zero at p = 1 and p = 4-the residual concavity (Figure 4) that a linear correction cannot remove, and the reason NonSW corrected RTV can take negative (adjusted-score) values. We evaluated whether correcting for scanning load improves the biomarker by comparing the uncorrected RTV score against both corrections-ME and the simpler per-item PC alternative on both reliability and validity, for each per-target-type biomarker and the across-target composite. Split-half reliabilities (odd vs even trials, Spearman-Brown corrected to full length) are shown in Figure 6, with the external criterion (TOVA low-frequency RTV) as a benchmark.

Correcting for scanning load improved reliability, especially for Non-switched targets. Raw Non-switched RTV was essentially unreliable (~ .04). As shown in Figure 3, its variability rises sharply from p = 1 to p = 2 before saturating, so the uncorrected score is dominated by this large load-driven swing rather than by stable individual differences. Removing the load trend rescued it to a usable level (ME ~ .27, PC ~ .26). First target RTV, by contrast, was unaffected by correction (raw ~ .45, ME ~ .45), possibly because its load-driven variance is small relative to its large baseline variability. Likewise, Switched RTV improved modestly (raw ~ .43, ME ~ .46, PC ~ .50). Correction thus helped most where the load confound was largest (NonSW) and least where it was smallest (FT). Correcting NonSW with a quadratic rather than a linear term neither improved reliability (~ .24 vs ~ .27) nor convergent validity (TOVA ρ ~ .11 vs ~ .11), confirming the decision to retain the linear correction. In other words, the more complex model adds parameters with no psychometric benefit. The across-target composite was the most reliable index (raw ~ .49, ME ~ .60, PC ~ .66), exceeding every single track and the criterion itself. The two corrections were comparable to each other, with PC marginally more reliable than ME for SW and the composite.

3.3.3. Validity of the RTV biomarkers

Within the TOVA subsample (n = 373), convergent validity was assessed as the Spearman correlation between each RTV biomarker and TOVA low-frequency RTV, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (Table 3). First target RTV was the strongest correlate under either correction (ME ρ = .18, 95% CI [.09, .28]; PC ρ = .15 [.05, .24]), followed by the composite (ME ρ = .19 [.08, .29]; PC ρ = .16 [.06, .27]). Switched RTV was weaker (ME ρ = .14 [.03, .24]) and Non-switched weakest (ME ρ = .11 [.00, .21]). All correlations were positive and their intervals excluded zero. Three points frame these values. First, correcting for scanning load improved convergent validity most where it improved reliability most. For instance, raw Non-switched RTV did not correlate with the criterion (ρ = .02, CI including zero), whereas corrected Non-switched RTV did (ME ρ = .11, CI excluding zero). In contrast, First target validity was essentially unchanged by correction (raw and ME both ρ ~ .18), mirroring the reliability pattern. Second, the correlations are modest in absolute terms but must be read against the criterion's own reliability, which caps the observable correlation. In other word, with FT reliability of ~ .45 and TOVA-LF reliability of ~ .52, the maximum attainable convergent correlation is approximately √(.45 × .52) ~ .48, so the observed FT correlation of .18 is roughly 40% of the achievable validity ceiling-a substantial fraction for cross-instrument convergence with a moderately reliable criterion. Third, ME and PC did not differ meaningfully in validity. ME point estimates were slightly higher for FT, SW, and the composite and essentially equal for NonSW, but every ME and PC interval overlapped extensively, so the two corrections are equivalent on convergent validity.

Interestingly, the composite matched the best single track (FT) on validity while exceeding it on reliability (Figure 6). Aggregating the three target types added reliable variance without adding or subtracting construct-relevant validity. The composite is therefore the more reliable single index, not a more valid one.

3.3.4. ME versus PC

As shown above, the ME and PC RTV corrections were empirically equivalent as scores and both improved reliability and validity over the uncorrected score. We showed that convergent validity was statistically indistinguishable (overlapping CIs throughout, Table 3) and reliability was comparable, with PC marginally higher for SW and the composite (Figure 6). A reliability edge without a corresponding validity gain is uninformative about construct quality. On the psychometric evidence, then, the two corrections are interchangeable as scores.

The case for the ME framework therefore rests on what PC cannot do rather than on score quality. PC is a fixed proportional transform with no parameters. As a result, it cannot represent the concave NonSW load function (Figure 4), cannot yield the per-target-type slopes whose differences constitute the reaction-time dissociation, and cannot be diagnosed for lack of fit or extended when its functional assumption fails. ME supplies all of these limitations. We therefore suggest adopting the ME decomposition as the analytic framework and report ME-corrected RTV biomarkers, while noting that for pure deployment the simpler PC transform yields a nearidentical index.

4. Discussion

In the present study we derived two RT biomarkers from the UCancellation task-reaction-time variability (RTV) and switch cost-and characterized their behaviors as a function of scanning load (p), the number of items processed since the previous target. Both proved informative, but in different ways and to different evidentiary standards. RTV behaved as a scored individualdifference measure of sustained attention, showing reliable convergent validity with an established continuous-performance criterion (TOVA). Switch cost behaved as a within-task phenomenon whose sign reverses with scanning load. Below we interpret each in turn, then consider the correction framework and the study's limitations.

One interesting behavioral finding was the reversal of switch cost across p. When targets were adjacent (p = 1), switching to a new target category was substantially slower than maintaining it-the classic switch cost expected from the task-switching literature, in which rearranging resources to a competing and similar task carries a processing penalty (Allport et al., 1994; Los, 1996; Wylie & Allport, 2000). As scanning load increased, however, this cost shrank to zero (near p = 2) and then reversed by p = 3 non-switched targets were slower than switched targets.

Decomposing the reversal showed that it arises not from any change in switching per se but from a difference in how steeply reaction time grows with search load across target types. Specifically, non-switched targets accumulated per-item search cost most rapidly, surpassing switched targets as more items intervened. Importantly, this steeper growth was approximately linear rather than accelerating, indicating a constant elevated per-item cost rather than a runaway process.

This pattern is consistent with inhibition of return (IOR), the tendency for attention to be discouraged from returning to recently inspected locations or items (Klein, 2000). Under the foraging-facilitator account, inhibitory tags accumulate on inspected items to bias search forward (Klein & MacInnes, 1999), and such tags have been estimated to persist over roughly four previously inspected items (Wang & Klein, 2010)-the same window over which our reversal appears. When a target repeats its category (non-switch), the searcher must continue scanning increasingly tagged and recently rejected items of the same kind, resulting in a increasing per-item penalty. In contrast, a category switch redirects attention toward a differently coded set that is less subject to accumulated inhibition. On this reading, the "reverse switch cost" at high p is not a failure of switching but a signature of the inhibitory machinery that makes serial search efficient. Because a value collapsed across p averages the positive and negative regimes to near zero, the phenomenon is best described by its full p-profile, and the p = 1 value-where switching is isolated from these search dynamics-is the appropriate singlecondition readout for applications.

The non-switched condition was distinctive on both biomarkers, but in different ways. In mean reaction time it showed the steepest, and essentially linear, growth with load whereas in variability it showed a strongly concave, saturating profile, with most of its individual-difference signal concentrated at low p. That a single condition is linear in central tendency yet nonlinear in dispersion underscores that RT level and RT variability are separable aspects of performance rather than redundant summaries (Yamashita et al., 2021), and that variability carries dynamics not visible in the mean.

RTV is among the most robust behavioural markers of sustained-attention capacity and is elevated across ADHD, ageing, and other conditions marked by attentional lapses (Castellanos et al., 2005; Kofler et al., 2013; Machida et al., 2019). Our RTV biomarkers converged with TOVA low-frequency variability, a theoretically matched criterion, with first-target RTV the strongest single correlate. These correlations were modest in absolute terms but should be read against the psychometric ceiling because the criterion is itself only moderately reliable, the maximum attainable convergent correlation is well below one, and the observed correlations recover a substantial fraction of that ceiling (~ 40%). A composite across the three target types was the most reliable index-exceeding every single track and the criterion's own reliability-and matched the best single track on validity. The composite is therefore the more reliable single index for deployment, though not a more valid one; the reliability gain reflects the cancellation of track-specific error through aggregation rather than the addition of new attention-relevant variance. This strong internal consistency establishes the composite as a promising candidate index, and a natural next step is to characterize its temporal stability, or test-retest reliability, which is the property most relevant to tracking individuals and detecting change over time. As UCancellation is brief and repeatable, it is well suited to such an evaluation, and benchmarking its across-session stability against that of established continuous-performance measures such as the TOVA would help confirm its utility as a practical alternative.

Removing scanning-load variance is essential before RTV can be compared across trials, and the benefit of doing so was demonstrable by the improved reliability and convergent validity of the RTV biomarkers after correction. The effect was especially dramatic for non-switched targets, whose steep load dependence rendered the raw score neither reliable nor valid, but which became a usable biomarker once the load trend was removed. Having established that correcting helps, we contrasted a mixed-effects correction with a simpler per-item transform. Empirically the two were interchangeable as scores. That is, convergent validity was statistically indistinguishable, and reliability was comparable across both metrics. The case for the mixedeffects framework therefore is not based on score superiority but on what it makes possible: it yields interpretable per-target-type slopes whose differences constitute the reaction-time dissociation described above, it can be diagnosed for lack of fit, and it can be extended when its functional assumptions fail-none of which a fixed proportional transform permits. We therefore adopt the mixed-effects decomposition as the analytic framework while noting that, for pure deployment, the simpler transform yields a near-identical index.

There are several limitations to our current study. First, the convergent criterion was moderately reliable, capping observable validity and leaving the true convergence underestimated. Switch cost is reported as a group-level phenomenon rather than a validated individual-difference trait. Because its sign depends on scanning load, it does not behave as a stable scored measure, and we make no claim about its reliability or its convergent validity. Non-switched RTV, though a genuine biomarker, was the least reliable track, and its curvature means a linear correction is a deliberate approximation rather than a faithful model of its dynamics. Finally, the IOR account of the reversal, while consistent with the data and with the known time-course of inhibitory tagging, is an interpretation of an observational pattern; direct tests-for example, manipulating inter-target spacing or probing previously inspected items-would be needed to establish the mechanism.

Extracting reaction-time variability and switch cost from the UCancellation task broadens a brief, engaging cancellation measure into a richer assessment of attentional dynamics. RTV provides a deployable, criterion-valid index of sustained attention, most reliable as a cross-target composite; switch cost reveals a load-dependent reversal that indexes the inhibitory dynamics of serial search. Together they illustrate that reaction-time structure within a cancellation task carries information about attention and inhibitory control that traditional measures, such as mean accuracy/concentration scores alone do not capture.
