Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The Culture of Dissemblance Among Black American Women: A Mixed Methods

Assessment

Black women continue to suffer from health disparities in the remission and treatment rates of mental health disorders (Jacob Arriola et al., 2007; Lacey et al., 2015; Zheng & Echave, 2021). One contributing factor to these disparities may be that culturally specific, transdiagnostic emotion regulation strategies prevalent within the Black community remain poorly understood within mainstream psychological frameworks, limiting the development of treatments that are responsive to the unique emotional experiences of Black women. Contemporary research on the Strong Black Woman and Superwoman schemas has begun to document how cultural expectations of strength and emotional restraint relate to Black women's mental health, relating emotional suppression to depressive symptoms through social isolation and disengagement coping (Jones et al., 2021; Liao et al., 2020; Nelson et al., 2024). However, these frameworks focus on contemporary role expectations, and less is known about historically rooted, culturally learned strategies of emotional concealment.

There is need for a theoretical shift towards the intellectual and experiential contributions of Black women as a way to address disparities in mental health treatment. The culture of dissemblance (COD) is a concept created by Darlene Clark Hine, in which Black women are understood to create an illusion of openness while hiding the intimate, emotional aspects of their identity as an act of self-preservation (Hine, 1989). This adaptive strategy allows for consistent navigation of heightened societal perception and demands that Black women endure across public and private spaces via the suppression of emotional expression.

Interpersonal Violence and Sociopolitical Positioning

The COD was originally described to explain the main drivers of migration for American Black women relocating to the Midwest and Northern cities from the South, based on a desire to escape sexual and domestic violence while also searching for better economic opportunity (Hine, 1989). The COD was conceptualized to hold nuance and contradiction much like Black women's positioning in American society. Within this positioning, women have to navigate life at the intersection of racial and gender hierarchies where the tools of oppression are often in tension with each other (Spillers, 1987). For example, oppression of women is rationalized via infantilization while oppression based on race via dehumanization. This is evident in the ways Black women were expected to perform domestic and manual labor across gender and racial normative roles (Camp, 2004) while also being exposed to compounded racial and sexual violence as well as domestic violence (Haley, 2016). As a result of the contradicting nature of this positioning, Black women become hypervisible by way of increased risk for gender and race-related interpersonal violence combined with elevated importance within domestic spaces as wives, mothers, and caregivers. In other ways, Black women are largely invisible due to societal tendencies that dismiss and undermine Black women's emotional realities (e.g., stereotypes like the "angry Black woman"). This contradiction builds on Black feminist scholarship that highlights the unique role Black women possess in American society at the intersection of racial and gender hierarchies (Combahee River Collective, 1979; Collins, 1986 Collins, , 2000;; Spillers, 1987). Further, societal narratives about Black women work to silence and dismiss their feelings and objections about these harmful experiences (Spillers, 1987). Stereotypes like the "angry Black woman" or "strong Black woman" obscure the intimate realities and experiences Black women have, making the negative effects of hypervisibility invisible. Going further, these stereotypes come with measurable consequences such that in professional settings beliefs about Black women's expression of anger negatively influence how they are evaluated by others (Motro et al., 2022). In order to navigate this paradox, Black women developed a "guise of openness" which allows them to exist and move freely across various social hierarchies while also hiding and protecting their true feelings and inner lives from a society designed to misunderstand and reject them (Hine, 1989).

Relating the Culture of Dissemblance to PTSD Scholars like Sami Schalk have connected the COD to the prevalence of untreated mental health disorders like PTSD and depression amongst Black women (Schalk, 2020). In this framework the COD is largely influenced by Black women's experiences of harmful psychosocial stressors like race-related discrimination, interpersonal violence, and sexual violence. As such, COD emerges as a culturally relevant protective strategy when emotional invalidation is expected, however it also obscures women's need for emotional care and mental health treatment.

Black women report higher PTSD symptoms and a higher prevalence of depression than Black men and other racial groups (Cénat et al., 2025; Himle et al., 2009; Lacey et al., 2015), and stigma, cost, and a lack of culturally responsive care are significant barriers to treatment (Holden & Xanthos, 2009; Modeste-James et al., 2024). Importantly, emotional dysregulation is a vital element of many psychiatric conditions like PTSD and depression (Abravanel & Sinha, 2015; Crow et al., 2014; Klimova et al., 2013; Mekawi et al., 2021). Underscoring the relevance of emotion regulation to race-related stress, it was found that emotion dysregulation partially accounted for the association between racial discrimination and PTSD symptoms in African American adults, even after accounting for trauma exposure (Mekawi et al., 2020). Thus, being able to understand what distinguishes successful emotion regulation from emotional dysregulation is important in order to address mental health disparities for Black women. While Black feminist literature has explored how the COD manifests in the lives of American Black women, it is unknown how this cultural emotional regulation strategy intersects with known psychology-based theories of emotion regulation like reappraisal and suppression (Cutuli, 2014; Gross, 2015).

Emotion Regulation Strategies

Successful emotion regulation allows individuals to adapt to everyday social situations by employing cognitive strategies that modify how situations and resulting emotions are experienced (Cutuli, 2014; Goldin et al., 2008; Gross, 2015). Antecedent-focused emotion regulation strategies modify the context surrounding the emotion before the emotional response has occurred (Gross, 1998) and response-focused strategies control one's behavior following the emotional response (Gross, 1998; Volokhov & Demaree, 2010). Reappraisal, a well-studied antecedent strategy, involves cognitively altering the context surrounding the emotional stimulus whereas suppression ,a response-focused strategy, involves inhibition of physical emotional expression (Volokhov & Demaree, 2010). Although both strategies help individuals adapt to social contexts, reappraisal more effectively reduces internal negative emotion and physiological reactivity, with long-term adaptive effects on emotional processing (Cutuli, 2014; Goldin et al., 2008; Gross, 1998 Gross, , 2015;; Volokhov & Demaree, 2010). In contrast, suppression modulates only the internal perception of positive stimuli (Volokhov & Demaree, 2010), increases stress physiology (Butler et al., 2007; Gross & Levenson, 1993; Roberts et al., 2008), negative affect, and depressive symptoms (Moore et al., 2008), and impairs cognition (Moore & Zoellner, 2012; Niermeyer et al., 2019). Habitual, trait-like suppression is further associated with greater negative affect, lower life satisfaction, increased cardiovascular disease risk, and altered immune function (Sikka et al., 2022).

There are notable cultural and situational differences in the effectiveness of reappraisal and suppression. For example, Soto et al. (2011) emphasized sociocultural effects on emotional processing such that suppression appears to be an effective long-term emotion regulation strategy, especially for minority groups that emphasize collectivistic values like social cohesion.

In this case it may be that suppression is a beneficial strategy for minority groups that need to perform harmony with outgroups during times of discrimination or social stress. Among Black Americans specifically, suppression following discrimination appears to depend on how individuals interpret prejudice such that believing that prejudice stems from shared societal ignorance was associated with greater expressive suppression, which in turn predicted psychological distress (Chaney et al., 2024). This suggests that beliefs about how society perceives one's group may shape emotional concealment, which this current study examines directly.

Current Study

Although Black feminist scholars have theorized the COD as a culturally learned emotional masking that can have potential impacts on mental health (Hine, 1989; Schalk, 2020), it has not yet been empirically examined within psychological science. Existing research on Black women's emotional experiences has focused largely on contemporary role expectations, such as the Strong Black Woman and Superwoman schemas (Jones et al., 2021; Liao et al., 2020; Nelson et al., 2024), and on conventional emotion regulation strategies conceptualized within mainstream psychological frameworks (Gross & John, 2003). It therefore is still unclear whether and how COD has changed within Black women's emotional experiences more than 30 years following the initial conceptualization of this phenomenon. It is also unclear how COD relates to established emotional regulatory constructs like suppression, and how it associates with stressors and mental health outcomes. Addressing these gaps is necessary to develop culturally informed models of emotional processing and treatment approaches that reflect Black women's lived experiences.

In this mixed methods study, Black women completed qualitative interviews about their emotional experiences and self-report questionnaires assessing hypothesized contributors to the COD (lifetime trauma, interpersonal and sexual violence, racial discrimination) and hypothesized correlates (emotion regulation, PTSD symptoms, beliefs about societal perceptions of Black people). The study addressed three main research questions. Question 1 (qualitative): How do participants report their experiences of emotional expression in public and private spaces, how does identity (influenced by gender and race) influence emotional expression, and what is the historical context of where and how they learned to engage with their emotions? We hypothesized that participants would endorse emotion regulation strategies consistent with the COD within the sample. Specifically, it was predicted that themes related to the importance of emotional stereotypes and public dismissal of emotions would emerge as motivators for dissemblance behaviors and that the importance of safe spaces would emerge as a mitigation strategy that allows for emotional expression. Question 2 (mixed methods): Are experiences of racial discrimination and trauma associated with interview endorsement of COD? It was hypothesized that individuals who experienced more traumatic experiences and racial discrimination, would endorse more COD in their interviews (measured by reporting of dissemblance-related emotional behaviors). Question 3 (mixed methods): Is COD endorsement associated with PTSD symptoms, habitual emotion regulation strategies, and beliefs about societal perceptions of Black people? It was hypothesized that COD endorsement would correlate with greater PTSD symptoms, lower perceived public regard of Black identity, and more habitual usage of suppression.

Method Study Design

The current study was an empirical study that utilized a convergent mixed methods design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018) because qualitative and quantitative data addressed complementary questions. This methodology is fitting for the current study due to the fact COD has not been explored empirically and thus qualitative interviews were necessary to capture how Black women describe dissemblance in their own terms. Additionally, being able to understand COD in relation to habitual suppression, stressors, mental health outcomes required the usage of quantitative measures. Integration occurred through quantitizing qualitative data (Sandelowski et al., 2009) in which the frequencies of dissemblance-related codes were summed to create a COD endorsement score. This score was then examined in relation to questionnaire measures.

Integrating the two approaches allowed participants' understandings to define the construct while being able to tests its theorized correlates within the same population. As such, this mixed methods approach aligns with Black feminist principles that center lived experience as a source of knowledge.

Participants

Participants were 49 Black women aged 40-55 recruited through a collaborative research effort investigating civilian trauma and PTSD at a large publicly funded hospital in the southeastern United States. We focused on Black women because the COD derives from Black women's sociopolitical experiences in recent American history. Because the parent study examined the perimenopausal transition and mental health, inclusion criteria were age 40-55, an intact uterus and at least one ovary, the ability to give informed consent, a phone for contact, and the ability to speak and understand English. Exclusion criteria were use of hormonal or psychotropic medications, pregnancy or breastfeeding, breast or uterine cancer, MRI contraindications, and mental health hospitalization within the past month.

More than half of participants were college educated (57.1%), and 67.3% reported a monthly income of $2,000 or more. On average, participants reported about four lifetime traumatic events, five situations of racial discrimination and low PTSD symptoms (M = 17.39, SD = 15.88; Table 1). Most participants (59%) were raised in the southern United States, and 10% were raised outside the United States (Figure 2). Full demographics are reported in Results.

Data Collection Procedures

All participants provided written informed consent, and procedures were approved by the university Institutional Review Board and the hospital Research Oversight Committee. After the parent study visit, participants were invited to a virtual or in-person visit consisting of questionnaires and a semi-structured interview; all 49 agreed, and 3 chose to be interviewed in person. Interviews asked how participants understood emotions and feelings, societal and familial influences on emotional expression, and general emotional engagement (Appendix A).

The first author conducted all interviews between June 2023 and December 2024. Interviews lasted 12 min 48 s to 1 hr 27 min 21 s (M = 29 min 7 s). All interviews, including the three conducted in person, were audio recorded via Zoom and transcribed with Zoom transcription software (Version 7.0.5; 2025). The first author then checked and corrected the transcripts for accuracy. Identifiable information was removed, transcripts were stored on locked devices, and cleaned transcripts were imported into MAXQDA (Version 24.10.0; 2026) for analysis.

Quantitative Approach

Self-Report Measures

Participants completed the following measures and a demographics questionnaire assessing age, education, and income.

Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ). The ERQ (Gross & John, 2003) assesses habitual use of reappraisal and suppression with 10 items rated from 1 to 7. Subscale totals reflect habitual use (Reappraisal: 6-42; Suppression: 4-28). For analyses, subscale scores were expressed as percentages of the maximum possible score for the respective subscale. In community samples, internal consistency is acceptable to excellent (Reappraisal α = .89-.90; Suppression α = .76-.80; Preece et al., 2020), and 3-month test-retest reliability is .69 for both subscales (Gross & John, 2003). Confirmatory factor analyses support a two-factor model with largely independent factors and excellent fit, SBχ²(34) = 92.68, CFI = .96, RMSEA = .058, 90% CI [.048, .069], and fit was consistent across age, education, and gender (Preece et al., 2020). Gross and John (2003) found greater suppression among African American than European American participants, supporting the scale's use with Black women. For convergent validity, reappraisal was associated with experiencing and expressing positive emotion (β = .42 and .37), and suppression was associated with inauthenticity (β = .47) and less sharing of positive (β = -.37) and negative (β = -.26) emotions. For discriminant validity, neither subscale was meaningfully associated with cognitive ability (Reappraisal β = .02; Suppression β = -.09) or social desirability (Reappraisal β = .11; Suppression β = -.09; Gross & John, 2003). Outlying ERQ scores (Suppression: n = 5; Reappraisal: n = 3) were winsorized at the 5th and 95th percentiles, which replaces extreme values with the percentile value rather than removing cases and reducing the sample (Wilcox, 2012).

Experiences of Discrimination (EOD).

The EOD (Krieger et al., 2005) assesses whether participants have experienced discrimination due to race, color, or ethnicity in nine situations (yes/no). Scores reflect the number of situations endorsed (range: 0-9). Internal consistency was α = .74 overall and α = .81 among Black participants, and a single-factor structure fit well among Black and Latinx participants (CFI = .966; RMSEA = .069; Krieger et al., 2005). Among Black women recruited through the same hospital-based trauma research program, α = .84 (Mekawi et al., 2021). The EOD correlates with Williams' Everyday Discrimination and Major Experiences of Discrimination scales (both r = .61, p < .001), supporting convergent validity, and does not correlate with social desirability (r = -.05, p = .54), supporting discriminant validity (Krieger et al., 2005).

Multidimensional Inventory of Black Identity (MIBI).

The 27-item MIBI Short Form assesses Assimilation, Centrality, Humanist, Nationalist, Oppressed Minority, Public Regard, and Private Regard (Sellers et al., 1997; Cokley & Helm, 2001). Items are rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Only Public Regard (range: 4-28) was analyzed, to examine how perceptions of society's views of Black people relate to COD endorsement. Internal consistency was α = .60-.79 in the original validation with African American college students (Sellers et al., 1997). Later samples showed α = .72-.83 in a general sample and α = .70-.85 in an HBCU sample (Cokley & Helm, 2001). Confirmatory factor analysis showed mixed support for the multidimensional structure, χ²(1465, N = 279) = 2921.20, p < .001, χ²/df = 1.99, NFI = .56, CFI = .71, SRMR = .09 (Cokley & Helm, 2001). For convergent validity, Centrality predicted interactions with other Black people (r = .39, p < .01) and enrollment in Black studies courses, F(1, 472) = 7.98, p < .01 (Sellers et al., 1997). For discriminant validity, Public Regard was unrelated to personal self-esteem (β = -.01; Rowley et al., 1998), indicating that the subscale captures beliefs about societal views rather than self-evaluation.

Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5). The LEC-5 (Gray et al., 2004; Weathers et al., 2013) assesses 17 potentially traumatic events. For each event, participants indicate whether it happened to them, they witnessed it, they learned about it, they were exposed to it as part of their job, or it does not apply. The number of events directly experienced (range: 0-17) was analyzed, along with two subscales. Sexual violence was coded dichotomously (0 = no,1 = yes) from endorsement of sexual violence having happened to the participant. Interpersonal violence (IPV) was scored continuously from endorsement of being attacked with and/or without a weapon. The original LEC reliably measured direct trauma exposure (κ = .61) and showed a high test-retest correlation (r = .82, p < .001; Gray et al., 2004). The LEC-5 retains that format with minor wording changes reflecting DSM-5 and shows fair to good test-retest reliability for direct exposure (ICCs = .62-.64; Pugach et al., 2021). For convergent validity, LEC direct-exposure scores correlated with the Traumatic Life Events Questionnaire (r = .55, p < .001) and with PTSD symptom severity (r = .48, p < .05; Gray et al., 2004). Discriminant validity has not been formally established because the LEC-5 is an inventory of event exposure rather than a measure of a latent construct (Gray et al., 2004; Pugach et al., 2021).

PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5).

The PCL-5 uses 20 items to assess how much participants were bothered by trauma-related symptoms in the past month. Items are rated from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely), and total scores range from 0 to 80 (Blevins et al., 2015). Two participants who reported no traumatic event did not complete the PCL-5, and their scores were coded as 0. The PCL-5 shows strong internal consistency (α = .94) and test-retest reliability (r = .82). It also shows convergent validity with the Personality Assessment Inventory Traumatic Stress scale (r = .74; Blevins et al., 2015). Among Black adults recruited from the same hospitalbased research program, internal consistency was excellent (full scale α = .96; subscales α = .85-.91). A unidimensional model showed adequate fit given high intercorrelations among DSM-5 symptom clusters (r = .63-.82; Mekawi et al., 2022), supporting use of the total score. For discriminant validity, correlations with constructs unrelated to PTSD, such as antisocial personality features (r = .39) and mania (r = .31), were lower (Blevins et al., 2015).

Data Analytic Plan

Analyses were exploratory because this is the first quantitative examination of the COD.

Analyses were conducted in RStudio (R version 4.2.1), and there were no missing data. All variables were continuous except sexual violence (0 = no,1 = yes). After parametric assumptions were checked, analyses proceeded in three steps.

First, potential confounds were identified. Pearson correlations tested associations of age with the predictors, which were lifetime traumatic events (LEC-5) and discrimination (EOD), and with the outcomes, which were PTSD symptoms (PCL-5), reappraisal and suppression (ERQ), and public regard (MIBI). One-way ANOVAs tested whether these variables differed across education and income categories, both of which are ordinal. Demographic variables significantly associated with a study variable were included as covariates in analyses involving that variable.

Second, Pearson correlations tested associations between adjusted COD scores and the predictor and outcome variables. The false discovery rate was controlled with Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

Third, for associations that remained significant after correction (pFDR < .05), hierarchical linear regression tested whether COD remained associated with the outcome after controlling for covariates. Covariate-adjusted regressions were also run for theoretically central associations. To limit overfitting with 49 participants, covariates were restricted to variables that were both theoretically relevant and significantly associated with the outcome. This is consistent with simulation evidence that small subjects-per-variable ratios yield acceptable coefficient estimates in linear regression (Austin & Steyerberg, 2015). Discrimination, lifetime trauma exposure, and sexual violence were included as covariates in the public regard model based on theoretical relevance and their associations with public regard (pFDR < .05). No interaction terms were tested.

Based on prior research linking racial discrimination and racial identity attitudes (e.g., Sellers & Shelton, 2003), we anticipated moderate associations (r ≈ .30). A G*Power 3.1.9.7 sensitivity power analysis (Faul et al., 2009) indicated that the sample provided 80% power to detect correlations of r ≥ .39 (two-tailed, α = .05).

Parametric Assumptions

Distributions were examined using skewness, kurtosis, Shapiro-Wilk tests, and Q-Q plots. Although several variables departed from normality on Shapiro-Wilk tests, all skewness (-0.27 to 1.00) and kurtosis (-1.26 to 0.25) values for continuous variables fell within recommended thresholds for approximate normality (|skew| < 2, |kurtosis| < 7; West, 1995).

Pearson correlations were therefore retained, and Spearman correlations were computed as a sensitivity check; the pattern of results was unchanged, including the association between COD and public regard (r = -.38; ρ = -.34). In regards to the public regard model, assumptions were met (residual normality p = .37; Breusch-Pagan p = .40; variance inflation factors (VIFs) ≤ 2.36; Durbin-Watson = 2.12). One case exceeded the Cook's distance threshold (4/n); results were unchanged when it was excluded (b = -0.37, p = .018). All descriptive statistics are reported in Table 1.

Qualitative Approach

Theoretical Framework

Codebook thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021) provided a structured coding framework for interpretive theme development (Alase, 2017; Smith et al., 2009). This method was selected because the study was grounded in Hine's (1989) theory of the COD while remaining open to patterns the theory did not anticipate. The orienting frameworks were constructivist grounded theory (Charmaz, 2017) and qualitative critical phenomenology (de Boer & Zeiler, 2024). Both emphasize learning from subjective lived experience and the importance of flexibility and reflexivity in analysis. Both also align with Black feminist principles emphasizing the everyday expert and dialogue in knowledge creation (Collins, 2000; Combahee River Collective, 1979; Lorde, 1984; Patterson et al., 2016; Williams & Lewis, 2021; Williamson, 2017).

Researcher Positionality

The first author identifies as a Black femme individual, and the research questions emerged in part from personal and scholarly engagement with the culture of dissemblance. This insider position shaped the study's design, including the decision to center participants' own language for emotional experience. To guard against interpreting the data solely through the first author's experience, the first author wrote memos after each interview documenting the interviewer's reactions and assumptions. A second coder coded interviews independently, all disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion, and a community advisory board reviewed the interview guide before data collection

Sample Adequacy

The qualitative sample was the same 49 women included in the quantitative analyses.

Purposive criterion sampling was used: all participants were enrolled in the parent study and selfidentified as Black women aged 40-55 (Palinkas et al., 2015). Sample adequacy was evaluated using information power (Malterud et al., 2016). Information power was considered high given the specificity of the sample (Black women aged 40-55 with a shared sociopolitical context), the use of an established theoretical framework (the culture of dissemblance; Hine, 1989), and the depth of the interview dialogue. The final sample was determined by the number of parent study participants who agreed to be interviewed. It substantially exceeds the 9-17 interviews typically found sufficient to reach saturation (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022).

Interview Guide Development

The conceptual framework (Figure 1) was developed as a part of a larger study to understand how the COD is engaged in Black women in both experimental conditions and everyday life and was used to establish the initial set of interview questions. The qualitative aim of the conceptual framework details research questions intended to be answered by the interviews. Interview questions were then derived by assessing how to frame questions so that they would address the research aims while also providing space for personal interpretation and anecdotal responses. The interview process was approached with flexibility and openness as to what participants would share about their emotional engagement: questions were intentionally designed not to bias how participants talk about their emotional engagement (i.e., using leading language, or focusing overly on all negative or all positive experiences). Once the interview guide was established, it was presented to Grady Trauma Project's Community Advisory Board in which members suggested changes pertaining to question wording and stylistic presentation of questions. Accordingly, these changes were incorporated into the interview guide prior to data collection. Final interview questions are reported in the supplemental materials under Interview Guide (Appendix A).

Coding and Theme Development

After the conceptual framework and interview guide were developed, a coding guide (Figure 3) was created based on Hine's (1989) theory of the COD. Coding followed a hybrid deductive-inductive approach (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006): initial codes were derived from the COD, and additional codes were developed inductively from the data. Coders first identified common segments and key words (e.g., "angry Black woman") in a subset of interviews (n = 4), and the codebook went through three revisions before it was applied to the remaining interviews. This process produced three superordinate codes: (1) "Influences on Emotional Engagement," (2) "Perceived Reasons for Emotional Engagement," and (3) "Emotional Expression." Some subcodes followed the coding guide (e.g., "Gendered and Racialized Negative Experiences," "Social Surveillance," "Emotion Regulation"), and others emerged from patterns in the interviews (e.g., "Refusing to Engage," "Desire to Teach Next Generation"). Both coders agreed on all codes. The full coding scheme (Supplemental Appendix S1), main code frequencies (Supplemental Figure S1), and intercode associations computed in MAXQDA (Supplemental Figure S2) are provided in the supplemental materials. After interrater reliability was checked, the first author conducted the thematic analysis in MAXQDA, and the second coder reviewed and approved it. Coded segments were summarized for each code according to how they addressed the main concepts in the concept map. Patterns were then identified across code summaries and developed into themes addressing the research questions.

Code Reliability

The first author and a second coder first coded four interviews together to develop and refine the codebook. Of the remaining interviews, the first author coded 31 interviews and the second coder reviewed 30, with 16 interviews coded by both, so that intercoder reliability was established for approximately one third of the interviews. The overlapping interviews were coded independently, and reliability was then assessed jointly. Disagreements were discussed and resolved before final codes were recorded. This approach is consistent with established recommendations for qualitative research (Belotto, 2018; Hemmler et al., 2022; O'Connor & Joffe, 2020).

κ and F1 scores are reported for each code and subcode (Table 2). Kappa calculations followed interrater reliability standards (Gisev et al., 2013; McHugh, 2012). Values of .61-.80 were considered substantial and .81-1.00 almost perfect (Gisev et al., 2013). We therefore aimed for κ ≥ .61 for each code and a mean κ > .81.

Mixed-Methods Integration

COD Endorsement Score

To quantify each participant's endorsement of dissemblance, we summed the frequencies (presence) of codes reflecting dissemblance-related behavior. These were the subcode "Protective Strategies" and the subordinate codes "Dissemblance Response," "Discernment," "Language and Silence," "Refusing to Engage" and "Internal Conflict and Strain." The sum was merged with the quantitative dataset in RStudio. Two outlying scores were winsorized at the 5th and 95th percentiles (Wilcox, 2012).

Longer interviews offer more opportunities for codes to be applied, so raw frequencies could partly reflect participant verbosity rather than dissemblance. We therefore regressed the COD score on the number of words each participant spoke. The standardized residuals served as the adjusted COD score in all subsequent analyses.

Integration of Findings

Integration occurred at two points. First, during analysis, qualitative data were quantitized into the COD endorsement score, linking interview content directly to questionnaire measures (Sandelowski et al., 2009). Second, during interpretation, qualitative themes and quantitative results were compared to assess whether findings converged, diverged or expanded on one another (Fetters et al., 2013; Guetterman et al., 2015). For example, themes describing heightened emotional monitoring in public and workplace settings were examined alongside the association between COD and public regard. Themes From Interviews

The qualitative analysis addressed how Black women described their emotional experience and expression across public and private spaces, the influence of gendered and racialized identity on expression, and where and how they learned to engage with their emotions.

Four interrelated themes were identified, which map onto the dimensions of the COD outlined in Hine's (1989) framework and our coding guide (Figure 3). The first two themes describe the conditions that give rise to dissemblance: (1) Influence of Stressors on Emotional Expression, which captures how workplace, familial, and gendered experiences shape what emotions feel safe to express; and (2) Influence of Social Perception on Emotional Expression, capturing participants' awareness that their emotions are frequently misread through stereotypes such as the "angry Black woman." The third theme, (3) Emotional Regulation Strategies, describes the dissemblance response utilizing behaviors like self-monitoring, code-switching, and strategic disengagement participants use to navigate this reality. The fourth theme, (4) Safe Spaces for Emotional Expression, describes the relationships and settings in which participants felt free to express emotions authentically. Together, these themes support the presence of the COD in participants' lives, consistent with our hypothesis that emotional stereotypes and public dismissal would emerge as motivators of dissemblance and that safe spaces would emerge as a site of emotional freedom. They also indicate that the COD is shifting across generations.

Before addressing the themes, it was important to understand how participants understood emotions and feelings and how they function in their lives. Participants generally described emotions and feelings as the same or overlapping, and noted that naming emotions aids self-awareness and gives them direction

Influence of Stressors on Emotional Expression

The first theme captures the gendered and racialized stressors that participants identified as shaping their emotional expression, corresponding to the first dimension of the COD.

Participants described three primary contexts in which these stressors operated (the workplace, the family, and gendered expectations), each of which taught them about emotional safety and risk.

Workplace Influences. The workplace was the main site for anecdotes about the influence of public perception on emotion. The racial and gender composition and power dynamics of the work environment shaped how emotions were perceived. In predominately Black environments it was considered safer for emotional expression, especially for anger, compared to predominantly White spaces. Participants also recognized that certain emotions (i.e., anger, crying/sadness) are inappropriate for more professional settings (i.e., work or church). Furthermore, while emotions like happiness and joy tended to be expressed and accepted in a variety of public spaces, participants expressed that there was still a need to be mindful in terms of how positive emotions are expressed (i.e., happy dancing is not appropriate in public, "don't act too impressed" when good things happen). "Yes, I still feel like I have to put on a different like a mask, I guess would be the easiest way to describe it when I'm out at work in particular, I don't work with a lot of Black people where I work at, and so I feel like I have to act a certain way and suppress certain things and not say certain things around them... I don't have to do that when I'm around other Black people in my workplace. So, changing the way that I act, changing the way that I speak, so code switching to be specific." (Participant 4, Audio Transcript) "I do think there is a little bit of thought in the workplace to not come across as the whole angry Black woman" (Participant 19, Audio Transcript) Familial Influences. Participants also reported a considerable influence of family on emotional expression, noting that their upbringing and related life experiences influence how they engage with their emotions presently. Most participants reported that emotions were not discussed growing up, and many described emotional control as necessary for survival. Positive emotions were expressed more freely than sadness or depression, which surfaced only when the context allowed like at a funeral. Interestingly, participants stated that even though emotions were not expressed openly, they did observe a pattern where emotions would still come out unintentionally (i.e., angry outbursts, nervous and emotional breakdowns). Participants who grew up with more open discussion of emotions described greater comfort expressing emotions across contexts, and several noted that mental health stigma within the Black community shaped emotional conversation.

"As a child growing up, myself and all of my friends, we were always taught to hide the emotion that didn't serve your purpose in the moment. And I've discovered in my adult life, when I've hid whatever was distressing me, it blossoms into something I really didn't even want to be a part of… And because I'm a product of children should be seen and not heard, that wandered into my adult life… It took my voice. It took a while for me to realize that I still had a voice... It took me a while to realize that I needed that voice as a Black mother. Because when you can't speak up for yourself, you can't speak up for your children. And you need to be to speak up for them. Raising children, made me learn." (Participant 28, Audio Transcript)

Gender Influences. Participants described that societal expectations of women assume them to be more emotional and expressive, alongside increased shame around the body, sexuality related to gender stereotypes and violence. Women noted that their biological sex influences their emotional engagement due to changing hormone levels and a few women mentioned that the perimenopausal transition made them more sensitive/reactive. A subset of women described intergenerational and personal experiences of domestic violence and how that influenced their dating choices and emotional triggers. Women emphasized a need to learn from these experiences (i.e., what to be aware of and how to respond) and applying these lessons going forward.

Many women, especially first-born daughters, described bearing responsibility for family wellbeing; because their family trusted them to "keep it together," emotional check-ins were rare. A similar dynamic extended to work and group settings, where Black women were expected to advocate against the mistreatment of others and "take on the brunt of work for racial harmony" (Participant 31, Audio Transcript). These experiences taught participants to "protect their peace," to choose which situations merit their time and effort, and to rely on emotional suppression, selfmonitoring, and careful language to navigate confrontation. "I'm the firstborn, we were raised with grandmother. They were all cousins, but I was always the oldest, and so if I've always kind of been, and even in my marriages, even now, as a single parent of 3 boys, if I am emotionally unstable, and I express that, if I show fear. If I show anger, then that to me in my mind is going to be a trickle-down effect, and I'm not going to be able to control the situation. So, I've just never had the opportunity to do that. And it just grew into my personality." (Participant 3, Audio Transcript)

Influence of Social Perception on Emotional Expression

The second theme reflects the COD's emphasis on hypervisibility and invisibility: participants' awareness that their emotions are observed, judged, and often misinterpreted by others. Overall participants reported that perception of Black women's emotions is more prone to misinterpretation and negative judgments, often citing the "angry Black woman" as a common stereotype projected onto them. Many believed that expressing emotions as a Black woman made others uncomfortable or feel threatened. These women also found that when they do express their emotions, they may be dismissed, invalidated, or they receive less sympathy. Participants raised outside of the United States (n = 5) described adjusting to U.S. society's negative perception of Black women, noting differences in how public space is policed in the U.S. and the influence this has on emotional expression. "When it comes to being in public, it's just about having a boundary with other individuals, so that they aren't able to deem my expression of certain emotions. For example, if it were sadness or anger, those things that may be viewed as negative emotions. So that those couldn't be used to categorize me in a certain way, as an example, "Angry Black woman," and be able to use it against me saying that that's a weakness that

I have." (Participant 48, Audio Transcript) "Jamaicans, we would say, like manners. It's like what's considered, how you act at home versus how you act in public like you're sharing public space with people. So even if you're having like, you just got good news. And you just wanna celebrate loudly, there's always going to be some people who are just like what is going on. You're disturbing me in a public space. So, I find that a lot in this country. As opposed to people being like, Oh, that's awesome, You're happy, great…. So public space is really public space in Jamaica." (Participant 19, Audio Transcript)

Although nearly all participants described home as a safe space, some felt their emotions carried more weight within the family, such that expressing authentic emotions could destabilize the family unit, and composure was needed to keep it functioning. Thus, there is an understanding that they must maintain their emotional composure so the family can remain functioning. "Yeah. I think, being a Black woman in particular, it's almost like we're not allowed to feel anything or everything. At the same time, we're expected to suppress our feelings and not talk about them with anybody. That is kind of how I spent my childhood, and much of my young adulthood, feeling like I couldn't talk to anyone or really express how I felt, because it would be a sign of weakness, particularly if I was angry about something, or sad, or frustrated or upset. Those were feelings that I had to hold in because we just weren't raised to do that. If you felt a way about something. You just kept it to yourself and try to work it out with yourself like you didn't talk about that with anyone, and I think that shaped who I became as a woman, not being able to express myself well, which I think aided in my anxiety. I'm clinically anxious." (Participant 4, Audio Transcript)

Emotional Regulation Strategies

The third theme describes the strategies participants used to manage emotional expression in response to the stressors and social perceptions described above. These strategies closely mirror Hine's (1989) description of a "guise of openness," in which outward composure protects inner emotional life.

Most women noted needing to appear tough, pleasant, or neutral when out in public, which they achieve via increased self-monitoring, having a conscious awareness of emotional expression and knowing when to "code switch." Although their feelings were similar across public and private spaces, their expression differed. Women noted attuning to this difference in expression was exhausting, and frequent masking of emotions could make their authentic emotional reactions surprising to others. "I'm more guarded outside the home. It's not fair, but I think most Black women would agree that if you're assertive and you stand up for yourself. oftentimes we're seen as the angry black woman or were hostile. And so I think that sometimes there's some code switching going on when we're outside our houses or in our friendships. And I think once again it goes back to that it's a necessity for us to navigate." (Participant 35, Audio Transcript) Participants also described holding a contradiction between wanting to address wrongdoing and knowing their emotions would likely be dismissed, which led some to hold back preemptively. A few women noted the importance of creating their own narrative and taking on agency in their lives via emotional engagement and management. This might look like refusing to respond to people who are rude and might project stereotypes onto the participant. For these women, age has helped them realize their peace is valued and meant to be protected. As such, they have had to learn which battles are worth the fight.

Safe Spaces for Emotional Expression

The fourth theme captures the people, places, and practices in which participants felt free to express emotions authentically. Consistent with our hypothesis, safe spaces emerged as a central mitigating factor, and "Trust and Intimacy" was the most frequently applied code across interviews. This theme also captures a generational shift in how participants engage with, and teach, emotional expression.

Participants identified family, friends, community, spirituality or religion, and therapy as spaces for emotional expression, and described sharing positive emotions readily because they are "infectious." They felt most comfortable with people who were familiar, shared their experiences, and reciprocated emotional openness, particularly other Black women, while a lack of trust or empathy led them to withhold emotions. Mental health, traumatic experiences, and disability also shaped how well others could support them.

When alone, participants expressed sadness less often than anger or happiness, preferring to "keep busy, keep going" rather than dwell in "low energy emotions." Solitary strategies included journaling, prayer and Bible reading, exercise, music, creative hobbies, and meditation.

A small number managed emotions through isolation or suppression, but home was generally described as a space for authentic expression. We also observed a trend in how being a parent influenced emotional engagement.

Participants note that as parents they sought to teach their children the emotional understanding they had lacked growing up, and they credited age and experience with improving their own emotional expression. Additionally participants described younger generations as being advocates for more emotional expression and mental health awareness in the Black community.

"It seems to be a shift in especially with the younger Black generation. And us wanted to take care of our to mental health and take care of our well-being more so than maybe my generation" (Participant 32, Audio Transcript) "I think it's because we had kids ourselves, and we wanted to be better for our children.

And I think you know, even in my own family…. We all kind of started forcing those conversations because we started having children of our own, and we didn't want them to grow up and not be able to. What's the word I'm looking for? Recognize their emotions, and not only recognize them, but know how to handle them effectively." (Participant 3, Audio Transcript)

Discussion

This study examined how middle-aged Black women in a major southern U.S. city describe and engage the culture of dissemblance, and how interview endorsement of the COD relates to discrimination, trauma exposure, emotion regulation, PTSD symptoms, and beliefs about how society views Black people. Interview themes supported the presence of the COD, with participants describing stereotypes and emotional dismissal as motivators for dissemblance and emphasizing the importance of emotional safe spaces. Contrary to our hypotheses, COD endorsement was not associated with suppression, discrimination, trauma exposure, or PTSD symptoms, but greater endorsement was associated with lower perceived public regard for Black people. These findings suggest that dissemblance, as a culturally learned emotion regulation strategy, may function differently from conventionally measured strategies such as reappraisal and suppression, and may be shaped less by one's own gendered and racialized experiences than by how one believes society perceives one's racial group.

Understanding Themes in Broader Literature

Influence of Stereotypes and Negative Societal Perceptions

Themes emerging from the interviews describe a compound effect of race and gender on emotional expression, particularly when expressing anger or sadness or addressing mistreatment. Similar themes have been found in other qualitative studies centered on Black women's experiences and wellbeing highlighting the influence of the "angry Black woman" stereotype on their wellbeing (Ashley, 2014; Erving et al., 2024). Stereotypical understandings of Black women (e.g., angry Black woman, strong Black woman, mammy, etc.) serve as a lexicon of oppression that successfully dismisses and obscures Black women's true feelings, thus reducing Black women's existence down to one dimension and aiding in dehumanization (Ashley, 2014; Thomas et al., 2004). As such, stereotypes have negative effects on women's self-esteem and increase anxiety and anticipatory anxiety as it relates to being perceived as a stereotype (Ashley, 2014; Thomas et al., 2004). Specifically in professional settings, it has been found others are more likely to attribute Black women's anger to internal disposition rather than circumstance and as a result they have lower performance and leadership evaluations (Motro et al., 2022).

The current study found that those who believe Black people are viewed negatively by society also tend to endorse more COD-related behaviors in the interviews. As such, belief in more negative societal perceptions may be linked to greater use of the COD in their day-to-day lives. This aligns with themes from the interviews which indicate that dissemblance is often used in the workplace or places where negative external perceptions surrounding Black women are more likely to be prevalent. This pattern is supported by Chaney et al.'s study (2024) that found that Black Americans who more strongly believe prejudice comes from a shared or societal ignorance were more likely to use more expressive suppression following racial discrimination.

Code-Switching and Regulatory Strategies

Themes from interviews detail the difference between emotional expression in both public and private space with many women emphasizing the need to code switch. Code switching is thought to be a strategy that manages others' perception by altering various aspects of one's appearance, behavior, language, or expressions and comes into its most effective and common usage in professional contexts (McCluney et al., 2019). Experimental evidence shows that Black employees who codeswitch are evaluated as more professional by Black and White observers (McCluney et al., 2021), and thus codeswitching becomes incentivized in these settings. Concordantly, the workplace was where our participants reported that they have the greatest risk of having their emotions dismissed or being stereotyped as well as having the highest consequences (i.e., getting fired or reprimanded). While code switching allows individuals to navigate professional spaces with ease, the act of code switching itself can be understood to be exhausting by both our participants and broader existing literature (Johnson et al., 2022). The efforts Black women take to avoid misinterpretation or negative assessments from others can result in emotional exhaustion and burnout (Johnson et al., 2022). Going further, literature shows that Black women cope with workplace stress through self-care, support systems with other Black women, and spiritual and religious practices (Hall et al., 2012).. All of these coping strategies are effective in mitigating racial stress (Hall et al., 2012). Similar sentiments were also endorsed by our participants, with many participants noting the importance of sharing emotions with others who have similar experiences like other Black woman. Women also reported praying, talking to God, and reading the Bible as significant emotion regulation strategies. Thus, as Black women already adopt strategies to manage the negative effects of racial and gendered stress, there is a need to learn from and incorporate these understandings within treatment approaches for mood and stress disorders in Black women.

The absence of an association between COD and suppression or reappraisal may reflect that the method for quantitizing interview endorsement of dissemblance behaviors does not capture COD with enough sensitivity to detect a correlation with the psychological variables assessed. Alternatively, these findings may point to the COD as a culturally specific emotion regulation strategy that may operate outside the frameworks of antecedent and response-focused strategies.

Influence of Relational Identities, Age, and Individual Experience

Another theme from the interviews emerged around the pressures of having a relational identity as a mother, wife, daughter, caregiver, etc. and the ensuing demand on emotional expression. This theme seems to align with the "strong Black woman" stereotype which speaks to the intersectional ways Black women's labor is used within society paired with expectations of selflessness and self-sacrificial care to others while appearing strong and unaffected (Erving et al., 2024). Erving et al. (2024) assessed how this stereotype relates to mental health in a sample of Black women 50 years and older and found that suppression of anger was associated with greater risk for depressive symptoms. Additionally, emotional suppression and resistance to vulnerability are associated with depressive symptoms via social isolation (Nelson et al., 2024), and endorsement of the "strong Black woman" schema is related to depressive symptoms via disengagement related coping strategies (Jones et al., 2021).

Interestingly, women in our study are in a unique age group, such that they sit between generations that are more emotionally silent (Baby Boomers and the Silent Generation) and another that is more emotionally expressive (Millennials and Gen Z). Based upon themes surrounding the desire to teach the next generation about emotions and emotional expression, these women have played an important role in this shift and, if not directly, they see the importance and benefit of it. This shift is supported in recent work with focus groups of Black women in which they have described that younger generations have reduced mental health stigma and have an increasing openness to therapy (McCall et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings suggest the COD is changing across generations.

The COD also emphasizes the role of domestic and interpersonal violence as significant drivers of this emotional concealment. We observe this trend in the interviews as well, such that several women who endorsed extensive dissemblance behaviors also spoke more openly about the influence domestic violence had within their upbringing and adult lives. Thus, how the COD is engaged with and coping strategies for dealing with related stressors vary based on individual experiences. Surprisingly, the current findings indicate that usage of the COD was not significantly associated with one's lifetime traumatic experiences or current PTSD symptoms.

Although disability studies scholars have theorized a connection between the COD and mental health disparities for mood and stress disorders like PTSD (Schalk, 2020), the current study did not observe this relationship. Consistent with participants' descriptions of more open emotional expression with age, it may be that more maladaptive correlates of the COD, such as PTSD symptoms, are less evident in midlife. As such, it would be of value to explore how younger cohorts of Black women experience the COD and how this relates to trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms.

Taken together, findings imply, that acceptance of Black women's emotional expression is needed across all spaces, but it is extremely important for therapeutic settings which seek to address disparities in mental health.

Implications for Research and Practice

Future Research

First, larger and longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether beliefs about negative societal perceptions precede dissemblance, result from it, or reinforce it over time.

Second, developing a validated quantitative measure of the COD, informed by the themes identified here, would allow the construct to be examined in larger samples alongside established measures. Third, given that Black women do not experience the effects of race and gendered stressors separately (Woods-Giscombé & Lobel, 2008), future research should explore how negative discriminatory experiences at the intersection of race and gender, especially domestic or interpersonal violence, may be related to dissemblance usage. Fourth, given participants' descriptions of generational change, research with younger cohorts of Black women is needed to understand how the COD is evolving across the life course. Finally, although the COD is rooted in the experiences of Black American women, understanding how social perception structured by systems of oppression shapes emotional processing may inform broader psychological understandings of what constitutes effective emotion regulation among other groups navigating marginalization.

Clinical and Community Implications

For clinicians, these findings suggest that emotional masking among Black women may reflect a culturally learned, protective response to anticipated misperception rather than avoidance or resistance to treatment. Assessing where and why clients conceal emotion, and validating dissemblance as an adaptive response to social conditions, may strengthen Black women's willingness to engage in therapy and support authentic emotional expression, particularly of anger. Consistent with participants' emphasis on shared experience, Black women report a preference for therapists who share their identity and for culturally competent care (McCall et al., 2025). Additionally, community-based programs that create culturally grounded safe spaces are promising. For example, participation in a peer-led sister circle intervention reduced depressive symptoms among African American women aged 40-75 (Zare et al., 2023).

Finally, because workplaces were the primary site of dissemblance in this study, organizations should recognize the costs of professional norms that reward codeswitching (McCluney et al., 2021) and work to create environments where Black women's emotional expression is not penalized.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings highlight the influence of sociopolitical positioning on Black women's emotional experience. Negative understandings of how society perceives Black people are meaningfully associated with how emotions are expressed and processed. Our findings support that dissemblance-related emotional behaviors are present within the lives of Black American women and are significantly tied to broader societal narratives about Blackness.

These findings extend research on racial identity by linking public regard, a core dimension of the Multidimensional Model of Racial Identity (Sellers et al., 1997), to how Black women regulate emotional expression. These results highlight the importance of centering Black women's expertise about their own emotional realities in clinical contexts. Black women possess lived knowledge about how their sociopolitical positioning shapes their inner lives, and that knowledge should be treated as clinically relevant. This research demonstrates that a Black feminist lens allows for research to understand Black women's emotional masking as an adaptive response to anticipated misunderstandings and not as a maladaptive. Mental health providers treating Black women for mood and stress disorders should be equipped to recognize culturally learned emotional masking, understand the racial and social forces that reinforce it, and adapt their approaches accordingly.
