Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Full text

Abstract

A developmentally-informed early years system is one that is underpinned by a clear understanding of children. In this chapter we argue that this involves integrating scientific insights into child development with expertise in how public policy, services and practices can influence this development. We present a framework of six core principles capturing essential knowledge about child development, which we propose should inform decisions about early childhood education and associated policies and services. We explore these principles in the context of the Early Years landscape in England. We consider challenges for this framework in the current system and explore what might be done to make the whole system -from front line practice to national policy -more developmentally-informed.

Introduction

There are widespread reports that, post-COVID19 pandemic, increasing numbers of young children are arriving at school unprepared and lacking in basic skills needed to benefit from formal schooling. In this chapter we argue that this situation underscores the necessity to position education in the earliest years of a child's life within a framework informed by the science of child development and delivered by practitioners who understand this science.

This chapter has two parts: First we explore what a developmentally-informed system might look like, describing universal principles which might be adopted and adapted in many places. Second, we focus in on the English context and consider challenges in the current system and what might be done to make the whole system -from front line practice to national policy -more developmentally-informed.

The challenge -the long shadows of the COVID-19 Pandemic

In recent years, there has been increasing concern about the number of young children in the UK who are not ready for school, that is to say, they have not yet developed the basic skills they need to participate in learning in the reception year and beyond [Kindred Squared 2025]. Increasing levels of developmental delay and additional needs in four-and five-year-olds have been attributed to the lasting impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on babies, young children, their families and the services that work with them [Ofsted 2022]. Children starting school in 2024 and 2025 were the babies of lockdown, many of whom spent a vital period of their early development with limited social interactions, and in houses with increased stress and isolation. Many services, such as health visiting, family support, and informal play groups, stopped or went online in lockdown and many have not fully returned to full face-to-face operation [Hogg and Mayes 2022]. The babies of the pandemic and their families are therefore doubly disadvantaged, facing the adversity of lockdown and without the support available to cohorts that preceded them [Ofsted 2022].

The growth in developmental delays in the under-fives has also been attributed to other factors, such as cuts in public services, the cost-of-living crisis, increasing child poverty and the ubiquity of screens in family lives which disrupt healthy interactions and experiences [Nevin and Jacques 2024].

Clearly, wide-ranging and asserted action is needed to ensure all our children are developing well in early childhood, with the support they need to thrive. In this chapter, we argue that, to deliver better experiences and outcomes for the babies of the lockdown era -and indeed all children -our education system must be developmentally-informed, which is not always the case at present.

A developmentally-informed system

At its most basic, a developmentally-informed system is one that is underpinned by a clear understanding of children, how they develop, and how public policy and services can influence this development. It is one where decision-makers at all levels, from policy to practice, have a clear understanding of the biological, psychological and social processes that influence child development, and where an understanding of child development (amongst other considerations) informs their decisions. A developmentally-informed system is a child-centred system, where children are well understood and their needs guide the work of the professionals and services around them.

Human developmental science is the study of the processes and mechanisms of stability and change over time [Petersen 2024] and in this chapter we narrow the scope to the early childhood period of 0-5 years. Later in this chapter we outline six core principles of child development which a developmentally-informed system should acknowledge and act upon.

In advocating for the science of child development to inform early education, we are not aligning with any particular theoretical account. Indeed, we take the view that different theories of human development can bring different perspectives that help elucidate aspects of a child's life in important ways [Newman and Newman 2022].

A focus on the early years

Understanding human development is particularly pertinent in early childhood (the period from birth until age 5). We believe that being developmentally-informed is an important component of quality in services during this period.

It is widely recognised that the earliest years of life are a vital period in development, which lay the foundations for lifelong health and wellbeing. During this period of rapid development, babies and young children's brains are shaped by their experiences, environments and interactions with the people around them [National Scientific Council on the Developing Child 2004]. Brain plasticity is greatest in the earliest years of life, so this is a prime time to support children's learning and development. It is easier to shape neural connections and circuits as they are forming, rather than to modify them once they have matured [Hambrick et al. 2019; National Scientific Council on the Developing Child 2007].

Experiences in early childhood play a particularly important role in shaping children's lives and life chances: positive early experiences can set children on a positive developmental trajectory, better able to seize opportunities ahead of them and to thrive.

Positive experiences and positive relationships with adults in early childhood have been linked to a wide range of outcomes throughout the life course, underpinning our mental and physical health, our learning and earning, and our ability to form positive relationships and contribute to society [National Scientific Council on the Developing Child 2004].

In contrast, because development in early childhood is the foundation for later development, disruptions in early development can have a disproportionate impact on children's wellbeing and development, leading to a child being more susceptible to poor outcomes throughout their lives [Hambrick et al. 2019]. Early experiences do not determine our later outcomes, but their ripples can be felt throughout our lives, for better or worse.

Alongside being an important stage in development, early childhood is also qualitatively different from other life stages (and indeed in itself consists of several different stages). Babies and young children are not just smaller versions of older children. Their needs and abilities are fundamentally different, and this must inform how we develop services and policies to support them. This is particularly true of the youngest babies, who are non-verbal and totally dependent on adults for their care [Winnicott 2018].

Current policy changes also make it pertinent to consider the earliest years of education. Thanks to policy and funding changes in recent decades, and because more children now grow up in families where both parents are in paid employment, more children are in formal early education and childcare. In England, more than 90% of children are in formal early education and childcare before they start school [Department for Education 2025]. As time goes by there is a clear trend: more children are accessing early education for more of their young lives.

Children are also starting early education and childcare at an earlier age. In 2025, the age at which families are entitled to Government subsidised early education and childcare in England is lowering to nine-months old. Babies are more reliant on the adults around them compared to older children, and their brains are more malleable and influenced by experience . Therefore, the quality of early education and childcare matters even more at this age [Sylva et al. 2011].

Core principles of being developmentally-informed

We have developed a framework of six core principles (see list below), which we believe capture essential knowledge about child development that should inform decisions about early childhood education and associated policies and services. These principles are inter-related and overlapping. They are based on our own expert judgement, reading of the academic and grey literature, and many years working in the system. This framework has not been described in this way before and would benefit from being tested and refined in practice and research.

(1) First things First. In the first years of life, children acquire skills and abilities that form the foundations of later learning and development.

(2) Always Changing. Development is dynamic, complex and not deterministic.

(3) Relationships Matter. Relationships are perhaps the most influential factor in early development. These six principles provide a framework for understanding early childhood development. Such a framework does not negate the need for specific knowledge informed by, say, the latest insights from cognitive science or domain specific knowledge such as typical language development or executive functioning trajectories. Rather, the principles are intended as a broader context that will support practitioners and others to understand how such specific knowledge 'fits in' to the broader learning and development journey of each child. We now explain each principle in more depth.

1. First things First -The importance of foundational learning.

Children develop in a particular way -they develop core foundational skills early in childhood, which then support the development of more specific and sophisticated knowledge and skills. For example, a child must develop the ability to focus their attention, and must have some basic language skills, before they can learn to read. Skills such as the ability to focus thoughts, to communicate, and to regulate oneself are vital foundations on which other learning and development is built.

Practitioners and policy makers may focus on particular aspects of early development, such as 'language development', 'motor skills' and 'mathematics'. But knowledge of child development shows that these domains are not as distinct as sometimes imagined.

They have common developmental pathways and are mutually influential -for example language development is influenced by and influences emotional regulation, motor development, mathematical competence and so on [Adolph and Hoch 2019; Kuzik et al. 2020; Turan and De Smedt 2023].

Because of the foundational nature of early skills, perturbations to the complex web of inputs to the developmental system in the early years can have outsized impacts compared to similar disruptions experienced by those in different developmental periods [Dunn et al. 2017; Hambrick et al. 2019]. This may be why children who were babies and young children during the pandemic experience more pervasive and lasting effects than those who were older.

One implication of this principle is that, to ensure good outcomes in childhood and later, it is important to support the development of foundational skills in early childhood. Developmentally-informed practice recognises the importance of foundational learning and development, and how it underpins the acquisition of academic knowledge and technical skills. This leads to a confidence, for example, in supporting children to learn through play rather than rushing to learn explicit phonics, literacy or numeracy too young-knowing that the skills and development acquired through positive play experiences will provide strong foundations on which more academic learning will build, when the time is right. For example, pretend play can support children's social and linguistic skills, which are important for later literacy [Gibson et al. 2020 [Gibson et al. , 2021]].

Another implication is that children who have experienced adversity or lacked the opportunities to develop foundational skills in early childhood will need support to develop these skills first before they can meaningfully acquire other skills. Importantly, a developmentally-informed perspective here does not align with a simple 'catch up' model, even if we were to 'hot house' reception aged children to teach them skills valued in the Early Years Foundation Stage (EYFS) (e.g. holding a pencil correctly or recognising phonics) we would not fully compensate for the missed early life experiences that would typically underpin the emergence of such skills.

2. Always Changing -Development is dynamic, complex and not deterministic.

Child development is a highly complex, nonlinear, process of change. Development happens as the result of a vast array of interacting biological, psychological, environmental, relational and societal factors. At any given point in time, a child's current developmental state (or profile) will be a consequence of these interactions and of their preceding developmental state and trajectory [Karmiloff-Smith 2009].

To paraphrase Heckman, the science of child development shows us that the 'nature vs nurture' debate is no longer relevant [Heckman 2008]. A child's nature and their experiences shape each other. For example, a child whose temperament leads them to be more irritable, and to react more quickly and intensely to negative experiences will influence how the adults around them act towards them. This child will also experience and respond to the same adult behaviours differently to a child with lower reactivity and a calmer temperament. Every individual child both shapes and experiences their environment differently [Bates et al. 2012].

Our understanding of child development has been transformed by research showing that when experiencing the same types of adversity, some children show more susceptibility than others to adverse outcomes [Moffitt et al. 2006; Zhang et al. 2023]. Factors protecting against adverse outcomes include biological factors, positive relationships, high-quality early education, economic stability, parental harmony, diverse social relationships, community cohesion and resources, belonging within a religious or cultural community, participation in arts and cultural activities like sports or dance, and so on [Schoon et al. 2004; Vanderbilt-Adriance and Shaw 2008].

Because of the complexity of early childhood development. Outcomes in early childhood are therefore not deterministic but probabilistic. Any particular input or combination of inputs rarely determines a particular outcome but simply increases or decreases the risk of it occurring. For instance, nurturing care and good early relationships make it more likely a child will thrive, while adversity may increase the risk of difficulties. There is always the opportunity to influence a child's developmental trajectory through increasing protective factors and addressing risks.

Whilst the existence of one particular risk factor does not determine poor outcomes, the accumulation of risk factors increases the probability of poor outcomes substantially, as does the depth and persistence of adversity. A child experiencing a temporary period of poverty, for example, may only have a small risk of poor outcomes, whereas a child experiencing deep and persistent poverty, alongside factors such as parental stress, poor housing, and exposure to conflict and abuse, will be at heightened risk of a range of poor outcomes [Felitti et al. 1998; Rutter 1979].

The implication of this dynamic complexity is that educators must be able to recognise and respond to the different needs and experiences of different children, and systems must support this. In the example above, we described how pretend play can support the development of a range of skills. However, the implication of this second principle is that the developmental 'gain' associated with such a play episode would depend on many factors about the child and their circumstances, such as whether the child had previously experience of socialising with other children, whether they are tired, hungry or feeling unsafe, whether they have a caregiver who is able to support and scaffold their play experiences, and so on. This shows us that early childhood education can never be a one-size fits all endeavour. Practitioners must be skilled in fine-tuning education to each child's individual characteristics, experiences and context. They must also be curious about each child understanding that the same profile may emerge in children for different reasons [Cicchetti and Rogosch 1996]. For example, language delay may be due to physiological or neurological issues, wider developmental delay, or early neglect and adversity. To support a child's development, a practitioner consider the factors that have influenced their development and therefore the most appropriate response to any challenges [Shaw et al. 2024].

At a systems level, early education systems must enable practitioners to gain a deep understanding of an individual child (for example, by enabling continuity of care, supporting home-school relationships, and facilitating information sharing between services). In recognition of the cumulative impact of risk, additional support and targeted provision should respond to patterns of risk factors in children and families rather than a binary response to individual risks.

3. Relationships Matter -Relationships are the essence of survival and flourishing.

Developmental science has also shown us the central importance of relationships to young children's development [Sroufe 2000]. From an evolutionary perspective the relational aspect of the extended period of immaturity experienced by humans has been proposed as essential for the development of advanced environmental adaptability [Sander 1975]. Bonds between parent and child are essential for immediate and long-term survival and flourishing.

Beyond immediate survival, nurturing adult-child relationships support a wide range of developmental pathways. For example, an adult's attunement to a child matters, for their learning of self-and co-regulation, and of the motivation and intentions of others [Stern 1985]. Adults' attunement, and the extent to which they follow and respond to child's cues and attention has been linked to a range of later outcomes, including language learning and vocabulary [Tamis-LeMonda et al. 2019; Tomasello and Farrar 1986].

Relatedly, research grounded in attachment theory has shown how early relationships underpin the development of emotional and social functioning, as well as the development of cognitive skills such as working memory, and inhibitory and attentional control [Bernier et al. 2010; Ding et al. 2014].

The impact of adult-child relationships is not just about parents. Research in the US has found that extended family can be important for supporting child development [Jæger 2012], with effects varying across different ethnic groups [Mollborn et al. 2011]. Beyond family relationships, strong educator-child relationship quality is linked to positive developmental gains at preschool, showing that the underlying relational principle is important across caregiver roles and types [Pianta and Stuhlman 2004; Verschueren and Koomen 2012]. Furthermore, opportunities for relational engagement with other children, whether siblings, extended family or peers, support young children's learning. Research into peer effects is relatively underdeveloped in comparison to adult-child relationships but peers have been shown to influence language development [Mashburn et al. 2009], social-emotional development [Zhao and Gibson 2023], academic attainment [Hanushek et al. 2003; Neidell and Waldfogel 2010] and school readiness [Delay et al. 2015].

The implications of this third principle include the importance of nurturing responsive relationships in early education settings, both between early educators and children, and between children and their peers. In addition, early educators must be alert to a child's early relational experiences and how these may have shaped their development: children arriving in early education having experienced relational adversity are likely to need additional support.

4. Shaped by Surroundings -Contexts, culture and circumstances shape development.

A developmentally-informed education system must recognise that the biggest influences on a child's educational outcomes lie outside the education system -not only in children's early relationships, but also in their wider environments and experiences. In recent years in the UK, there has been a drive for more trauma-informed practice, with practitioners across the workforce trained to understand more about the impacts of trauma and adverse child experiences, on development, wellbeing and behaviours. This increased understanding is welcome, but trauma is only one part of the complex array of factors that shape us in early life, and indeed the impacts of trauma on humans can vary [Hambrick et al. 2019]. A developmentally-informed system moves us beyond being simply trauma-informed, to develop understanding and responses to all of the factors which may have shaped development.

Children's life circumstances and socio-cultural contexts are highly impactful upon their learning and growth [Shonkoff and Phillips 2000]. Research shows, for example, that both parent-child relationships and the activities and resources allocated to children's learning by their families can have a lasting educational impact [Parker et al. 1999; Rose et al. 2018; Tamis-LeMonda et al. 2019].

The time and resource a family can devote to their child's development is highly influenced by their economic and social resources [Conger and Donnellan 2007; Masarik and Conger 2017]. Furthermore, growing up in economically deprived neighbourhoods can also have a lasting impact upon child development, above and beyond effects explained by immediate family circumstances [Aikens and Barbarin 2008; Oliver et al. 2007; Wodtke et al. 2011]. These effects exert influence in different ways, for example via the quantity, quality and coordination of local services or the quality, safety and availability of local cultural assets like parks and museums [Aikens and Barbarin 2008].

Beyond economic and physical circumstances and contexts, cultural values, beliefs and experiences also impact upon the developing child. For example, motor milestones such as age of sitting and walking or the presence of a crawling stage vary according to cultural child rearing practices such as supine sleeping or infant massage [Karasik et al. 2010].

The implications of this fourth principle are that educators must be alert to a child's family, culture, community and economic position and the impact these might have on their needs and abilities. A system that aims to support children to reach their full potential, cannot focus only on what happens in the education institutions but must look to influence the broader societal systems which support our children to learn and grow. Outcomes-and mission-focused approaches, which integrate the design and delivery of education into a wider policy agenda could leverage change at this systemic level.

5. Desirable Differences -Developmental processes are characterised by individual variability.

As well as understanding contexts and processes of development, it is also essential that we understand children at an individual level. While 'staged' models of child development have an intuitive appeal, and checklists for developmental milestones are popular, decades of developmental science have shown that child development is characterised by high levels of variability, even amongst children of similar ages and backgrounds. There is also substantial within child variability. Children's developmental competences can vary by situation and across different aspects of development. For example, a child may demonstrate confidence, independence and strong language development at home, but may be reserved, quiet and unforthcoming in a nursery setting. Note that we are not claiming there are no developmental generalisations to be made, but rather that recognising and understanding individual variability is key to an understanding of children and how to support their learning [Pérez-Edgar et al. 2020].

We further argue, in line with a neurodiversity-based perspective, that developmental variability is a desirable characteristic of human populations. Understanding this is especially important for young children displaying initial signs of neurodivergence where environmental and cognitive factors mean that careful appreciation of and adjustment to their needs and profile by caregivers and educators is essential to wellbeing, learning and participation [Gibson and McNally 2024].

6. Being and becoming -The importance of children's rights, perspectives and agency.

The final tenet of developmentally-informed early childhood education is that children play an active role in their own learning and development. Social constructivist approaches [Vygotsky 1978] propose that children bring their own experiences, interests, and prior knowledge to education, which shape how they engage with and construct new understanding. Given the huge number of external influences and maturational processes at play in early childhood development, this factor is sometimes overlooked but it has important implications for how we conceptualise and enact early learning.

One intriguing finding to support this view is that children have been shown to learn words more readily when they belong to categories that they are interested in [Ackermann et al. 2020] -for example, a child preferring animals to vehicles might learn the words lion, elephant and capybara more quickly than ambulance, tank or hovercraft.

Linked to this, it is also important that education systems recognise the rights of babies and young children, and the importance of their views and interests being taken into account. Education systems can sometimes be so focussed on end goals -on the production of competent, skilled, engaged young adults -that the subjective experiences, perspectives and rights of children at every stage of the system are overlooked. This is particularly true in early childhood, where a focus on improving outcomes often takes priority over a focus on the in-the-moment experiences of babies and young children, and it can be assumed that, because they are often non-verbal, young children do not have a voice that needs to be considered in decision making.

In fact, from birth babies are able to communicate their preferences and interests -if only adults take the time to tune in and notice these non-verbal cues. Babies and young children thrive when the adults around them notice and respond to their preferences and needs. In a multitude of ways, babies and young children are able to voice their own opinions about what supports their learning and development and this too can and should shape educational experiences [O'Farrelly et al. 2020].

Developmentally appropriate expectations

When putting these principles to work in the design of early childhood services and settings, it is important that decision makers take a full view of the variability within the early childhood period, ensuring the needs of different age groups and profiles are taken into account. In previous work, we have highlighted instances where policy makers, local commissioners and service managers have not taken the specific developmental needs of the youngest children into account and where babies and young children have been systematically excluded from benefiting from policies or services. These include the lockdown rules in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, where, as lockdown eased, there were rules which only enabled each of us to meet only one other person. For many months, these applied to all children too. Given that young children cannot go out without a parent, this meant they were not able to see friends or family members. Another example is that child mental health services nominally commissioned for from 0-18 years often have none of the expertise to support babies and young children and have inclusion criteria that often explicitly or implicitly excludes babies and young children [Hogg 2019].

Arguably at present, dominant voices in the early childhood sector do not promote developmentally appropriate expectations of children. A 2025 report on school readiness in England, for example, reported a 'problem' that -according to a survey of over 1000 reception teachers in 2024, 45% of children are unable to sit still [Kindred Squared 2025, p.7.]. This is, we argue, an example of a system that is not developmentally-informed: very few children of that age could be reasonably expected to sit still for any length of time.

This example is illustrative of the conflation of school readiness and a good level of development in the English early education system. Discussions about the development of our four-and five-year-olds, are often not about whether children are showing a healthy level of development, but more about whether they are demonstrating a specific set of behaviours that ensure classroom compliance.

As an aside, this might, perhaps, be why so many boys fall behind in measures of school readiness and early development, compared to girls. Sixty-one percent of boys in England in 2024 were recorded as having a good level of development at the end of reception, compared to 75% of girls [Department for Education 2024]. Maybe many boys are demonstrating the levels of health and development that would be expected at their age, but they are less likely to behave in ways which are valued in the classroom (such as sitting still, playing quietly). School readiness scores might be more equal if our teachers had developmentally appropriate expectations of boys and understood what healthy development looks like for different children at different points in their developmental journey.

Developmentally-informed practice as an element of evidence-informed practice

It is widely agreed that decision makers, both in policy and practice, should be informed by evidence, which can help them make the best choices for babies, children and families, in order to improve outcomes, make good use of resources, and avoid harm [Pampaka et al. 2016; Styles and Torgerson 2018].

There has, however, in children's policy, been a narrow understanding of what it means to be evidence-informed. In recent decades UK policy discourse -including in early childhood -has been influenced significantly by a 'what works' agenda [Foundations 2025; Pampaka et al. 2016]. From classroom practice through to national policy, decision makers are increasingly expected to draw upon high-quality, research-based evidence about which interventions make most difference when selecting actions to make a positive impact in their sphere of influence. Traditionally, this emphasis on evidence-based practice has tended to focus on interventions that can be implemented and tested within a randomised controlled trial, with clearly defined control and intervention groups, a readily manipulated independent variable and a clear outcome measure.

Applying the developmental principles outlined above also helps to show the limitations of Randomised Control Trials (RCTs) and other quasi-experimental evidence about the impact of interventions. Every child is an individual, and each one shaped in their own unique and complex way to the factors in their environment, experiences and relationships. Therefore, while useful, RCT evidence must be interpreted with care: What works in one context, or for one child, for example, therefore may not work for others. RCT evidence may tell decision makers that an intervention worked in a particular time and place, but that does not necessarily mean it will work in their community in future.

It would be difficult for any decision maker to select interventions and navigate research evidence, without also having a good knowledge of child development. Without being developmentally-informed, how do decision makers identify where variations in children's outcomes are of concern, and where they reflect expected variation? How do they understand which outcomes interventions should be influencing at which stages of development in order to change children's developmental trajectories and later outcomes? Furthermore, to deliver high-quality services, early educators need to know more than simply how to deliver specific interventions. We wouldn't think someone was a good mechanic if they could only change a tyre or refill a fuel tank -we expect mechanics to know how engines work, and how to make them work better. Similarly, an early educator must not only understand a techniques and strategies for educating children, but should also have a deep understanding of how children 'work' -that is, their development and how it can be supported. This is not to say that evidence about the impact of interventions is not useful. Rather that it should not be used to support the 'lift and shift' of interventions from one community to another, with the assumption that they will be as effective. A broader conceptualisation of evidence-informed practice [Nelson and Campbell 2017], emphasises how the impact of interventions provides a tool for decision makers, which, together with their own expertise (including an understanding of child development) and their knowledge of the child, family and community in front of them, can support them to make good decisions. In short, we must broaden our understanding of what it means to be evidence-informed, to include being informed by evidence of child development, as well as by evidence of interventions and their impact.

The early childhood education and care workforce

Given that early development is so important, and can be shaped by children's environments and experiences, early childhood presents an opportunity when well-designed interventions and high-quality services can change a child's developmental trajectory, influencing their life and life chances. A wide range of services can have contact with babies, children and families during this time, including early childhood education and care, but also maternity, health visiting and wider health, education and family support services delivered by both the public, private and voluntary sectors. Research clearly demonstrates what is intuitively obvious: It is not enough simply to offer services to babies and young children, the quality of services matters. Quality determines the extent to which any service or intervention can deliver its desired results. Good quality care can improve outcomes for children, whilst exposure to poor quality services can harm child development [Sylva et al. 2011].

In early childhood education and care, the quality of the workforce makes a significant impact on children's wellbeing, enjoyment and outcomes. A wealth of evidence demonstrates that a more highly educated, well-trained workforce is associated with higher quality provision and better outcomes for children both in the early years and later at school [Burchinal et al. 2002; Gambaro 2017]. This is particularly important for children from more disadvantaged backgrounds or minoritised communities, and those with additional needs [Pascal et al. 2020]. It is therefore vital to ensure that practitioners have a good understanding of early childhood development, have developmentally appropriate expectations of children, and can tailor their approaches for the children they work with. This is not a new argument. In her 2012 review of the early years workforce Cathy Nutbrown put understanding of child development as her first priority in her list of what we should expect of practitioners with childcare and early education qualifications [Nutbrown 2012]. Nutbrown describes 'a thorough understanding of child development and play, . . . and how best to meet their development needs and interests how to encourage their play at different stages of their development and what a practitioner can and should do to encourage and support children as they grow and learn' [Nutbrown 2012, p. 18.].

More highly qualified early educators tend to be more developmentally-informed, better able than other workers to offer developmentally appropriate activities and to interact with children in a sensitive and responsive way [Mathers et al. 2011].

Educators who are intentional and have a good knowledge of child development use this knowledge to inform their work with different children; planning specific activities to engage each child and move them forward from their current level of development [Siraj et al. 2023; Sylva and Eisenstadt 2024].

Sadly however, despite the acknowledgement of the importance of good knowledge of child development, this is often lacking. This is part of a broader challenge relating to quality in the workforce, which has been an issue not only in England, but in many countries for many years [Gambaro 2017]. Levels of training and qualification can be low. Low pay and demanding conditions make it hard to recruit and retain talented staff and to incentivise professional development [Pascal et al. 2020]. Even if practitioners are trained, large group sizes and scarce resources may make it difficult for them to implement what they have learned [Gambaro 2017].

The quality of the early education and childcare profession is varied. There are many routes into the early years workforce, and to develop once in the workforce. The nature, quality and content of early educators' training and development programmes vary hugely [Education Endowment Foundation 2025]. There are many exemplary professionals, but there are also widespread gaps in knowledge, competencies and skills. These include gaps in knowledge about child development.

Very few training and development programmes seem to consistently provide a good grounding in developmental science [Campbell-Barr et al. 2020; Pascal et al. 2020]. Analysis of the content covered in university-level early childhood programmes showed that just over half of the degrees reviewed referenced content on child development [Education Endowment Foundation 2025]. To create a developmentally-informed system, it will be vital to ensure that all early educators and allied professionals have high-quality training in child development, as described by Nutbrown above.

This is not just about the front line

As we described at the start of this chapter, in a developmentally-informed system decision-makers at all levels, from policy to practice, would be informed by an understanding of child development. This is, therefore, not just about early educators or other service providers, but also about national and local decision makers having a clear understanding of early development.

To design policies, interventions or services that will work, decision-makers need at least a basic understanding of which children are at risk of poor outcomes, the developmental pathways that lead to different outcomes, and the mechanisms through which interventions can produce change [Olds and Kitzman 1993].

The system in England is not currently configured to support this; Civil Servants in the UK Government, for example, often move around policy areas and come to post without deep expertise in the specific areas of policy they work on and the underpinning science. They are usually expected to be generalists -able to work across a broad range of policy areas and regularly move between them. Professional development in the Civil Service tends to focus on the generic skills required to perform a broad function, such as policy development, not on enhancing understanding relating to an area of public service delivery, such as child development or education [Croxford and Hogg 2024].

Improving policy makers' understanding of child development feels critical to enable national and local decision makers to make informed decisions about where to invest or how to configure and commission services. At the University of Cambridge, we ran a small programme to enable policy makers to learn more about early child development, with positive impacts on their work [Croxford and Hogg 2024]. Such programmes might be developed as a routine part of civil servant's professional development.

The solution?

So, to end, how do we create a developmentally-informed system? Our goal should be to work towards a system where practitioners and decision-makers have a clear understanding of the biological, psychological and social processes that influence child development, and where an understanding of child development informs their decisions. A developmentally-informed system is a child-centred system, where children are well understood and their needs guide the work of the professionals and services around them.

Part of the solution is about training, ensuring all early childhood education and care practitioners have a good grounding of early child development in their pre-qualification training and ongoing professional development, and ensuring others in the system -including commissioners and policy makers at a local and national level -also have opportunities to develop this knowledge.

But change is about more than professional development and training, it is about looking at the values and principles which inform and shape how systems work: what is valued and rewarded in commissioning decisions, funding and performance management systems, and accountability frameworks? What is the focus of management discussions and supervision? Creating a developmentally-informed system requires system change -creating, over a time, a child-centred system, where children are well understood and their needs consistently guide the work of the professionals and services around them.

Summary and Conclusions

The profound educational impacts of the pandemic lockdowns on babies and young children demonstrate that the foundations of learning lie in the earliest years of life. We have set out a rationale for a new approach to the early years education system that takes into account the cumulative body of evidence about the processes of learning and development at this crucial life stage.

We advocate for a developmentally-informed model, and for freeing the concept of education from schools, settings or curricula.

We set out six core principles that underly a developmentally-informed approach, together with practical examples of how these principles might inform the actions of different stakeholders in an educational system. We hope that research, policy and practice communities will be inspired to apply these principles to their work.
