Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Social science theories about how people and environments change each other are mostly stated in words. Verbal statements leave open the quantities and functional forms needed to derive outcomes. Tests of association cannot distinguish the individual rules that could have produced an association, and causal structures established separately are rarely combined. Computational cognitive science offers a remedy: it specifies theories as rules that generate individual judgment and choice, runs the rules, and checks before data collection whether rival rules can be distinguished. Cognitive agent modeling adapts this practice to theories of person-environment relations. Four causal structures recur in social science: the environment changes people, people select their environments, the two change each other over time, and environmental effects differ across people. Cognitive agent modeling specifies these structures as four components of one agent: learning, choice, feedback, and individual differences. The article compares the approach with other computational approaches and works through media effects theories as a case, giving an example specification and four propositions. In a simulation, three rival explanations of crime viewing and fear yielded positive mean correlations, and a design alternating assigned and freely chosen exposure identified the generating specification in 96% to 100% of data sets.

media effects model holds that the size of an effect differs across people (Valkenburg & Peter, 2013). Together the four theories describe four causal structures: the environment changes people (environment → person), people select their environments (person → environment), the two change each other over time (transaction), and the environment's effect differs across people (moderation). Each structure also appears in developmental, organizational, or network research, as the third section shows.

In the literatures examined below, social science theories have mostly been tested by estimating the direction and size of associations between variables, and few studies of personenvironment relations have compared the individual rules that could produce an association. An individual rule is a stated procedure that takes what a person has seen and what the person wants and returns a judgment or a choice. For example, a person estimates how common crime is from how easily instances of crime come to mind (Shrum, 1995). Rival rules are different procedures that could produce the same observed association; the fifth section compares two such rules for cultivation. Causal inference states the assumptions under which a causal effect can be identified from observed data (Hernán & Robins, 2020), but identifying an effect does not reveal which rules produced the association.

To ask which rules produced an association, researchers first need a theory that can be run. Making a theory runnable takes two steps: specification and implementation. A specification is a formal or formalizable description of the system a theory implies (see Box 1); it constrains the possible models so that a model can be re-implemented from it (Guest & Martin, 2021). One verbal statement admits several specifications, so a verbal statement alone does not determine what a theory predicts (Smaldino, 2017). Implementation turns one specification into rules a computer can execute. Researchers can then run the model, inspect its outputs, and compare rival models. This article calls specification and implementation together formalization.

Computational cognitive science has long specified and formalized the rules behind human judgment and choice. Cognitive agent modeling, as this article uses the term, adapts that practice to social science theories of person-environment relations. The next section derives three requirements for stating theories and compares computational approaches; the third shows three limits of verbal theory; the fourth defines cognitive agent modeling; the fifth presents four types of simulation; and the sixth discusses contributions and limitations.

Why Computational Cognitive Science

What Is an Agent?

An agent produces behavior in a closed loop: it senses, chooses, and acts, and the consequences of its action change what it senses next (Franklin & Graesser, 1997). The closed loop alone does not distinguish a simple copying rule from a person deliberating. Agents differ in what they pursue and how they choose. An agent's description specifies its environment, sensing capabilities, actions, motivations, and action-selection architecture, or how it picks one available action (Franklin & Graesser, 1997). Motivations and action selection answer why the agent acts as it does, so their content must come from theory. If theory does not supply them, researchers fill them in for convenience, and the outputs then reflect the researcher's choices as much as the theory. In what follows, an agent is a computational model of a person, and the person it models is called a participant.

What Should Theories Provide?

For theory to supply motivations and action selection, it must state them precisely enough for a model to run, and most social science theories do not. In social psychology, theory building has been dominated by variable-based models, which link variables without representing how agents interact (Smith & Conrey, 2007). Descriptions of psychological research most often leave out the levels of theory, specification, and implementation, as research moves from a framework directly to hypotheses and data (Guest & Martin, 2021). The formal theory movement in psychology replies that formalization exposes gaps and hidden assumptions (van Rooij & Blokpoel, 2020) and that formal models should build theory, not only test it (Robinaugh et al., 2021).

How Computational Cognitive Science Meets the Requirements

Computational cognitive science states theories as computations that generate judgment and choice, a practice long established in the modeling of decision-making, learning, and vision (Grahek et al., 2021). Its first question is what the agent is trying to achieve, which Marr (1982) placed at the level of computational theory. In Marr's sense, "computational" names a level of analysis; in computational social science it refers to computational methods and large-scale data.

A specification lets the researcher separate a theory's core claims from auxiliary assumptions (Guest & Martin, 2021). Specification meets Requirement 1 because every quantity needed to run the model must then be assigned either to the theory or to an explicit auxiliary assumption.

Requirement 2 is met by fitting rival models to repeated choices and checking recovery.

A trial is one of many occasions on which a participant makes the same kind of choice, such as one round of a laboratory task or one recorded episode in experience sampling (Valkenburg et al., 2021). A model's parameters are person-level quantities, such as how fast a person updates a belief; when a participant contributes many trials, the parameters can be estimated for that participant (Daw, 2011). A panel survey with a few waves gives each participant only a few trials. Two checks decide whether that is enough. Parameter recovery simulates data from known parameter values, fits the model to those data, and asks how closely the generating values come back; if they do not, the planned study cannot interpret the estimates. Model recovery simulates data from each of several rival models and asks whether model comparison picks the model that generated each data set; if it does not, the planned study cannot rank those models (Wilson & Collins, 2019). Both checks need only simulated data, so they can be run before data collection; their conclusions hold only for the candidates, measurement conditions, and parameter ranges examined.

Requirement 3 can be met by a generative model: a model that states step by step and with probabilities how judgments and actions are produced from what an agent perceives and wants. The term is statistical and unrelated to generative artificial intelligence. Because each step is an explicit rule, several processes can be written as steps of one model and run together (Kleiman-Weiner et al., 2017). In such a model, the theory states the goals from which every choice follows, so the combined processes operate as one person's rules. Such models have rarely been used to combine the structures of person-environment theories.

Comparing Computational Approaches

Computational approaches differ in how far they specify individual rules. A path in a structural equation model states which variable affects which, but not the rules of judgment and choice that produce it, and different rules can produce the same covariance structure. The empirical models of computational social science estimate causal effects or predict outcomes (Hofman et al., 2021), and neither task requires stating the individual rules that produce the relations between variables. This article calls such work data-centered computational social science.

Theory-driven approaches specify individual rules in agents, some more fully than others. This article calls an agent thin when its rules are given as fixed numbers or simple conditions rather than computed from goals. In threshold models of collective behavior, each agent joins once the number or proportion of others already taking part reaches its threshold (Granovetter, 1978). The threshold stands for a balance of costs and benefits, but the model takes the distribution of thresholds as given rather than computing it. Computational sociology has used interacting thin agents to explain collective patterns (Macy & Willer, 2002). Such a model shows that agents following given rules can produce a pattern. The rules remain a candidate explanation: generating a pattern does not show how the pattern formed, and other rules may generate it too (Epstein, 1999).

A thick agent computes its choices from goals and an action-selection procedure that a theory specifies: an option's value is how well its expected outcome would serve those goals. Sun's (2006) diagnosis anticipated the contrast: most social simulations assume rudimentary cognition in their agents, and simulating social phenomena should draw on cognitive science.

The explanatory target sets the thickness: thin agents can explain collective patterns, and explaining individual judgment and choice calls for thick ones. Agent-based models in communication research translate rules derived from verbal theories into program commands and can combine several theories in one model (Waldherr & Wettstein, 2019). When the agents are thin and only collective patterns are tested, however, the individual rules that generated the agents' choices remain undetermined. Which Agent Is Needed?

In the theories examined here, each of the four causal structures operates through individual judgment and choice, so specifying them requires thick agents. A single thick agent, however, cannot represent everything the four structures address. Individual choices aggregate into distributions that change what each person sees, and in simulation even error-free Bayesian agents can form echo chambers (Madsen et al., 2018). In the many-agent form of this article's approach, each agent in an agent-based model carries a computational-level cognitive model; van Rooij and Blokpoel (2020) call building such models the "cognitive-agent based modeling approach" (p. 297).

Deriving outcomes under new conditions assumes that rules hold under those conditions.

Statistical extrapolation needs the same kind of assumption, but an agent model states it explicitly, rule by rule. A rule must be distinguishable in individual data before the output of an interacting model can be attributed to it, so the design below starts from a single, non-interacting agent with person-specific parameters.

Three Limits of Verbal Theory: Media Effects as a Case

The four causal structures recur across social science. Developmental science names each of them. Rearing environments shape children (environment → person). Children elicit responses from others and select environments that suit them (person → environment; Scarr & McCartney, 1983). Development proceeds through increasingly complex, two-way exchanges between an active person and the people, objects, and symbols nearby; these exchanges shape development only when they recur regularly over long periods (transaction; Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006). Some children and adults are more affected than others by both supportive and unsupportive environments (moderation; Belsky & Pluess, 2009). Organizational research describes how people are attracted to organizations, selected by them, and leave them, and how their membership in turn shapes each organization's character (Schneider, 1987). Network research separates the first two structures as contagion and homophily. Observational data generically confound the two when the traits behind tie formation or behind the behavior studied are latent (Shalizi & Thomas, 2011).

With an established theory for each structure, media effects research is a convenient case for examining how the theories have been stated and tested, and what remains uncombined when they are stated separately. Three limits correspond to the three requirements. The theories leave open the quantities and forms needed to derive outcomes (the limit of statement). Repeatedly observed associations between environment and person cannot distinguish the individual rules that produced them (the limit of testing). The theories themselves called for combining the structures, but in the work reviewed here no specification joined them (the limit of combination).

The Limit of Statement: Quantities Theories Leave Open

Cultivation theory rests on the claim that television disseminates a shared view of the world (Gerbner & Gross, 1976). Two concepts came later (Gerbner et al., 1980): mainstreaming (the convergence of the outlooks of heavy viewers from different groups) and resonance (the amplification of cultivation when television content matches direct experience). Applying the two concepts to individuals requires fixing how exposure, direct experience, and prior judgment combine, which the concepts leave unspecified. A meta-analysis of two decades of research reanalyzed the neighborhood correlations of one study from which resonance had been inferred; sampling error could explain more than 80% of their variance, leaving resonance an open question (Morgan & Shanahan, 1997). A meta-analysis of five decades found a pattern of group differences that strongly supports mainstreaming (Hermann et al., 2021). Neither meta-analysis offered rules that compute when the two processes appear. A micro-level account holds that heavy viewers retrieve frequently televised instances readily when making estimates (Shrum, 1995); the account leaves open how much is stored and by what function retrieved instances become an estimate. Selective exposure research leaves open how goals determine what people choose.

Defense motivation directs people toward attitude-consistent information, and accuracy motivation can direct them toward useful counter-attitudinal information (Hart et al., 2009); how a particular person weighs the two motives cannot be derived from knowing that both exist.

The Limit of Testing: Seven Readings of an Association

An observed association between environment and person can be read in seven ways.

Four readings are the four causal structures. In media effects research, cultivation and selective exposure exemplify the two directions, reinforcing spirals are transactional, and differential susceptibility is moderation (Valkenburg et al., 2016).

The other three readings add no process; they mistake which structure produced the association: reverse causation, spurious association through a third variable, and collider bias from selecting the sample on a variable affected by both (Hernán & Robins, 2020).

Associations Do Not Distinguish Rules

An association alone cannot tell the seven readings apart, and even when the causal structure is fixed, different rules can generate similar data (Wilson & Collins, 2019). A small correlation between viewing and worldview (mean r = .091) has been observed repeatedly (Morgan & Shanahan, 1997), but the cumulative evidence is cross-sectional, so the direction of causation cannot be inferred (Hermann et al., 2021). Van den Bulck (2004) compared a norelation model with three causal explanations of the relation between crime drama viewing and fear of crime: cultivation, fearful choice (fear draws people to crime drama), and withdrawal (fear keeps people at home, which increases viewing). Fearful choice and withdrawal both run from person to environment and differ only in the rule. All four models fit the data. Cultivation was preferred on fit and path significance, but Van den Bulck cautioned that "one set of observations may support different, even opposing models" (p. 243). The limit is not peculiar to cultivation theory; it arises whenever theories are tested through associations alone.

Testing requires two judgments. First, where did an observed association come from?

Research design addresses this question. Second, which individual rules produced its size and form? This question remains open even when an association is shown to be causal. Epstein's (1999) criterion of generative sufficiency asks whether agent rules generate a target macrostructure. Applied to an observed association, it asks: if the rules are written down and run, do they produce an association of the observed size and form, and do rival rules produce it too?

The answer comes from analyzing simulated data with the statistics applied to the empirical data (Robinaugh et al., 2021). A yes to the first part shows that the rules suffice to generate the observed association; a yes to the second shows that sufficiency alone does not single them out.

The Limit of Combination: Overlapping Structures, Separate Specifications

The reinforcing spirals model built the feedback between media selection and effects into theory (Slater, 2007). A later statement of the model held that under identity threat, selective use of identity-consistent content increases until a satisfactory equilibrium is reached, one in which identity-relevant attitudes are adequately maintained. Selectivity can decline once the threat diminishes, and the model's time-dynamic aspects make it hard to test (Slater, 2015). The model did not fix the stopping conditions as quantities. The differential susceptibility model holds that susceptibility variables both predict media use and moderate its effects. Earlier theories had assigned this dual role only implicitly, and the model's authors knew of no empirical test of it (Valkenburg & Peter, 2013). Both models called for combining selection and effects, yet neither fixed the rules by which the combination works. When the structures are stated and tested separately, each theory may assume different rules for the same person. Deriving what happens when supply changes requires one specification that states which rules stay the same and which change.

Formalization Attempts and Their Gaps

Earlier formal models each captured part of this process. A neural network trained with a larger share of television scenes, total training held constant, produced larger crime-related outputs when familiar scenes were shown without source cues (Bradley, 2007). An agent-based model of reinforcing spirals simulated selective exposure, discussion networks, and attitude polarization over three simulated years, with and without election cycles (Song & Boomgaarden, 2017). Bradley's network contained no user choice, and Song and Boomgaarden's model contained no cultivation learning. A model of mood management theory computed option value as a weighted sum of two affective message attributes, valence and arousal, moderated by current mood, rather than as the expected change in mood, the goal the theory names (Gong et al., 2023).

Models have combined selection and influence without computing agents' choices from goals: Bayesian agents choose whom to listen to (Madsen et al., 2018), or information passes through individual, social, and technological filters (Geschke et al., 2019). Learners who observe only the outcomes of the options they choose come to avoid risky and new options even without trait-like risk preferences (Denrell & March, 2001). Elsewhere, agents with beliefs, goals, and policies have simulated social influence (Marsella et al., 2004), and several theories of attitude change have been combined as parameters of one function (Lorenz et al., 2021). An agent built on a cognitive architecture was compared with a Bayesian learning model on survey data from the 2000 U.S. presidential campaign, with parameters fitted to the same data (Kim et al., 2010).

In economics and marketing, structural models estimate forward-looking choice rules from individual decisions (Rust, 1987). Models fitted to household panel data joined these rules to Bayesian learning from experience produced by consumers' own purchases (Erdem & Keane, 1996). Among the formalizations reviewed here, however, none combined the four causal structures of media effects theories within one thick agent with person-specific parameters.

Cognitive Agent Modeling

Definition and Agent Thickness Computational cognitive science runs agents specified from theory and tests them against empirical data; cognitive agent modeling adapts that practice to social science theories of person -environment relations. The agent realizes a specification as a complete set of rules, each a single computation; a component groups the rules or parameters that implement one causal structure. The approach has three features. First, the agent chooses by planning: it computes the value of each option as how well the option is expected to achieve the goals a theory specifies, weighed against the option's cost (Baker et al., 2009). Second, each participant has their own parameter values, estimated separately or hierarchically. Third, the approach asks whether the specified agent generates the observed data and whether rival specifications, which differ from it in one or more rules or components, are distinguishable in those data. It does not restrict the number of agents; interacting agents' actions change the environment the others observe (Figure 1). The examples below have no interaction.

Modelers must choose how much detail a thick agent carries, because models can become as hard to interpret as the phenomena they model (Macy & Willer, 2002). The standard used here is to specify only the goals a theory defines and the rules that compute option values from them, and to judge that level of detail by whether parameter and model recovery succeed under the planned measurement (Wilson & Collins, 2019).

Four Causal Structures as Four Components

Consider one participant over a series of evenings. Each evening the participant sees some crime on television and some in daily life, revises an estimate of how common crime is, and picks the next program; under self-selected supply, what the participant picks changes what is offered the next evening. In this loop, the four causal structures correspond to four components (Table 2): learning (revising the estimate), choice (picking a program), feedback (the carryover from one evening to the next), and individual differences (the person-specific settings that make one participant differ from another).

The notation below is borrowed from partially observable Markov decision processes (Kaelbling et al., 1998), a standard framework for agents that cannot observe their environment directly. In this notation, each evening is a trial, and the environment is in some state, here defined by what the media offer and what the participant directly experiences. The agent holds a belief state: its current estimate of social reality and its attitudes.

 Choice. From the belief state, the agent computes the value Q of each available action, and a choice rule, called the policy π in decision theory, picks one.

 Learning. After acting, the agent receives an observation of the state through an observation function O. This function gives the probability of each observation given the state and the chosen action. The agent then updates its belief state using the state estimator SE.

 Feedback. The chosen action changes the next state of the environment through the transition function T, and beliefs carry over to the next trial.

 Individual differences. Person-specific parameters θᵢ enter these rules.

In the original framework, SE and Q are fixed by what an optimal agent would compute; cognitive agent modeling replaces them with the rules a theory states, so the agent can depart from optimality in the ways the theory says people do. Four Types of Simulation

The specified agent supports four types of simulation. Varying functional forms and parameter values addresses the limit of statement; switching components on and off addresses the limit of testing; changing the environment addresses the limit of combination. The fourth type adds a mechanism and asks whether it produces outcomes that existing specifications fail to produce. Here a mechanism is a rule or component that a revised specification adds to an existing one. This is narrower than the usual social science sense of any causal process. The first two types are run here (Supplementary Material S1 to S3): S1 and S2 run only Box 1's state estimators, and S3 runs simpler rules. The last two types are proposed. Each type yields a proposition that data can test.

Varying Functional Forms and Parameter Values

Translating a theory into a formal model requires fixing what the theory leaves unsaid, and different choices can yield different predictions from the same theory (Robinaugh et al., 2021). Cultivation theory does not specify the function by which the state estimator combines televised and directly experienced instances. Box 1 gives two candidates, which differ in their inputs (shares versus counts of instances) as well as in shape: a weighted average that gives television more weight the more a participant watches, and a sigmoid function in which estimates rise steeply once retrieved instances exceed a certain number.

An illustrative calculation (Supplementary Material S1) ran both candidates for four kinds of viewers, crossing light versus heavy viewing with direct experience of crime that differs from or resembles television content. Under the weighted average, the estimates of the two heavy-viewer groups converged (mainstreaming), but viewing raised estimates less among those whose experience resembled television (no resonance). Both results hold for any values shared by the two direct-experience groups, including κ and the televised content. For groups with direct-experience shares d₁ and d₂, the gap between their estimates is (1 -w)|d₁ -d₂|, which shrinks as television's weight w grows; the size of the viewing effect is proportional to the television-experience gap |m -d|, which is smaller when experience resembles television.

Under the sigmoid function with the S1 values, the pattern reversed: mainstreaming did not appear, and resonance did. With more directly experienced events (18 rather than 10), heavy viewing pushed those whose experience resembled television onto the flat upper part of the curve, and resonance disappeared; with heavier exposure or a lower center, the sigmoid yielded mainstreaming without resonance. Of the two candidates, only the sigmoid can produce resonance.

Supplementary Material S2 (seed 73) checks, before data collection, whether the two functions can be told apart. Simulated participants shared one function and reported a crime estimate after each assigned program; all other constants stayed at their generating values. Under these conditions, model comparison selected the generating function for nearly every data set, except with 10 trials and very noisy reports (61% for the weighted average). Classifying each participant separately, as needed if functions differ across people, required more data: with 20 trials and moderately noisy reports, the generating function was recovered for 86% of weightedaverage and 97% of sigmoid participants, and with 10 trials as few as 54%.

Proposition 1. Suppose televised and direct instances combine as a weighted average, and κ (how slowly estimates follow television), televised content, and viewing amounts are common to the experience groups. Then resonance cannot occur at any parameter values. Robust resonance in data that measure viewing and direct experience for the same participants would therefore rule out the weighted average unless κ varies with direct experience. Mainstreaming without resonance fits both specifications.

Adding Mechanisms

Model comparison can address why reinforcing spirals stop. In Box 1, the defense weight ωᵢ is fixed for each participant. Two specifications instead let it change across trials, separating conditions that Slater (2015) states together: identity threat sets the weight in a threat specification, and the degree to which attitudes have been maintained sets it in a satisfaction specification. When the weight falls, attitude-consistent choice weakens and the spiral can stop.

Both specifications allow this, so conditions under which their predictions diverge must be found and tested with repeated measurement.

The differential susceptibility model proposes a candidate mechanism: one susceptibility acts in two rules. Model comparison between nested specifications can test one strict version of this dual role, in which one latent susceptibility links the two rules. Choice susceptibility is the person-specific parameter through which a trait enters the choice rule (in Box 1, the defense weight ωᵢ), and learning susceptibility is the one through which it enters the learning rule (in Box 1, κᵢ). In a shared-susceptibility specification, one latent susceptibility per participant determines both parameters on transformed scales (for example, logit ωᵢ and log κᵢ). Each transformed parameter is a linear function of that susceptibility. A separate-susceptibility specification estimates the two parameters separately; the group-level correlation between them is estimated on the same transformed scales.

Proposition 4. Suppose the separate-susceptibility specification is fitted to all participants at once, with each participant's two susceptibilities varying around group means (a hierarchical fit). If one latent susceptibility drives both rules, the group-level correlation between the choice and learning susceptibilities on the transformed scales should be near 1 in absolute value, and the shared-susceptibility specification should fit nearly as well as the separate one. Parameter recovery checks whether this correlation can be estimated with the planned number of trials.

Discussion and Conclusion

Contributions

The prescriptions this article draws on are established: specify theories before testing them (Guest & Martin, 2021), use formal models to build theory (Robinaugh et al., 2021), and check recovery before collecting data (Wilson & Collins, 2019). This article maps the four causal structures of person-environment theories onto four components of one thick agent. The mapping makes four contrasts testable: rival combining functions of cultivation (Proposition 1); specifications of the four models of viewing and fear of crime, distinguished before data collection by a simulation-tested design (Proposition 2); one person's learning and choice rules under broadcast versus self-selected supply (Proposition 3); and nested specifications of susceptibility's dual role (Proposition 4). This article also gives a criterion for specifying a generative artificial intelligence system as part of the environment or as a second agent.

Generative Artificial Intelligence

Code generation lowers the cost of a first implementation, so more models are built.

Recovery checks therefore become more important for distinguishing models that test a theory from models that merely produce plausible results. Language models can also be the agents: in simulations with up to about 100,000 of them, group dynamics grew stronger in larger populations, and the agents herded more than humans did (Yang et al., 2024). Because black-box components make processes hard to isolate (Larooij & Törnberg, 2026), researchers cannot easily determine whether this intensification reflects a property of society or a bias of the agents.

Limitations

This article is a conceptual argument. It does not verify testing practices through systematic coding of the literature, and its judgment that formalization attempts left the three limits in place covers only the work reviewed. The example specification, the combiningfunction result, and the recovery simulations use illustrative values and were not fitted to data.

Model comparison only ranks the candidates it is given, and real data may arise from factors absent from every candidate (Wilson & Collins, 2019).

Cultivation and other effects that accumulate over years are studied here trial by trial.

This design assumes that long-term effects accumulate from trial-level updates, as the count of televised instances in Box 1 implies; Table 3 tests the trial-level rules, and the accumulation remains an assumption. The components are specified substantively only for media effects theories; the other traditions in Table 2 require their own specifications. No simulation here runs the combined agent. Proposition 3 and the stopping specifications therefore rest on the argument in the text. The examples do not show what interaction among thick agents adds; diffusion of culture and change in structure require interaction models.

Conclusion

Cognitive agent modeling adapts the practice of computational cognitive science to social science theories of person-environment relations by specifying the four structures as the learning, choice, feedback, and individual-differences components of one agent. Theories specified this way can be run, shown to fail, and revised.
