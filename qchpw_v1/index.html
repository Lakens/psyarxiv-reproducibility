How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Down Syndrome (DS) or trisomy 21 is one of the most common chromosomal aneuploidies in humans, with a global incidence of about 80,000 people per year (1). Individuals born with this congenital trisomy are at increased risk of physical and mental health difficulties throughout their lifespan, such as congenital heart abnormalities, duodenal atresia, obstructive sleep apnoea, haematological malignancies, and Alzheimer's dementia (2) (3) (4).

Despite increased health risks, randomised controlled trials (RCTs) in this population are limited (5), and many current treatments rely on consensus guidelines based on lower levels of evidence and/or clinician preference. In some cases, evidence may be extrapolated from non-DS populations, such as research involving individuals with intellectual disability (ID) more generally (5). Trials conducted with DS populations are often limited by small sample sizes or weaker levels of evidence due to study design, meaning that medications may be prescribed "off-label" (5). Such underrepresentation of people with DS in research further perpetuates health disparities, and a robust evidence base of treatment interventions is needed to address longstanding health inequities.

Poor recruitment and retention remain the leading causes of RCTs failing to obtain reliable results within time and budget allocations (6), leading to trials requiring significant additional resources, being underpowered, or ultimately not being completed (7). Large systematic reviews suggest that psychosocial determinants of reluctance to participate in trials in the general population include concerns about risks and uncertainty, lack of trust in research or researchers, and practical and logistical access issues (8) (9) (10).

Although some of the barriers that may be pertinent to trials of people with ID have been described previously (5), including complexities around informed consent, substantial logistical burden for participants and caregivers, and heightened ethical and legal considerations, barriers and facilitators of participation are context and population specific. This methodological field-wide review therefore examines the types of interventions evaluated in RCTs involving adults with DS, as well as recruitment and retention rates, and barriers and facilitators to participation. The findings will help clinicians and researchers in designing more accessible and effective RCTs for this population, and improving future trial methodology that ultimately contributes to their health and wellbeing. The objectives were: 1.

Compare planned versus actual recruitment and attrition rates; 2. Explore barriers to trial participation; 3. Identify strategies used to improve access and participation.

Examples of large-scale trials

Three of the included studies recruited >100 participants. The largest trial explored vitamin E supplementation in adults with DS aged 50+, recruiting participants over an 8 year period (18). An international consortium of 21 experienced clinical sites across 5 countries aimed to recruit 400 participants. 349 people were screened, with 337 randomised (84.25% of the target sample). The authors aimed to include as many participants aged >40 as possible. To achieve this, they did not exclude individuals based on severity of ID or for having dementia (common exclusion criteria in other studies). To address concerns about the target population's "high likelihood of limited decision-making capacity", the study engaged families and legally authorised representatives. Other strategies to minimise burden included avoiding collecting blood biomarkers where possible, and shortening cognitive tests. Family members or carers were also designated as 'study partners' and were key in supervising medication adherence. Overall, 274 individuals were retained over 36 months of follow-up (attrition 19%).

MEADOWS was another large trial (19) examining Memantine vs placebo for cognitive decline. Despite a relatively short 1-year recruitment window, and restricting inclusion to adults with DS aged >40 and/or with dementia, 173 were successfully randomised (96.11% of the 180 target). Authors described premature closure of the recruitment window (once 173 of the target 180 participants were randomised) due to a 'funding constraint' preventing them from achieving their target. Recruitment was via four learning disability centres in the UK and Norway. 254 people were assessed for eligibility, suggesting good conversion of eligible to recruited participants. The trial had broad inclusion criteria permitting participation irrespective of dementia diagnosis, which may also have aided recruitment. Participants were eligible if they were residents in a care facility or had a carer willing to help ensure compliance, also likely contributing to low attrition (16%; n=146) at primary outcome (52 weeks). Participants were also permitted to decline some study visits without withdrawing from the trial. Altogether, high recruitment and low attrition rates were achieved, and failure to reach the target sample minimally reduced power from 91% to 90%.

The third largest trial (20) explored the effect of Donepezil on Severe Impairment Battery scores (neuropsychological evaluation of cognitive function) for young adults with DS. 123 of the N=160 target sample were recruited from 24 centres experienced in working with this population across the USA. Despite this, and a 3-year recruitment period, authors described premature closure of recruitment due to 'slow individual enrolment', attributing this to feedback from those declining participation that they had reached a period of "relative stability" in their lives at ages 18-35 and did not want to disrupt this by participating. However, just 7% of participants were lost to follow-up, with 115 completing primary outcome at 12 weeks.

The largest pilot trial identified was conducted at two secondary care sites and explored antioxidant supplementation in adults with DS and dementia (21). Of 73 people screened, 59 were eligible and 58 randomised. Primary outcomes were assessed at 2 years for 31 people (47% attrition), a higher rate than expected, but reported by the authors to be in line with dementia trials in the general population (22, 23). They compared this high attrition rate to a much lower rate in a trial of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors in those with Alzheimer's without DS (24), a much larger trial (N=686) which may have achieved better retention due to recruitment of those with mild-moderate dementia, compared to those with more severe dementia here (21).

Given reasons for attrition before 2-year follow-up included refusal to participate (n=9), use of non-permitted concomitant medications (n=3), and death (n=2). Of those retained, supplement adherence was high (90.4% placebo, 91.9% antioxidant); above the 70% rate required for efficacy analyses.

Reported barriers to recruitment and retention

On the whole, included studies did not provide much detail regarding their participant recruitment methods and associated challenges. However, some trials described recruitment difficulties, which could be broadly divided into concerns related to study design, and those attributable to participant-level factors:

Study-level factors

Design factors included sampling method, recruitment site, inclusion criteria, and delays in obtaining ethical approval. For example, Cooper et al. (2016) recruited via community sources, Down Syndrome Scotland, Local Authorities, and Scottish NHS services. Researchers made significant efforts during the yearlong recruitment period to meet with managers of local support organisations, but found that this did not translate to recruitment. Instead, most participants were recruited from health professionals and day services, particularly via a national primary care research network. The authors described recruitment as highly labourintensive, particularly contacting each primary care practice in the network, whereby many declined to assist due to a perception that their service would have too few eligible participants. The trial team suggested that future RCTs should make clear that although each service may have only a few potential participants, their input is crucial in facilitating recruitment from as many sources as possible. Similarly, authors were optimistic that the adoption of remote access to general practice records could streamline this process, targeting resources to specific practices more likely to have multiple eligible participants (25).

Recruitment methods varied significantly between trials, often due in part to the varying nature of the interventions themselves (e.g., recruitment for surgical or medication trials is naturally different to recruitment for exercise interventions). Enrolling successive patients referred to an anaesthetic service over a 24-month period for surgical trial (26) was described as "challenging and time-consuming". Other methods used across included studies were recruiting from community support groups for people with DS and ID (27), residential facilities (28) (29) (30), or special education schools (15), which, while limiting sample size, often produced lower attrition rates (0-9%).

Relatedly, reported barriers to retention were often context-specific, including patients not attending inperson appointments due to concerns about catching Covid-19 (31). The same study also described abandonment of a secondary outcome cognitive test as completion took too long, highlighting the need to ensure tests are appropriate for this population (31). The TOP-COG trial described barriers inherent to trials assessing treatments for cognitive decline (25); inclusion criteria were restricted to adults with DS aged ³50 years, in an attempt to keep inclusion broad enough to facilitate recruitment, but also limiting the length of the trial period during which cognitive decline would likely be seen. The authors explained that altering the inclusion criteria to include those aged 40-45 may have meant that a longer follow-up period was required (and associated funding/resources). Similarly, longer follow-up could further reduce retention, affecting statistical power. The TESDAD trial (32) also described sub-optimal concordance with a cognitive training programme, with an average of 1.9 out of 3 sessions completed per week, but were able to retain 84 of their 87 participants (96.6%) at 52 weeks. The highly demanding nature of the intervention may explain in part why the trial design was later adapted (i.e., widening of inclusion criteria).

Delays in ethical approval also posed a recruitment barrier for at least one trial. A trial of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) for obstructive sleep apnoea (33) initially showed promise as a large recruiter, with invitations sent to over 5,000 adults with DS who had been involved in a prior related trial (34). 97 respondents were eligible. However, only 28 (28.9%) were enrolled due to 'unacceptable delays in ethical approval', which delayed the trial team's ability to contact potential participants, and resulted in funding running out, despite a short treatment window of 4 weeks.

Participant-level factors

Participant-level factors impacting recruitment included demographics (e.g., age, employment status, living arrangements, dementia diagnosis status), participant awareness about health concerns, and concerns about research and trial processes. For example, multiple studies reported difficulty recruiting younger participants. Similar to concerns about disrupting a period of relative stability (20), in a study evaluating a fitness and health education programme (14) which initially successfully recruited 89 people, almost one-third (n=28, 31.5%) withdrew consent before randomisation, with the most common reason being conflict with work schedules. This clash between work and trial participation may be further compounded by the time of day of interventions, and scheduling inflexibility, e.g., evening fitness programme sessions may be more compatible with a standard working/volunteering day.

Severe ID was also indirectly discussed by some as a potential barrier to recruitment. Concerns regarding ethicality, and the logistics of obtaining consent were common. Strategies to overcome this included taking an individualised approach to consent, such that degree of ID was not an exclusion criterion per se (35), with capacity to consent instead being evaluated on a case by case basis by clinicians. In addition, several studies obtained consent from legal guardians when participants were unable to consent for themselves (36, 37), and in some cases also demonstrated how carers and family could be involved in the research process beyond consent retrieval (38), e.g., playing a role as facilitators for participant concordance with physical activity or medication adherence. Study teams emphasised the importance of targeting both participants and their parents/carers in the recruitment process.

A further participant factor explored was awareness of the health issues and inequalities faced by people with DS. A study of statin therapy and cognition in ageing adults with DS identified a lack of awareness around the risk of Alzheimer's dementia for this population (25), negatively impacting desire to participate. Lack of understanding about trial processes was also reported as a potential barrier to recruitment. In one study of a cycling intervention, authors described difficulty recruiting due to potential participants' guardians having a clear preference for the active intervention (motor-assisted cycling vs. no cycling (problem of equipoise; (39)).

Therefore, researchers decided to adapt the control group and instead compare self-paced with motor-assisted cycling to aid recruitment, but with the potential of limiting the likelihood of finding a difference between groups.

Some studies reported reasons given by those declining to participate, including not wanting to take medication outside of the need to "treat" a condition, rather than for prevention, and also managing uncertainty about RCT processes, such as blinding and randomisation (25). Difficulty was also reported in recruiting people from underrepresented ethnic groups (31), though none of the included studies described strategies to ameliorate this.

Facilitators of recruitment and retention

Trial design

In a pilot RCT of Donepezil versus placebo for adults with DS and Alzheimer's (40), authors identified that the recruitment target would not be achieved unless significant methodological changes were made (e.g., broadening inclusion criteria and expanding recruitment sites). However, although it was known that the trial would be underpowered, it was continued (recruited: n=31, retained: n=27 by 24 weeks). Other trials described modifying their design to facilitate participation. For example, the TESDAD trial (41) explored the safety and tolerability of dietary supplementation with Epigallocetachin-3-gallate (EGCG), or placebo plus cognitive training to improve cognitive abilities. The authors described initial difficulty recruiting from outpatient settings across the Catalan Down Syndrome Association, and so the eligible age range was widened (from 18-30 to 16-34yrs; recruited n=87, retained: n=84; 96.6% to primary outcome at 52 weeks).

Strategies were also incorporated in study design to maximise retention, including using a crossover or delayed intervention (42) as these were felt to increase the likelihood that control group participants would be retained, as they knew they would receive the intervention later.

Recruiting from lists of past study participants also proved effective for some (43), resulting in higher eligibility rates (as participants had effectively been pre-screened in previous trials), and may also promote retention, as participants were highly motivated, already known to be able to tolerate similar interventions, and may have developed good relationships with study teams. In other trials however, benefits of recruiting past participants were more modest ((44): 74 people contacted; 26 assessed for eligibility; 16 randomised).

Maximising participant convenience and minimising burden

Many strategies to promote participation centred around two themes of maximising participant convenience and minimising burden. Carer-participant dyads in the TOP-COG study expressed a preference for "unobtrusive" trials which do not "impact (my) daily life" (25). Participant convenience was maximised in several studies by offering home visits or online appointments (25, 45), which in some cases also inadvertently increased the accuracy of cognitive measures, with participants able to complete these in their familiar home setting. Shields et al. (2008) also achieved 0% attrition of their 20 participants (and 92.8% concordance with the intervention) at 10 weeks via several strategies to minimise participant burden, including providing transport to the exercise intervention, and limiting the intervention period to 10 weeks (46).

Other studies also described shortening intervention duration or frequency of sessions (e.g., exercise interventions (47)), to boost intervention concordance and sustainability of behaviour change. Shorter intervention periods may also boost study retention. For example, the low attrition rate (8%, n=37) at 16 weeks seen in a trial of NMDA-antagonism for cognition in 40 young adults with DS (a demographically challenging group to recruit and retain (35)), may be at least in part attributable to the short 16-week trial duration. Other factors such as involvement of carers/family to support concordance (e.g., overseeing medication administration) may also contribute.

Considering acceptability of interventions and outcome measures

In a pilot trial exploring the effect of NMDA antagonism on cognitive function in young adults with DS, a short form version of a cognitive test was used after the first enrolled participant could not complete the outcome measure 'in a timely manner' (35). Another exercise intervention trial described considerations made to avoid potential issues with physical discomfort and injuries specific to individuals with ID, including a physical examination conducted pre-participation to rule out underlying disease that could affect physical performance (47). The trial team also incorporated fewer exercise sessions per week than is usually for such studies (3 instead of 5), over a relatively longer period (10 weeks) to improve compliance (47). Another exercise intervention trial conducted sessions in the mornings, due to an understanding that people with ID tend to be less active in afternoons and evenings, achieving 100% retention of 36 participants over 12 weeks and 96% adherence (37). Further exercise studies described varying class intensity/task to maintain engagement (48). Trial teams also recognised that invasive procedures such as blood tests can be particularly aversive for people with ID (37, 49), deciding to only taking one blood sample at a timepoint most likely to detect a change (37). Other exercise intervention trials often considered that people with DS may require adapted interventions due to vulnerability to injury (38) caused by physiological differences such as joint hypermobility, hypotonia, and chronotropic incompetence (16, 50). Common comorbidities such as congenital heart abnormalities were also perceived as too high-risk, and so were often applied as exclusion criteria (39), further reducing the pool of eligible participants. However, despite concern regarding injuries, no studies reported sport injury-related reasons for participant drop out. This may therefore reflect a discordance between researcher and participant concerns. Indeed, Heller The study also used a sticker book to log sessions attended, and participants met with a researcher 2-3 times per week to discuss and ameliorate any participation barriers (e.g., offering an alternative if a particular exercise was not enjoyed/too challenging) (42).

Providing incentives or motivators

Another key strategy to maximise participation was providing incentives or motivation. One trial sent motivational postcard reminders (44) and provided reimbursement for travel expenses (44), with a compliance rate of 96%. Other incentives included weekly virtual meetings with health educators, allowing participants to keep a FitBit (fitness smart watch), and a $25 payment at the beginning and end of the trial, with no attrition of their 27 participants and 83.5-89.5% concordance with the exercise intervention (45). Another study also achieved 0% attrition (primary outcome at 56±14 days post-baseline), having selected a motivated cohort of people who had been involved in previous research, shortening assessment tools, and allowing participants to choose the design of their eye-glasses as part of the study (43).

The relational aspects of participation were also thought to be important for retention. Encouragement provided by research teams at weekly meetings (44) or during exercise sessions (36,50) were found to improve retention and increase participant satisfaction. One study even held an awards ceremony at the end of participation to celebrate participants' achievements (14), which may also have incentivised retention.

Finally, participant-carer dyads in the TOP-COG study reported gratitude to researchers for home visits ("it was nice for you to come and visit us") and providing emotional support ("I've unloaded on you… you've been a good sounding board") (25). One RCT exploring an adapted group cycling intervention for self-efficacy (50) also discussed peer-support as a motivational tool, with one parent of a participant describing a "comradery" between participants, with interpersonal dynamics encouraging her son's participation.

Discussion

This systematic review examined the recruitment and retention strategies employed by 44 RCTs involving adults with DS. We identified a predominance of small-scale trials, with variable reporting practices both before and after the implementation of CONSORT guidelines (51), with very few included studies providing target sample sizes, power calculations, or pre-registered protocols. Recruitment appeared to be difficult, although regular omission of target Ns made calculating recruitment success challenging. Retention was also variable between trials. Only one third of the included trials recruited N ≥40 in total (i.e., 20 per arm in a standard two-arm RCT). Some trials reported strategies to minimise participant burden and provided incentives to maximise recruitment and minimise attrition. The most successful trials capitalised on multicentre collaboration and reaching established participant networks at experienced centres (52), which may go some way to addressing recruitment challenges when conducting research with people with rare conditions. In designing the trials, they also adapted interventions and assessments to reduce study burden and facilitate engagement, and involved family or carers throughout participation. The findings are discussed in turn below.

Importance to clinical trial teams Small recruitable population

Prasher et al. (2002) described inherent challenges associated with recruiting people with ID to trials, including the relative rarity of the condition, such that the potentially recruitable population is small, and the likelihood of reaching recruitment goals for sufficient statistical power is therefore reduced. This challenge was reflected in the included studies, e.g., with few eligible participants with DS in primary care (53), or <10% of residents at a specialist ID centre having DS (54). The relatively high incidence of comorbidity associated with DS, including obstructive sleep apnoea, leukaemia, metabolic syndrome, epilepsy, ASD, and Alzheimer's dementia (2-5) can also further limit the number of eligible participants depending on how stringent studyspecific exclusion criteria are. For example, those with Alzheimer's were often excluded, despite high prevalence in the ageing DS population. Some studies described attempts to address this, including making live changes to trial inclusion criteria (41) and targeting recruitment from previous participant pools (43, 44).

Ethics and consent

Difficulties gaining ethical approval represent a further challenge to trials with ID populations, primarily due to concerns about obtaining informed consent. In one study (40), obtaining approval took over a year due e.g., to the perceived vulnerability of the population and their difficulty providing consent, or potentially reduced ability to recognise, interpret, or communicate internal experiences. The TOP-COG study also discussed such concerns during nested qualitative interviews with participants and carers; e.g., a carer voiced fears that research processes may take advantage of or 'use' people with DS, exploiting perceived vulnerability when testing a drug that they worried "could be harmful" (25). A similar exploration of parent attitudes towards clinical trials of cognition-enhancing treatments highlighted a reluctance to allow their children to take part for fear of altering their personality, or risking new mental health concerns as a result of cognitive improvements without corresponding improvements to medical/physical impairments (55). Parents wished for researchers to use the priorities of individuals with DS and their families to inform their work (see e.g., Lim et al., 2019). However, protecting vulnerable populations must be balanced with respect for individual autonomy and the right to selfdetermination (52), e.g., in deciding to participate in a trial. TOP-COG and other studies highlight the importance of careful communication with participants and carers to address concerns and dispel misconceptions, recognising the important role family and carers play in facilitating study engagement.

Although research shows that most people with DS have paid carers, yet there is no clear guidance on how best to involve carers and family members in trials (57). Novel methods of obtaining informed consent, such as picture-based or electronic consenting methods have also been discussed (52). However, none of the included studies reported employing such methods, perhaps reflecting concerns about ethical implications.

Appropriate outcome measures and interventions

Individuals with DS are a highly heterogenous group, making study design and selection of outcome measures challenging. Validated DS-specific instruments are only recently emerging (58, 59), and ceiling or floor effects of existing tools are demonstrated in the included trials (18). Trials in this review largely employed self-report, subjective assessment tools, which may also be particularly susceptible to placebo effects (52).

Equally, heterogeneity in outcomes may lead to a greater likelihood of trials prematurely concluding that treatments offer no benefit, when there may be a clear benefit for a distinct subgroup (52). However, some studies discussed initial attempts to adapt outcome measures and interventions after participant feedback, as well as based on assumptions about people with DS (35, 37, 47), showing initial promise for the application of co-production methods in RCTs with adults with DS (42). It should however be noted that, wherever possible, trial teams should engage with individuals with DS and their families at the earliest opportunity (i.e., during protocol design) to ensure that accessible protocols are co-produced prior to trial commencement, after which amendments to trial interventions and outcome measures are usually not permitted. Although trial teams should consider focusing on quality of the recruitment as represented by engaged, wellinformed, consenting participants, retained for the study duration (60), studies not recruiting to their target sample size, nor recruiting a sufficiently large sample (i.e., N ≥40 as a minimum), are at risk of compromising external validity. Public health communications should also make clear the necessity of RCT processes, such as blinding and randomisation (5, 25, 52). Meaningful PPIE and co-production has also been shown to improve trust in study processes in other seldom-reached groups (e.g., autistic adults) (61, 62).

Recommendations

Strengths & Limitations

This review is the first to provide a broad picture of the field of RCTs in adults with DS, with the focus on recruitment and retention providing useful strategies for future trials. We implemented a systematic search, with multiple authors contributing to the search. We also retrieved studies from a long timeframe, ensuring maximum understanding of the history of RCTs in the DS population. Although every effort was made to maximise the retrieval of relevant literature, searches may have missed articles not published in English.

The scale of recruitment and retention difficulties is likely under-estimated here due to publication bias in the traditional sense, as well as in the failure of trials to publish target recruitment sample sizes and preregistered protocols, and/or not reporting recruitment and retention strategies utilised. With the available information, it is not possible to fully capture the 'clinical iceberg' of trials that may have been terminated prematurely due to poor recruitment and retention. Therefore, the severity of recruitment difficulties outlined here is likely under-reported. It is also unclear whether some smaller studies may have originally set out to recruit higher numbers, due to a lack of reported target Ns. This clinical iceberg is further reinforced by our reporting of retention, due to necessary inferences about the number of participants retained as a result of some studies not reporting drop-out rates (i.e., attrition may appear more optimistic than was actually the case; see Table 2).

In conducting this review it was often difficult to know whether multiple identified papers were reporting on the same trial, with some cases requiring the piecing together of 'clues', such as identical interventions and durations, sample sizes, attrition rates, and numbers allocated to each group, overlapping authorship of manuscripts, etc. Inadequacies in reporting were also a common feature, with less than a third of trials having been pre-registered, and only a handful pre-publishing a trial protocol. In addition, the majority of included trials did not report a pre-specified target N nor power calculation.

Given the multifactorial nature of recruitment, encompassing participant-level factors, trial design, and other external factors, recruitment success or difficulty is unlikely to be determined by a single strategy. If possible, key implementation aspects such as organisational readiness or buy-in should be explored as part of the trial implementation phase (prior to the trial opening) which may further aid recruitment efforts (63, 64). Trial teams often apply many recruitment strategies, making it difficult to understand which specific measures are successful. In addition, understandable priority given to reporting of primary and secondary outcomes may mean that recruitment and retention strategies are under reported in trial publications.

Future research

Trial teams should provide comprehensive reports of trial data as per CONSORT guidelines (65), as well as being explicit about the core trial being referred to when multiple papers are published. Trial teams should consider reporting all recruitment and retention strategies used, their success (actual/estimated), and the contextual facilitators and barriers to participation at an early stage, such as i) working with clinical teams experienced in recruiting adults with DS; ii) integrating PPI, co-production, and qualitative research in the trial to address participation accessibility early; iii) pragmatic inclusion/exclusion criteria based on previous successful studies, exploring if excluding e.g., those with a dementia diagnosis is necessary, or whether a more pragmatic approach such as clinician assessment of capacity to consent can be taken (35); iv) appropriate incentives; v) caregiver concerns, e.g., at the consent stage; and vi) the place where testing and/or follow-up visits take place, including home visits (44). Future trials should also consider exploring PPIE and co-production (42). When asked about healthcare and research priorities, parents of people with DS most valued research to improve quality of life (67). Parents also challenge the premise of research into cognitive enhancement (55), preferring research on education, resources, day-to-day support for families, maintaining daily routines, and opportunities for social interaction (68). There are some powerful examples of studies involving those with DS as 'authentic co-researchers' and experts by experience, which empowered individuals to contribute in a process they were previously excluded from (69). Relatedly, research must aim to involve people with more severe ID (52), who are generally excluded from RCTs, further entrenching health disparities for this group.

Adults with DS can be facilitated to enrol to and remain in RCTs. Trial teams should engage people with DS and their carers in meaningful PPI and co-production at an early stage of trial design, as well as involving carers (with participant permission) beyond the consent stage. Successful strategies to maximise recruitment and retention may include considering whether wider inclusion criteria may be appropriate, minimising study burden, and maximising convenience, e.g., via flexible appointment times and formats, using the shortest suitable intervention duration, and reducing invasive measures. Research teams should carefully consider the suitability of interventions and outcome measures for their target population and provide support for completing self-report measures. Retention can also be encouraged by developing a strong therapeutic alliance between trial teams and participants, alongside appropriate incentives, and implementing explorative qualitative work to identify context-specific factors, with the ability to address these sufficiently quickly.
