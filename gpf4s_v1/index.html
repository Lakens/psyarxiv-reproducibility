About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.

A flag means look at this column. Where the data is already de-identified (an ip column reading Anonymized, a name column the authors emptied), no action is needed.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Application of Bayesian Sequential Analyses to Single Case Data: A Proof-of-Principle Study

Identifying whether a psychological intervention is providing the expected benefits for a patient is crucial if we wish to optimize treatment outcomes. For example, noticing that a patient is not "on track" for a successful outcome provides the opportunity to intervene, for example via feedback to their therapist (e.g., de Jong et al., 2021; Lutz et al., 2022), or switching to another intervention. Monitoring of treatment progress therefore plays a key role in personalization of psychological treatments (e.g., Deisenhofer et al., 2024; Nye et al., 2023). Established methods for monitoring treatment progress largely rely on comparing the trajectory of repeated outcome measurements to benchmarks for expected trajectories, ideally based on large datasets (e.g., Lutz et al., 2022). However, in the absence of suitable data, for example when interventions are novel or outcomes highly idiosyncratic, it would be helpful to be able to perform this monitoring at an individual patient level, that is, via a single case approach.

In a single case approach, the data provided by an individual patient is analysed without recourse to other external data. Prior to starting a treatment the patient completes a baseline phase of repeated measurement only, and via comparison of the pattern of data in the treatment phase to the baseline phase, a judgement can be made as to whether the data are consistent with the treatment having an effect (e.g., Kazdin, 2011; Kininger et al., 2025; Morley, 2017). With some exceptions (e.g., a changing criterion design), this judgement is normally made retrospectively, after treatment completion. However, this is too late to affect the course of treatment itself, and ideally if a treatment is not working it would be useful to be able to identify this as soon as possible.

One method for analysing data as it accumulates that has received increasing attention in the context of between-group designs is sequential Bayesian analysis, for example based on calculation of Bayes factors, which provides a simple means for repeated analysis (even after PREPRINT v1.0 28.10.26 https://osf.io/qcwfx/ each new data point is collected) while preserving the statistical error rate at a desired level (Schönbrodt et al., 2017). Within the context of randomized controlled trials, taking a sequential Bayesian approach can substantially reduce sample sizes by allowing ineffective treatments to be rapidly identified and discarded (Blackwell et al., 2019 (Blackwell et al., , 2023)). Theoretically, applying this approach to single case data would mean that rather than relying on a fixed intervention period, data in the intervention phase would be analysed as it is obtained, and an ineffective treatment could be identified as soon as enough evidence had accumulated. How might this work in practice?

The current paper introduces one potential method for applying sequential Bayesian analyses to single case data, building on the "leapfrog" randomized controlled trial approach outlined by Blackwell et al. (2019). According to this approach, a patient would start with a baseline monitoring period during which they complete daily outcome measures. Daily monitoring continues after initiation of treatment ('intervention period'). After a pre-specified minimum number of data points in the intervention period had been collected (Nmin), sequential Bayesian analysis would be initiated, comparing the data from the baseline phase to the data collected so far in the intervention phase. Specifically, a Bayes factor would be calculated that quantified the relative likelihood of the observed data under the null hypothesis (no improvement in the intervention phase compared to the baseline phase) relative to the alternative hypothesis (that there is improvement in the intervention phase compared to the baseline phase). If at any point a pre-specified Bayes factor threshold for failure (BFfail) were reached, providing sufficient evidence for the null hypothesis, the treatment could be discontinued and a different treatment could be tried, or some kind of modification or augmentation could be attempted .

A time limit (Nmax days/data points) would be set for the treatment to demonstrate efficacy; if the Bayes factor had not yet reached a pre-specified threshold for success (BFsuccess) by this point, then it would also be concluded that the treatment was not sufficiently effective. The exact specification of these parameters (Nmin, PREPRINT v1.0 28.10.26 https://osf.io/qcwfx/ Nmax, BFfail, BFsuccess; see Table 1 for an overview) would determine the sensitivity to detect whether an intervention is effective or not, as well as the efficiency of the design. For example, a BFfail threshold closer to one would make it easier and faster to identify ineffective treatments, but raises the risk of incorrectly discarding an effective treatment (see Table 1 for an overview). Within the leapfrog approach for clinical trials, these parameters are determined by simulating a range of potential outcomes to arrive at a set of parameters giving the required power and false positive rate within a feasible time-frame (see Blackwell et al., 2019).

Applying such an approach to single case data could theoretically bring further advantages: using the baseline phase data to simulate possible continuations into the intervention phase would allow personalization of the parameters based on the individual's data and its characteristics.

For example, if the baseline data showed higher variability and higher levels of autocorrelation, it is likely that more intervention phase data would be needed to draw confident conclusions.

The current study aimed to provide a preliminary illustration of sequential Bayesian analyses in the context of single case data, applying the principles to two potential lowintensity internet-delivered interventions for depression: a cognitive control training (Hoorelbeke et al., 2015; Siegle et al., 2007) and an imagery cognitive bias modification training (imagery CBM; Blackwell et al., 2015; Blackwell & Holmes, 2010). As a proof-ofprinciple demonstration these interventions have the advantage of providing a feasible and resource-efficient way to test the basic idea of applying sequential Bayesian analyses to single case data, especially as the imagery CBM is generally associated with rapid onset of symptom improvement (e.g. within a week; Blackwell & Holmes, 2010; Lang et al., 2012). In the present study, the interventions were delivered to patients waiting for treatment at an outpatient psychological therapy centre, a setting in which such low-intensity interventions could also potentially be useful. The study aimed to collect data from patients while they completed the interventions and then use the data as the basis for exploration of how able to complete all study procedures. Exclusion criteria were existence of a condition or circumstances that would interfere with completion of the study procedures (e.g., severe visual impairment, neurological problem, acute psychosis, or substance withdrawal symptoms); acute crisis with high levels of suicidal ideations or high levels of self-harm, identified either through therapist's assessment or score >1 on item 12 (suicidal ideation) of the QIDS (if then confirmed via further assessment with the patient).

Simulated Examples of Prospective Planning for Bayesian Sequential Analyses

For the purpose of investigating and illustrating how sequential Bayesian analyses could be prospectively planned prior to commencing an intervention, the transition from baseline to the first intervention was examined, with the following two steps: simulation of possible continuations of the daily data, then examination of how different analysis PREPRINT v1.0 28.10.26 https://osf.io/qcwfx/ parameters would impact the ability to identify different levels of treatment effects, and how quickly.

Simulation of Data Continuations.

Possible continuations of the QIDS daily data were simulated over a range of possible treatment effects. A first order autoregressive model was fitted to the baseline data, and then continuations were simulated with the same first order autoregressive structure. A simple linear trend was fitted to the baseline data, and the variance around this trend line used as the variance in the simulated continuation. That is, the continuations were personalized to the individual by taking the auto-regressive structure and variance from their baseline data. The null model (i.e., no treatment effect) was then simulated as a continuation of the baseline data with the same linear trend. Other possible continuations, reflecting different possible levels of treatment success, were defined in terms of the daily QIDS score reached at the end of a two-week intervention period, in intervals of two (e.g., if the null model resulted in a predicted QIDS score of 8 by the end of the two-week period, continuations with linear slopes leading to predicted QIDS scores of 6, 4, 2, and 0 were simulated). A set of 1000 simulations were created for each possible QIDS end score.

Following simulation of each continuation, simulated QIDS scores were rounded to the nearest integer and predicted QIDS scores < 0 were changed to 0. Although our intervention phases were only 14 days, continuations were simulated for 28 days in total to allow exploration of the possibility that longer periods might be needed to detect treatment effects in some cases.

Determining Analysis Parameters.

For each simulated continuation, sequential Bayesian analyses were conducted using the method described in the section "Sequential Bayesian Analyses" above. Sets of possible analysis parameters were then tested in an iterative manner in order to find a set that i) had an ɑ (false-positive rate) of < .05, and ii) provided the potential to identify the greatest range of successful treatment effects, iii) while preserving the possibility of identifying treatments with no effect relatively quickly. The PREPRINT v1.0 28.10.26 https://osf.io/qcwfx/ parameters varied were i) Nmin and Nmax, i.e., the starting point and stopping for the sequential analyses, ii) BFfail and BFsuccess for the comparison of means and slopes between phases, iii) whether "failure" was based on only the mean or slope, required hitting BFfail for both the mean and slope, or could be either one of them. Having found a set of parameters that could not be improved upon, these were then applied to the actual data collected in the first intervention phase to investigate the counterfactual scenario that these had in fact been applied and to see what decision, and how quickly, would have been made.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the possibility of applying sequential Bayesian The study demonstrated a simulation-based method for generating a set of decision parameters and by applying this retrospectively to the collected data found that in 5 cases the first intervention would have been stopped earlier than the two-week trial period, and that in one case it would have identified the treatment as effective.

The study demonstrated that in principle the approach could be applied and could bring some of the hypothesised advantages. That is, personalized decision parameters could be derived based on each patient's individual data pattern in the baseline (e.g., including the variability, autocorrelation, and slope), and potentially this could lead to shortened decision times in the presence of an ineffective intervention. However, the study also illustrated potential complexities of taking a simulation-based approach to trial planning. Specifically, although simulating the null hypothesis, i.e., continuation of the trajectory from the baseline phase, is relatively straightforward, simulating successful treatment outcomes requires more complex decision-making, which in turn will affect the power of the design to detect PREPRINT v1.0 28.10.26 https://osf.io/qcwfx/ successful or unsuccessful treatments. While the current study chose one way of defining and simulating successful outcomes for demonstration purposes, there are of course many different ways one could do this. It could also be argued that in the case of the "failures", simple visual analysis would lead one to conclude that the treatments were not effective much faster than the Bayesian analyses, which seemed relatively inefficient in practice. This could reflect the analysis method being relatively conservative within the context of the measurement schedule used in the current study.

This study applied a particular kind of analysis in a specific intervention context, but there would be many different ways to apply the basic idea, that is, using a participant's own baseline data to simulate a range of possible continuations and using these to determine a set of appropriate analysis and decision parameters with the desired statistical power and falsepositive rate. For example, while this study took the de Vries and Morey (2013) method for Bayesian single case analyses as a ready-to-use "off the shelf" R package (Morey & de Vries, 2014), there are other ways in which Bayesian analyses could be applied, and the general principles could also be applied to non-Bayesian analyses. For example, Simulation Modelling Analysis (Borckardt & Nash, 2014) uses a similar simulation-based approach to single case data within a frequentist approach and could theoretically be applied sequentially with either simulations or statistical considerations used to make the necessary p-value adjustments (see e.g., Lakens, 2014). The exact analysis method that would be most appropriate for any given case is likely to be dependent on the kind of data collected and patterns of change expected (Kininger et al., 2025).

Consideration of the method used in the current study and the results obtained does raise a number of factors that researchers or clinicians would need to think through in applying this or similar methods. First, the question of the appropriate measure for the analyses and the frequency of measurement and analysis is key for determining the utility of the approach. In the current study an adapted daily measure of the ultimate clinical outcome, PREPRINT v1.0 28.10.26 https://osf.io/qcwfx/ symptoms of depression, was used, with the rationale that changes in these symptoms would be the crucial criterion for determining whether the treatment had worked. The changes observed in the daily QIDS were largely consistent with those seen for the standard weekly QIDS (see Table 3 and Figure 1). However, this is a relatively "blunt" measure and potentially a more sensitive and specific measure focussing on e.g., a mechanism targeted by the intervention could have been more appropriate; such a measure might not only be more sensitive to detecting intervention success, reducing the potential false-negative error rate, but also make it easier to detect treatment failure earlier on, in that a lack of early change would provide a clear signal of no effect.

The daily measurement schedule was chosen based on previous literature using the interventions, which indicated that for one of the interventions (the imagery CBM), observable change should happen within one week (e.g., Blackwell & Holmes, 2010; Lang et al., 2012). However, with other interventions where the timescale of change is expected to be slower it might be that such frequent data collection does not add additional useful information, and that daily repeated analyses are not appropriate. Therefore, the exact measurement and analysis schedule would need to be adjusted to the treatment applied. Practical considerations could also play a role in these decisions. For example, in the case of remotely-delivered interventions that are not tied to a particular appointment schedule, daily testing and decision-making could be helpful in switching someone to a new intervention as soon as treatment failure is detected. Conversely, when a treatment is tied to weekly appointments, it could be questioned whether finding out that a treatment is not working after 5 days offers any real advantage to finding out at the appointment 2 days later, except in the case of acute risk, and hence repeated analyses could simply be tied to appointment dates. Another question in the context of an open-ended intervention is what to do with the knowledge that an intervention has hit the success criterion, BFsuccess. In this case one would continue with the intervention, but potentially it would be then useful to build in a mechanism to detect if it ceases to bring further benefits. Finally, while some patients may PREPRINT v1.0 28.10.26 https://osf.io/qcwfx/ welcome rapid detection of a lack of treatment effect and having the chance to try something else, others may interpret this as a sign of personal failure or treatment-resistance, or nevertheless wish to continue with the ineffective treatment.

Use of this sequential approach and any treatment-switching decisions would therefore need to be carefully discussed, explained, and agreed with each individual patient.

While the purpose of this study was to illustrate the basic principles of the approach, it applied the sequential analyses and determined the personalized sets of analysis parameters retrospectively, which could introduce some bias into the decision parameters chosen. This retrospective nature also means that it could not demonstrate "live" application of the decision-making and how that would work from a practical perspective. The study also applied the ideas in a somewhat artificial approach with fixed baseline and intervention timeperiods. For this reason, the simulations relating to determining the decision parameters were only applied to the transition from the baseline to the first intervention. However, in a "realworld" application it might also be worth using the baseline data to make decisions on how to proceed with the next intervention phase. For example, if a participant's data were showing high variability and autocorrelation in the baseline it might be useful to extend the baseline further to acquire more data points, or if a participant's baseline data were already showing a strong downward trend (as was the case for several participants in this study, see Figure 1), it may also not make sense to introduce an intervention, first because it may not be necessary at that time, and second because it would be infeasible to detect treatment success.

Overall, this study presents the idea of applying sequential Bayesian analyses to single case data and illustrates one method for doing this, applied retrospectively to data collected from outpatients completing computerized interventions. While demonstrating the basic idea, the study raises a number of questions about potential future implementations and it seems likely that further optimization will be useful. However, in principle, building on this basic idea opens a number of opportunities, perhaps especially in relation to remotely-delivered https://osf.io/qcwfx/ (e.g., web-or app-based) interventions. Especially in the context of depression, there are many such interventions and while it may be the case that for any one patient there is one specific intervention that will help them most, currently this is difficult to identify with confidence (e.g., Lorenzo-Luaces et al., 2021). A patient could therefore be allocated to or recommended one such intervention as a "best guess" starting point based on their personal characteristics, and sequential analyses of their single case data thereafter used to detect if the treatment is working, and otherwise switch them to the next "best guess" as soon as it has been determined that it is not having an effect. By embedding single case data collection within routine treatment provision (e.g., Scholten et al., 2026), simulation and analyses parameters (e.g., the Bayesian analysis prior) could also potentially be informed by data collected over a larger number of individuals, in this way connecting single case and "big data" approaches. By offering a way to perform sequential analyses and make intervention decisions that require only one specific patient's data, further development of these methods could bring useful advantages to clinical decision-making. Cognitive Control Training.

The cognitive control training was an adaptive Paced Auditory Serial Addition Task (PASAT), as implemented in several studies investigating cognitive control training in the context of depression/rumination (e.g., Calkins et al., 2015; Hoorelbeke et al., 2015; Siegle et al., 2007). The training aims to improve participants' control over the content of their cognitions, especially under conditions of stress, and thus increase their ability to disengage from maladaptive patterns of thinking such as rumination.

During the PASAT, participants heard a continuous stream of digits from 1 to 9 (presented via headphones), with the task of immediately calculating the sum of the last two digits heard and clicking a corresponding button (1-18). Presentation of the digits was separated by an inter-stimulus interval (ISI) that started at 3000ms each session. The ISI was then reduced by 100ms following four consecutive correct responses, or increased by 100ms following four consecutive incorrect responses. In this way the difficulty of the task was adapted to the participant's performance and increased as their performance improved.

Participants received feedback on their performance via presentation of their ISI and number https://osf.io/qcwfx/ Dimensional Anhedonia Rating Scale (DARS; Rizvi et al., 2015). The DARS is a 17-item self-report measure of different facets of anhedonia (desire, motivation, effort and consummatory pleasure) across different hedonic domains (hobbies/past-times, food/drinks, social activities, and sensory experiences). For each hedonic domain participants were asked to list two of their favourite activities or experiences. They then made ratings of the different facets of anhedonia on scales from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much). The German translation from Blackwell et al. (2018) was used.

Randomization

Participants were randomized to one of either i) iCBM first or ii) CCT first, following completion of all baseline measures and discussion of the upcoming training schedule. After confirming with the assessing researcher that they were ready to start, the participant clicked within the online training platform to activate the training schedule, at which point randomization occurred. The randomization sequences were generated via code written by SEB within the online training platform using the java.util.concurrent.

ThreadLocalRandom class, with the default Java initiation (i.e. no specified seed set). Imagery Cognitive Bias Modification (Imagery CBM).

The imagery-based cognitive bias modification (imagery CBM) intervention was adapted from that developed via experimental psychopathology work (e.g., Holmes et al., 2009) and used in previous clinical studies in the context of depression (e.g., Blackwell et al., 2015; Blackwell & Holmes, 2010).

Training stimuli were audio recordings (~10 -15s) of mostly everyday scenarios, which were structured so that they started ambiguous but always resolved positively. Participants were instructed to shut their eyes while listening to each scenario and imagine themselves in the situation described as if actively involved. Via this repeated practice in imagining positive resolutions for ambiguous situations, the training aims to instil a bias to automatically imagine positive resolutions for ambiguous situations encountered in daily life.

The training schedule was adapted from that used by Westermann et al. (2021), using the training scenarios and general instructions from Blackwell et al. (2023). The schedule started with an initial introductory session followed by 8 online sessions over two weeks (with four sessions scheduled for each week). Sessions started with some brief mood ratings, followed by a reminder of the instructions and a practice scenario, and then 5 blocks of 8 scenarios (lasting about 15 -20 minutes). After each scenario participants were asked to rate how vividly they had been able to imagine the scenario, on a scale from 1 (not at all vivid) to 7 (extremely vivid). Between each block participants could take a brief break if needed, and were shown a graphical display of their vividness scores over the blocks completed so far, with instructions to reflect on these and try to improve or maintain their imagery vividness in the upcoming block. Each session ended with brief ratings of state mood. The introductory session included an extended introduction to mental imagery, with several practice examples, followed by 4 blocks of 5 scenarios.
