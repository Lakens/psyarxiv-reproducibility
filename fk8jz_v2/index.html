How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The use of multilevel models in behavioral and social sciences has risen dramatically in the past several decades. Researchers using these models often consult highly-referenced simulation studies when designing their own studies, especially for sample-size planning.

However, current literature suggests two issues with this practice. First, recent work has called into the question the replicability of prior simulation studies. Second, foundational simulations were conducted under relatively simple conditions, but researchers now use more complex models. To address these issues, we conducted a replication and extension of Maas & Hox (2005), a study that is widely cited for sample size planning. We replicated their core findings, suggesting prior results may still inform sample size planning contingent on study design. The extension introduced design features common in modern research, including multiple random slopes, cross-level interactions, and imbalanced Level-1 samples.

These conditions revealed that variance-component estimates are highly sensitive to ICC magnitude, imbalance, and Level-1 sample size, with acceptable coverage typically requiring Level-2 sample sizes N ≥ 100 and Level-1 sample sizes n ≥ 30. Our findings converge on three general principles: random-effects are more difficult to appropriately estimate than fixed-effects, model complexity amplifies sample size demands, and imbalance can undermine coverage even in seemingly adequate designs.

Introduction

Nested data-such as students nested within classrooms or clients nested within therapists-are ubiquitous in the social-behavioral sciences, especially psychology. Given the unique structure of these data, particular statistical methods are needed to appropriately analyze them in order to ensure accurate parameter estimation and subsequent inference (Bauer, McNeish, Baldwin, & Curran, 2020). One such method, multilevel modeling (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002; Raudenbush, 1993), has become increasingly popular since the late 1980s as both an analytic tool for substantive psychological science and a topic of study in methodological development research (Snijders & Bosker, 2012; LeRoux, Cappelli, & Fikis, 2021; Liu & Rhemtulla, 2022; Tong, Zhang, & Zhou, 2021; Kim & Steiner, 2021).

Multilevel models, also referred to as hierarchical, random coefficient, or mixed effects models, are a class of models designed to appropriately handle nested data. Data are considered nested when the individual observations belong to a higher-order unit or entity that can be drawn exchangeably from its own population and possesses its own observable characteristics 1 . Nested structures must be taken into account. Treating the data as though they are not nested and proceeding with a 'standard' ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis has a tendency to downwardly bias coefficient standard errors and inflate false positive rates beyond their nominal level (Barcikowski, 1981). Ignoring the nesting structure of a dataset obstructs the goal of conducting reliable science.

While this versatility makes multilevel modeling a powerful tool, it also means that virtually every aspect of multilevel modeling is more complex than traditional regression methods and gives researchers more to consider when selecting their analysis model and designing their studies (McNeish et al., 2017). This is especially true for determining sample sizes. In multilevel modeling, researchers must determine the appropriate sample sizes for Level-1 and Level-2. While closed-form, analytic solutions exist to help analysts compute sample size requirements for given effects (Scherbaum & Ferreter, 2009; Westfall, Kenny, & Judd, 2014; Moerbeek, van Breukelen, & Berger, 2000; Moerbeek & Maas, 2005; Spybrook, Kelcey, & Dong, 2016; Raudenbush & Liu, 2000; Murayama, Judd, & Sakaki, 2022), they are limited insofar that they primarily apply to simplistic model specifications that are less complex than the models substantive researchers typically want to use, or they rely on using problematic test statistics (Luke, 2017).

Because the issue of sample size requirements is not straightforward to address with analytic approaches, the field has relied heavily on simulation studies (Arnold, Hogan, Colford, & Hubbard, 2011). Perhaps the single most influential simulation study for multilevel sample size is Maas and Hox (2005). In their original study, the authors found that Level-2 sample sizes greater than 50 units tended to result in unbiased standard errors, and Level-2 sample sizes as small as 10 units tended to result in unbiased regression coefficients at both levels. These findings have informed thousands of published research studies, as shown by its high citation count (over 4,550 times, according to an April, 2024 hierarchical data-pooling.

Google Scholar search).

In many scientific fields, potentially high impact research is subject to additional scrutiny and replication to ensure the veracity of its findings. For example in the field of astronomy, a report claiming to discover phosphine in Venus' atmosphere, which could imply the presence of life, was immediately replicated by other independent teams and quickly debunked (Villanueva et al., 2021). Scholars acknowledge that not all studies need to be replicated, and that replications should not be done at random (Kuehberger & Schulte-Mecklenbeck, 2018). Rather, the potential and realized impact plays an important role in determining what to replicate (Witte & Zenker, 2018; Coles, Tiokhin, Scheel, Isager, & Lakens, 2018). Indeed, recent attempts to replicate simulation studies focus on high citation count as criteria for inclusion (Luijken et al., 2024; Fairchild, Yin, Baraldi, Astivia, & Shi, 2024). The study by Maas and Hox (2005) is very highly cited, and, as will be demonstrated later, used to justify sample size for many substantive studies, meaning that this study has a core impact on a broad research literature. If its findings are not replicable, this could have a large impact on our trust in the citing literature and have practical implications for how multilevel sample size planning is conducted in the future.

While high impact studies are important to replicate, others have warned against committing additional resources to already precise findings (Singh Chawla, 2016). This is perhaps a reason why statistical simulation research has seen little scrutiny until recent years: the methods are often very precise, leaving little room for sampling variability as an explanation for failure to replicate. However, this does not make simulations immune to a lack of replicability. Recent examples of replications in simulations include a failure to replicate the oft cited "1 variable for 10 events" rule in logistic regression (van Smeden et al., 2016); by contrast, Fairchild et al. (2024) generally replicated the results of Curran, West, and Finch (1996) on the robustness of test statistics in confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to non-normal data, but this result did not generalize. Replications are also often included either implicitly or explicitly as part of extensions of existing research (McNeish & Wolf, 2021; Montoya, 2022; Plantz, Brown, Wright, & Flake, 2024). Jelizarow, Guillemot, Tenenhaus, Strimmer, and Boulesteix (2010) demonstrated how different choices of conditions, datasets, and competing methods (i.e., simulator degrees of freedom) can be manipulated to demonstrate the superiority of a clearly inferior method. Ultimately, this suggests that the replicability of simulation studies should not be taken as a certainty.

These issues have led to recent calls for more replication attempts of simulation studies (Boulesteix, Hoffman, Charlton, & Seibold, 2020; Rougier et al., 2017; Lohmann, Astivia, Morris, & Groenwold, 2022). This burgeoning area of research has identified several reasons why simulation studies might be subject to the same replicability concerns as substantive empirical work. First, simulation studies are just as subject to clerical errors as any scientific endeavor (Lohmann et al., 2022). Even innocuous software bugs or coding errors can be enough to derail a simulation study. Second, analytic flexibility can also apply to simulation studies in the form of selective or incomplete reporting, choosing random number generator seeds that produce outlier results ("seed hacking"), unclear decision boundaries about what constitutes model or method failure, and so on (Luijken et al., 2024; Jelizarow et al., 2010). Third, descriptions of simulation studies may contain ambiguous information (Luijken et al., 2024). A study may inadvertently introduce ambiguity by using imprecise or flexible notation, omitting descriptions of intermediate steps, or providing contradicting information. This fourth reason is particularly germane to the current replication, as we detail in the next section. Collectively, these reasons underscore the growing need to replicate published simulation studies, particularly those of high impact and low precision. A failure to replicate could help identify errors in the original simulation, software specific properties, and help to resolve ambiguities in the existing literature. A successful replication may add to the existing evidence for a certain claim within the parameters of the specific study, but does not contribute to the claim's generalizability.

While replication is important for simulation studies, so too is generalizability.

Simulations are often conducted under limited conditions, and it is important to evaluate how generalizable the claims are to broader conditions. Fairchild et al. (2024) demonstrated how different methods for generating non-normal data provide different results with respect to robustness of CFA statistics. This result calls to question much of the existing literature on robustness to assumption violations, particularly in cases where there are multiple ways to simulate those violations. The generalizability of Maas and Hox (2005) is important because the original study was simplistic. This had the effect of leading substantive researchers to cite Maas and Hox (2005) in justification of modeling specifications and research designs that were never tested in the original study. While plenty of other simulation studies on multilevel sample size requirements were published after Maas and Hox (2005), their subsequent designs did not necessarily extend the complexity of model parameters that correspond to routine use cases encountered by substantive researchers (e.g., cases with more than one predictor at each level, testing a relatively restricted range of standardized effect sizes, etc.) (Arend & Schäfer, 2019; Lane & Hennes, 2018; Lafit, Adolf, Dejonckeere, Viechtbauer, & Ceulemans, 2021; Mathieu, Aguinis, Culpepper, & Chen, 2012). These studies focus on cases with only one predictor at each level, or probe needed sample sizes for highly specialized use cases (e.g., dyadic models, intensive longitudinal studies). This begs the question: Does the original recommendation of 50 Level-2 units and 10 Level-1 units generalize to more complex use cases? To put this point differently, the field's enthusiasm for multilevel modeling has resulted in the implementation of increasingly complex modeling cases, yet collective interest in verifying whether foundational research on sample size sufficiency generalizes to such cases is only starting to emerge (Enders, Keller, & Woller, 2023).

In response to these issues, this registered report presents a replication of Maas and Hox (2005) and proposes an extension of their original study driven by trends in research papers that have recently cited it. We also encourage better adoption of established reporting guidelines for multilevel analyses aimed at enhancing scholarly communication about model specification, as this issue is critical to the current study. The following sections review the original specifications of Maas and Hox (2005) and their results, present the results of our replication simulation study, describe findings from a brief survey of substantive literature citing the original simulation, and summarize our proposed extension.

Assuming the original authors intended to use ρ res , the original simulation employed a fully crossed 3 x 3 x 3 factorial design in which they varied the rICC (ρ res = {0.1, 0.2, 0.3}), Level-2 sample size (N = {30, 50, 100}), and Level-1 sample size (n = {5, 30, 50}).

Restricted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (RML) in MLwiN. X ij and Z j values were originally generated from a standard normal distribution for the smallest cell size (N = 30, n = 5) and were 'repeated' for conditions with larger sample sizes. X ij was generated to have an ICC = 0 (reflecting centering within groups), and is thus uncorrelated with Z j.

Consequently, this also means that X ij Z j is uncorrelated with both X ij and Z j . Regression coefficients were specified such that γ00 = 1.00 and all other slopes were equal to 0.3, and the residual (Level-1) variance σ 2 e was set to 0.5. Maas and Hox (2005) calculated τ 00 from σ 2 e and the rICC from a given condition (setting τ00 = τ 11 ). Unfortunately the original article did not report the average parameter estimates, so it is not possible to confirm our assumptions about the values of τ 00 from their simulation results.

The primary outcome of the study was percent relative bias, a metric of parameter estimate accuracy, given by 100 × ( θ -θ) / θ, such that θ is the population parameter and θ is the average observed parameter estimate. Confidence interval (CI) accuracy (95%) was analyzed by examining each interval's non-coverage for all parameters (regression coefficients, variance components, and Level-1 variance). Maas and Hox (2005) conducted a series of logistic regressions to determine whether a given factor (rICC, Level-1 sample size, Level-2 sample size) predicted CI non-coverage, using an α threshold of .01 for significance.

The results of the study showed negligible bias across regression coefficients, having an average of 0.05%, with the greatest relative bias overall in the condition with the smallest sample sizes and largest rICC (0.3). With respect to non-coverage, the authors found that the Level-2 sample size influenced non-coverage of the variance components (T), intercept (γ 00 ), and the coefficient for X ij (γ 10 ), with the non-coverage rates being higher for the variance components (approx. 8%) compared to the fixed effects (approx. 6%). A similar pattern was observed for Level-1 sample size, such that varying the sample size more drastically affected variance components than regression coefficients. The same pattern was observed with respect to rICC, as varying the Level-2 sample size affected the variance components (T) but not the fixed effect regression coefficients (γ 00 , γ 01 , γ 10 , γ 11 ). Fixed effect regression coefficients and Level-1 error (σ 2 e ) had non-coverage rates comparable to the nominal non-coverage rate (5%-6%) across the other conditions. From these results, Maas and Hox (2005) conclude that a small Level-2 sample size (equal to or less than 50) lead to biased Level-2 standard errors (e.g., non-coverage rates on parameters decreased as sample sizes increased); the authors deemed non-coverage rates around 7% with 50 units was acceptable in practice. The authors reported that all 27,000 fitted models converged.

When simulating data and fitting this model, Maas and Hox (2005) made several notable assumptions and analytic choices that limit the original study's generalizability.

We focus on four major limitations of the original simulation. First, the model presented in Equation 3 was the only model in the simulation, meaning the effect of adding additional predictors (at either level) and cross-level interactions on sample size requirements cannot be inferred from the results of their simulation study. Second, the original study assumed perfectly balanced sample sizes across all Level-2 units (i.e., all Level-2 units shared the same number of Level-1 units nested within them). Third, they assumed no covariance between random effects (i.e., τ01 = 0) when simulating the datafoot_1 . Fourth, they also assumed the random effects variances for the random slopes and intercepts were identical, τ00 = τ 11 . These latter three assumptions are rarely satisfied in practice -Level-2 units are routinely imbalanced, random effects tend to covary to a non-zero degree, and random variances are almost never equal (Enders et al., 2023). While these simplifying assumptions were necessary as a first step when the original study was conducted, the consequence of violating them is unknown but will be explored in our extensions of the simulation after our replication.

A Replication of the Original Simulation Model and Procedure

Here we present our replication of Maas and Hox (2005), following their original simulation procedures as closely as possible. Our goal in conducting the replication was to confirm or dispute the original study's recommendation that N = 50 is needed for sufficiently estimating parameters and standard errors without bias. In practice, this meant that we were looking to observe the same conditions that led the original authors to make that recommendation. This meant that in a successful replication (i) the bias of all model parameters did not exceed 5%foot_2 and (ii) non-coverage rates did not vary significantly as a function of condition or, if they were significant, exhibited a non-coverage rate below 7.5%.

All simulation code and results can be accessed on the Open Science Framework (osf.io/yx7f3). One potential deviation from the original study, and another point of ambiguity, concerns the data simulation procedure. The authors' noted that data were simulated for the smallest possible Level-1 and Level-2 sample size combination and then duplicated for larger combinations. It was unclear how this duplication was conducted. In our replication, we duplicated the initial data from the smallest combination however many times needed to achieve a larger sample size. In the event that duplicating the initial dataset exceeded the desired sample size, the final copy of the initial dataset was truncated. We fitted the multilevel models in this study using the lme4 package in R using REML (Bates, Mächler, Bolker, & Walker, 2015). The random effect covariance was not set to zero in this part of the simulation (see Footnote 3). Notably, lme4 does not offer Wald confidence intervals for the variance components. In order to be consistent with the original study, we wrote custom code to compute the necessary statistics. Results are reported below. Relative bias is reported first, followed by non-coverage. Methods for analyzing each outcome are detailed in the respective subsections.

Relative Bias. Results for relative bias are listed in the Appendix (Supplementary Tables 1 2 3). Bias was low across the board for all parameters. Because bias values in these conditions were less than 10% (the largest cell among them was 5.9% for random slope variance in the n = 5 level, though all but eight values fell below 1%), we conclude that the original results replicate with respect to relative bias and these particular parameters.

Coverage. We created an indicator variable for each parameter within each replicated dataset to code for whether the 95% confidence intervals included the true parameter (1 = non-coverage,0 = coverage). To produce a table of results aligned with the original study, we regressed these indicator values over a given condition of interest (Level-2 sample size, Level-1 sample size, or ICC) for each parameter in the simulation.

This resulted in 21 separate logistic regressions with each model corresponding to one condition and one parameter (3 conditions x 7 parameters). Within each regression the levels of the predictor variable were entered as two dummy codes. We then conducted a likelihood ratio test of the full model containing the dummy codes compared to the null model to determine whether adding the codes (corresponding to a simulation condition) to the model explained more variability than would be expected under the null. For consistency with the original simulation, we adopted an alpha threshold of 0.01.

Non-coverage rates in our replication were broadly comparable to those reported in Maas and Hox (2005). We did observe some slight deviations. First, non-coverage rates across the board were generally lower. Second, the results of a few significance tests (i.e., reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis that a given condition had no effect on non-coverage for a given parameter) were different than the original study. However, even in these cases, non-coverage values were still quite low (slightly lower than observed in the original study) such that it would likely not make a practical difference.

While the original authors did not examine interactions between conditions, these interactions may exist. Mimicking the analytical process in our extension (see below), we tested for interactions in the non-coverage replication simulation data. We did so by first testing for the significance of the highest order (three-way) interaction and then iteratively tested all possible lower order (two-way) interactions for all parameter estimates. We observed no significant three-way or subsequent two-way interactions for the intercept (γ 00 ), slopes (γ 01 , γ 10 , γ 11 ), and Level-1 error variance (σ 2 e ).

We first observed a significant three-way interaction between rICC, N (number of groups) and n (group size) for the random slope variance (τ 10 , p = 0.003). Unpacking this three-way interaction revealed significant two-way interactions between N and n at two levels of rICC. First, when rICC = 0.1 and n = 5 (p = 0.008) non-coverage decreases with increasing N , but not at other levels of n (n = 30, p > 0.250; n = 50, p > 0.250).

Non-coverage rates decrease with increasing n at all levels of N but more so at higher N (N = 30, p < .001; N = 50, p = 0.002; N = 100, p < .001). When rICC = 0.2, there was a significant interaction between n and N (p = .002) such that standard error bias increases with N when n = 5 (p = .003), but less so at other levels of n (n = 30, p = 0.050; n = 50, p > 0.250). Non-coverage decreases with changes in n when N = 30 (p < 0.001) but not when N = 50 (p = 0.072) or N = 100 (p > 0.250). When rICC = 0.3, there was not a significant two-way interaction between n and N (p = 0.232) and the simple effects show no significant effects of n on non-coverage at any level of N , nor any significant effects of N at any level of n (p-values ranging between 0.043 and 0.977).

Looking at the interaction another way, there is a significant two-way interaction between N and rICC when n = 5 (p = 0.001), such that non-coverage decreased with larger N when rICC = 0.1 (p = 0.004) but increased with larger N when rICC = 0.2 (no significant simple slope found when rICC = 0.3, p = 0.044). Non-coverage increased with larger rICC values across levels of N (N = 30, p = 0.009; N = 50, p < 0.001; N = 100, p < 0.001).

Finally, when N = 100 we observed a significant interaction between n and rICC (p < 0.001). Non-coverage increased with n when rICC = 0.1 (p < 0.001) but not when rICC = 0.2 (p > 0.250) or 0.3 (p = 0.118), and it increased with rICC when n = 5 (p < 0.001) but not when n = 30 (p > 0.250) or 50 (p > 0.250).

Next, we observed a significant two-way interaction between rICC and n for the random intercept (τ 00 ) parameter (p = 0.001). Unpacking this interaction revealed significant effects of rICC on non-coverage rates when n = 5 (p < 0.001), and of n on standard error bias when rICC was equal to 0.1 (p < 0.001) and 0.2 (p = 0.002). In all cases, bias increased with increases in the focal predictor (rICC or n). Non-coverage rates for all cells in these interactions were all below 7.5%.

Replication Summary.

We generally replicated the results of the original simulation, confirming the authors' conclusion that achieving acceptably low bias and non-coverage in the model requires Level-2 sample sizes of at least N = 50. In fact, this replication shows that some of the non-coverage rates in the original study were perhaps slightly inflated. We are encouraged by the relatively high rate of replication across model parameters with respect to non-coverage as well as the fact that most of the non-replications indicated less non-coverage than before. We take this to be good news for studies in the last twenty years that have relied on the guidelines initially proffered by Maas and Hox (2005).

Surveying How Scholars Typically Cite These Results

While it is encouraging the findings from Maas and Hox (2005) replicated, it is important to contextualize how other researchers cite the original study in the broader literature. Simulation studies are only informative to the extent that substantive researchers do not deviate too far from the original conditions of the simulation.

Extrapolating from these conditions does not guarantee comparable model performance.

To that point, little is known about how other researchers use Maas and Hox (2005) to justify their research designs. This is critical to know because it is unclear whether the main recommendation by the authors would hold up under more realistic data structures and more complicated models encountered in modern research settings. In response to this, we surveyed recently published literature to motivate and inform the proposed extension of the original study.

We conducted a survey of 50 journal articles reporting substantive empirical reports between 2018 and 2022 to determine how scholars have been citing Maas and Hox (2005) in their own work. All papers examined were published in peer-reviewed journals spanning several fields (psychology, neuroscience, political science, marketing, nursing, etc.), mostly within the social and biomedical sciences. Peer-review articles for the survey were identified by using Google Scholar's interface to retrieve papers that cited the original study and were published in the aforementioned time frame. The search returned 2,490 articles. Papers were selected at random from the list returned by Google Scholar. Review articles or papers on quantitative methods research were excluded such that they were screened out of sample papers and then replaced with a new one from the population. The following 11 items of information were extracted from each paper to evaluate how Maas and Hox (2005) is being cited: (1) reason for citing, (2) Level-1 sample sizefoot_3 , (3) whether there were imbalanced units, (4) Level-2 sample size, (5) number of random effects (including random intercepts), (6) number of Level-1 fixed effect regression coefficients (excluding intercepts), (7) number of Level-2 regression coefficients (excluding intercepts), (8) number of cross-level interactions, (9) ICC, (10) whether the authors reported random effect covariance (if applicable), (11) any other noteworthy information (e.g., if they specified a multilevel structural equation model, if they used multilevel logistic regression, if a three-level model was specified, etc.).

The spreadsheet summarizing these findings can be accessed on our OSF page. Two coders (JB, AC) independently evaluated the journal articles according to the 11 items listed above. If information was unavailable, the field was marked as missing. The two coders met approximately on a weekly basis with the first author to resolve disagreements in the coding. Disagreements were resolved by returning to the given article, evaluating the information as a group, and achieving unanimous consensus. The most common disagreements were when one coder did not find relevant information, and so listed a field as "missing" whereas the other coder found the information. A summary of journals and articles represented in our literature search, aggregated by field, is displayed in Supplementary Table 4 (Appendix). A disaggregated list can be accessed on our OSF page.

The majority of authors cite Maas and Hox (2005) to justify their sample size (most frequently Level-2, 44% of studies explicitly citing for Level-2 sample size). Alarmingly, a notable subset (8%) of them miscite the paper in some way (e.g., claiming a smaller sample size is acceptable than what was originally reported, justifying a drastically different type of model like a three-level model, etc.). Other reasons for citing Maas and Hox (2005) were to justify the general use of a multilevel model, or justify not using a three-level model. In terms of Level-1 sample size, 26%, 66%, and 72% of studies reported Level-1 sample sizes below n = 5, 30, and 50, respectively. These values correspond to the three levels of n included in the original simulation; we present these as benchmarks since Maas and Hox (2005) did not make strong or explicit recommendations about Level-1 sample size. 0%, 10%, and 48% report Level-2 sample sizes below N = 30, 50, and 100, respectively.

Notably, 88% report imbalanced Level-1 sample sizes across Level-2 units (6% report balance; 6% were unclear). Only 46% of papers surveyed reported an ICC, and of those reported, the range was .01 -.83 (0.32 mean, 0.26 median), exceeding the range examined in the original Maas and Hox (2005) report. This wide range of ICC values is consistent with meta-analyses of various subfields (Glassman, Potter, Baumler, & Coyle, 2015; Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013; Arend & Schäfer, 2019), many of which were published after the original report. Of the papers reporting an intraclass correlation coefficient, 47.82% did not clearly designate whether they used the null ICC or the rICC, 43.48% reported the null ICC, and 8.70% reported both quantities.

The majority of published studies ran models that were more complex than Maas and Hox (2005). Seventy-four percent included more than one Level-1 predictor, 66% included more than one Level-2 predictor, and 30% included more than one cross-level interaction.

Nineteen percent of studies reported running models with more than two random effects.

Only three papers reported the covariance between random effects when applicable-in fact, most papers did not even specify whether they constrained these parameters to zero or whether they let it vary freely. Anecdotally, several papers provided an impoverished account of the models they were specifying. It was difficult to identify basic information in several papers, such as the number of predictors at either level, number of random slopes, and even, in some cases, the sample size. This review suggests the modern multilevel modeling studies have exceeded the complexity of the initial simulations conducted to inform them. Although this is typically a hallmark of scientific progress, enthusiasm for more involved modeling has ostensibly outstripped the guidelines that substantive researchers actively use to plan and justify their study designs. This is in spite of subsequent work that advanced our understanding of sample size planning for multilevel modeling studies, such as by providing closed-form solutions for simplistic models or simulations for special modeling cases (e.g., logistic regression, variable skew) (Westfall et al., 2014; Astivia, Gadermann, & Guhn, 2019; Judd, Westfall, & Kenny, 2017; Mathieu et al., 2012; Murayama et al., 2022). While these aforementioned studies have received considerable attention in their own right, we believe the continued reliance on Maas and Hox (2005) is centered on the fact that more work is needed to align simulation studies of multilevel sample size with common practice.

An Extension of Maas and Hox (2005)

Overview. In response to our replication findings and the literature survey, we proposed four extensions to the original Maas and Hox (2005) simulation. We briefly introduce these extensions here and then detail their implementation in the context of the broader simulation procedure below. First, we varied the number of Level-1 slopes and cross-level interactions. Second, we derived the population parameters in a different way than the original authors (Enders et al., 2023; Rights & Sterba, 2019), opting to simulate data and subsequently model coefficients in terms of the total variance explained by various parts of the model (e.g., within-unit predictors, cross-level interactions, etc.). Third, we added levels with unbalanced Level-1 units (i.e., the value of n varies across Level-2 units).

Fourth, and last, we used the total ICC (i.e., ρ * ) to simulate data (instead of the rICC) and added an additional ICC value (0.4) to better reflect results from our literature survey.

For the sake of transparency, we note here that we initially wanted to include a condition that tested how the correlation between random effects impacted bias and coverage, but we decided to not pursue this because there was not enough information in the published literature to identify values for such a condition and also because others suggest that fixed effect coefficients are not sensitive to these covariances (Snijders & Bosker, 2012).

Simulation Models & Procedures.

Our simulation used three two-level models, constituting the first proposed extension. In addition to the original two-level model from Equation (3), the following two models were included.

The model in equation 10 has two Level-1 predictors and two cross-level interactions. The model in equation 11 has three Level-1 predictors and three cross-level interactions. Note that a numeral has been added to the X variables to differentiate between Level-1 predictors.

We used a different data generation mechanism for the proposed extension, representing our second proposed extension. Previously, Maas and Hox (2005) simulated X and Z data from standard normal distributions with a sample size corresponding to the smallest combination of N and n and 'repeated' rows if necessary for larger sample size combinations. They then generated the stochastic components of the slope and intercept, used the full model coefficients to compute E(Y |X, Z, u), and then added residuals. Here we took a different approach by deriving the population parameters based on percent variance explained by the model terms (Rights & Sterba, 2019). In other words, instead of setting population parameters and then generating data, we set the percent variance explained by each model component, derived population parameters, and then simulated data. This procedure involved specifying the percentage of variance explained by various sources: within-unit predictors, between-unit predictors, cross-level interactions, and random slopes. Two additional sources of variance-random intercept variance and Level-1 residual variance-are obtained by subtraction. The percent variances explained by the model components are used to systematically reverse engineer model coefficients and variance components. Finally, we note that we did not repeat, or 'stack' rows in our design matrix as the original study.

We planned to test the following three different combinations of percent variance explained proportions by Level-1, Level-2, and cross-level fixed effect terms: (Level-1 -0.095, Level-2 -0.095, cross-level -0.095; Level-1 -0.065, Level-2 -0.065, cross-level -0.025;

Level-1 -0.035, Level-2 -0.035, cross-level -0.01). Proportion variance explained by random slopes was held at 0.1 across all levels of this condition. In models with more than one predictor, the percent variance was partitioned across the predictors such that the total percent variance explained for each set of predictors (Level-1, Level-2, cross-level) was distributed equally across predictors. Each set of values assumed an outcome variable with a mean of 1. The first set of values were specified to approximate the initial model coefficients specified by Maas and Hox (2005). The latter two sets reflect realistic values observed in the literature (Rights & Sterba, 2019). Functionalities of the mlmpower package (Enders et al., 2023) were used to calculate model coefficients and simulate data.

The Level-1 predictors were generated with a population ICC of zero, such that there was no systematic between-cluster variability in the predictors. Cluster means therefore varied across clusters by chance, meaning that observed ICC values of the Level-1 predictors were close to, but not exactly, zerofoot_4 . Note that in other contexts, one would need to also be mindful of the means and variances of cross-product terms; here the means and variances are equal to 0 and 1, respectively, because of our particular centering choices, but it would otherwise depend on the scaling of the factors comprising the product terms and the ICCs of the Level-1 terms. Consequently, because the Level-1 and Level-2 predictors had zero means and were uncorrelated with one another owing to the Level-1 predictors having an ICC of zero, the percent variance explained from each model component (Level-1 slopes, Level-2 slopes, cross-level interactions) was additive. Finally, for the sake of comparison, it is worth noting that based on the model coefficients and random effects variances of the original study, the proportion variance explained by the Level-1 and cross-level interaction (centering within groups effectively turns the cross-level interaction into a Level-1 slope, for variance partitioning purposes) ranged from 13 to 19%, whereas the proportion variance explained by the Level-2 slopes ranged from 6 to 10%.

Data were simulated to include different degrees of imbalance between Level-1 predictors, representing our third proposed extension. Specifically, we added levels 7 with unbalanced cases such that the sample size ratio between the largest unit size and smallest unit size varied from 1:1, 1.5:1, 2:1, and 2.5:1. The center of the range of Level-1 group sizes corresponds to the average Level-1 group size level, corresponding to the ideal value given no imbalance (5, 30, or 50). Arithmetic is then used to solve for the minimum and maximum sample size. For example, if the average group size 30 and the ratio is 2:1 then the minimum was 20 and the max was 30 (some ratios result in non-whole numbers, so these numbers are rounded to the closest whole number prior to data generation). Level-1 group sizes (n) for a given simulated dataset were drawn from a uniform distribution. The lower bound of the distribution's range corresponded to the minimum sample size and the upper bound corresponded to a maximum sample size. Finally, data generation included an additional level for the ICC (0.4) in line with the results from our literature survey.

This constitutes the fourth and final proposed extension of the original simulation.

Given these four extensions and their respective levels (summarized in Table 5), the proposed extension was intended to comprise 3,888 unique conditions. The extension was designed to employ a comparable procedure as the original study and our replication. A unique dataset was simulated within each cell (1,000/cell), lme4 fit the relevant model to the data using RML, and the statistical quantities of interest were extracted (parameter estimates, confidence intervals).

Variables & Analysis.

We proposed to analyze percent relative bias by examining the degree of bias in each level within each condition while collapsing across all other conditions. If bias exceeded 5% but was less than 10%, we proposed to perform a one-sample t-test to determine whether the bias is significantly different from zero. If levels within a condition were found to be significantly different from zero, we planned to perform a between-samples t-test for all pairwise levels within the same condition to determine whether they are significantly different from each other. If bias exceeded 10%, we planned to simply conclude the degree of bias is unacceptably high.

We planned to analyze non-coverage rates by creating an indicator variable for non-coverage (1: 95% CI did not contain the parameter value, 0: it did) and then regressing these indicator values on the simulation conditions (consistent with the analysis of the original simulation). This was intended to be an iterative process whereby all possible higher order interactions were to be tested, beginning with an interaction between all conditions. If the highest order interaction(s) was not significant, we would drop to the next highest order interaction(s) until a model with no interactions was tested. If any interaction was significant, we intended to test for simple slope significance for the lower order terms comprising the interaction. If the interaction comprised three or more terms, then this would proceed in an iterative fashion. These tests were intended help us determine whether non-coverage rates vary as a function of a given condition, or interactions between conditions.

Extension Simulation Results

Overview

The extension simulation explored how more realistic and complex multilevel model specifications-motivated by common practices in the current literature-impact relative bias and confidence interval coverage across a range of conditions. We manipulated the number of Level-1 slopes and cross-level interactions ('Model Type'), the degree of imbalance in Level-1 sample sizes ('Imbalance'), the percentage of variance explained by fixed effects ('Variance Explained'), the total ICC ('ICC'), as well as both the Level-1 ('n') and Level-2 ('N') sample sizes.

In sum, the simulation was meant to comprise 1,296 conditions across three model specifications (3,888 total), with 1,000 replications per condition. The following results section details deviations from our pre-planned analyses and then breaks down the results in terms of (i) percent relative bias of parameter estimates and (ii) non-coverage of 95% CIs.

Notably, although our simulation was initially meant to comprise 3,888 conditions in total, upon running the simulation we discovered unforeseen model identification issues.

Specifically, models that include more than one Level-1 predictor and cross-level interaction at the lowest Level-1 sample size (n) and with any imbalance could not be estimated. This forced us to drop 864 conditions from the simulation.

Percent Relative Bias

We decided a priori to analyze relative bias by flagging values exceeding 5% and conducting a statistical test to determine if those rates differed from zero. However, this analysis plan proved to be problematic to implement. When planning, we inadvertently overlooked the fact that the disaggregated data comprises over 2,500,000 unique cases, which virtually guarantees that any statistical tests for cells exceeding the relative percent bias threshold of 5% would be statistically significant. In our view, this obviates the utility of significance testing, which does not provide any additional meaningful information beyond plots and figures of relative bias rates. In response to this, we opted to plot and table percent relative bias rates and highlight cells that are particularly high.

Relative bias values for unique simulation cells are depicted in Figures 5 6 7. Notably, bias was observed to be close to zero for most cells involving γ 00 and γ 01 , and was within the nominal 5% cut-off for all other slope parameters. Level-1 error variance (σ 2 e ) showed minimal bias across conditions. The range of values is lengthened for models that are more complex (Figure 6), those that include more than three fixed effect terms. However, the worst bias was observed in the variance components, specifically the random effect variances (Figure 7). Bias for τ 00 was exceedingly high in conditions marked by low ICC values (i.e., ICC = 0.1), particularly when the variance explained by fixed effects is high.

Moreover, τ 00 and the rest of the random slope variances showed extremely high biases in conditions where the Level-1 sample sizes were lowest (equal to n = 5).

Model parameters were somewhat robust to imbalanced cases, with modest (less than 5%) increases in relative bias observed between the highest and lowest imbalance conditions for most parameters. One exception was the τ 11 and τ 33 parameters, which showed greater bias in highly imbalanced designs (and the parameter τ²2 to a lesser extent). A subset of percent-relative bias rates from the most relevant conditions are reported in Table 6.

Non-Coverage

Initially, we had decided a priori to analyze non-coverage outcomes by regressing them on the simulation conditions using logistic regression and stepwise model comparison approach where the highest possible order interaction would be tested and then dropped iteratively as needed. However, this too proved to be problematic. Specifically, the large number of cases drastically inflated the computational cost of analyses when considering the proposed stepwise approach and that each of the study variables were factors comprised of several levels. While we did indeed attempt to run the analyses we originally specified, the memory costs of fitting the models outstripped what was available to us (high performance computing cluster at the university) and prevented us from analyzing anything greater than a three-way interaction. We proceeded with analyzing all possible three-way interactions for each parameter of interest, however most of these interactions and ensuing lower order (two-way) interactions were significant in a manner that was unwieldy for interpretation. Specifically, the volume of significant interactions was sufficiently high and results were diffuse enough that it was conceptually challenging to interpret the results in a meaningful way. To address this, we only examined main effects across all parameters while also plotting and tabling non-coverage statistics to show visual information about the interactions while also identifying individual cells that are particularly high.

Rates of non-coverage for select conditions are shown in Table 7; results for quantitative analyses of non-coverage are presented in Tables 8 -10 and visualized in Figures 8 -10. In terms of fixed effects, the γ 10 and γ 11 parameters were most susceptible to high non-coverage across several simulation conditions, usually those involving high imbalance or smaller Level-1 sample sizes. Other fixed effects were less susceptible, with the γ 30 parameter not showing any significant main effects. Notably, the variance explained condition had a significant main effect on non-coverage for all cross-level interactions (Table 10). However, visual inspection of non-coverage rates show modest and inconsistent differences between conditions, suggesting that the observed effects were driven by the high sample size (i.e., number of unique conditions in a given analysis) and by rates that only slightly exceed the nominal 5% convention in the field of psychology (up to 7 or 8%).

By contrast, the variance components showed differential levels of non-coverage, primarily depending on the specific type of parameter. Specifically, the random slope variances were overwhelmingly prone to high non-coverage. The Level-1 error variance (σ 2 e ) was most susceptible to non-coverage when the Level-1 sample sizes were low (n = 5), and the random intercept (τ 10 ) was prone to high non-coverage when the ICC was low (0.1) for models where the set of fixed effects explain relatively higher proportion of variance, as well as in models where the ICC and Level-1 sample size are low.

There is however again a disconnect between the quantitative results and the visualizations of the individual condition cells. For instance, Table 10 shows significant main effects of all conditions on Level-1 error variance, but the plots did not indicate any stark apparent differences between cells.

Summary of Extension

Taken together, the extension results reveal a clear divide between fixed effects and variance components. Fixed effect estimates remained largely unbiased across conditions, even as model complexity increased, though modest elevations appeared in the most complex specifications. Variance components were far more fragile: the random intercept variance showed pronounced bias under low ICC conditions, random slope variances were severely biased when Level-1 sample sizes were small, and imbalance amplified these problems. Confidence interval coverage followed the same pattern, with acceptable coverage for variance components generally requiring at least Level-2 and Level-1 sample sizes in excess of 100 and 5, respectively.

Discussion

The present study replicated and extended Maas and Hox's (2005) influential simulation on sample size requirements for multilevel models. We replicated their core findings: relative bias for fixed effects was negligible across most conditions, and the largest threats to coverage were observed for variance components, particularly random slope variances, when the number of Level-2 units was small. These results reinforce the original recommendation that Level-2 sample sizes of at least 50 are generally needed to obtain unbiased parameter estimates for variance components, but suggest that accurate confidence intervals require larger sample sizes (in excess of N = 100).

Our extension addressed two broad gaps in the original work: the use of more Relative Bias. Across the extended design, relative bias remained close to zero for most Level-1 and Level-2 fixed effects. While bias was slightly elevated in various instances involving more complex models (e.g., those with more than one Level-1 predictor and cross-level interaction), values tended to remain below |10%|. This calls into question how much of a substantive impact such biases would have across common research use cases in psychology, which often emphasize significance testing over precise estimation. In other words, the fact that complex models may be nominally more biased in their parameter estimation may have little appreciable impact given how these models are used in psychology. With that said, for certain research implications (e.g., precision modeling), these biases may be enough to substantively affect conclusions.

Serious bias problems (>|10|%) emerged primarily for variance components under certain conditions. Random intercept variance (τ 00 ) was especially biased when ICC was low (0.1), and all random slope variances (τ 11 , τ²2 , τ 33 ) were highly biased when Level-1 sample sizes were small (n = 5). Imbalance in Level-1 units increased bias for most parameters, but effects were most pronounced for the variance components. These patterns suggest that parameter recovery for variance components is more sensitive to sample size structure and ICC than for fixed effects, particularly when estimating multiple random slopes. Consequently, variance-component estimates under small-sample or imbalanced conditions are unlikely to represent reliable features of the population data-generating process. This tracks with prior studies (Cools, den Noortgate, & Onghena, 2009; Lee & Hong, 2021), though it is worth noting that those prior investigations emphasize the importance of sample size and ICC over imbalance. Researchers with a strong interest in studying variance components should thus ensure they have large Level-2 sample sizes and avoid extreme case imbalance.

Confidence Interval Non-Coverage.

Main-effects analyses of non-coverage revealed that fixed effects were generally well-covered, with only modest increases above the nominal 5% non-coverage rate (typically less than 7-8%) under specific conditions. Cross-level interactions were more sensitive to variance explained by fixed effects, but visual inspection of non-coverage plots indicated small absolute differences between conditions. In contrast, variance components showed consistently high non-coverage. Random slope variances, in particular, exhibited substantial non-coverage inflation under low n, high imbalance, or low ICC. The Level-1 error variance (σ 2 e ) was most affected by small n, though ICC, N, and imbalance also played significant roles. This underscores a growing notion in the multilevel modeling literature that increasingly complicated modeling specifications are subject to various considerations that are idiosyncratic to individual models and datasets. On the flip side, however, these results also underscore the fact that the overall model itself-i.e., every single model component-does not become uniformly worse with increasing complexity.

More broadly, these results converge with and extend a growing body of methodological work on sample-size planning and parameter estimation for multilevel models. First, these data highlight that Level-2 sample size and extreme case imbalance are the key determinants of variance-component accuracy and CI coverage (Westfall et al., 2014; Murayama et al., 2022). Second, they emphasize that models with cross-level interactions and multiple random slopes demand a balance of information at both levels to achieve stable estimation (Judd et al., 2017; Mathieu et al., 2012). Finally, our results highlight that interactions among model specifications, design elements, and population parameters (e.g., ICC) can produce complex, non-linear effects on bias and non-coverage rates (Astivia et al., 2019).

Taken together, these converging lines of evidence suggest a set of broad motifs, rather than strict guidelines, for future best practices. First, bias and non-coverage tend to be higher when both the number of Level-2 units and the within-unit sample sizes are small; no single "magic number" (such as N = 50) guarantees stability across modeling contexts. Second, model complexity increases the inaccuracy of the model estimates, particularly variance components. Third, while fixed-effect estimates are relatively robust, variance components remain fragile under realistic imbalances, reinforcing the need for replication, sensitivity analyses, and transparent reporting of ICCs and random-effect variances and covariances. Critically, these motifs concern only the lowest bar of accurate parameter estimation and confidence interval coverage, as they do not directly address statistical power. The sample sizes needed to reliably recover population parameters are thus a floor, not a ceiling, and researchers should expect that achieving adequate power to detect effects of interest may require considerably larger samples than those needed to ensure estimation accuracy.

Recommended Guidelines for Future Studies.

The present findings resist simple translation into universal rules of thumb, and we caution against interpreting them as such.

The extension reveals that sample size adequacy is highly context-dependent: the same N and n that yield problematic variance-component coverage under low ICC and high imbalance may perform acceptably when the ICC is large and the design is balanced. This is particularly relevant for longitudinal and growth-curve applications, which tend to exhibit substantially higher ICCs than those examined in Maas and Hox (2005) and in the extension, contexts in which our results suggest considerably more optimistic performance than a blanket rule would imply. Rather than prescribing minimum sample sizes, we encourage researchers to use tools like the mlmpower package (Enders et al., 2023) to conduct prospective, model-specific power analyses that incorporate their anticipated ICC, effect sizes expressed as variance explained, and degree of anticipated imbalance. Balanced designs should be preferred whenever feasible, and researchers with a primary interest in variance components (particularly multiple random slopes) should be especially attentive to both Level-1 and Level-2 sample size.

We also recommend that researchers report their model outputs more thoroughly, specifically following the guidelines specified by McCoach (2019). Foremost, this means researchers ought to clearly report their Level-1 and Level-2 sample sizes. In instances where imbalance exists, they should take care to detail the distribution of Level-1 sample sizes, including the mean, standard deviation, and range of Level-1 sample sizes (and include visualizations if possible). Researchers should then report the ICC, full variance components and degrees of freedom. If possible researchers should include an appropriately notated model equation of the model and code snippets in manuscripts or supplements to help ensure that others unambiguously understand how the reported model was specified.

Limitations & Future Directions. Two deviations from our preregistered analysis plan merit consideration. First, identification issues in high-complexity, low n, imbalanced designs required dropping a subset of conditions; this may mean that our results underrepresent the most extreme modeling challenges. Second, the volume and complexity of interaction effects in the planned logistic regressions proved impractical to interpret meaningfully, leading us to focus on main effects supplemented by cell-level visualizations.

While these changes do not affect the central conclusions, they limit our ability to comment on nuanced interaction patterns among design factors.

Future work could address these limitations by developing more targeted simulations of problematic parameter combinations (e.g., multiple random slopes with high imbalance) and by examining how correlations between random slopes and intercepts influence bias and coverage.

Extending our approach described here would also be helpful, specifically to generalized linear mixed models, multilevel structural equation models, and three-level designs would further align simulation evidence with contemporary applied practice.

In the same vein, we note our use of Wald confidence intervals as a limitation that could be remedied in follow-up studies by leverage other approaches such as resampling methods or profile confidence intervals. A final consideration worth raising for future simulation researchers concerns the specification of plausible parameter values. Simulations of multilevel models, whether for substantive use cases or for empirical quantitative research, have historically specified fixed effect coefficients and variance components in isolation, without carefully considering their combined implications. This matters because often the implied R² values and proportion of variance explained by various model components that follow from a given parameter configuration may be unrealistic relative to what is observed in actual data. A simulation that specifies individually plausible coefficients can nonetheless imply an overall model that explains an implausibly large proportion of outcome variance. We therefore encourage future simulators to explicitly derive and report the implied variance-explained quantities associated with their chosen parameter values -as we attempted to do in our extension via the framework of Rights and Sterba (2019) -and to benchmark those values against empirical estimates from the relevant literature. Doing so would help ensure that simulation conditions reflect the data-generating processes researchers actually encounter in practice, and would strengthen the generalizability of recommendations derived from such work.

Conclusion.

Overall, our literature review indicates that many published studies employ models more complex than those evaluated in Maas and Hox (2005) and do so under different conditions (e.g., imbalanced designs, greater ICC values), yet still rely on the original N = 50 guideline. Our extension suggests that continued reliance on their original recommendations may underestimate the sample sizes needed to ensure accurate variance component estimation and coverage. Fixed effect parameters for relatively complex models (those involving multiple Level-1 slopes and cross-level interactions) are at risk of modest increases in parameter bias, but in ways that are dependent on modeling application.
