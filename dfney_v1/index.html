About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

A flag means look at this column. Where the data is already de-identified (an ip column reading Anonymized, a name column the authors emptied), no action is needed.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Momentary emotional experience is shaped by the contexts in which individuals are embedded, yet little is known about how time spent in certain locations (e.g., at home versus in public spaces) relates to affect in daily life. In the current study, 74 young adults completed 14 days of smartphone-based ecological momentary assessment and GPS-based geolocation. We examined three contextual dimensions in relation to momentary affect (happiness, anxiety, and sadness) in young adults: environmental context (at home versus outside), recent exposure duration (time spent at home in the preceding three hours), and temporal context (time of day and day of week).

Multilevel models indicated that participants reported higher happiness and lower anxiety in the evening relative to morning, and higher happiness on weekends than on weekdays. Within individuals, being at home (and time spent at home) was associated with lower happiness than being outside the home. Furthermore, the exploratory analysis suggested that the association between time spent at home and lower happiness was stronger among individuals who typically spent less time at home. These findings suggest that temporal and environmental contexts are associated with momentary happiness, whereas environmental context was not associated with anxiety or sadness.

Affect fluctuates dynamically in daily life (Koval et al., 2013; Kuppens et al., 2010; Li et al., 2026) and plays a central role in psychological well-being (Posner et al., 2005; Trull et al., 2015). These fluctuations are shaped by the contexts in which individuals are embedded, including where they spend their time and for how long, and when within daily and weekly rhythms those exposures occur. Young adulthood is characterized by elevated loneliness and a particularly vulnerable developmental window for the onset of psychological disorders (Li et al., 2025; Pedrelli et al., 2015; Shah & Househ, 2023; Storrie et al., 2010). This period involves significant life transitions, including changes in social networks, academic pressures, and evolving autonomy (Kohler Giancola et al., 2009; Saleh et al., 2017); these circumstances may amplify the influence of daily contexts, such as social engagement, isolation, and activity patterns, on emotional well-being. Examining how environmental context shapes momentary affect during this life stage may therefore provide insight into how daily contexts influence emotional experiences.

Recent advances in digital assessment methods have expanded researchers' ability to study psychological processes in naturalistic settings. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) allows for repeated, real-time measurement of individuals' affect, behavior, and context, providing insights into within-person dynamics as they unfold in daily life (Shiffman et al., 2008). EMA studies have demonstrated that emotions vary within individuals over time, reflecting both internal regulation processes and changing environmental contexts (Chen et al., 2024 (Chen et al., , 2026;; Ebner-Priemer & Trull, 2009; Trull et al., 2015). These within-person fluctuations are clinically meaningful; for example, intraindividual variability in negative affect is associated with future depressive symptoms (Wichers et al., 2010), as well as the onset of bipolar spectrum disorders over time (Sperry et al., 2020).

Digital phenotyping leverages passive data collected from personal digital devices to capture indicators of movement, app usage, communication (text messages and calling), and geolocation, without requiring active participant input and thereby reducing participant burden (Langener et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023; Torous et al., 2016). When combined with EMA, passive sensing enables researchers to examine how contextual data correspond to individuals' momentary emotional experiences in real time, allowing fine-grained analyses of how daily environments shape affect as it unfolds (Fulford & Jacobson, 2025; Langener et al., 2024; Torous et al., 2016).

Among passive sensing indicators, GPS-based geolocation data (e.g., time spent at home, distance traveled, and number of locations visited) have been widely used to examine associations between mobility and mental health outcomes (Auerbach et al., 2022; Barnett & Onnela, 2020; Filip et al., 2025; Fulford et al., 2021). Specifically, reduced mobilitycharacterized by shorter distances traveled, fewer locations visited, less variability in locations visited (i.e., entropy), and greater time spent at home-has been linked to greater depression symptoms, stress, and negative affect (Auerbach et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2024; Filip et al., 2025).

Similarly, broader evidence suggests that spending more time at home is associated with greater negative symptom burden and functional impairment among individuals with serious mental illness (Filip et al., 2025). To date, digital phenotyping studies have typically examined geolocation data aggregated at the daily or weekly level, such as average time spent at home or total daily movement. Although informative, these summary indicators may overlook potentially important temporal dynamics: short exposure windows may capture acute contextual influences on affect, whereas longer windows may reflect sustained behavioral patterns such as prolonged social engagement or withdrawal. Additionally, little is known about how recent, short-term environmental exposure, measured over brief windows aligned with EMA prompts, relates to momentary affect. For example, two days with the same total time at home or the same number of locations visited can still look very different hour by hour: on one day a participant may have just returned home before an afternoon prompt, whereas on another day the participant may have been at home for the entire afternoon. Daily or weekly aggregates cannot distinguish these situations, yet they may correspond to distinct emotional experiences at the moment affect is assessed. Evaluating affect across different timescales may therefore clarify how spatial context relates to dynamics of emotional experience.

The goal of the present study was to evaluate the impact of three related but conceptually distinct contexts on affect in daily life. The environmental context is where an individual is located at a given moment. In the present study, we operationalized environmental context as being at home versus outside the home. Recent exposure duration is the time an individual spent in that environment. In the present study, we operationalized this as the time spent at home (versus outside) within the 3-hour window preceding each EMA report, reflecting recent environmental exposure between prompts. The temporal context is when that exposure occurs within broader daily rhythms, particularly the time of day (e.g., morning vs. evening) and the day of the week (e.g., weekday vs weekend). Although these contextual factors are interrelated, each may influence emotional experience through distinct mechanisms.

Environmental Context and Affective Experience

Environmental context may influence emotional experience through two primary pathways: social (opportunities for interpersonal contact) and non-social (in out-of-home settings through drivers such as exposure to natural environments). Time spent outside the home may increase opportunities for and variation in face-to-face interaction. Extended time at home, for example, particularly when spent alone, may limit opportunities for social interaction, which has been associated with increased loneliness (Kuczynski et al., 2022; Sugiyama et al., 2008; Yanos et al., 2001). For young adults whose social lives are often structured by work or school, prolonged residence at home could reflect social disengagement, potentially increasing risk for negative affect and associated mental health concerns. In addition, exposure to varied environments outside the home, including natural settings such as parks and green spaces, has been associated with higher positive affect (Houlden et al., 2018; Kangarloo et al., 2023). This pattern is theoretically grounded in Attention Restoration Theory (R. Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989; S. Kaplan, 1995) and Stress Recovery Theory (Ulrich et al., 1991), both of which hold that natural and varied environments replenish attentional resources and reduce negative arousal, creating conditions more conducive to positive affect (Felsten, 2009; Houlden et al., 2018). In contrast, the home environment may reduce arousal and novelty-seeking, contributing to disengagement when exposure is prolonged. For college students in particular, the home environment is often a functionally constrained space, frequently a dormitory room or shared apartment, with limited capacity for varied activities, making prolonged time at home especially likely to dampen positive affective states and potentially increase low-grade negative states such as boredom (Eastwood et al., 2012).

Both the social and environmental pathways suggest that being at home, relative to being outside the home, may be associated with lower high-arousal positive affect and, with prolonged exposure, potentially higher negative states such as boredom. It bears noting, however, that these expectations apply most clearly to high-arousal positive affect such as excitement or surprise (McManus et al., 2024; Russell, 1980; Swerdlow et al., 2022) 1 . Temporal Context and Affective Experience

Emotional experience is also shaped by temporal context, including both time of day and daily and weekly rhythms. Research consistently demonstrates systematic diurnal patterns in affect. In large population-based samples of adults, individuals tend to report the highest wellbeing and positive affect earlier in the day, with declines toward the evening, while negative affect and distress show corresponding increases, peaking later at night (Bu et al., 2025). These patterns likely reflect interactions among circadian processes, physiological rhythms, nutrition (e.g., caffeine or meal consumption), and the structured demands of daily activities such as work, study, and social engagement (Cardinali, 2000; Clark et al., 1989; Golder & Macy, 2011). These patterns may vary across populations, however; in young adults and college students, daytime hours structured around academic demands may shift peaks in positive affect toward the evening, when obligations recede and social opportunities increase. Weekly rhythms also influence emotional experience. Compared with weekdays, weekends are often associated with higher positive affect and lower negative affect, a pattern attributed to greater autonomy and increased opportunities for social interaction during non-obligatory days. For young adults, this distinction may be particularly salient because weekdays are typically structured around work or academic responsibilities, whereas weekends allow greater flexibility in social and recreational activities (Adams et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2024).

Environmental and temporal contexts are closely intertwined in daily life. Individuals differ in their daily routines, shaped by work schedules, lifestyles, and personal habits, yet a general pattern holds that people tend to spend more time at home during early-morning and latenight hours and more time outside during daytime hours. Studies examining environmental context alone may therefore inadvertently confound location with time of day or week. More importantly, time of day may moderate the psychological impact of variation in environmental context on affect. For example, time spent at home on a weekday afternoon may reflect disengagement from academic or social activities, whereas the same amount of time at home on a weekend evening may reflect intentional leisure or rest. From an appraisal perspective, the emotional significance of a situation depends on how it relates to an individual's goals and expectations, which themselves vary across daily rhythms (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2002; Li & Chen, 2026; Smith & Kirby, 2009). Examining environmental exposure without considering time of day may therefore obscure meaningful heterogeneity in how contexts shape momentary emotional experiences.

The Present Study

By examining the current environmental context (at home versus outside the home), time spent in that environment over the past three hours, and variation across daily and weekly rhythms, we aimed to clarify how spatial and temporal contexts shape momentary emotional experience. Our approach aligns geolocation-based indicators with the temporal scale at which momentary affect is assessed, allowing us to examine how short-term environmental exposure relates to emotional experience in daily life. We also explored the extent to which time of day and day of the week moderated associations between spatial contexts and momentary affective outcomes. Given the context-dependent nature of time spent at home, we did not impose directional hypotheses regarding moderation effects.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Procedure

Data were collected as part of the Digital Phenotyping of Isolation and Loneliness (DPhIL) study (See more about the parent study at https://osf.io/42r3w). We recruited 120 English-speaking young adults through the psychology research participant pool at Boston University and through study advertisements posted in the Greater Boston metropolitan area between September 2021 and December 2022. Eligibility required owning a smartphone and willingness to install the study app. Participants completed a baseline assessment followed by a 14-day EMA period and provided written informed consent prior to participation. Among all participants, 113 completed the EMA phase or provided at least some EMA data. Each EMA prompt was administered a minimum of 90 minutes apart throughout each day at pseudorandom time points within 8-11 AM, 12-3 PM, 4-7 PM, and 8-11 PM blocks. Ethical approval was granted by the Boston University Charles River Campus Institutional Review Board (Protocol #5684E). Participants recruited through the university pool received up to four course credits; community participants received up to a $60 gift card. Compensation was awarded proportionally to EMA adherence, with maximum compensation provided to participants who completed at least 75% (42) of 56 possible surveys.

Of the 113 participants who completed the EMA phase or provided at least some EMA data, 39 were excluded from analyses: seven had no GPS data collected during the study period, three had GPS data but insufficient nighttime observations to identify a stable home location cluster, three showed systematic home misclassification on GPS validation (100% false negative rate with ≥ 5 EMA-reported home observations), and 26 had GPS data too sparse to estimate hourly mobility, so the time-spent-at-home measure could not be computed for them. These 26 participants did have a valid at-home classification, which is derived separately from raw GPS, and their agreement with self-reported location was comparable to that of the retained participants (86.5% with them included versus 86.7% without; see Passive Geolocation). We nonetheless excluded them from all analyses so that every model was estimated on the same 74 participants, which allows the models with and without time spent at home to be compared directly. The final sample therefore comprised 74 young adults (mean age = 21.68 years, SD = 6.32). About half of the participants identified as female (54.05%). The sample was racially and ethnically diverse: 47.30% Asian, 32.43% White, 5.41% Black, and 9.46% Multiracial; 10.81% of participants identified as Hispanic or Latine (reported as an overlapping ethnic category).

Nearly all participants were undergraduate or graduate students (93.24%). See Table 1 for full demographic information. To assess the robustness of the final analytic sample, we compared the participants that were excluded based on GPS data quality on demographic characteristics (Supplementary Table S2). Excluded participants did not differ on gender, race, ethnicity, education level, or number of completed EMA prompts (all p values > .05), but they were younger (p = .031). The 74 participants completed 2,773 prompts (M = 37.47 of 56), and 515 (18.6%) had no GPS fix within 15 minutes and were excluded, leaving 2,258 prompts for the athome models. Hourly estimates were unavailable for a further 163 of these prompts (7.2%), so the time-spent-at-home models used 2,095 prompts for happiness (2,088 for anxiety and 2,093 for sadness).

Measures

Passive Geolocation

The GPS data were collected through the Beiwe app using a burst-sampling approach: the GPS sensor was activated every 15 minutes for 90 consecutive seconds (on-cycle), during which location coordinates were recorded approximately every second when the device was in motion. This approach was intended to minimize battery consumption while maintaining the ability to determine participant movement patterns and behavior throughout the day (Onnela et al., 2021; Straczkiewicz et al., 2021; Torous et al., 2016).

Momentary Affect

Participants reported their momentary emotional experiences at each prompt, including positive affect (Right now, how happy do you feel? ; between-person Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) = .36) and negative affect (Right now, how anxious do you feel? ; ICC = .43; Right now, how sad do you feel? ; ICC = .40), using a 4-point Likert scale from "Not at all" to "Very."

Statistical Analysis

We used the Jasmine package (https://forest.beiwe.org/en/latest/jasmine.html) from the Forest Python library (Onnela et al., 2021; https://github.com/onnela-lab/forest) to impute missing data using a bidirectional sparse online Gaussian process approach and to characterize the resulting mobility trajectories from GPS data (Liu & Onnela, 2021). Specifically, we used the software package to compute hourly estimates of time spent at home based on inferred daily mobility trajectories. We then calculated the time spent at home in minutes (range: 0-180) during the 3-hour period preceding each EMA prompt. This window was selected to align with the EMA sampling design, in which participants reported on their current affect approximately every three hours. This operationalization allows for the examination of how short-term cumulative time spent at home relates to momentary affect. These analyses were not preregistered.

In addition, because Jasmine does not readily generate a binary indicator of whether participants were at home at the exact time of each EMA prompt, we implemented a data-driven clustering approach to estimate participants' home locations from raw, unprocessed GPS data.

Specifically, we used the density-based spatial clustering package DBSCAN to extract and cluster nighttime GPS observations (8 PM to 8 AM) within each participant's EMA study window. The largest spatial cluster was defined as the participant's home location, and the home centroid was computed as the median latitude and longitude of this cluster. We defined a participant-specific radius using the 95 th percentile of distances from cluster points to the centroid, capped at 200 meters to reduce the influence of GPS noise. We then classified each EMA prompt as occurring at home versus outside the home based on the nearest GPS observation within a ±15-minute window and its distance from the home centroid.

The GPS-derived home indicator was validated against participants' self-reported location at each EMA prompt. Agreement between the GPS-based classification and EMA selfreport was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy. The GPS-based indicator demonstrated good agreement with self-report (sensitivity = 87.3%, specificity = 85.8%, PPV = 88.7%, NPV = 84.1%, accuracy = 86.7%). Agreement was similar before any participant was excluded on the basis of GPS quality (accuracy = 84.9% across all 103 participants for whom a home location could be detected), indicating that the exclusions did not inflate the agreement estimate. The high PPV indicates that when the GPS algorithm classified a participant as being at home, this classification was highly reliable. A small number of participants for whom the algorithm consistently failed to detect home were excluded from analyses. Exclusion required both a 100% false negative rate and a minimum of five EMA-reported home observations, a threshold chosen to parallel the minimum cluster density parameter (minPts = 5) used in the DBSCAN home detection algorithm, ensuring that exclusions reflected systematic algorithm failure rather than insufficient opportunity for home detection. Time of day was categorized based on EMA sampling blocks (morning: 8 AM to 11:59 AM; midday: 12 PM to 3:59 PM; afternoon: 4 PM to 7:59 PM; evening: after 8 PM), and day of week was coded as weekday versus weekend. These variables were included to examine temporal context and to test whether associations between time spent at home (at home versus outside) and affect varied across daily and weekly rhythms.

We used multilevel linear models with EMA prompts (Level 1) nested within individuals (Level 2). Because prompts were also nested within study days, we examined a three-level null model (prompts within days within persons); the day-level intraclass correlation was modest (.14 for happiness, .18 for anxiety, and .19 for sadness). We therefore retained the two-level specification for the primary analyses and re-estimated all substantive models with a three-level random-intercept structure (see Robustness Analyses below). Separate models were fit for each affective outcome (happiness, anxiety, and sadness). For models examining time spent at home, predictors were decomposed into within-person (WP) and between-person (BP) components.

The within-person component represented deviations from each participant's own mean level of time spent at home at a given prompt, whereas the between-person component represented each participant's average time spent at home across the study period. Temporal Context and Momentary Affect

Time of day and day of week were differentially associated with the three affective outcomes (See Table 2, Temporal Context-only Model column for each outcome and Figure 1).

Participants reported significantly higher happiness in the evening (β = 0.20, SE = 0.04, p < .001) and at midday (β = 0.10, SE = 0.04, p = .023) relative to morning.

Happiness in the afternoon did not differ significantly from the morning (p = .150). Happiness was also higher on weekends than on weekdays (β = 0.09, SE = 0.03, p = .011). Participants reported lower anxiety in the evening relative to morning (β = -0.08, SE = 0.04, p = .038), but anxiety showed no significant associations with midday, afternoon, or weekend (all ps > .05). Neither time of day nor weekend was significantly associated with momentary differences in sadness (all ps > .05).

Environmental Context (At Home versus Outside) and Momentary Affect

In the unadjusted, within-person model, participants reported significantly lower momentary happiness when at home versus outside the home (β = -0.130, SE = 0.032, p < .001).

When temporal predictors were added (See Table 2, Temporal + At-Home Model column for each outcome and Figure 1), the within-person at home versus outside effect remained significant (β = -0.158, SE = 0.032, p < .001), and the model fit significantly better than the temporal predictors-only model (χ²(2) = 24.88, p < .001), indicating that being at home versus outside improved model fit beyond temporal context. Because this likelihood ratio test jointly evaluates WP and BP at-home terms, we note that the BP term was not significant (see Table 2); the improvement in fit therefore reflects the WP association. Reports of anxiety and sadness were unrelated to being at home versus outside the home in unadjusted models (anxiety: p = .550; sadness: p = .354), and adding at home versus outside to the temporal context-only model did not improve fit for either outcome (anxiety: χ²(2) = 2.42, p = .298; sadness: χ²(2) = 2.31, p = .315).

More time spent at home in the preceding three hours was associated with significantly lower momentary happiness (β = -0.0008, SE = 0.0003, p = .004). Discussion

In the present study, we examined how temporal context, environmental context (at home versus outside), and recent exposure duration (time spent at home) related to momentary emotional experiences in young adults' daily lives. Using a combination of EMA and passive GPS sensing data over 14 days, we found that spatial and temporal context were more consistently associated with variations in happiness than with negative affect in this young adult sample. These findings extend digital phenotyping research by suggesting that GPS-derived environmental indicators relate to momentary emotional experience at the time when affect is assessed and that where and when people spend time are correlates of positive emotional experience in daily life.

The pattern of diurnal and weekly variation in affect was partly consistent with prior findings. Participants reported greater happiness in the evening and at midday relative to morning, and on weekends relative to weekdays. They also reported lower anxiety in the evening relative to the morning. The evening peak in happiness is consistent with evidence of systematic diurnal variation in positive affect (Clark et al., 1989). The direction of this variation, however, is inconsistent with that observed in large population-based samples, in which positive affect is highest in the morning and declines across the day (Bu et al., 2025; Golder & Macy, 2011). For young adults in particular, whose mornings are often characterized by occupational and academic obligations across multiple life domains, along with fatigue following disrupted sleep (Adams et al., 2017; Dettmers et al., 2020; Kohler Giancola et al., 2009; Lu et al., 2024), the evening may represent a period of leisure and social engagement that promotes positive affect (e.g., less likely to work or attend classes in the evening). The weekend happiness effect is consistent with research linking days free of structured obligations with increased opportunity for social interaction and reduced academic and work stress (Adams et al., 2017; Ryan et al., 2010; Saleh et al., 2017). In contrast, anxiety did not differ between weekdays and weekends, which may reflect the stable nature of stress and obligation in young adulthood: occupational responsibilities, academic work, and interpersonal obligations do not fully recede on weekends for many young adults (Dettmers et al., 2020; Ryan et al., 2010). It is also likely that distributional constraints limited sensitivity in this sample: most participants reported consistently low levels of anxiety (M = 1.64, SD = 0.85) and sadness (M = 1.41, SD = 0.71), with happiness (M = 2.72, SD = 0.86) falling closer to the scale midpoint and showing greater variability.

Participants also reported lower happiness when at home than when outside of the home at the within-person level. Time outside the home provides greater opportunities for social interaction, which is associated with improved mood and reduced loneliness (Kuczynski et al., 2022). For young adults whose social lives are often structured around academic/work and other obligations, being outside the home likely corresponds to greater engagement with peers and social environments. Being outside also affords individuals greater access to varied and potentially restorative settings (e.g., natural environments and green spaces) that have been linked to replenishment of attentional resources and promotion of well-being (Houlden et al., 2018; Kangarloo et al., 2023; S. Kaplan, 1995). The fact that this association persisted after accounting for temporal context suggests that environmental context was associated with happiness independently of when these exposures occurred. Of note is that the GPS-based home classification, while validated against self-report with acceptable accuracy (overall accuracy = 86.7%), is an approximation; some EMA prompts may have been misclassified, and the precision of the ±15-minute GPS window may introduce noise, particularly during transition periods.

Additionally, being at home versus outside was not associated with anxiety or sadness.

One possibility is that the contextual factors examined here are insufficient to produce detectable within-person shifts in negative affect, which may require more psychologically potent triggers such as interpersonal conflict, academic failure, or illness. Future work with samples showing greater affective variability would provide a stronger test of whether environmental context is associated with negative affect.

Within-person analysis results indicated that when individuals spent more time at home in the three hours preceding an EMA prompt (relative to their own typical level of home exposure), they reported lower happiness. These associations are distinct from between-person differences: the absence of significant between-person effects for both at home versus outside and time spent at home indicates that individuals who habitually spent more time at home did not differ in average happiness from those who spent less. Rather, it is the momentary deviation from one's own typical environmental exposure pattern, being at home more than usual on a given occasion, that is associated with lower happiness in that moment. This within-person specificity suggests that the association cannot be attributed to stable between-person differences in lifestyle or routine. However, this association does not rule out time-varying confounding or reverse directionality; for example, individuals may stay home more on occasions when they are already feeling less happy. Unmeasured time-varying factors, including social engagement and sleep quality, may account for part of these associations, and future studies should consider multivariate passive sensing models that jointly model geolocation-derived indicators alongside social and activity data. Additionally, the three-hour duration window was selected to align with the EMA sampling interval; shorter windows might capture more proximal associations while longer windows might better index sustained exposure patterns, and examining associations across multiple timescales would clarify the temporal grain at which home exposure is most strongly associated with affective experiences. However, although the three-hour exposure window preceded each affect rating, affect during the exposure window itself was not assessed, so lower happiness may have preceded and contributed to remaining at home. This caveat has particular relevance for the duration measure, which aggregates exposure over the preceding hours, whereas the concurrent at-home indicator captures location at the moment of assessment; neither measure supports causal inference.

Exploratory cross-level interactions tested whether these within-person associations varied as a function of participants' typical level of home exposure. The within-person association between momentary home presence and affect generally did not depend on how much time participants typically spent at home. In contrast, the within-person association between more time spent at home and lower happiness was stronger among individuals who typically spent less time at home. Spending an unusually home-heavy few hours was associated with a larger reduction in happiness. However, this cross-level interaction analysis was exploratory and was estimated without a random slope for the within-person term, so it should be treated as a hypothesis for future work. If replicated, the pattern would be broadly consistent with evidence that day-to-day variety in the places people visit is associated with higher positive affect (Heller et al., 2020) and that being kept at home is harder on people who are usually out and about (Giuntella et al., 2021; Wijngaards et al., 2020). The cross-level interaction was not significant for anxiety or sadness, consistent with the absence of main effects for those outcomes.

In addition, happiness measured by a single item may not capture the full landscape of positive emotional experience, including distinctions in arousal (Russell, 1980). Within the circumplex model of affect (Posner et al., 2005; Russell, 1980), positive states vary along both valence and arousal dimensions. The contextual factors examined here may be more closely tied to higher-arousal positive experiences such as social excitement and engagement (McManus et al., 2024; Swerdlow et al., 2022). In contrast, home environments and extended time at home may support low-arousal positive states such as calmness and contentment that a single happiness item would not distinguish. All associations with happiness reported here should therefore be interpreted as pertaining to the specific positive state captured by this item rather than to positive emotional experience broadly, and studies assessing affect across both valence and arousal dimensions are needed.

Exploratory analyses did not support a model whereby the association between location and affect varied as a function of time of day or day of week. It is possible that with a larger sample, power to detect small interaction effects would be sufficient for moderation to emerge. It is also possible that moderation would be more pronounced in populations for whom the psychological meaning of home time differs across temporal contexts. For example, moderation might be more detectable among individuals with clearly demarcated work and leisure schedules, for whom being home on a weekday evening versus a weekend evening carries distinct implications for social opportunity and role obligation. Alternatively, the absence of moderation may indicate that the association between home exposure and momentary happiness is relatively consistent across daily and weekly rhythms.

Conclusions

This study suggests that environmental contexts (i.e., at home versus outside and time spent at home) are associated with momentary happiness over and above temporal context in daily life, with young adults reporting lower happiness when at home and when they have spent more time at home in the preceding hours. These associations were observed for happiness but not for anxiety or sadness and operated at the within-person level rather than between-person, a pattern consistent with, though not demonstrative of, dynamic context-affect coupling in real time. The absence of associations with negative affect may point to differential contextsensitivity across the positive and negative dimensions of affect, although this possibility was not tested directly here. Taken together, these findings suggest that where and when people spend time are associated with momentary positive emotional experience, and that integrating EMA with passive GPS sensing offers a productive path toward understanding how the spatial and temporal structure of daily life relates to emotional experiences.
