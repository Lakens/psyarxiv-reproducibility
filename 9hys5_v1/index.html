Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

This study was not pre-registered. The data presented in this study may be made available upon request from the corresponding author. The data are not publicly available due to the potential for personal identification of participants in the present sensitive population.

Introduction

Social exclusion is a powerful social stressor because acceptance and belonging are fundamental human needs (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Even brief experiences of exclusion can produce immediate changes in affect, perceived social regard, and satisfaction of fundamental psychological needs, including belonging, control, self-esteem, and meaningful existence (Gerber & Wheeler, 2009; Nida & Williams, 2017; Richman & Leary, 2009; Williams, 2009; Zadro et al., 2004). These responses are particularly important during adolescence, a developmental period characterized by heightened sensitivity to peer evaluation, social acceptance, and affiliation (Blakemore & Mills, 2014; Crone & Dahl, 2012; Somerville, 2013).

Prior research has demonstrated that social exclusion influences adolescents' mood, satisfaction of fundamental psychological needs, and neural systems involved in social evaluation and distress (Masten et al., 2009; Ruggieri et al., 2013; Sebastian et al., 2010). Consequently, adolescence represents a critical developmental period for investigating how young people perceive and respond to social exclusion.

Among adolescents, understanding responses to social exclusion may be particularly important in youth with conduct disorder (CD), a common and highly impairing neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent patterns of aggressive, antisocial, and rule-breaking behavior that disrupt social, academic, and occupational functioning (Fairchild et al., 2019). Interpersonal difficulties are prominent among adolescents with CD, who experience disproportionately high rates of peer rejection, victimization, and relationship instability (Baumann et al., 2023; Girard et al., 2024). Importantly, these social experiences are likely to be reciprocal, as peer rejection can both contribute to and result from aggressive and antisocial behavior across development (Dodge et al., 2003; Girard et al., 2024; Robertson et al., 2023).

Although substantial progress has been made in understanding the neurocognitive, emotional, and developmental characteristics of youth with CD (Blair, 2013; Blair & Zhang, 2020; Frick et al., 2014; Frick & Kemp, 2021), CD is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in its clinical presentation and similar behavioral manifestations may not reflect uniform cognitive and emotional functioning across affected youth (Fairchild et al., 2019; Kohls et al., 2020). Despite advances in characterizing differences in emotion processing and related neurocognitive functions, comparatively little is known about how adolescents with CD subjectively experience and respond to socially meaningful interpersonal interactions. Social exclusion provides a particularly informative interpersonal context for addressing this gap because it directly challenges processes that are fundamental to peer relationships such as social acceptance and belonging. Understanding how adolescents with CD perceive and affectively respond to varying experiences of social exclusion may therefore provide important insight into the social-affective processes underlying their interpersonal functioning.

Addressing this gap requires experimental paradigms capable of systematically creating experiences of social exclusion under controlled conditions. To date, research on social exclusion has relied predominantly on the Cyberball paradigm (Williams et al., 2000; Williams & Jarvis, 2006), in which participants believe they are playing an online ball-tossing game with other individuals who are experimentally programmed to include or exclude them. Owing to its brevity, standardization, experimental control, and ease of implementation, Cyberball has become the most widely used experimental paradigm for investigating social exclusion (Vanhollebeke et al., 2023; Williams & Jarvis, 2006). Across child and adolescent clinical and community samples, Cyberball research has consistently demonstrated that even brief experiences of exclusion threaten fundamental psychological needs including belonging, control, self-esteem, and meaningful existence, and influence emotional, cognitive, and behavioral responses to social interactions (Hartgerink et al., 2015; Masten et al., 2009; Sebastian et al., 2010; Vanhollebeke et al., 2023; Williams, 2009). Despite this extensive evidence base, investigations of social exclusion in adolescents with CD remain comparatively limited.

Consequently, it remains unclear whether established paradigms can effectively characterize how adolescents with CD perceive and respond to social exclusion or distinguish responses across varying degrees of exclusion.

More broadly, extending experimental paradigms for social exclusion provides opportunities for developmentally appropriate and ecologically valid assessment of multiple dimensions of social-affective functioning. This emphasis is consistent with recommendations from the National Institute of mental Health's (NIMH) National Advisory Mental Health Council (NAMHC) Workgroup on the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework (Cuthbert & Insel, 2013; Insel et al., 2010; Morris et al., 2022), which identified rejection sensitivity as a priority construct within the Social Processes domain and emphasized the importance of developing ecologically valid experimental paradigms capable of measuring dimensional variation in social processes across development (NAMHC Workgroup, 2016). Although Cyberball was identified as a state-of-the-art paradigm for assessing rejection sensitivity, the NAMHC Workgroup ( 2016)also identified opportunities for its continued development, including greater dimensionality of outcomes, further evaluation across developmental and participant groups, and greater standardization, while more broadly emphasizing the need for measures of social processes that optimize ecological validity.

Building on these priorities, recent advances in immersive virtual reality (VR) offer a promising approach for developing experimental paradigms that measure multidimensional aspects of social-affective functioning within ecologically meaningful social interactions. Unlike traditional computerized programs, immersive VR places participants within dynamic, reciprocal social environments that more closely approximate everyday peer interactions while maintaining experimental control. Traditional Cyberball affords substantial experimental control but provides a more limited approximation of the perceptual immersion and social presence characteristic of everyday peer interactions. Thus, immersive VR offers a complementary approach that preserves experimental control while increasing the ecological validity of experimentally induced social interactions. Emerging evidence suggests that immersive VR paradigms can reliably elicit psychological and emotional responses to social exclusion similar to those observed with Cyberball, while providing greater realism and social presence (De Luca et al., 2026; Stallmann et al., 2023). Importantly, VR also provides an opportunity to extend how social exclusion itself is experimentally represented. Although Cyberball permits variation in the degree of social inclusion and exclusion, much of the literature has focused on the effects of ostracism relative to inclusion (Hartgerink et al., 2015). Yet exclusion in everyday interpersonal interactions can vary in degree, from relatively subtle reductions in social engagement to more overt exclusion.

Examining responses across varying levels of exclusion can clarify whether different dimensions of social-affective functioning change progressively with increasing social exclusion, emerge only at more pronounced levels of exclusion, or show distinct patterns of sensitivity across response domains. VR provides the experimental flexibility to model varying levels of exclusion while assessing multiple dimensions of social-affective responding within an immersive interpersonal context.

To address the need for ecologically valid experimental paradigms for assessing multidimensional social-affective responses across varying levels of social exclusion, we developed Interact VR, an immersive VR paradigm adapted from Cyberball (Williams et al., 2000; Williams & Jarvis, 2006). Building on prior applications of immersive VR to social exclusion (e.g., De Luca et al., 2026; Stallmann et al., 2023), Interact VR integrates an immersive Cyberball-inspired peer interaction with systematically varied levels of social exclusion and repeated within-person assessment across multiple dimensions of social-affective responding. Specifically, Interact VR retains Cyberball's experimental control over peer inclusion and exclusion while placing adolescents in a dynamic virtual peer environment and assessing perceived social regard, fundamental psychological needs, affective responses, and discrete emotions following each experimental condition.

The present study sought to provide initial validation evidence for Interact VR in adolescents with CD by examining whether social-affective responses varied systematically with experimentally varied levels of social exclusion. In so doing, we sought to characterize how youth with CD subjectively respond across multiple social-affective domains as exclusion became increasingly pronounced. Specifically, we tested four hypotheses. First, increasing exclusion severity was expected to be associated with lower perceived social regard, reflected in reduced perceptions of being liked by the virtual peers and lower liking of the virtual peers.

Second, increasing exclusion severity was expected to be associated with greater threat to belonging and meaningful existence. Third, greater exclusion severity was expected to be associated with increasingly negative affective responses, reflected by lower affective valence and perceived control, whereas arousal was examined separately given its less predictable role during social stress. Finally, increasing exclusion severity was expected to be associated with greater likelihood of experiencing sadness, anger, and disgust, lower likelihood of happiness.

Fear and surprise were examined as exploratory discrete emotions because theory and prior evidence provide less specific directional expectations for these responses in this context.

Method Participants

The sample comprised 262 adolescents diagnosed with CD who were enrolled in a larger NIMH-funded longitudinal study (R01MH123535: PI Thomson). Participants ranged in age from 13 to 17 years (Mage = 15.75, SD = 1.28). The sample was predominantly male (63.40%), with participants identifying as African American (57%), White (35%), American Indian or Alaska Native (1%), Asian (1%), or multiracial (6%). Participants received $160 for their participation.

All study procedures were approved by the Virginia Commonwealth University Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Feeling Liked by and Liking the Avatars

Perceived social regard was assessed using four items assessing (a) how much participants thought Alex and Riley liked them and (b) how much participants liked Alex and Riley. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). Combining the two items demonstrated high internal reliability (via Spearman-Brown split-half correlations) during all three task conditions (Table 1).

Adapted Cyberball Need-Threat Scale

To assess changes in perceived belonging, competence, and meaningful existence following each Interact VR condition, participants completed an adapted version of the Cyberball Need-Threat Scale (NTS; Williams & Jarvis, 2006). The adapted measure consisted of 12 items assessing the fundamental psychological needs of belonging (e.g., "I felt rejected," "I felt excluded by the other players"), competence (e.g., "I felt bad about myself," "I felt dissatisfied with how I played"), and meaningful existence (e.g., "I felt that I didn't matter," "I felt that it didn't make a difference that I was there"), with four items assessing each construct. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely), with higher scores indicating greater threat to the corresponding psychological need. Sum scores were calculated separately for each subscale. All three subscales demonstrated good internal consistency via Cronbach's alpha across the Interact VR conditions (Table 1).

Emotions

To assess emotional responses following each Interact VR condition, participants selected emotions that best described how they felt after each condition. Emotions included sadness, anger, surprise, fear, disgust, happiness. Each emotion was scored dichotomously (0 = not endorsed,1 = endorsed) and analyzed separately.

Subjective Emoji Affect Scale

To assess changes in subjective affect following each Interact VR condition, participants compelled the Subjective Emoji Affect Scale (SEAS; Kjærvik & Thomson, 2026). The SEAS is a brief graphical self-report measure designed to assess three dimensions of affective experience: affective valence, subjective arousal, and perceived control. The measure uses developmentally appropriate emoji-style visual anchors accompanied by brief text descriptors to facilitate selfreport in adolescent samples. Participants rated each dimension using bi-polar 10-point visual scales immediately following each condition (e.g., "During the game, how did you feel from 1 (Simley & Happy), to 10 (Frowning & Unhappy)?"). Higher valence scores indicated more negative affective valence, higher arousal scores indicated greater subjective arousal, and higher control scores indicated greater perceived control.

Procedure

Participants were recruited by trained research coordinators from a Level 1 Trauma Center in Virginia. Adolescents who met study eligibility criteria and expressed interest in participating were scheduled for an in-person laboratory assessment. Upon arrival, written informed assent and consent were obtained from adolescents and caregivers in separate rooms prior to participation. Participants were fitted with a VR headset and completed a brief orientation to the virtual environment during which they were asked to describe the surroundings to confirm adequate visualization and familiarize themselves with the immersive environment before the experimental task began. The research team simultaneously observed the VR display on an external monitor to ensure proper task presentation and recording throughout the session.

Participants first completed a 3-minute control condition in which they were instructed to sit quietly and relax while viewing a virtual room containing a fish tank. Next, participants completed Interact VR, an immersive social interaction paradigm adapted from the Cyberball task (Williams & Jarvis, 2006). Within the three virtual environment conditions, participants believed they were playing a ball-tossing game with two other human participants, represented by computer-controlled avatars named Alex and Riley. During each condition, participants tossed and received an "orb blast" with the avatars according to the programmed interaction sequence (Figure 1). The experimental manipulation involved systematically varying the number of orb blasts received by participants while keeping all other aspects of the interaction constant. Participants completed three experimental conditions representing varying levels of social inclusion. During the inclusion condition, participants received five orb tosses from the other players. During the subtle exclusion condition, participants received four orb tosses, and during the severe exclusion condition, participants received only one orb toss. The inclusion condition was always administered first to establish expectations of reciprocal interaction before participants experienced exclusion. Presenting the inclusion condition first ensured that subsequent exclusion conditions represented a deviation from an established interaction pattern rather than an initially unfamiliar social environment. The subtle and severe exclusion conditions were subsequently presented in randomized order to minimize order effects. Each condition lasted approximately 3 minutes. Immediately following each condition, participants completed self-report measures assessing perceived social regard (i.e., how much they liked the virtual players and how much they believed the virtual players liked them), belonging, competence, meaningful existence, valence, arousal, control, and six emotions. Data Analysis

To examine whether perceived social regard, belonging, competence, meaningful existence, and affective responses differed across the three Interact VR conditions, separate linear mixed-effects models were estimated for each continuous outcome, including feeling liked by the avatars, liking the avatars, belonging, competence, meaningful existence, valence, arousal, and control. Condition was entered into model as a fixed within-subjects effect and participant intercepts were allowed to vary randomly. Linear mixed-effects models were selected because they account for the within-subject dependence of repeated observations while accommodating incomplete repeated-measures data through maximum likelihood estimation. Significant omnibus effects were followed by Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means. Raw scores were analyzed rather than change scores because the primary objective was to compare psychological responses across the three experimentally manipulated conditions. We also examined a multilevel logistic regression model that estimated the effect of each condition on the probability of reporting each of the six emotions we measured. Exploratory contrasts of emotion probabilities were tested using the Benjamin-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction. Analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2025).

Discussion

The present study provides initial validation evidence for Interact VR, an immersive VR paradigm designed to examine multidimensional social-affective responses across varying levels of social exclusion in adolescents with CD. Across three within-person conditions, increasing exclusion produced systematic changes across multiple domains of social-affective functioning.

Most outcomes, including feeling liked by the other 'players', belonging, meaningful existence, valence, control, and discrete emotions, distinguished inclusion, subtle exclusion, and severe exclusion, demonstrating strong condition sensitivity and construct-consistent response patterns.

However, not all domains responded uniformly. Competence threat only differentiated severe exclusion from the other conditions, arousal decreased rather than increased as exclusion intensified, and the likelihood of experiencing fear and surprise did not vary by condition.

Together, these findings suggest that Interact VR captures expected and theoretically meaningful patterns of responding to varying levels of social exclusion, providing initial support for its use as an ecologically valid, experimentally controlled paradigm for investigating multidimensional social-affective functioning.

One of the principal findings of the present study was that adolescents with CD systematically distinguished among inclusion, subtle exclusion, and severe exclusion across multiple domains of social-affective functioning. The effect of Interact VR condition was graded by the degree of exclusion which evoked lower perceptions of being liked by the task partners, reduced liking of the task partners, and greater threat to belonging, meaningful existence, and competence needs supporting evidence that even brief experiences of social exclusion disrupt fundamental psychological needs and alter interpersonal experiences (Hartgerink et al., 2015; Nida & Williams, 2017; Williams & Jarvis, 2006; Zadro et al., 2004). Importantly, these effects emerged not only during severe exclusion but also during subtle exclusion, suggesting that relatively modest reductions in reciprocal social interaction were sufficient to alter adolescents' perceptions of their social experiences. This sensitivity to varying levels of exclusion is particularly noteworthy because day-to-day peer interactions often involve subtle rather than overt forms of exclusion. Although previous immersive VR paradigms have demonstrated that ostracism effects can be elicited in immersive virtual environments (Kassner et al., 2012), and have been used to investigate peer-exclusion, emotion regulation, and social support in children and adolescents (De Luca et al., 2026; Stallmann et al., 2023), the present findings extend this literature in two important ways.

First, rather than relying on a traditional inclusion-versus-exclusion comparison, Interact

VR provides a means of examining sensitivity to varied levels of social exclusion, allowing investigators to distinguish responses to subtle and severe exclusion within a single immersive paradigm. Second, by applying this paradigm in a clinically relevant sample, the present findings provide new insight into how adolescents with CD subjectively experience and interpret socially meaningful exclusion, an area that has received comparatively little experimental attention.

Together, these findings highlight the importance of paradigms capable of detecting responses to both subtle and overt forms of social exclusion. By providing an ecologically valid platform for investigating multidimensional social-affective functioning, Interact VR offers a novel approach for examining the subjective experiences of adolescents with CD. In addition, the consistent differences between the subtle exclusion condition and the inclusion and severe inclusion conditions suggest some incremental improvement over the traditional task as studies applying

Cyberball with the three-condition design of the current study produce inconsistent, and often null, differences between subtle (sometime referred to as ambiguous rejection) and severe exclusion conditions (e.g., Chernyak et al., 2010; Sandstrom et al., 2017). The emotional profile evoked by the Interact VR conditions was also consistent with the broader social exclusion literature.

The pattern of discrete emotional responses further supports the construct validity of Interact VR as a paradigm for eliciting meaningful emotional responses to social exclusion. As social exclusion increased, participants had a progressively greater probability of reporting sadness, anger, and disgust, alongside reduced happiness, demonstrating that increasing social exclusion elicited distinct patterns of emotional responding. This pattern is consistent with theoretical models of social rejection, which propose that threats to social acceptance and belonging evoke differentiated emotional responses rather than a unitary experience of distress (Leary et al., 2006; Richman & Leary, 2009). Our findings are also consistent with empirical evidence indicating that Cyberball rejection elicits the greatest state emotion increases in anger and decreases in positive affect (e.g., Peterson et al., 2011; Widiastuti et al., 2026). We also found that the likelihood of reporting fear or surprised remained unchanged despite increasing severity of exclusion. These findings are generally consistent with work suggesting that social rejection does not consistently effect fear or surprise, although the published evidence comparing these specific emotions across conditions of experimentally manipulated social rejection is sparse (e.g., Arango-de-Montis et al., 2024; Lawrence et al., 2011). Interact VR thus appears to effectively evoke and capture the emotional consequences of social exclusion, rather than simply inducing a broad negative affect. These discrete emotional responses provide important insight into how adolescents with CD subjectively experienced varying levels of social exclusion, representing an important component of social-affective functioning.

One curious pattern of findings was the divergent pattern observed across the three dimensions of affective experience. As social exclusion intensified, adolescents with CD reported increasingly negative affective valence and reduced perceived control but lower subjective arousal. Theoretical models of ostracism predict that increasingly aversive experiences of social exclusion evoke progressively stronger emotional reactions (Richman & Leary, 2009; Williams, 2007). In contrast, participants in our study had a greater probability of reporting emotions that comprise high levels of arousal (e.g., anger, disgust) yet reported lower levels of subjective arousal following rejection. This pattern of results is likely an artifact of how we measured subjective arousal. We asked participants to report subjective arousal following each condition on a scale of 1 (Relaxed & Calm) to 10 (Very Excited). The framing of this question however may have been interpreted by participants as asking them how excited they were to continue playing with the 'partners' that had just rejected them. This interpretation is consistent with our finding that the inclusion condition had the highest level of our self-reported arousal measure, which may not have been well understood by our young participants.

Implications for Understanding Social-Affective Functioning in CD

Because current diagnostic frameworks define CD primarily in terms of observable behavior, comparatively less is known about how adolescents with CD subjectively experience and respond to socially meaningful interpersonal interactions. The present study provides initial validation evidence that Interact VR offers a promising, ecologically valid paradigm for examining such processes, providing an experimentally controlled platform for investigating how adolescents with CD subjectively experience and respond to varying levels of social exclusion. Adolescence is characterized by heightened sensitivity to peer evaluation and social status, and experimental studies using Cyberball have consistently demonstrated that social exclusion influences adolescents' mood, psychological need satisfaction, and neural systems involved in social evaluation and distress (Masten et al., 2009; Ruggieri et al., 2013; Sebastian et al., 2010). Given the high prevalence of peer rejection and interpersonal difficulties among adolescents with CD, experimental paradigms capable of examining how these youth subjectively experience socially meaningful interpersonal interactions may provide valuable insight into the social-affective processes that contribute to persistent interpersonal difficulties.

By demonstrating systematic and theoretically coherent responses across multiple domains of social-affective functioning, the present study provides initial validation evidence that Interact VR can be used to investigate social-affective processes that have been difficult to capture using traditional behavioral paradigms. As such, Interact VR provides an experimentally controlled, standardized, and ecologically valid platform for investigating social-affective processes that cannot be inferred from behavioral symptoms alone. Importantly, CD is characterized by substantial clinical and behavioral heterogeneity (Fairchild et al., 2019). Consequently, it is unlikely that adolescents with CD exhibit a single pattern of social-affective functioning. Rather, considerable heterogeneity likely exists in how they perceive and respond to socially meaningful interactions. By providing a standardized yet ecologically valid social context, Interact VR offers a promising platform for characterizing this social-affective heterogeneity beyond observable behavioral symptoms and for determining whether distinct patterns of social-affective functioning are associated with differences in peer functioning, aggression, treatment response, or the persistence of antisocial behavior. More broadly, Interact VR aligns with the goals outlined by NIMH's RDoC framework, which emphasizes the need for experimentally controlled paradigms that provide ecologically valid, developmentally appropriate assessment of dimensional variation in social processes (Cuthbert & Insel, 2013; Morris et al., 2022; National Institute of Mental Health, 2016). Interact VR addresses these measurement priorities by systematically varying the severity of social exclusion within an immersive, standardized social environment while providing multidimensional assessment of social-affective functioning. As such, the paradigm could be integrated with behavioral, self-report, and psychophysiological measures to advance understanding of social-affective functioning in adolescents with CD.

Strengths and Limitations

The present study has several notable strengths, including the use of an immersive, experimentally controlled paradigm that systematically varied levels of social exclusion, the examination of multiple dimensions of social-affective functioning, and the focus on a clinically relevant sample of adolescents with CD. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the inclusion condition was always completed before the exclusion conditions to establish a credible social interaction prior to exclusion. Although this sequencing was necessary to establish a credible social interaction prior to exclusion and thereby enhanced the ecological plausibility of the paradigm, comparisons between inclusion and exclusion cannot be fully separated from potential order, acclimation, or carryover effects.

However, because the subtle and severe exclusion conditions were randomized, differences between these conditions are less likely to reflect sequence effects. Future studies should examine alternative sequencing approaches or directly evaluate potential order effects while preserving the progressive nature of the social interaction. Conclusion

The present study provides initial validation evidence that Interact VR is an ecologically valid paradigm for examining social-affective functioning across varying levels of social exclusion in adolescents with CD. Subtle and severe levels of exclusion across were distinguished across multiple domains of social-affective functioning, including perceived social regard, belonging, meaningful existence, discrete emotional responses, and broader affective dimensions. Together, these findings demonstrate that Interact VR captures social exclusion as a multidimensional social-affective experience, eliciting theoretically coherent patterns of responding across multiple domains of social-affective functioning. By capturing these converging patterns within a single immersive paradigm, Interact VR provides a richer characterization of adolescents' experiences of social exclusion than traditional behavioral paradigms alone. Because current diagnostic frameworks define CD primarily in terms of observable behavior, the social-affective processes underlying social dysfunction remain less understood. The present validation study demonstrates that Interact VR provides a promising, ecologically valid, and experimentally controlled platform for examining these processes, offering a means of moving beyond behavioral characterization to investigate how adolescents with CD subjectively experience and respond to social exclusion. By providing an experimentally controlled yet socially meaningful platform for investigating these processes, Interact VR lays the foundation for future research aimed at advancing the understanding of social functioning and informing more developmentally sensitive models of CD.
