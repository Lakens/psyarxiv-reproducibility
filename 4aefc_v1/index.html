Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Full text

Abstract

Associations between personality and resting brain activity have proved difficult to reproduce, leaving unresolved whether electrophysiological information is broadly absent or selectively detectable across personality dimensions. Here, we investigated this question in 254 adults undergoing clinical assessment, examining 15 personality-pattern scales of the Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory-IV using resting-state electroencephalography (EEG). We evaluated eight complementary EEG feature families across four representations of personality scores-continuous, two binary thresholds and ordinal-using nested participant-level cross-validation.

Most of the 60 scale-formulation combinations showed little predictive performance beyond chance. The strongest evidence concerned Masochistic scores dichotomized at a base-rate score of 60 (area under the curve, 0.630 and 0.594 across two modelaggregation widths), the only combination meeting the within-formulation falsediscovery-rate criterion at both widths in fixed-candidate permutation tests. Weaker evidence emerged for continuous Turbulent scores and thresholded Negativistic scores, while an ordinal Histrionic association was identified in a post hoc three-class analysis. Although particular EEG feature families recurred among better-performing configurations, the exact configurations varied across folds. These findings do not support broadly accurate prediction of clinically oriented personality dimensions from resting EEG. Instead, they reveal modest, selective associations whose detection varies across personality dimensions and their operational definitions. Whether these associations generalize, reflect enduring individual differences or provide clinically useful information remains to be established.

Introduction

Can a brief recording of the brain at rest reveal clinically relevant differences in personality? The question is compelling because personality describes relatively enduring tendencies in affect, motivation and behaviour, whereas spontaneous neural activity fluctuates from moment to moment [1, 2]. A useful physiological correlate must therefore be detectable against substantial state variation, measurement noise and overlap among psychological dimensions. Success for one personality score would not imply that resting electrophysiology can decode personality in general; failure for one EEG measure would not rule out information in others [3] [4] [5].

Evidence from resting-state EEG has so far been mixed. In 289 participants, Korjus et al. [6] found no reliable prediction of Big Five dimensions or their narrower aspects from resting spectral power. Jach et al. [7] subsequently reported multivariate spectral prediction of Agreeableness, with less consistent evidence for Neuroticism, but a preregistered replication in 772 participants did not reproduce the Agreeableness result, including analyses separating periodic and aperiodic spectral components [8]. Other work has reported selected trait associations when oscillatory and aperiodic activity are considered separately [9]. These apparently divergent findings do not establish that personality is either present or absent in resting EEG. Rather, they underscore the need to distinguish exploratory associations from reliable person-level prediction and to specify which traits, representations and validation procedures are being evaluated.

Most of this literature concerns normative traits, particularly the Big Five [4]. Clinically oriented personality dimensions constitute a different assessment problem: they capture maladaptive or clinically salient patterns in interpersonal functioning, emotional regulation, self-concept and behavioural control that are not equivalent to broad normative trait scores [10, 11]. The Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory-IV (MCMI-IV) assesses 15 personality-pattern dimensions in clinical contexts, reporting base-rate (BR) scores designed for interpretation within a broader assessment profile [12, 13]. Although their names are clinically familiar, neither a scale elevation nor a threshold-defined group is a diagnosis; each scale should be interpreted as defined in the test manual. Nonetheless, the same scale can be analysed as a continuously varying score, a lower-threshold contrast, a higher-threshold elevation or ordered score bands. These alternatives retain different information and create distinct prediction tasks.

How personality scores are defined changes the prediction question. A continuous model asks whether EEG covaries with graded differences across the observed score range. Threshold-based models instead ask whether recordings discriminate participants on either side of a chosen boundary; their apparent performance can be affected by the number and composition of participants near that boundary. Ordinal models preserve ordering while sacrificing within-category distinctions and require sufficient representation of each class. Thus, different performance across formulations need not imply different biological substrates: the models, loss functions, summary metrics and group sizes also change. Of particular importance, BR60 marks the lower benchmark of the MCMI-IV personality-style range, whereas BR75 marks a more problematic level of personality-pattern elevation [13]. Testing multiple formulations can reveal where prediction is detectable, provided their evidential status and non-equivalence are made explicit.

Related electrophysiological work has examined PID-5 anxiousness and depressivity using resting EEG, and CAT-PD pathological personality dimensions and lowerorder traits using an emotion-evoked response [14, 15]. These studies address different personality measures and recording contexts, providing relevant precedents without directly testing the MCMI-IV prediction tasks examined here. Direct EEG-MCMI evidence is limited. An early study associated elevated posterior fast-alpha activity with higher scores on the earlier MCMI Histrionic-Gregarious scale, but did not test cross-validated prediction of individuals [16]. Other early reports were small or centred on clinical-group description [17, 18]. More recently, a convolutionalnetwork study examined MCMI-IV score prediction in 30 participants; 19 of those participants are also included in the present cohort [19]. The current study extends that preliminary computational work rather than providing an independent replication, with a larger sample, multiple EEG representations and explicit held-out evaluation.

These studies motivate a held-out assessment across MCMI-IV scales, EEG representations and score formulations.

A further uncertainty concerns what is measured in the EEG. Spectral power captures only one aspect of ongoing neural activity. Lateralized power, periodic and aperiodic spectral structure, time-domain variability, signal complexity, connectivity summaries and recurrent scalp topographies characterize partly different signal properties [20] [21] [22] [23]. Their usefulness may vary across personality measures, but high-dimensional searches introduce another risk: a favourable configuration may be selected by chance, particularly when the number of candidate feature sets is large relative to the sample [24, 25]. Repeated selection of a feature family can guide subsequent hypotheses, but alone cannot demonstrate its independent physiological contribution.

Here we present MAPPER (Multidimensional Analysis of Personality Prediction from

Electroencephalographic Representations), an exploratory map of resting-EEG prediction across 15 MCMI-IV personality-pattern scales in 254 adults presenting for routine clinical care. We compare continuous, BR60 and BR75 binary, and ordinal outcomes across eight feature families, selecting configurations within nested participant-level cross-validation. Most scale-formulation combinations prove poorly predicted; a smaller set shows modest held-out associations, led by Masochistic scores at BR60. The strongest results vary with target formulation, while recurring feature families do not resolve into a single stable configuration. Rather than establishing a general EEG decoder of personality or a set of clinical biomarkers, the study defines a selective prediction landscape and identifies precise targets for independent confirmation.

Methods

Design and participants

This was a cross-sectional predictive study of resting-state EEG and MCMI-IV scores. Participants were adults who presented to the Pole-Maghz Psychology Clinic in Tehran, Iran, between April 2025 and May 2026. EEG and MCMI-IV assessments were administered to all presenting clients as part of routine clinical care and informed subsequent treatment planning. Participants were not recruited as a conventional healthy-volunteer sample, nor were they specifically selected for this study on the basis of hospitalization, a particular diagnosis, or extreme clinical presentation. However, the clinic's routine admission protocol excluded individuals presenting with impaired reality testing, including psychotic disorders and other conditions involving loss of contact with reality, as well as dissociative disorders and presentations requiring immediate intervention, such as acute suicide risk. Eligible records were identified from the clinic archive based on adulthood and the availability of valid paired EEG and MCMI-IV assessments. No additional study-specific clinical or demographic exclusion criteria were applied.

The source dataset contained 286 paired EEG-questionnaire records. During basic EEG preprocessing, 31 recordings were excluded: 19 because more than three channels met the bad-channel criterion, 7 because less than 30 s of usable recording was available, and 5 because the EDF file was corrupted. The remaining 255 recordings proceeded to epoch-level quality control, where 1 additional recording failed the duration-specific retention requirements, yielding a final analytic sample of 254 participants.

Demographic information was available for all 254 participants. Mean age was 31.60 years (SD = 9.82; range = 18-63); 176 participants were female (69.3%) and 78 were male (30.7%). Marital status was recorded as single for 144 participants (56.7%) and married for 110 (43.3%). Nineteen participants had also been included in the 30-participant analysis reported by Nosouhi and Esghaei [19], resulting in partial sample overlap. The analysis was not preregistered.

Personality assessment and prediction targets

Personality was assessed using the Persian version of the Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory-IV [26]. The MCMI-IV is a 195-item self-report inventory designed for clinical populations. It was administered in computerized format as part of the participants' psychological assessment, yielding base-rate scores for the personalitypattern scales. MCMI-IV validity screening had been performed as part of the clinical assessment procedure before dataset assembly; invalid protocols (V ≥ 2) were not included in the analytic dataset. We analyzed the 15 personality-pattern scales available in the completed dataset: Schizoid (1), Avoidant (2A), Melancholic (2B), Dependent (3), Histrionic (4A), Turbulent (4B), Narcissistic (5), Antisocial (6A), Sadistic (6B), Compulsive (7), Negativistic (8A), Masochistic (8B), Schizotypal (S), Borderline (C), and Paranoid (P). Clinical-syndrome and modifying-index scales were not prediction targets in this study.

The pipeline used base-rate (BR) scores as prediction targets without converting them into diagnoses. The planned analyses were continuous, BR60, BR75, and fourclass ordinal prediction. Because the two upper ordinal categories were sparse, the ordinal analysis was repeated post hoc using three classes. The three-class analysis is used for the main reported ordinal analysis in this study. First, the continuous task predicted the original BR score using ridge regression and was evaluated by Pearson correlation between observed and predicted scores. Second, the ordinal task assigned scores to three ordered categories: < 60, 60 -74, and ≥ 75; the original analysis used < 60, 60 -74, 75 -84, and ≥ 85. A cumulative ordinal logistic model was used, with quadratic-weighted kappa (QWK) as the primary performance metric. Third, the BR60 task classified scores as <60 versus ≥60 using a class-weighted linear support-vector classifier and was evaluated by receiver-operatingcharacteristic area under the curve (ROC AUC). Fourth, the BR75 task similarly classified scores as <75 versus ≥75 and was evaluated by ROC AUC. BR60 provided a lower-threshold binary formulation, whereas BR75 represented a higherthreshold elevation. The ordinal formulations retained the ordered threshold structure of the BR scores, while continuous prediction retained the full numerical score variation. These operationalizations were psychometric prediction targets rather than diagnostic classifications. All 254 participants had valid scores for all 15 target scales, although the resulting binary and ordinal class distributions varied substantially across scales. Score summaries and class distributions for all 15 scales are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

EEG acquisition

Resting-state EEG was recorded using a NeuroCAI amplifier (NeuroCAI, Iran) with 19 Ag/AgCl scalp electrodes arranged according to the international 10-20 system and referenced to physically linked ears, with the ground electrode at Fpz. The recorded channels were Fp1, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, and O2. EEG signals were sampled at 500 Hz. Acquisition filters were configured through CAICA HUB software at 0.5 Hz (high-pass), 30 Hz (lowpass), and 50 Hz (notch). Electrode-skin impedance was monitored by a technician, who manually corrected electrode contacts exceeding 10 kΩ. Recordings were acquired in CAI format and exported to EDF for analysis using the software's built-in export function. EEG was recorded during eyes-closed rest in a quiet, dimly lit room.

Basic preprocessing and quality control

EEG import, preprocessing, and independent component analysis (ICA) were implemented using MNE-Python [27]. EDF recordings were imported, participant identifiers were normalized, and the trigger channel was removed. Recordings shorter than 30 s were excluded, and a standard international 10-20 montage was assigned. Data were finite-impulse-response filtered from 0.5 to 30 Hz, notch filtered at 50 Hz, and re-referenced to the average of the EEG channels.

Channels were marked as bad when their variance was < 10⁻¹⁰ 𝑉² or exceeded five times the median channel variance. Recordings with more than three bad channels failed preprocessing; recordings with one to three bad channels underwent montagebased interpolation. Among the 254 recordings in the final analytic sample, 159 required no interpolation, 44 required interpolation of one channel, 28 of two channels, and 23 of three channels. All retained recordings contained 19 EEG channels after interpolation.

ICA was fitted using MNE-Python with max_iter="auto". The number of components was set to the largest feasible decomposition and reduced sequentially if fitting failed, from 19 to a minimum of 15 components. Because no dedicated electrooculography channel was available, ocular components were identified using an available frontal EEG proxy, preferentially Fp1 and then Fp2, with the find_bads_eog threshold set to 3. ICA was applied in 244 recordings and skipped in 10 because no usable frontal proxy remained after bad-channel detection. Among recordings undergoing ICA, one ocular component was removed in 46, two components in 14, and none in 184. ECG-related component detection was also attempted using the CTPS method, but no ECG components were removed.

For epoch-based features, each cleaned continuous recording was separately divided into non-overlapping epochs of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 s, with no baseline correction and rejection of epochs overlapping MNE annotations. Epochs containing flat EEG activity, defined by a peak-to-peak amplitude below 1 𝜇𝑉, were rejected. Artifact rejection was then performed using AutoReject [28]. If AutoReject failed, exceeded the configured processing timeout, or retained less than the required minimum clean duration, a variance-based fallback was used. The fallback retained epochs whose log₁₀ variance fell within the median ±3.5 median absolute deviations. At least 30 s of retained EEG was required at each epoch duration, and successful processing at all five durations was required for inclusion in the final cohort. 255 recordings entered epoch-level processing and 1 failed to retain 30 s at four of the five durations. The remaining 254 recordings satisfied the minimum-duration rule at all five epoch lengths and formed the analytic cohort.

Continuous feature families-asymmetry, connectivity, entropy, and microstateswere computed directly from the basic cleaned continuous recordings rather than from concatenated artifact-rejected epochs.

EEG feature representations

Eight feature families were evaluated: 4 from duration-specific epochs and 4 from the continuous cleaned recording. All 255 nonempty subsets of the eight families were considered. The 15 subsets containing only continuous features were evaluated once, whereas the remaining 240 subsets, each containing at least one epoch-based family, were evaluated separately at epoch durations of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 s. This yielded 15 + (240 × 5) = 1,215 candidate feature configurations per prediction target.

Band power (608 features) was estimated using Welch spectra with a segment length of the shorter of the epoch duration and 2 s, with 50% overlap. Absolute power was log₁₀-transformed, and relative power was calculated relative to total 0.5-30-Hz power, for delta (0.5-4 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz), and beta (13-30 Hz). For each channel and frequency band, epoch-level values were summarized by the mean, population standard deviation, median, and interquartile range (IQR).

Hjorth features (228 features) comprised log₁₀-transformed activity and untransformed mobility and complexity for each channel and epoch, summarized by the mean, population standard deviation, median, and IQR [20]. Kurtosis (76 features) was calculated as bias-corrected Fisher excess kurtosis for each channel and epoch and summarized across epochs using the same four statistics.

Spectral parameterization (16 features) first averaged Welch spectra across epochs within each channel and fitted the 1-30-Hz spectrum using a fixed aperiodic mode and up to six peaks, peak widths of 1-8 Hz adjusted where necessary to spectral resolution, a minimum peak height of 0.05, and a relative peak threshold of 2 [23]. normalized permutation entropy at orders 3, 4, and 5 with delay 1 and at order 3 with delay 2 [29]; sample entropy with embedding dimension 2, tolerance equal to 0.2 channel standard deviations, and Chebyshev distance [30]; full-spectrum entropy; spectral entropy within the four canonical frequency bands; and Higuchi fractal dimension with kmax = 8 [31]. These 12 measures were retained separately for each of the 19 channels. Asymmetry (56 features) was calculated for seven homologous electrode pairs: Fp1/Fp2, F7/F8, F3/F4, C3/C4, T3/T4, P3/P4, and O1/O2. For each canonical frequency band, right-minus-left log₁₀ power and normalized power asymmetry were computed.

Connectivity and Graph (1,484 features) were calculated separately within each canonical frequency band after fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth filtering.

Matrices were defined by full-recording Pearson correlations between sensor time series. Features included summaries of signed and absolute edge weights and graph measures calculated after proportional thresholding at densities of .10, .20, and .30. These comprised actual density, weighted global efficiency, weighted characteristic path length, weighted local efficiency, binary transitivity, deterministic two-way spectral modularity, and both summary and channel-level measures of degree, strength, binary clustering, Onnela weighted clustering, and eigenvector centrality. Because these measures were based on zero-lag sensor-space correlations, they may reflect volume conduction and common-reference effects as well as functional coupling [32].

Microstate features (108 features) were derived independently for each participant.

At each time point, the mean across channels was removed to center the scalp topography, after which global field power (GFP) was calculated. GFP peaks were separated by at least 20 ms, with at most the strongest 2,000 peaks retained. Unitnormalized peak maps were clustered into four states using ordinary Euclidean Kmeans (50 initializations; seed 27), and all time points were subsequently backfitted according to maximum absolute spatial correlation. States were ordered within each participant according to global explained variance. Features comprised GEV, coverage, occurrence rate, and mean segment duration for each of the four states; the transition-probability matrix; and the centroid-map coefficients, yielding 108 features in total. No group-level microstate template, polarity-invariant clustering objective, or across-participant topographic alignment was used. Accordingly, the resulting state labels and map coefficients were treated as predictive features rather than canonical microstate classes [22].

Fixed outer folds and nested configuration selection

One common set of five subject-level outer folds was used across all scales, tasks, and feature configurations. Fold sizes were 51, 51, 51, 51, and 50. The foldgeneration algorithm produced 250 candidate assignments using a base seed of 27 and selected the assignment minimizing a predefined joint balance score across all Models and regularization parameters were fixed rather than tuned. Binary tasks used a class-weighted linear support-vector classifier with a linear kernel and C = 1.0; continuous decision scores were used to calculate ROC AUC. The three-class ordinal task used two cumulative binary logistic models with L2 regularization, C = 1.0, class_weight = "balanced", the lbfgs solver, and a maximum of 1,000 iterations; cumulative probabilities were monotonized before deriving three-class probabilities; the original four-class analysis used three cumulative models. Continuous scores were modeled using ridge regression with alpha = 1.0. All variance filtering and standardization occurred within the corresponding training partition.

For each configuration and inner repetition, predictions from the five validation folds were pooled to obtain out-of-fold predictions for the complete outer-training sample, from which a single task-specific primary metric was calculated. Configuration performance was defined as the mean of the repetition-level metrics: 10 repetitions for the original analyses and 20 for the final three-class ordinal rerun. The corresponding inner standard deviation described variability across repeated, overlapping resamples and was not interpreted as an independent-sample standard error. Configurations were ranked separately within each outer-fold × task × scale cell; ties favored fewer features and then the lower configuration identifier. The 20 highest-ranked configurations were retained, refitted on the complete outer-training set, and evaluated on that fold's held-out subjects. Outer-test performance was never used to rank or select configurations.

Statistical Analyses

Performance was summarized at two aggregation widths, 𝐾 = 5 (Top-5) and 𝐾 = 20 (Top-20) and both are reported in parallel. Top-5 emphasizes the most highly ranked candidates, whereas Top-20 averages across a broader selected set. The two widths use the same participants, outer folds, and inner-ranking framework and therefore constitute an aggregation-width sensitivity analysis rather than independent replications.

For each aggregation width (𝐾 = 5 and 𝐾 = 20), predictions corresponding to ranks 1 through 𝐾 were pooled across the five held-out outer folds, and the task-specific primary metric was calculated separately for each rank. The rank-mean statistic was the arithmetic mean of these 𝐾 rank-specific metrics. Because configurations were selected independently within each outer training set, a given rank could correspond to different EEG configurations across outer folds. As a secondary analysis, the retained models were combined at the participant level before calculating a single ensemble performance metric. For continuous prediction, model predictions were averaged directly. For ordinal prediction, expected class scores were averaged and converted to the nearest valid class. For binary prediction, continuous decision scores were centred relative to each model's decision threshold before averaging.

Both procedures were applied separately for Top-5 and Top-20. For example, the Top-5 rank mean averages five held-out performance values, one for each retained rank. The Top-5 ensemble instead combines those five predictions for each participant before calculating a single performance value. All reported outer-fold estimates therefore represent nested out-of-sample evaluation within the source cohort rather than external validation.

Uncertainty was estimated using 1,000 subject-level bootstrap resamples of the cross-fitted outer-test predictions. For each resample, the corresponding Top-5 and Top-20 rank-mean statistics were recalculated separately, and 95% confidence intervals were defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of each bootstrap distribution. These intervals describe participant-sampling uncertainty conditional on the fitted candidate set, fixed outer-fold assignment, and completed configurationselection procedure; they do not incorporate uncertainty from repeating the complete configuration search.

Fixed-candidate permutation p values were estimated using 1,000 label permutations for the continuous, BR60, and BR75 formulations and 100 label permutations for the final three-class ordinal formulation. For each permutation, each participant's complete 15-scale MCMI profile was permuted as a unit, thereby preserving correlations among scales. The previously inner-selected configurations contributing to the Top-5 and Top-20 summaries were then refitted using the permuted labels within each outer training set and evaluated in the corresponding held-out outer fold. The complete 1,215-configuration inner search was not repeated under each permutation. These analyses are therefore referred to as fixed-candidate permutation tests, rather than fully selection-aware nested permutation tests [33].

Unadjusted fixed-candidate permutation p values were interpreted as graded exploratory evidence rather than as a stand-alone decision rule.

Within-formulation Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate (BH-FDR) correction was applied across the 15 scales separately for each target formulation, aggregation width, and summary statistic (rank mean or ensemble). Max-statistic p values were likewise calculated separately within each formulation × aggregation-width × summary-statistic family using the largest statistic across the 15 scales in each permutation. No omnibus adjustment across all four target formulations was applied.

Effect magnitude, confidence intervals, held-out fold consistency, agreement across 𝑘 = 5 and 𝑘 = 20, class balance, and selection recurrence were considered jointly.

Discussion

Principal findings

The central result of MAPPER is a highly uneven prediction landscape. Across 15 MCMI-IV personality-pattern scales and four outcome formulations, most held-out estimates were weak or near their respective reference values (Fig. 1). A small number of scale-formulation combinations nevertheless showed concordant performance across the two retained-configuration aggregation widths. This pattern argues against treating resting EEG as a general-purpose decoder of clinically oriented personality scores, while identifying a limited set of associations that warrant closer examination.

The strength of evidence differs materially among those associations. Masochistic scores at the BR60 split showed the clearest result (rank-mean AUC 0.630 for Top-5 and 0.594 for Top-20); both estimates survived false-discovery-rate correction across the 15 scales within that formulation, and all five outer folds exceeded chance at both widths. For continuous Turbulent scores, Pearson correlations were .149 and .145, with max-statistic support at Top-20 only. BR75 Negativistic classification yielded AUCs of 0.599 and 0.585 but did not survive multiplicity correction (Table 1).

Three-class ordinal Histrionic prediction showed corrected max-statistic evidence (quadratic-weighted kappa 0.171 and 0.150); importantly, that formulation was introduced after the sparsity of the originally planned four-class outcome became apparent and remains a secondary, post hoc observation. BR60 Sadistic also met the within-formulation max-statistic criterion at both widths, but only ten participants fell below the threshold. Agreement between Top-5 and Top-20 indicates limited sensitivity to aggregation width within this analysis; it is not independent replication (Fig. 3). Equally, statistical evidence for a modest association should not be confused with useful discrimination for individual clinical decisions.

Relation to previous EEG-personality evidence

The predominance of weak prediction places these findings closer to the cautionary side of the resting-EEG personality literature than to claims of broad neural trait decoding. Korjus et al.'s null spectral prediction [6] and Fruehlinger et al.'s failed preregistered replication of the Agreeableness finding [8] illustrate the difficulty of establishing reliable resting-EEG prediction of normative personality traits. Jach et al.'s selected Agreeableness finding [7] and Pacheco et al.'s periodic-aperiodic associations [9] show that weak general performance can coexist with narrower positive findings. Together, these studies make selectivity and reproducibility the relevant points of comparison with MAPPER; they do not establish an expected effect size for clinically oriented MCMI-IV scales.

MAPPER applies this question to clinically oriented MCMI-IV outcomes and a broader EEG feature search under one participant-level framework. Differences in outcomes, samples and analyses prevent its selective findings from resolving disagreements among the normative-trait studies or overturning their null results.

Reporting weak and stronger outcomes together makes the selectivity visible and identifies targets for independent testing.

Wall et al.'s association between posterior fast-alpha activity and the earlier MCMI

Histrionic-Gregarious scale [16] provides a historical parallel to the present Histrionic result, but the two are not replications: inventory version, target definition, EEG measure, sample and analysis differ, and our ordinal grouping was revised post hoc.

The early descriptive MCMI reports [17, 18] and the preliminary MCMI-IV prediction study with partial participant overlap [19] likewise motivate this question without independently confirming the held-out findings. The psychometric structure of the MCMI also complicates a one-scale-one-braincorrelate interpretation. Personality-pattern scales share content and covary with broader dispositions, so an apparent association with one scale may partly reflect variation common to several scales or nonspecific response tendencies. In MCMI-III data, Rossi et al. [34] supported four dimensions corresponding to emotional dysregulation-stability, antagonism-compliance, extraversion-introversion and constraint-impulsivity. In a nonclinical sample, Cuevas et al. [35] also favoured a four-factor solution and found better fit for models based on non-overlapping than overlapping scales. These findings provide a psychometric precedent, but neither the instrument version nor the samples establish an equivalent factor structure for the MCMI-IV. Hierarchical frameworks such as HiTOP offer a further conceptual reason to distinguish broad liability from narrow scale-specific variance [36]. The present study did not separate these components, so it cannot determine whether its leading effects belong to the named scales specifically or to shared features of the assessment profile.

What target formulation changes-and what it does not

Interpreting EEG representation and epoch duration

The feature-selection patterns refine the descriptive picture without resolving mechanism. Asymmetry and kurtosis recurred particularly often among stronger Masochistic configurations, whereas entropy, spectral parameterization and microstates appeared more frequently for continuous Turbulent prediction; the Negativistic and Histrionic findings showed other recurring combinations. Such patterns suggest which signal properties might be useful for follow-up, but should not be called distinct physiological signatures. Families differed in their prevalence within the search space, several were included together, and exact selected configurations varied across folds. Selection frequency is therefore neither an effect estimate for an individual feature nor a demonstration of incremental predictive value. Ablation against common baselines and independent testing would be needed to distinguish uniquely informative families from correlated alternatives. These interpretive constraints are particularly relevant for sensor-space measures.

Power asymmetry cannot by itself identify an anatomical or functional lateralization mechanism; zero-lag connectivity is susceptible to volume conduction and commonreference effects [32]; and participant-specific microstate labels were not aligned to canonical scalp maps across individuals [22]. Moreover, continuous-recording and epoch-derived families followed partly different artifact-processing pathways. A family-level difference may consequently reflect feature definitions, recording properties and preprocessing as well as underlying physiological variation. The present data identify candidate representations, not neural pathways linking particular MCMI scales to specific circuits.

The preference for intermediate 3-4-s epochs is likewise best understood as an estimation result (Fig. 5). Epoch length alters the number of segments, spectral resolution, averaging, sensitivity to nonstationarity and artifact rejection, making a performance difference plausible without invoking a trait-specific neural timescale. In a resting-EEG effective-connectivity analysis, Góngora et al. [37] found that a foursecond window provided the most uniform stationary properties across channels overall. That result concerns stationarity for connectivity estimation, whereas the present result concerns predictive performance across several feature families.

Because epoch duration in MAPPER affected only the epoch-derived families, its descriptive advantage should not be interpreted as an intrinsic personality-related temporal rhythm.

Limitations

A methodological strength is that candidate configurations were ranked within the outer-training data and then evaluated on participants withheld from that ranking. This separation reduces the optimism that would arise from choosing a model and reporting its performance on the same individuals. The shared outer folds and parallel Top-5/Top-20 summaries further make the broad landscape internally comparable. However, this is still internal cross-validation from a single cohort and one outcome-balanced outer-fold assignment-not evidence of replication across samples, recording sessions or clinics.

The inferential tests also answer a narrower question than fully search-aware significance would. Label permutations refitted configurations selected under the observed labels; the complete 1,215-configuration search was not repeated for every permuted dataset. The permutation benchmark held the configurations selected using the observed labels fixed and did not account for the full configuration search. Multiplicity correction was conducted across 15 scales within each formulation, aggregation width and summary type, without a single correction spanning all formulations. Moreover, the post hoc three-class ordinal analysis used100 permutations, limiting p-value resolution. These restrictions do not erase the reported out-of-sample performance, but they make the corrected results exploratory rather than definitive population-level discoveries.

Several measurement and sampling constraints also shape interpretation. Some binary splits were highly unbalanced-for example, only ten participants fell below BR60 for Sadistic-so even apparently large AUCs may depend on very few individuals. The clinical sample came from one service, with admission exclusions and no research-available information on diagnoses, medication or referral characteristics. Those factors, together with age, sex, momentary arousal and other clinical states, could contribute to both EEG variation and questionnaire scores; incremental information beyond demographic or clinical predictors was not assessed. The 19-channel eyes-closed recordings also provide limited spatial specificity. Finally, the cross-sectional design cannot determine whether any selected signal reflects stable personality-related variation rather than recording-day state. These are important boundaries on both causal interpretation and clinical translation.

Priorities for independent testing

The immediate priority is not a larger search over model architectures, but a prospectively specified test of the limited set of promising associations. An independent clinic or recording cohort should provide a locked test sample, with score definitions, feature preprocessing, candidate-selection rules and evaluation metrics fixed in advance. BR60 Masochistic is the clearest initial benchmark, while continuous Turbulent and BR75 Negativistic are plausible secondary targets; ordinal Histrionic requires prospectively defined class boundaries and adequate representation across score ranges. Repeated EEG recordings paired with repeated MCMI-IV assessments would test whether these associations persist across sessions and help distinguish stable between-person information from recording-day state variation. In a separate extension, standardized interpersonal or emotional tasks could be compared with rest, while EEG models would be tested for added predictive value beyond demographic, clinical, cognitive and autonomic measures.

A second priority is to distinguish scale-specific from profile-level information. A model could estimate broader MCMI-IV dimensions using independent questionnaire data or only the training participants, predict those dimensions from EEG, and retain scale-specific residual variation where supported. Its performance should be compared with separate-scale models and a score-covariance baseline, so that relations among questionnaire scales are not mistaken for participant-specific information from EEG. Targeted family ablations could test whether recurrent engineered features add value to a common baseline. An EEG encoder pretrained on independent recordings, initially kept fixed and aggregated to participant-level features, could then be compared with the engineered representations under the same locked evaluation. Greater model complexity alone would not resolve weak effects, correlated outcomes or limited sample size.

Conclusions

Overall, MAPPER found little held-out predictive performance across most of the 15 MCMI-IV personality-pattern scales and four outcome formulations examined in this clinical cohort. The clearest exploratory finding concerned Masochistic scores at the base-rate threshold of 60; evidence for continuous Turbulent and thresholded Negativistic scores was weaker, and the three-category Histrionic finding arose from a post hoc analysis. The substantial landscape of weak results is as important as these modest associations: the findings identify selected targets for independent testing, but do not support broadly accurate prediction of clinically oriented personality scores from resting EEG. Progress will depend less on enlarging the search for favourable models than on specifying the psychological target, testing what the EEG adds, and demonstrating that the relationship persists in new individuals and settings.

One implication of these findings is that the apparent availability of EEG information varies with the operational definition of the psychological outcome. Graded prediction, BR60 and BR75 discrimination, and ordinal ranking retain different aspects of the same BR score distribution. A weak continuous association may coexist with modest threshold discrimination if the model primarily separates particular regions of the distribution; conversely, dichotomization can discard information available to a continuous model. These are different statistical questions, not interchangeable measures of predictive strength. In particular, AUC, Pearson correlation and weighted kappa cannot be ranked against one another as though they shared a common effect-size scale. This distinction is especially important for clinical interpretation. The BR60 split used for the strongest result is an analytical lower-threshold contrast, not an established MCMI-IV diagnostic boundary. The more familiar BR75 elevation threshold addresses a different range of the score distribution, often with substantially different class support. Because every formulation is derived from the same questionnaire scale and uses different estimators, our results do not prove that a trait has a different neural basis at different thresholds. Nor does significance in one formulation and nonsignificance in another establish a statistically tested difference between their effects. Formulation dependence here means a pattern of observed predictive performance across specified tasks; establishing differential underlying associations would require direct, appropriately controlled comparisons.
