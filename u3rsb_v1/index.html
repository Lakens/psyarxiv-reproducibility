Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Abstract

The subjective sense of mentally reliving the past is considered the key distinguishing feature of episodic memory-a neurocognitive system supporting recollection of specific life events. We present data from two pre-registered studies of musically-evoked autobiographical memory demonstrating that this subjective sense of mental reliving accompanies not just memories for specific events, but also more general autobiographical representations. Groups of 35-, 45-, and 55-year-old participants reported memories in response to musical cues that had been popular during their time in high-school, university, and early adulthood. Participants also rated the level of specificity and sense of mental time travel associated with each memory. Cumulative link mixed model analyses revealed that participants endorsed the presence of the subjective sense of mental time travel for specific, general, and lifetime period memories. Implication of reliving broader swaths of the personal past are discussed in relation to theories of episodic and autobiographical memory.

Mental Time Travel is not Unique to Episodic Memory

A familiar song, a distinct scent, or an old photograph can sometimes make the present moment give way to the past in a way that transports people to an earlier point in their life. Cognitive psychologists metaphorically refer to this subjective sense of displacement as mental time travel (MTT), which has become closely associated with the concept of episodic memory-the capacity to remember events that occurred in a specific time and place, such as a first kiss or a car accident. Episodic memory was introduced as a heuristic distinction from shared knowledge of the world (i.e., semantic memory; Tulving, 1972), however, it was subsequently developed into a theory of a distinct neurocognitive system (Tulving, 1983 (Tulving, , 2002 (Tulving, , 2005)). On this later account, episodic memory does not merely provide information about what events occurred in the past. It allows the individual to become consciously aware of a prior experience as part of their own personal past. This formulation has shaped decades of research on remembering past events. Yet, as we argue below, the theory of episodic memory has positioned the concept of MTT in a way that obscures its possible role in non-specific representations of the past (Addis & Szpunar, 2024).

In formulating the theory of episodic memory, Tulving (1983 Tulving ( , 1985) ) initially leaned on evidence from a patient (K.C.) who had lost the capacity to mentally project their self to the personal past and future but retained general knowledge of the world (Rosenbaum et al., 2005). In addition, Tulving developed the remember/know paradigm to assess whether people with intact episodic memory could remember the moment in which they were presented specific laboratory stimuli, such as words from a list, or whether they only knew that information had been presented (Gardiner, 1988; Rajaram, 1993). Indeed, experimenters now commonly ask participants to rate feelings of being projected into the past when reflecting on memories of events (Zaman et al., 2024) or probe the recapitulation of the original sensoryperceptual context assumed to underpin feelings of re-experiencing (Levine et al., 2002). Critically, these various approaches to studying the role of conscious experience in memory have mostly overlooked the possibility that MTT may also be present when people reflect on non-specific memories of their past.

Autobiographical Memory

In contrast to traditional laboratory inquiries into memory for events, research on autobiographical memory focuses on how complex, self-relevant life experiences are framed, organized, and functionally applied across the lifespan (Bluck, 2003; McAdams, 2001). Autobiographical memories span both specific and conceptual aspects of experience. According to Conway and Pleydell-Pearce (2000), knowledge about the personal past is represented hierarchically across different levels of abstraction, including lifetime periods, general events, and event-specific knowledge. Lifetime periods organize broad stretches of experience (e.g., one's time in high school), general events represent repeated experiences (e.g., daily commutes to and from school), and event-specific knowledge captures isolated elements of prior experience (e.g., receiving one's diploma). Notably, they initially conceptualized eventspecific knowledge as a summary record of sensory-perceptual processing that could, in combination with memory for general events, evoke an episodic-like memory of repeated experiences accompanied by a sense of MTT. As further testament to the broad impact of episodic theory on the field of memory research, Conway later reconceptualized event-specific knowledge as episodic memory, rendering the subjective experience of MTT to only the lowest level of the autobiographical knowledge base (Conway, 2005).

Against this backdrop, autobiographical memory provides a useful testbed for evaluating the possible role of MTT in broader representations of the personal past. If MTT is unique to episodic memory, then autobiographical representations of lifetime periods or general events should not, on their own, evoke the experience. Yet, lifetime periods and general events both locate the self in time (Addis & Tippett, 2008; Prebble et al., 2013), which invites the question: Do individuals experience a sense of MTT when reflecting on less specific representations of their personal past? As we alluded to earlier, memory researchers have not yet fully addressed such questions because of the assumption that MTT is strictly the domain of episodic memory and thus only occurs when retrieving specific events. As a departure from this tradition, we set out to investigate whether people can feel transported to a broader period or general event from their past without recollecting a specific event.

Mental Time Travel 5

Music and Memory

Music provides a way to test for the presence of MTT in broader representations of the personal past. Familiar songs are potent cues for remembering autobiographical memories from across the lifespan (e.g., Schulkind et al., 1999). Moreover, musical cues do not restrict retrieval to one level of specificity.

Music is often embedded in daily experiences, recurring activities, and extended phases of life, and musically-evoked autobiographical memories commonly remind people of routines and lifetime periods (Jakubowski & Ghosh, 2021; Janata et al., 2007; Schulkind et al., 1999; for related discussion, see Tanberg et al., 2025).

Nonetheless, surprisingly few studies have assessed the extent to which musically-evoked autobiographical memories are associated with a subjective sense of MTT (e.g., Cady et al., 2008), and only one study of which we are aware asked participants to provide ratings of MTT in response to musically-evoked memories that referred to lifetime periods, general or repeated experiences, and specific events. Ford et al. (2011) asked participants to indicate the extent to which they were able to re-live or "place themselves back" in response to memories evoked by popular songs. Two findings are noteworthy.

First, participants provided higher ratings of re-living for specific events than general events and lifetime periods, supporting the close association of MTT to episodic memory. However, participants also reported a sense of re-living general and lifetime period memories, albeit to a lesser degree, suggesting that MTT may not be unique to episodic memory.

The Present Studies

The data reported by Ford et al. (2011) hold important implications for the central tenet of episodic memory theory, yet those findings have been largely overlooked since their publication. Across two pre-registered studies (N = 370), we systematically examined, across different age groups, life stages, musical cues, and behavioural indices of MTT, whether musically-evoked memories of unique events, general events, and lifetime periods are each accompanied by a subjective sense of mentally travelling back in time.

Method

Design and Materials

Study 1 made use of a 3 (Participant age: 35, 45, or 55 years old; between subjects) x 3 (Life stage: high school, university/college, and young adulthood; within subjects) mixed design that resulted in Mental Time Travel 7 'life stages' and the memories that broadly reflect these different stages as 'lifetime period memories' to avoid using the term 'lifetime period' to refer to both the independent and dependent variable. Stimuli for each condition were excerpts of the four most popular songs (as indexed from the Billboard 100 Charts) from the year in which the participant was assumed to be living through a particular life stage (i.e., high school, university/college, and early adulthood) (for details, see Supplemental Materials). Each song was 15-seconds in length and contained the most popular melody or lyrics associated with the song, which prior research has indicated provides participants with a sufficient representation of the song as well as enough time to retrieve a memory associated with the musical cue (e.g., Janata et al., 2007; Krumhansl & Zupnick, 2013). Each life stage was within the reminiscence bump (Rubin & Berntsen, 2003; Rubin and Schulkind, 1997) to maximize the number of evoked memories, as per norms in the music and memory literature (Belfi et al., 2020).

Participants

Participants were 35-, 45-, and 55-year-old Americans who were recruited through the Prolific research platform. The pre-registered sample size was based on power analyses of previous studies of musically-evoked autobiographical memories that: (i) included age and/or life stage as design factors, (ii) included specificity and/or MTT as a dependent variable (DV), and (iii) reported relevant effect sizes. Cady et al. (2008) examined the effect of life stage on memory specificity and MTT, reporting large effect sizes for both DVs (ηp² = 0.36 and 0.18, respectively). Jakubowski et al. (2023) examined the effect of age on memory specificity, reporting a medium effect size (ηp² = 0.07). Given the novelty of the research question addressed in the present study, G*Power version 3.1 was used to estimate the required sample size using the weakest relevant effect reported in the literature, i.e., a medium effect of ηp² = 0.07. This analysis indicated that a total sample size of N = 168 (or 56 participants per group) was needed to provide sufficient power (0.95) to detect a main effect in a mixed ANOVA. To account for up to 20% attrition (i.e., participants who do not consent to include their data in the final sample and participants who do not complete the survey with usable responses), we planned to collect data from a total of 201 participants (or 67 per condition). In total, 207 participants (N35 = 69, N45 = 67, N55 = 71) participated in Mental Time Travel 8 the study. Slightly higher counts were obtained for the 35-and 55-year-old cohorts due to automated platform buffers in Prolific for active submissions. Twenty-one participants were excluded prior to analyses: 12 did not pass the training phase, 4 were excluded for not meeting US citizenship criteria, 2 were found to be in the wrong age group, 2 did not consent for their data to be included following completion of the study, and 1 did not follow task instructions. This left a final sample of 186 participants (N35 = 59, N45 = 63, and N55 = 64; for demographic details, see Supplemental Materials).

Procedure

Participants completed the study online as a Qualtrics survey that was delivered to them using the Prolific platform. To assure data authenticity, we included a CAPTCHA test, and disabled copy and paste functions to prevent participants from directly pasting content generated using AI. Additionally, participants had to pass the specificity training described below, and at least one of two attention checks.

Participants began by providing consent and completing a demographic questionnaire that included information about age, gender/sex, ethnicity, citizenship, and length of residency in the United States prior to beginning the study. We asked about United States residency as a proxy for exposure to the top US-charting songs during the life stages studied. Participants were then trained to differentiate between unique, general, and lifetime period memories (Ford et al., 2011). Specifically, participants were provided with a detailed explanation of the distinction and then asked to classify three distinct memory descriptions, each depicting either a unique, general, or lifetime period memory. Participants who failed the test were provided with an additional opportunity to re-study the distinction and then to classify a new set of three memories. Any participant who failed both attempts was dismissed from the study. This training session helped to ensure that participants who completed the study were able to clearly differentiate between unique, general, and lifetime period memories.

Participants who continued onto the study were then tasked with listening to a total of 12 musical cues presented in random order that were popular during three different life stages. For each musical cue, participants were presented with the song, the artist's name, and the title of the song for a total of 15 seconds. They were then asked to think of the first memory that came to mind in relation to the song.

They could not advance to the following page of the survey until the full time had elapsed to ensure that they had taken the time to listen to the song and think of a relevant memory. Following the presentation of each song, participants were instructed to type a description of their memory. After providing their description and before moving onto the next musical cue, participants completed a categorical rating indicating the level of specificity of the memory (i.e., unique, general, or lifetime period) and then completed a set of phenomenological ratings. For present purposes, the most relevant phenomenological ratings probed the key features of the concept of MTT: projection, re-experiencing, and re-feeling (1 = not at all;7 = very much) (Rubin et al., 2003; Rubin et al., 2019). In addition to these ratings, participants also completed additional exploratory phenomenological ratings (i.e., overall detail, perceptual detail, conceptual detail, idiosyncrasy, shared content, positive and negative affect, emotional intensity, familiarity with the song, effort, and prior thinking associated with each reported memory). Although we made no predictions in relation to these additional ratings, we present descriptive statistics in the Supplemental Materials for completeness. As we were interested in the proportion of unique, general, and lifetime period memories, as well as the extent to which memories of differing specificity would be associated with MTT, we counterbalanced whether the categorical specificity ratings were completed immediately after describing each event and before the phenomenological ratings or after completing the study and then returning to the memory descriptions to provide the categorical specificity ratings. This manipulation allowed us to ensure that providing the categorical specificity ratings during the study did not alter the likelihood of reporting unique, general, or lifetime period memories.

At the conclusion of the study, participants were asked whether they were attending high school, university, or outside of university (i.e., early adulthood) in the years corresponding to the musical cues used in the study. We note that these data turned out to be more varied than we had anticipated, likely due to the fact that many people do not follow a canonical life path (e.g., some people do not go to high school right after elementary or middle school; some people do not go to university right after high school; some people take longer than four years to graduate university; and so on). Accordingly, we did not use this data as an exclusion criterion. Rather, we assumed that various changes that people are likely Mental Time Travel 10 to experience over the course of five years (the time between songs for different life stages), such as those involving where they live, who they socialize with, where they work, and so on, would be enough to bring about distinct life stages and lifetime period memories associated with those stages. Indeed, as we elaborate on below, lifetime period memories were the most common category of memory reported in the study.

Predictions and Data Analysis

The four dependent variables of particular interest were the categorical rating of memory specificity (i.e., unique, general, or lifetime period) and the 7-point ratings of MTT phenomenology (i.e., amount of projection, re-experiencing, and re-feeling) associated with the reported memories. Note that although our preregistration stated we would use JASP to carry out data analyses, it turned out that R was better suited to handle the cumulative link mixed models (CLMMs) we used to account for the ordinal nature of the phenomenological rating data. Accordingly, all analyses were carried out using R (Version 4.5.3; R Core Team, 2022).

We anticipated that unique events would make up approximately 50% of reported memories and that memory specificity would vary as a function of age and life stage, such that increasing age and time since a particular life stage would be associated with fewer memories of unique events. The lme4 package in R (Bates et al., 2015) was used to fit a linear mixed model (LMMs) examining the proportion of specific events, with age group and life stage as fixed effects, and a random intercept per participant.

Moreover, we anticipated that MTT as indexed by ratings of projection, re-experiencing, and re-feeling would be evident at all levels of memory specificity. The ordinal package in R (Christensen, 2023) was used to compute CLMMs with memory specificity as a fixed effect and a random intercept per participant to assess the relationships between memory specificity and MTT. We ran likelihood ratio tests on the models to test the main effect of memory specificity, comparing a full model to a reduced model using the afex package in R (Singmann et al., 2022). To assess whether participants reported at least some MTT for non-specific memories, we derived exceedance probabilities from the CLMMs using the exc.prob mode in the emmeans package (Lenth & Piaskowski, 2026). Specifically, we estimated, separately for unique, Mental Time Travel 11 general, and lifetime periods memories, the model-predicted probability that a response exceeded the lowest point of the rating scale. This was done using the first ordinal threshold, separating ratings of 1 ('not at all') from ratings of 2-7. The resulting exceedance probabilities therefore represent the estimated likelihood that participants provided a rating above 1.

Residual Episodic Memories

One possible explanation as to why ratings of MTT are so prominent for general and lifetime period memories is that participants may have remembered episodic memories in the context of reflecting on routines and eras from their lives. To address this possibility, we carried out an additional analysis that was not preregistered. Specifically, we had two research assistants independently rate whether each memory description alluded to some semblance of a unique event. One rater was aware of participants' categorical memory specificity ratings, whereas the other rater was blind to these ratings. Inter-rater agreement was substantial (Cohen's κ = .62, p < .001, agreement = 85.6%). Given the high level of agreement, we used only the blind rater's coding for subsequent analyses. Of the memories that participants labeled as either general events or lifetime periods, only 18.5% were coded by the rater as alluding to some semblance of a unique event. This demonstrates that, in most cases, when participants were categorizing their memories as general events or lifetime periods, they were not thinking about any related unique events.

Interim Summary

The results demonstrate that unique events evoke a strong sense of MTT, but also that MTT is present in relation to memories of general events and lifetime periods. These data were consistent irrespective of participant age, life stage, or index of MTT. Content analyses indicated that most general and lifetime period memories did not, in fact, allude to a unique event, suggesting that MTT may play a broader role in memory than previously thought. However, it is possible that participants retrieved episodic memories in relation to general and lifetime period memories but did not mention those memories in their descriptions. To address this possibility, we conducted an additional study that followed the same procedures as Study 1, but that asked participants to indicate whether any unique events came to mind whenever they reported general and lifetime period memories.

Method

Design and Materials

The design of Study 2 was identical to that of Study 1 but made use of a novel set of musical stimuli to account for the fact that Study 2 was run approximately one year after Study 1 (for details, see Supplemental Materials).

Participants

Participants were 35-, 45-, and 55-year-old Americans recruited through the Prolific research platform. We aimed to collect the same number of participants as in Study 1 (N = 201 participants; 67 per condition). In total, 200 participants (N35 = 67, N45 = 67, N55 = 66) took part in the study. Sixteen participants were excluded prior to analyses: 5 were excluded for failing the training phase, 3 were excluded for not meeting the citizenship criterion, 3 were identified as being in the wrong age group, 4 did not comply with instructions, and 1 did not consent for their data to be included following completion of the study. This resulted in a sample of 184 participants (N35 = 62, N45 = 64, and N55 = 58; for demographic details, see Supplemental Materials).

Procedure

The procedure for Study 2 was identical to that of Study 1, with the following exceptions. First, on each trial, participants were instructed to indicate whether any unique events came to mind whenever they reported general and lifetime period memories (yes/no). Second, because the results of Study 1 indicated that the timing of the categorical specificity rating did not influence the proportion of unique events reported by participants, the categorical memory specificity ratings in Study 2 were always made immediately after participants provided the memory description.

Predictions and Data Analysis

Our predictions were identical to that of Study 1, with the following exceptions. Based on the results of Study 1, we expected that lifetime period memories, as opposed to unique events, would make up approximately 50% of reported memories. Moreover, we expected that memory specificity would vary as a function of age, but not life stage. Finally, we anticipated that MTT would be evident at all levels of memory specificity, including general and lifetime period memories for which participants reported no residual episodic memories. Our approaches to data analysis were identical to that of Study 1.

Results

Memory Specificity

For each participant, we computed the proportion of unique, general, and lifetime period memories. Consistent with our prediction, memories most often referred to lifetime periods (M = 0.49, SD = 0.25), followed by unique (M = 0.29, SD = 0.22) and general events (M = 0.22, SD = 0.22). A onesample t-test showed that the proportion of lifetime period memories did not significantly differ from 50%, t(183) = -0.59, p = .553, d = -0.04, 95% CI [0.45, 0.53], confirming that approximately half of the memories reported were lifetime period memories.

Next, we tested whether the proportion of unique events differed as a function of participant age and life stage. Unlike Study 1, the proportion of unique events did not decline with participant age, b = -0.001, t(177.7) = -0.04, p = .967, β = -0.004, 95% CI [-0.18, 0.17] (M35 = 0.30, SE35 = 0.03; M45 = 0.23, SE45 = 0.03; M55 = 0.30, SE55 = 0.03). As in Study 1, life stage did not predict the proportion of unique Mental Time Travel 17 events, b = -0.02, t(355.4) = -1.03, p = .306, β = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.20, 0.06] (high school: M = 0.32, SE = 0.02; college/university: M = 0.22, SE = 0.02; young adulthood: M = 0.29, SE = 0.02).

Residual Episodic Memories

Participants reported that approximately 25% of general events and 30% of lifetime period memories were associated with a unique memory that came to mind as they were reflecting on the routine or era from their past. In order to rule out the possibility that the presence of unique memories in the context of reflecting on general and lifetime period memories was driving the sense of MTT associated with these broader memory representations, we carried out the same MTT analyses focusing on the probability of scoring above the bottom point of the rating scale but only including the general and lifetime period memories that were not associated with any residual episodic memory. These analyses confirmed that ratings of projection, re-experiencing, and re-feeling were all significantly above the scale minimum for both general and lifetime period memories when no residual episodic memories were recalled (all ps < .001).

General Discussion

Our findings challenge prevailing assumptions about MTT that are deeply rooted in episodic theory. Whereas episodic theory situates MTT as the quintessential feature of retrieval from episodic memory (Tulving, 1983 (Tulving, , 2002 (Tulving, , 2005)), the data reported here indicate that people also commonly experience a sense of projection, re-experiencing, and re-feeling when reflecting on memories that span broader aspects of their personal past. This pattern emerged irrespective of the age of the participant, the life stage under consideration, the musical cue used to evoke the memory, the index of MTT, and whether memories for general events and lifetime periods were accompanied by residual episodic memory.

Mental Time Travel 19

While episodic theory cannot easily accommodate these findings, multidimensional models that allow mental representations to vary along constellations of features in an assumption-free manner, such as the Multidimensional Model of Mental Representations (Addis & Szpunar, 2024), posit that MTT can be characteristic of both specific and non-specific memories of the personal past (see also , Brewer, 1986; Rubin, 2019). Lifetime periods, for example, are difficult to situate in the context of the episodic-semantic distinction (Thomsen, 2015). They do not refer to specific events, but they also do not reflect general facts about the world (Tulving, 1972). Accordingly, researchers have resorted to referring to lifetime periods as examples of personal semantics that fall along a continuum between episodic and semantic memory (e.g., Renoult et al., 2012). However, such approaches perpetuate a dichotomy assuming that MTT is unique to episodic memory. As a result, researchers rarely measure whether people experience MTT when recalling lifetime periods. On the contrary, the data reported here indicate that memories of lifetime periods, and general events, constitute rich, affectively laden configurations of self-knowledge that can evoke a distinct past state of being. These data raise novel questions. For instance, consider the sense of feeling what it felt like to be oneself in the context of a past lifetime period (for relevant discussion, see Mac Cumhaill, 2020). What is the nature of this affective state? Future studies will need to delimit whether the subjective sense of refeeling is associated with particular features or combinations of features of lifetime periods, such as people and places (e.g., Thomsen, 2015; see also, Rubin et al., 2019); the extent to which it is necessary for the stimuli that evoke these feelings to be repeatedly paired with features of lifetime periods over the course of experience; and how such feelings may be abstracted over time. Prospective methodologies that track the development and evolution of lifetime periods, or at least sub-periods (see Thomsen, 2015), may represent a fruitful approach for answering such questions (e.g., Cheng et al., 2026).

Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

Although the association between MTT and memory emerged irrespective of the age, life stage, musical cue, or index of MTT under consideration, it is nonetheless important to highlight that the work presented here was carried out solely with residents of the United States. Future work will need to Mental Time Travel 20 investigate whether these findings extend to other populations (Henrich et al., 2010). Second, the nature of the cuing method will also be important to consider in future work. For instance, images, food, and other stimuli, much like music, have been shown to evoke a sense of the personal past (e.g., Jakubowski et al., 2023). Whether these various classes of cues evoke a sense of MTT in relation to specific and nonspecific aspects of the personal past remains an open question (for philosophical considerations, see Mac Cumhaill, 2020).

It will also be important to consider the functional implications of broadening the concept of MTT to include both specific and non-specific representations of the personal past. The notion that people do not simply know about their non-specific past but are also able to re-experience it invites questions as to how these phenomenological experiences support various functions that are commonly associated with autobiographical memory, including those related to identity, interactions with others, and behavior (e.g., Harris et al., 2014; for related discussion, see Stephan & Sedikides, 2024).

Conclusion

The concept of MTT has played a critical role in positioning episodic memory as a unique neurocognitive system, both in relation to the types of memory seen in other species and the various ways in which humans reflect on the past. Yet, a growing body of literature challenges the unique nature of episodic memory. Comparative psychologists continue to demonstrate the highly proficient capabilities for species other than human beings to act on the contents of memories for specific experiences (e.g., Collaro et al., 2024) and cognitive neuroscientists continue to discover strikingly similar neural correlates associated with episodic and semantic memory (e.g., Tibon et al., 2026). The data presented here suggest that the subjective sense of revisiting the past extends beyond specific events to encompass the broader rhythms and periods of our lives.
