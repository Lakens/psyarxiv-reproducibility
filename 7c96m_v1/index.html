Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

A considerable proportion of the global adult population is bilingual; however, research on aphasia and language processing remains predominantly centered on monolingual speakers, leading to reduced understanding and guidance about bilingual aphasia. Bilingual aphasia is not equivalent to monolingual aphasia, as bilinguals process each language differently from monolinguals. As such, they are likely to have different error patterns and recovery processes. Best practice for assessing bilingual individuals with aphasia requires evaluating all languages they have sufficiently acquired. Because standardized normative tools are not available for many language combinations or even for numerous individual languages, clinicians can derive necessary insights into a bilingual patient's linguistic strengths and impairments through systematic discourse sampling. Consequently, we provide best practice recommendations for assessing and treating discourse production in bilingual aphasia. We synthesize current practices in language assessment and treatment of discourse production for bilingual aphasia populations, and provide clinically applicable recommendations for selecting, administering, coding, and interpreting discourse assessments across languages. We also provide recommendations for selecting and adapting discourse interventions, treatment languages, and cross-language targets within the constraints of multilingual clinical service delivery.

1. Introduction

An ever-increasing proportion of the world population is acquiring and using more than one language variety (Bialystok et al., 2009; Grosjean, 1989). The acquisition and use of two or more languages, resulting in a state of bilingualism, has profound and typically positive effects on an individual's cognitive systems (Abutalebi et al., 2001; Alladi et al., 2013; Bialystok, 2001; Bialystok et al., 2007; Bialystok et al., 2009; Dekhtyar et al., 2020; Fabbro, 2001; Grosjean, 1989; Grosjean & Li, 2013; Perani & Abutalebi, 2015). Bilinguals do not have a separate language system for each language they speak (Byers-Heinlein, 2014; Genesee, 1989; Grosjean, 1989; Krauska & Fahey, 2025; Kroll et al., 2012), and they rely on a language external system for managing the languages they speak or comprehend, known as the language control system (Crinion et al., 2006; Green & Abutalebi, 2013). Although bilinguals are not a homogenous group (Surrain & Luk, 2019), they can be distinguished from monolinguals, resulting in some similar cognitive and linguistic effects across bilingual populations. Nevertheless, bilingual individuals vary significantly in their linguistic backgrounds; these variations include the age of second language (L2) acquisition, proficiency levels, relative usage, and language dominance (Surrain & Luk, 2019), as well as the social contexts and personal relationships they have with each language (Fishman, 1980). Further, the interaction between their languages will be affected by the similarity of their typological features (Haspelmath, 2005; Selinker, 2015; Serratrice, 2013).

When a bilingual individual acquires aphasia, a language disorder typically resulting from stroke or other brain injury, all languages may be affected due to the underlying cognitive and neural interactions between them. While current estimates vary and suggest that between half and three-quarters of adults are bilingual (Bialystok et al., 2009; Grosjean, 1989), the majority of research on aphasia, indeed language processing generally, revolves around monolingual populations (Beveridge & Bak, 2011; Kuzmina et al., 2019).

Within the field of bilingualism, many definitions are possible (Bialystok et al., 2009; Surrain & Luk, 2019). Bilinguals may include individuals labeled as simultaneous bilinguals, two first languages (2L1), sequential bilinguals, (English) language learners (ELLs), limited English proficient (LEP), multilinguals, polyglots, or heritage (language) speakers (Grosjean, 1989; Montrul, 2010), reflecting the diversity of acquisition patterns, proficiencies, usages, and social identities. In this paper, bilingual is used as an umbrella term encompassing individuals described as multilingual speakers, second language learners (L2ers), English language learners (ELLs), heritage language speakers, and related labels, which capture variation in acquisition history, proficiency, language use, and social context rather than mutually exclusive categories (Grosjean, 1989; Montrul, 2010; Surrain & Luk, 2019). In the context of bilingual aphasia, this paper defines a bilingual individual as anyone who has achieved at least an intermediate proficiency in two or more language varieties at any point in their life. This clinical definition concerns language acquisition and its potential neurocognitive consequences, rather than whether an individual identifies as bilingual (Arce Rentería et al., 2026; Wagner et al., 2022). This position is motivated by case-study evidence that a language used infrequently for years before stroke may nevertheless recover relatively well afterward (Goral et al., 2010). While this clinical definition is distinct from more common research definitions of bilingualism (e.g., the use of 2+ languages in daily life; Grosjean, 1989), it includes patients who may revert to a previously learned but out-of-use language post-stroke (Fabbro, 2001; Peñaloza & Kiran, 2019). The structures of one language are not isolated from other languages in the mind, resulting in interlanguage influence (Selinker, 2015; Serratrice, 2013). As a result, even healthy bilinguals process all of their languages differently from monolinguals, so it is not uncommon for bilinguals to make non-native-like judgments in their first or dominant language(s) (e.g., Jegerski et al., 2016). In addition, healthy bilinguals often mix their languages when speaking to listeners who comprehend their languages, a phenomenon called codeswitching or code mixing (Heredia & Altarriba, 2001; Meisel, 1994; Myers-Scotton, 2020; Toribio, 2001). This healthy phenomenon can occur across various linguistic levels including discourse, morpheme boundaries, and phoneme targets, among others. Bilinguals may also supplant words or structures from a stronger to a weaker language in situations where they are unsure what to say. While these non-native-like language processing patterns in their languages are sometimes misinterpreted as deficits, improvements to metalinguistic awareness or executive control (Altman et al., 2018; Bialystok et al., 2014; Cummins, 1978; Galambos & Hakuta, 1988; Lehtonen et al., 2018) show that bilingualism is a net positive for individuals' brains. Advocates for bilingual aphasia populations champion integrating this understanding into a translanguaging framework, which highlights evaluating communication abilities across an individual's entire linguistic repertoire rather than treating language as distinct systems (Bortz & Goral, 2025). In a recent study, Goral et al. (2025) reported that assessing language performance across all languages yields more accurate and ecologically valid reflections of an individual's communication ability. Overall, this approach recognizes that multilingual speakers strategically blend languages, and assessing this behavior can reveal preserved language strengths that might otherwise be missed. Furthermore, the translanguaging framework promotes capturing realworld, functional communication and guides treatment that is meaningful for everyday functioning. Therefore, bilingual patients, in any context, should be assessed in each language they know. However, normative assessments are not available for bilinguals in each language pair or even monolinguals in each language (Wallace et al., 2022). As such, we recommend determining strengths and limitations of bilingual people with aphasia (bPWA) in each language through discourse sampling. Discourse is defined as ''a language unit whose organization supersedes any single word or sentence'' (Olness, 2006, p. 176). Discourse production involves integrating lexical, syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic processes across contexts (Alyahya et al., 2020). Discourse samples may provide a more holistic and naturalistic assessment, making them preferable even when impairment-based normative assessments are available for bilingual populations (Alyahya, Lambon Ralph, et al., 2022; Bose et al., 2026; Dutta et al., 2025; Stark, 2019; Stark et al., 2023). Likewise, treatment of discourse may provide patients with enhanced skills for functional communication (Ansaldo & Saidi, 2014; Dipper et al., 2021; Faroqi-Shah et al., 2010).

Guidelines for assessing and/or treating discourse production for bPWA do not currently exist. Accordingly, the aim of this paper is to provide these guidelines: best practices for the identification of a bilingual individual with aphasia, and their subsequent assessment and treatment of discourse production.

2. Recommendations for identifying bilingualism in aphasia patients and their relevant bilingualism factors

At the onset of any assessment, clinicians (i.e., speech-language pathologists/therapists) must gather background information about their patient. At this time, clinicians should determine whether their patient is bilingual, as bilingual patients will have different language performance than monolingual patients. To determine if a patient is bilingual, the clinician should ask every patient (and, when appropriate, their family) whether the patient has acquired and used another language to at least an intermediate level at any point in life, including languages that are no longer used regularly, and about the contexts in which those languages were acquired and used (cf. Birdsong et al., 2012). Asking only whether someone is "bilingual," "is fluent" in another language, or uses more than one language daily is insufficient because selfidentification may not reflect their holistic language experience (Wagner et al., 2022). Bilingualism isn't a monolithic category though; if the patient is bilingual, clinicians should delve deeper to determine their patient's type of bilingualism. And while it is widely reported that knowing a patient's first or dominant language will predict recovery patterns (i.e., Ribot's or Pitres' laws), the evidence to support those claims is insufficient (Pearce, 2005), while the wider body of evidence suggests that the recovery patterns of bilinguals vary (Peñaloza & Kiran, 2019). In clinical practice, assessment of multilingual patients' languages is frequently incomplete: Norvik et al. (2022) found that 53% of surveyed Norwegian SLPs did not normally assess their multilingual patients' other languages, while Arslan et al. (2026) found substantial training gaps among SLPs working with multilingual people with aphasia internationally.

If a clinician determines that a patient is bilingual, they need to gather additional information about the patient's type of bilingualism, the languages they speak, the age at which they first began acquiring the second language(s), their pre-morbid proficiency, and their language dominance (Grosjean, 1989; Surrain & Luk, 2019). These factors are particularly important to note because they are known to affect the cortical and mental organization of bilinguals' languages and may manifest in markedly different language behaviors for different groups of bilinguals. These behavioral differences, which are not clinical findings, should be predicted by clinicians so that they are not confused with aphasia deficits.

Many predictions about bilinguals' processing can be made merely from comparing the languages' structures. There are over 7000 documented languages (Eberhard et al., 2025), with hundreds of different structures in a given language (Haspelmath et al., 2005). Languages are traditionally compared phylogenetically (i.e., how the languages relate to one another historically), but such a comparison would not be useful for clinicians in decision making, as the historical relationship between two languages does not predict whether a given language structure is the same or similar between languages. For example, knowing that English and German are historically related Germanic languages is not useful for a clinician in understanding bilinguals' processing or perception of the languages. On the other hand, if a language structure is partially overlapping between languages, a bilingual may process it at a level between that of monolingual speakers of those languages. For example, Italian-English bilinguals have been shown to produce vowels that are halfway between the acoustic qualities of English and Italian vowels (Flege et al., 2003). Beneficial overlap can occur when languages have no historical relationship. For example, Japanese and English share word forms (e.g., スーツケース "Sūtsukēsu" / 'suitcase'), called cognates, which are processed faster and more accurately than words without overlap by Japanese-English bilinguals (Hoshino & Kroll, 2008). The relationship between bilinguals' languages is further affected by their individual combination of background factors, particularly the age at which they first began to acquire their L2 known as the age of acquisition (AoA), their proficiency levels, and their usage patterns (Bialystok et al., 2009; Surrain & Luk, 2019).

Individuals who begin acquiring 2+ languages from birth, simultaneous bilinguals, may show differences in neural organization relative to bilinguals with later ages of acquisition (Perani et al., 1998; Pliatsikas et al., 2015). Since they have received language input in more than one language at an early age, the organization of their languages, mentally and cortically, may be more distinct (Kousaie et al., 2017; Roesch & Chondrogianni, 2016). Some simultaneous bilinguals produce non-native-like sentences in one or more of their languages, and may have a non-native accent in either language (Hopp & Schmid, 2013; Kupisch et al., 2014). Similarly, children who begin acquiring an L2 very early, sequential bilinguals, may have stronger neural connections between languages (Perani et al., 1998; Pliatsikas et al., 2015), but similarly may demonstrate non-native-like performance in either language. In particular, early sequential bilinguals or simultaneous bilinguals who speak a minority language at home, heritage speakers, may have limited exposure to that language elsewhere and are at risk of not acquiring lower frequency structures due to limited input or chances for conversational practice (which reinforces input) (Montrul, 2010; Polinsky & Scontras, 2020). As a result, many heritage speakers have relatively strong comprehension skills but significantly weaker production skills. This may also be the result of heritage speakers attending mainstream schools and feeling social pressure to speak only the majority language, leading them to refuse to use the minority language, further affecting their proficiency (Cummins, 1978). Relatedly, heritage speakers are often intersectionally ethno-racial minorities who face wider social bias and discrimination (Cummins, 1978; Genesee, 2006). As a result, child heritage speakers typically encounter educational barriers and obstacles leading to lower educational attainment, despite the likely higher metacognitive and metalinguistic abilities than their peers, afforded by their bilingualism. Language testing of heritage speakers may show weaker proficiency, particularly in their first language (L1), but still show crosslinguistic influence (Montrul, 2010). Nevertheless, their neurophysiological testing should position them with other bilinguals of similar acquisition timeframes (Bialystok et al., 2009; Duñabeitia et al., 2016). As a result, clinicians should consider age with context of acquisition in determining potential effects on their patient, in order to determine whether language differences are due to interlanguage influence or incomplete acquisition (which are normal bilingualism effects), or deficits resulting from aphasia.

Clearly, AoA can affect proficiency levels, since many individuals who begin acquiring a new language will not attain native-like behavioral performance. The acquisition or learning process can plateau at any stage and for a variety of reasons, even in healthy populations. For example, a child may emigrate with family to a country where one language is no longer spoken, or an adult may discontinue language classes that were progressing their skills in complex grammar structures. If a person acquires aphasia prior to attaining native-like communication skills, they clearly should not be judged the same way as native speakers. However, awareness of the acquisition process is important even when a patient is an advanced speaker in an L2. The sequence of acquisition for language structures is not always intuitive and depends on when a learner began acquiring language, as well as how similar their languages are. When someone began acquiring an L2 as a child, particularly in earlier adolescence, they may have incompletely acquired the L1, resulting in swifter L2 acquisition (Hopp & Schmid, 2013; Kupisch et al., 2014; Polinsky & Scontras, 2020). On the other hand, when someone begins acquiring a new language as an adult, they will have less difficulty with language structures that are similar between their L1 and new L2 (or possibly the same between the new L2 and other previously learned languages) (Cho & Slabakova, 2014). Structures that are completely novel as well as structures with a partial overlap across languages will be more difficult to acquire, such as the plural system in Spanish for native English speakers (Zagona, 2002). Many of the language structures that are acquired later also mirror those considered in language assessments of aphasia deficits, such as the copula 'be' in English. Similarly, some cues, like word order, which are crucial in English, may hold little value in another language. Also, cues that are not possible in English may be important to use in other languages. In some languages, the frequency of canonical structures (e.g., active voice) also varies across languages. So, clinicians must make careful consideration of which structures had (not) been (fully) acquired for determining which structures are affected by a patient's aphasia.

Relatedly, individuals with incomplete acquisition as well as those comfortable in the language, may switch between the L2 and any other language. Those with incomplete acquisition may fill gaps in word knowledge or appropriate grammatical structures by using those from another language. Still, bilinguals may use multiple languages in a discourse context when their communication partner speaks more than one of their languages as well (Ansaldo & Marcotte, 2007; Mooijman et al., 2022). Switching between languages when a patient has aphasia should be considered parasitic switching when the patient has no ability to control which language they use. This would happen when a patient's language control is affected. Language control is part of the executive control network, separate from the language network in the brain. Bilingual aphasia patients who switch may not be aware they are switching languages, indicating impaired language control (Gray & Kiran, 2016; Green & Abutalebi, 2008; Kuzmina et al., 2019). Clinicians should consider impacts on language control by assessing patients in monolingual and bilingual contexts, and/or by asking patients if they are aware of their switches in switching contexts.

To collect bilingual background factors for patients, clinicians can conduct interviews or ask family members. Validated language history questionnaires such as the Bilingual Language Profile (BLP; Birdsong et al., 2012), the Language Experience and Proficiency Questionnaire (LEAP-Q; Marian et al., 2007), and the Language and Social Background Questionnaire (LSBQ; Anderson et al., 2018) can assist clinicians in identifying bilingual language backgrounds. When validated questionnaires are available, they should be prioritized over tools without established measurement properties and supplemented as needed with informal interviews with the patient and family members. These instruments collect data on multidimensional constructs such as AoA, language use, exposure patterns, and educational history, all of which shape the dynamic nature of bilingualism. 3. Recommendations for discourse assessment in bilingual populations with aphasia

Conventional diagnostic tests are often developed for monolingual speakers; if they are not normed for bilingual populations, they may over-or under-estimate language deficits and fail to account for interlanguage influence. Language assessments such as the Western Aphasia Battery (WAB; Kertesz, 2007), the Boston Naming Test (BNT; Kaplan et al., 2001), and the Comprehensive Aphasia Test (CAT; Swinburn et al., 2004), have translated or adapted versions. Equivalence across languages requires cultural, linguistic, and psychometric adaptation rather than direct translation alone (Alyahya, 2024), however, the absence of full equivalence does not make these tools clinically useless. When no fully adapted and normed instrument is available, a translated or partially adapted measure may still provide useful structured information, provided that clinicians do not apply inappropriate norms and interpret the findings alongside discourse samples, language-history information, and other converging evidence. Continued development and norming of language-specific adaptations are needed, although comprehensive coverage of all languages and language varieties may never be feasible. Even when a traditional assessment is adapted to a larger number of language pairs, not all pairs will have been considered in the innumerable possible bilingual pairs. For example, the Bilingual Aphasia Test (BAT; Paradis, 1987) was designed to assess two languages of a bilingual person with aphasia (bPWA) equivalently and is available in 65 languages. Similarly, the Comprehensive Aphasia Test (CAT; Swinburn et al., 2004) has been adapted in various European languages. Although standardized aphasia batteries may include connected-speech components, they generally provide limited sampling across discourse tasks, languages, and interactional contexts. Additional discourse sampling can therefore provide converging evidence across tasks and languages that helps clinicians distinguish language impairment from task-, language-, and culture-related variation.

3.1 Task Selection

When a clinician selects discourse as the mode of assessment for bPWA, there are a few factors to consider. First, bPWA must be evaluated in all the languages proficiently acquired. Furthermore, the discourse tasks should elicit structures relevant to interlanguage influence and cross-linguistic generalization, as well as be culturally appropriate. Task selection should also align with the specific linguistic domains most relevant to the individual's aphasia profile, including aphasia type and severity, if known. To compare grammatical structures across languages, clinicians can use freely available resources such as the World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS) (Dryer et al., 2013). This source even allows direct comparison of 2 languages. In considering the similarities of 2 languages, clinicians should reflect on whether a given structure overlaps across more than one language, is completely different (e.g., including existing in only one language), or is partially overlapping (Cho & Slabakova, 2014). For example, in a comparison of Spanish and English, both languages are exactly the same in the base sentence structure (subject-verb-object), completely different in noun grammatical gender (English does not mark nouns by gender), and partially overlapping in noun plural structure (Spanish marks the article and adjectives with the plural marker while English only marks the noun) (Zagona, 2002). The clinician would not need to examine these overlap levels for all the possible grammar structures and features. Instead they should pay particular attention to structures that have partial overlap but are used frequently. This is because fully overlapping structures will be strongest in the minds of any bilinguals, while partially overlapping structures will have relatively weaker representations (Cho & Slabakova, 2014). The strength or weakness of those representations premorbidly depends upon the individual's age and context of acquisition, proficiency, and patterns of language use.

WALS provides an accessible starting point for identifying cross-linguistic structural similarities and differences, although coverage varies across languages and linguistic features. When information for a particular language or structure is unavailable, clinicians can supplement WALS with reference grammars or consultation with a language specialist (Dryer & Haspelmath, 2013). These structural comparisons can guide the selection of discourse tasks that elicit clinically relevant linguistic features.

Different discourse genres used with aphasia populations are commonly classified as descriptive, narrative, procedural, personal, expository, and conversational. Language performance can vary across genres and tasks (Alyahya et al., 2020; Alyahya, Lambon Ralph, et al., 2022; Bryant et al., 2016; Stark, 2019). While genres are the forms or types of discourse with specific communicative purpose (e.g., a narration, conversation), tasks are the specific activities (e.g., storytelling, picture description) or prompts (e.g., 'tell me about your day so far', 'describe this picture') used to elicit discourse. One genre of discourse can be elicited using different tasks. As discourse performance varies with genre and tasks (Stark, 2019; Stark & Fukuyama, 2021), clinicians should consider the structures of interest in selecting the genre/task.

Conversation is an important discourse paradigm because it samples language during interactive communication rather than monologic production. It can be elicited through structured prompts or sampled from unstructured interaction, and the conversation partner need not be the clinician. Depending on the clinical question, partners may include the clinician, a family member or other familiar communication partner, or another interlocutor; these contexts may provide different information about functional communication. Because conversational performance is co-constructed, clinicians should document who participated and the partner's relationship to the patient and, when direct cross-language comparison is intended, consider whether differences in partner or interactional context could contribute to performance (Leaman & Edmonds, 2019 , 2021). Additionally, clinicians should employ multiple task types to elicit naturalistic communication. Evidence supports using a combination of narrative tasks, picture descriptions, and procedural discourse (Dutta et al., 2025).

For direct cross-language comparison, the same discourse tasks should be administered in each language being assessed. Thus, if two task types are selected, each task should be administered in each language; for a patient assessed in two languages, this would yield four discourse samples. If the selected tasks do not adequately sample the relevant linguistic structures, discourse genres, or communicative contexts, additional tasks should likewise be administered in each language. When identical elicitation materials are not culturally or linguistically appropriate across languages, parallel adaptations may be necessary, and those differences should be considered when interpreting performance.

Picture description tasks should also be culturally validated, as the content of the pictures influences narrative production. What might be culturally familiar or significant to one group may not be to another (Chen et al., 2026). For example, widely used narratives such as Cinderella, featured in the AphasiaBank protocol (MacWhinney et al., 2011), may not align with the cultural knowledge or lived experiences of speakers from diverse backgrounds. When patients are unfamiliar with the content of a task, it may potentially reduce their comfort and/or willingness to produce a detailed discourse sample (Bose et al., 2021). Similarly, picture description stimuli can evoke culturally specific interpretations. Studies have shown that South Asian individuals with Alzheimer's disease, when describing the picnic scene from Western Aphasia Battery-Revised (Kertesz, 2007), interpreted the woman in the image as pouring tea rather than wine, demonstrating how cultural context shapes discourse production (Bose et al., 2021; Dutta, Mello, et al., 2024). Kong (2006) also showed that using the "Cookie Theft" picture description (Goodglass & Kaplan, 1993) for the Chinese population was problematic, as the PWA identified the kitchen scene as a living room and were unable to relate to the theme of stealing cookies from the cookie jar, which led to limited language production and reduced the validity of the task. Empirical evidence also demonstrates that culturally relevant stimuli support more complex and productive language output. For instance, Bengali speakers with and without aphasia produced longer and more complex utterances when describing familiar, realistic color images compared to standard black-and-white line drawings from traditional aphasia batteries (Mazumdar et al., 2020 (Mazumdar et al., , 2023)).

Because discourse performance may vary across tasks and genres within the same language, no single discourse sample should be assumed to represent an individual's overall discourse abilities. For bilingual speakers, performance may also differ systematically across languages because of differences in linguistic structure, cultural knowledge, prior experience with the elicitation content, and task demands. Clinicians should therefore seek converging evidence from multiple discourse tasks in each clinically relevant language and interpret apparent cross-language differences in relation to the cultural and linguistic demands of the tasks. When normed discourse materials are unavailable, culturally and linguistically adapted tasks may be necessary to obtain representative samples; however, adaptation does not itself establish normative data, psychometric properties, or cross-language equivalence. Findings from adapted tasks should consequently be interpreted with caution and corroborated with additional discourse samples, language-history information, and other available assessment evidence (Bose et al., 2021 (Bose et al., , 2025;; Dutta et al., 2024 Dutta et al., , 2025)). 3.2 Task Administration

Once appropriate discourse tasks have been selected, clinicians must consider linguistically and culturally appropriate administration of the tasks given their contextual constraints. Clinicians are additionally advised to assess discourse in both monolingual and bilingual modes to determine which context elicits more robust language production and further use this information to develop intervention strategies to facilitate communication in bPWA. When feasible, language-specific discourse samples should be collected in separate sessions and under conditions that support a relatively monolingual language mode. For example, the clinician or interpreter can interact exclusively in the target language without signaling use or knowledge of the patient's other language(s). Collecting samples from different languages in separate sessions may further reduce activation associated with immediately preceding use of another language. Cross-language responses that nevertheless occur should be documented rather than automatically treated as errors, because difficulty maintaining the target language may itself provide information about lexical retrieval or language control (Grosjean, 2001).

Bilingual-mode samples provide complementary information. Importantly, clinicians should allow bPWA to use their full linguistic repertoire during assessment, including translanguaging across languages, rather than automatically restricting responses to a single language. When the patient interacts with someone who shares their languages and is free to use their full linguistic repertoire, this approach more closely reflects how individuals communicate in real-world contexts (Bortz & Goral, 2025). This approach also allows clinicians to recognize accurate responses regardless of the language in which they are produced, which can reveal substantially different ability levels compared to single-language assessment scoring (Goral et al., 2025). Therefore, when clinically feasible and time permits, clinicians should consider collecting relatively monolingual-mode and bilingual-mode discourse samples.

Clinicians should document the conditions under which each sample was elicited, including the language(s) used by the clinician, interpreter, or communication partner; the intended language mode; whether unrestricted translanguaging was permitted; and other administration differences that could affect comparisons across samples. Any translation, adaptation, or modification of instructions, stimuli, prompts, or responses should also be documented, including who performed the translation or adaptation and what was changed.

When the clinician does not speak a language used by the patient, a trained professional interpreter is preferable when available (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association [ASHA], n.d.; Langdon & Saenz, 2016). Professional interpreters provide language-access expertise and greater independence from the patient than family members or friends; however, clinicians should not assume that interpreters are trained in aphasia, discourse analysis, linguistics, or the administration of speech-language assessments. The speech-language pathologist (SLP) should therefore brief the interpreter before the session on the purpose and procedures of the assessment, allowable prompting, and the importance of documenting any modifications made during administration, and debrief with the interpreter after the session (Alani et al., 2024; ASHA, n.d.).

When an appropriate professional cannot participate during administration, recorded discourse samples may permit subsequent consultation with another qualified language professional. Depending on the clinical question, a linguist or other professional with expertise in the relevant language may assist with transcription, segmentation, identification of grammatical and lexical structures, and language-specific interpretation; a professional translator may assist with written transcripts or translations. These professionals provide linguistic information rather than clinical interpretation, which remains the responsibility of the SLP.

When professional language support cannot be obtained, family members or other communication partners may provide necessary language assistance in addition to their important role in supplying information about premorbid and functional language use. Because they may lack relevant training and cannot be assumed to be objective, their role and any resulting limitations should be documented and considered in interpretation (ASHA, n.d.). Potential resources for locating multilingual professionals include https://find.asha.org/pro/ https://www.atanet.org/directory/ https://www.ciol.org.uk/find-a-linguist https://linguistlist.org/ and university linguistics departments worldwide for language-specific linguistic consultants.

3.3 Task Coding and Interpretation

Discourse samples should be evaluated across languages rather than in isolation (Chen et al., 2026), and multiple levels of language structures such as macrostructure (organization, coherence, and informativeness) and microstructure analyses (morphosyntactic, lexical semantic, and phonological properties) should be assessed within the discourse sample (Sung et al., 2024).

Macrostructural analysis examines how effectively an individual conveys and organizes information across utterances and the discourse as a whole (Altman & Goral, 2026). Depending on the discourse task and genre, clinicians may examine informativeness or main concepts (whether essential information is conveyed; Kong, 2011; Ross & Wertz, 1999), local coherence (relations between adjacent utterances), global or topic coherence (maintenance of the overall topic), cohesion (linguistic devices that connect information across utterances), and narrative organization when applicable (Pritchard et al., 2018). These measures help characterize whether the individual successfully communicates the intended message and organizes it in a way that is interpretable to the listener. Across a bilingual patient's languages, apparent macrostructural differences should be interpreted in relation to the task, genre, linguistic structure, culturally shaped discourse conventions, and, for conversational samples, culturally and linguistically shaped interactional norms such as turn-taking, topic management, and repair rather than automatically attributed to aphasia (Dutta et al., 2025; Pritchard et al., 2018; Stivers et al., 2009). For conversational samples, clinicians can additionally examine topic initiation, responsiveness, communicative success, and the distribution of communicative responsibility across speakers (Leaman & Edmonds, 2019 , 2021; Stivers et al., 2009).

Microstructural analysis examines linguistic production at the word and utterance/sentence levels, including phonological and lexical properties, morphological marking, and phrase/clause structure (Sung et al., 2024). Clinicians can examine phonological errors; lexical retrieval, frequency, and diversity; morphological errors such as incorrect or omitted inflections; phrase/clause structure and grammatical complexity; and utterance/sentence-level measures such as mean length of utterance. Different patterns of grammatical errors may reflect distinct stages of grammatical encoding (Fahey et al., 2025; Yeaton et al., 2025).

For bilingual discourse assessment, analyzing both macro-and microstructure levels across languages is important because it reveals whether language breakdown patterns are parallel or language-specific. Where appropriate, coding should include both absolute counts (e.g., total tokens) and proportional measures (e.g., proportion of verbs), because raw output measures may not be directly comparable across languages with different linguistic structures. For example, a polysynthetic language may encode information that is expressed by multiple words in a more analytic language within a single word (Dryer et al., 2013). Clinicians should therefore interpret language-specific features according to the structure of each language while using overlapping structures to support more direct cross-language comparisons. Parallel impaired performance on comparable structures across languages provides stronger evidence that the underlying linguistic structure is impaired and may identify appropriate treatment targets. In contrast, differential performance across languages should prompt consideration of premorbid proficiency and use, task and cultural effects, language-specific grammatical properties, and possible impairment of language control before it is interpreted as differential aphasia severity (Chen et al., 2026). The proportion of cognates (words that share form and meaning across languages) will be of particular interest, since they are easier for bilinguals to access and their relative frequency is reinforced in each language when used in either language.

When selecting discourse measures for assessment, clinicians should prioritize the measures with documented construct validity and interrater reliability. Auditory-Perceptual Rating of Connected Speech in Aphasia (APROCSA) provides a transcription-less system for rating multiple speech and language impairment features (Casilio et al., 2019). The psychometric properties most relevant to measure selection depend on its intended clinical use: measures used to characterize discourse performance should demonstrate appropriate validity and reliability, whereas measures used to evaluate treatment outcomes should additionally demonstrate adequate test-retest reliability and sensitivity to change. Literature demonstrates that not all discourse measures are equally robust; some measures are vulnerable to variations in stimulus picture or differences in language groups (Alyahya, 2024). For instance, the Arabic Discourse Assessment Tool (ADAT) includes eight discourse measures with strong psychometric properties across both neurotypical and aphasia populations. These measures include lexical information units, content information units, words per minute, discourse duration, number of different words, number of complete sentences, and proportion of open and closed class words (Alyahya, 2024). Another analysis, the main concept analyses (MCA) have the potential to measure skills that have ecological validity of communication abilities (Ross & Wertz, 1999); are sensitive to measuring change in aphasia treatment studies; and have shown good test-retest reliability (i.e., MCA is consistent across multiple administrations) (Kong, 2011). MC analyses are available in languages other than English (e.g., Dutch : Criel et al., 2021; Cantonese: Kong, 2011; Spanish: Rivera et al., 2018; Japanese: Yazu et al., 2022). A detailed list of discourse measures with their psychometric properties is given in Dutta et al. (2025). For bilingual populations, clinicians should select measures that have been validated with populations similar to their clients and that have normative data or cut-off scores. Selecting measures with documented psychometric properties across the client's languages helps with culturally and linguistically relevant interpretation. Because codeswitching may occur at either microstructural or macrostructural levels, its interpretation should be grounded in the cross-language patterns described above as well as the individual's linguistic, cultural, and interactional context. The location and linguistic unit of each switch, its grammatical integration, its discourse-pragmatic function, and its fit with the interactional context should be considered. A switch should not be treated as pathological solely because it occurs; language alternation may reflect typical bilingual practice or compensation for lexical retrieval difficulty rather than impaired language control (Mooijman et al., 2025; Neumann et al., 2017). Evidence of impaired control should instead be based on converging observations, such as lack of awareness of switching, switches that conflict with the pragmatic context, or a credible change from premorbid behavior (Gray & Kiran, 2016; Green & Abutalebi, 2008; Mooijman et al., 2025). Premorbid switching is difficult to establish because patterns are highly individual and may vary with interlocutor and setting; reports from family members or partners may provide useful context but should not be treated as definitive evidence of the individual's premorbid pattern (Ansaldo & Marcotte, 2007; Myers-Scotton, 2020). A credible increase in unintended or context-inappropriate switching, difficulty maintaining a language when the interaction supports a relatively monolingual mode, or reduced ability to modify language choice when cued may contribute evidence of impaired language control. When such evidence converges across samples and with patient or family/care-partner report, language control may itself warrant consideration as a treatment target (Gray & Kiran, 2016; Green & Abutalebi, 2008; Mooijman et al., 2025). Discourse-level analysis of switching can also reveal language-specific lexical or grammatical difficulties that are not apparent on single-word or sentence-level tasks.

Because transcription and discourse coding can be time-and resource-intensive, clinicians may use technology-supported tools to facilitate these processes. Transcription software and tools can reduce the time required to transcribe discourse samples and aid clinicians' analyses. Most software is designed for English transcriptions, but certain software, such as VEED.IO, is also freely available for transcription in other languages. Automated transcription, translation, and analysis are not available or sufficiently accurate for many languages; caution should therefore be used with any automated tool, and automated output should be checked by a proficient human speaker whenever possible (Dutta et al., 2025; Stark & Dalton, 2024). Clinicians can use the aforementioned automated tools for transcribing language samples and thus reducing transcription time; however, these tools do not eliminate the need for appropriate clinical training or language-specific verification. Automated methods are also available for analyzing the discourse samples. For example, Computerized Language Analysis (CLAN) software (detailed manuals for coding and analysis freely available at https://aphasia.talkbank.org/) can be used by clinicians for analyzing macrolinguistic variables (e.g., main concepts), and microlinguistic variables (e.g., mean length of utterance, nouns and verbs per utterance, core lexicon) across languages when used in combination with CHAT (Codes for the Human Analysis of Transcripts). CHAT is freely available with detailed instructions for use provided by the AphasiaBank (MacWhinney et al., 2011). AphasiaBank is a shared database that consists of discourse samples from speakers performing various discourse tasks across multiple languages (e.g., English, French, Mandarin, and Spanish). Moreover, clinicians can use standardized transcription-less approaches for analyzing discourse production, such as coreLex (Kim et al., 2019), and Content Word Fluency (CWF) approach (Alyahya, 2025; Alyahya, Conroy, et al., 2022). Information on automated, transcription-less discourse analysis tools for assessing microlinguistic and macrolinguistic levels is summarized in a recently published study by Stark and Dalton (2024).

In summary, best practices for bilingual discourse assessment require clinicians to (a) establish comprehensive language background information; (b) select the same culturally and linguistically appropriate discourse tasks across the languages being assessed, adding multiple task types when needed; (c) collect samples in language modes appropriate to the clinical question and document administration conditions, translations or adaptations, language support, and other modifications; (d) code macrostructural, microstructural, and multilingual discourse features using measures with psychometric properties appropriate to their intended clinical use; (e) interpret cross-language patterns in relation to linguistic structure, premorbid language experience, task and cultural factors, and language control; and (f) integrate converging evidence across available assessment measures, discourse samples, patient and care-partner report, and functional communication; no single source of evidence should determine the clinical interpretation in isolation. By adopting these evidence-based practices, clinicians can conduct accurate, culturally responsive discourse assessments that reveal the true communicative abilities of bPWA and better inform individualized intervention planning.

4. Recommendations for discourse intervention for bilingual aphasia

>Recommendations for discourse intervention for bilingual aphasia

4.1 Rationale for Targeting Discourse Outcomes

We believe that the inclusion of discourse treatment offers important benefits over other treatment paradigms. Discourse-based intervention can directly target functional communication while integrating lexical, morphological, grammatical, and discourse-pragmatic processes within the same activity. For bPWA, a particular advantage is that more than one language can be incorporated within the same communicative activity, allowing treatment to reflect natural codeswitching, translanguaging, and the language practices of the patient and communication partners. Discourse activities can also be adapted around culturally relevant topics, routines, interlocutors, and communicative goals rather than relying solely on treatment materials developed for a single language or cultural context. These features are particularly important given the limited availability of empirically evaluated aphasia treatments across the breadth of languages used by bPWA (Faroqi-Shah et al., 2010; Imaezue et al., 2025). Accordingly, discourse-focused intervention should be considered a high-priority component of treatment for bPWA, although it can be combined with impairment-based treatment when lexical, morphological, or grammatical impairments also warrant direct treatment.

4.2 Selecting Treatment Languages, Targets, and Contexts

Providing discourse-level intervention to bPWA presents unique clinical and theoretical challenges. In addition to typical considerations in discourse treatment, clinicians working with bilingual patients must identify the most appropriate language(s) for treatment, account for variation in premorbid and postmorbid proficiency across languages, and work within the availability of linguistically appropriate therapy materials and language-matched clinicians (Faroqi-Shah et al., 2010). Selection of the language(s) targeted in treatment should therefore consider the patient's preferences and everyday communication needs, the availability of language-matched clinical resources, and cross-language generalization. The patient's language preference should be an important consideration when selecting the language(s) of treatment (Edmonds & Kiran, 2006; Imaezue et al., 2025). However, meaningful choice may be constrained by the availability of clinicians, interpreters, treatment materials, and other language-specific resources, and many multilingual patients receive services in the majority language because language-matched care is not available (Faroqi-Shah et al., 2010; Imaezue et al., 2025). Cross-language generalization should always be considered in treatment planning, whether treatment is provided in one language or across multiple languages (Ansaldo & Saidi, 2014), because its magnitude and direction vary across individuals, languages, and treatment targets and should not be assumed (Conner et al., 2018; Kuzmina et al., 2019; Lerman et al., 2019). When resources permit, directly treating each clinically relevant language reduces reliance on uncertain cross-language transfer; when treatment is limited to fewer languages, clinicians should select targets with the potential for cross-language generalization and measure outcomes in untreated languages.

It is important for clinicians to identify the bPWA's language use across different contexts (e.g., home, work, community) and tailor treatment goals to context-specific functional needs. The discourse assessment should guide target selection: parallel impairment of comparable structures across languages may support targeting shared linguistic or discourselevel processes, whereas differential impairment may warrant language-specific targets or further consideration of premorbid proficiency, language use, task effects, and language control.

Words and structures with cross-linguistic overlap may be especially useful treatment targets because they may support cross-language generalization. The factors that predict crosslanguage treatment generalization are not yet well understood (Kuzmina et al., 2019; Lai et al., 2022), but clinicians can consider overlapping structures, including cognates, when clinically appropriate (Falcon & Kurland, 2010; Hameau & Köpke, 2015; Kohnert & Peterson, 2012). Cross-language effects should nevertheless be measured rather than presumed. See Sections 3.1 and 3.3 for discussion of cross-language structural overlap, language-specific features, and crosslanguage interpretation.

A few discourse intervention techniques developed for monolingual speakers have been adapted for bPWA (Boles, 1998 (Boles, , 2000;; Lai et al., 2022). In such instances, clinicians should consider specific characteristics of each language while translating and adapting a technique developed in one language for use in another. Adaptation should preserve the therapeutic mechanism while modifying linguistic targets, stimuli, prompts, and expected responses as needed for the lexical, morphological, grammatical, and discourse structures of the language being treated. Direct translation should not be assumed to produce equivalent treatment targets across languages. Clinicians should also consider cultural familiarity and communicative relevance when adapting discourse topics or materials. When comparable or overlapping structures exist across languages, these may be deliberately targeted to support cross-language generalization; when relevant structures are language-specific, treatment should be adapted to those structures rather than forcing equivalence across languages (see Sections 3.1 and 3.3).

Generally, discourse treatments rely on verbal interaction between bPWA and clinicians, which can be compromised when clinicians do not speak the language(s) used by the patient (Larkman et al., 2023). Whenever possible, treatment should be delivered by an SLP who is proficient in the target language(s). When a language-matched SLP is unavailable, multilingual service delivery can be supported through collaboration with a trained professional interpreter or a bilingual co-clinician. However, ongoing interpreter involvement across a full course of treatment may be constrained by service availability, funding, or cost. If treatment is available only in a language shared by the patient and SLP, treatment should still be provided rather than withheld; the clinician should select targets with cross-language generalization in mind and assess whether gains extend to the patient's other language(s) (Larkman et al., 2023; Miller Amberber, 2012).

In either multilingual or single-language SLP treatment, communication partners can help extend communication support into the patient's other language(s) and everyday environments outside the formal treatment setting. Family members and other regular communication partners can be trained to identify barriers and facilitators, use supportive conversation strategies, and practice those strategies in the language(s) they ordinarily use with the patient. This role supplements rather than replaces professional treatment or language access and may be particularly valuable when formal treatment is available in only one of the patient's languages (Boles, 2000; Lai et al., 2022).

Code-switching should not automatically be discouraged when it supports functional communication. When assessment suggests a credible shift toward unintended or contextinappropriate switching or difficulty maintaining an intended language, language control may itself warrant treatment consideration; otherwise, code-switching may serve as a useful compensatory strategy (Neumann et al., 2017; Gray & Kiran, 2016; Green & Abutalebi, 2008).

4.3 Intervention Approaches and Evidence

Previous studies examining discourse outcomes following intervention in bPWA have employed conversation-based approaches (Boles, 1998 (Boles, , 2000;; Lai et al., 2022) and impairmentbased approaches in which generalization to discourse was measured (Bihovsky et al., 2025; Lerman et al., 2019). Conversation-based approaches such as Conducting Conversation (CC; Boles, 1998 Boles, , 2000) ) and Better Conversations with Aphasia (BCA; Beeke et al., 2013) directly target communicative effectiveness in naturalistic interaction. In contrast, Semantic Feature Analysis (SFA; Boyle & Coelho, 1995) and Verb Network Strengthening Treatment (VNeST; Edmonds, 2014) primarily target lexical or sentence-level impairments rather than discourse directly; they are relevant here because studies with bPWA have examined whether treatment gains generalize to narrative or connected-speech outcomes (Bihovsky et al., 2025; Lerman et al., 2019). The choice of approach should align with the patient's sociolinguistic and aphasia profile, communication priorities, and clinical goals. The available evidence does not support identifying a single intervention approach or treatment language as uniformly superior for bPWA. The studies summarized in Table 4 involve small samples, different language combinations, treatment targets, and discourse outcome measures. Nevertheless, several clinically useful patterns emerge: direct conversation-based treatment can improve functional interaction; impairment-based treatment may generalize to discourse even when discourse is not directly treated; cross-language generalization can occur but is inconsistent across individuals and linguistic levels; and effects observed on naming or sentence-level measures may not predict discourse-level change (Bihovsky et al., 2025; Boles, 2000; Lai et al., 2022; Lerman et al., 2019). Taken together, discourse intervention should be a high-priority component of treatment for bPWA because it can directly target functional communication while incorporating multiple linguistic levels, languages, culturally relevant content, and communication partners within meaningful interactions. Treatment planning should consider the patient's language preferences and everyday communication needs, the availability of language-matched clinical resources, and the potential for cross-language generalization (Ansaldo & Saidi, 2014; Faroqi-Shah et al., 2010). When feasible, directly treating each clinically relevant language reduces reliance on uncertain cross-language transfer; when treatment can be provided in only one or some of the patient's languages, treatment should not be withheld, but cross-language generalization should be deliberately considered and outcomes should be assessed across the patient's languages and at the discourse level (Bihovsky et al., 2025; Lerman et al., 2019). Discourse treatments should be adapted to the linguistic and cultural properties of the language and communication context rather than directly translated, and communication-partner training can extend treatment effects into languages and contexts not directly addressed during SLP treatment (Boles, 2000; Lai et al., 2022). Discourse-focused and impairment-based approaches may also be combined when the patient's goals and impairment profile warrant direct treatment of lexical, morphological, or grammatical processes alongside functional communication.

5. Summary of Recommendations

For bilingual patients, assessment should begin with a comprehensive language history that allows clinicians to consider how the structures of the patient's languages, age and context of acquisition, premorbid proficiency, and patterns of language use may affect language processing before and after the neurological event. These factors are necessary for distinguishing expected bilingual language patterns from aphasic impairment and for interpreting performance across the patient's languages.

Discourse assessment can then provide converging evidence about functional language performance across languages. Clinicians should select culturally and linguistically appropriate discourse tasks and, for direct cross-language comparison, administer the same tasks in each language being assessed, adding multiple task types when necessary to represent the patient's discourse abilities. Administration should consider relatively monolingual and bilingual language modes when clinically feasible and should document the language context, language support, translation or adaptation of materials, and other modifications that may affect interpretation. Discourse should be analyzed at macrostructural and microstructural levels, while multilingual behaviors such as code-switching should be interpreted in relation to the individual's language history, linguistic structures, cultural and interactional context, and evidence of language control. Raw and proportional measures, language-specific features, and comparable cross-language structures may provide complementary information. Clinical conclusions should therefore integrate converging evidence across discourse samples, available assessment measures, patient and care-partner report, and functional communication rather than relying on any single source of evidence.

For intervention, discourse should be considered a high-priority treatment component because it can directly target functional communication while integrating multiple linguistic levels, languages, culturally relevant content, and communication partners within meaningful interactions. Treatment-language decisions should consider patient preference, everyday communication needs, available clinical resources, and cross-language generalization. When feasible, treatment of each clinically relevant language reduces reliance on uncertain crosslanguage transfer; however, treatment should not be withheld when services are available in only one or some of the patient's languages. In those circumstances, clinicians should consider crosslanguage generalization when selecting targets and evaluate outcomes across the patient's languages. Treatment materials and approaches should be adapted to language-specific and cultural characteristics rather than directly translated, and communication-partner training can extend communication support into languages and contexts outside the formal treatment environment (Ansaldo & Saidi, 2014; Bihovsky et al., 2025; Boles, 2000; Faroqi-Shah et al., 2010; Lai et al., 2022; Lerman et al., 2019).

Research and evidence-based practice recommendations on bilingual aphasia remain significantly limited compared to monolingual aphasia (Ansaldo & Saidi, 2014; Beveridge & Bak, 2011; Faroqi-Shah et al., 2010; Grasso et al., 2023; Kuzmina et al., 2019; Lee & Faroqi-Shah, 2024), and only a fraction of the breadth of human languages has been studied in aphasiology (Beveridge & Bak, 2011). With standardized assessments and treatments available in only a limited number of languages (Wallace et al., 2022) and targeting a limited number of language structures, incorporating discourse measures may be an especially useful approach to assess and treat bilingual populations. Despite limited research on clinical bilingual populations, we have offered practice guidelines for use of discourse in assessment and treatment based on available evidence. This evidence originates from studies of healthy bilingual processing and bilingual identity, research on bilingual aphasia and cross-language treatment, evidence concerning differences between bilingual language patterns and aphasic deficits, available clinical tools, and the broader evidence base for discourse assessment and intervention.

These recommendations are necessarily constrained by the current evidence base. Studies of bilingual aphasia frequently include small samples or individual cases, represent a limited range of languages and language combinations, and vary substantially in assessment tasks, treatment approaches, and outcome measures. Normative and psychometric evidence is also uneven across languages, and some recommendations necessarily draw on converging evidence from healthy bilingual language processing or the broader aphasia literature where direct evidence in bPWA is unavailable. The recommendations should therefore be applied in relation to the linguistic, cultural, clinical, and resource context of the individual patient rather than interpreted as prescriptive rules applicable uniformly across multilingual populations.

Future research should consider the psychometric qualities of discourse analysis tools and the development of standardized discourse measures relevant to bilingual populations, systematically examine mechanisms of cross-language treatment generalization, and conduct larger, more methodologically rigorous treatment studies across diverse language combinations. Research should also expand the linguistic and cultural diversity of discourse tasks and treatment studies, evaluate approaches to multilingual service delivery when language-matched clinicians are unavailable, and identify which discourse measures are most sensitive to clinically meaningful within-and cross-language treatment change. Such studies will be necessary to generate evidence that reflects the linguistic complexity of aphasia in bilinguals and to establish robust guidelines that support equitable and effective discourse rehabilitation for bPWA.
