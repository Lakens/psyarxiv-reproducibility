Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The climate and ecological crises disproportionately affect communities from low-andmiddle-income-countries, yet to date most studies of eco-emotions have been with Western populations. This study sought to investigate the views and experiences of young adults in Nepal and India regarding emotional responses and coping to the ecological and climate crises.

Using constructivist grounded theory, interviews were conducted with twenty-four young adults, equally split between region (Delhi national-capital-region in India, and Kathmandu Valley in Nepal); poor and middle-class urban populations; and male/female sex, to ensure a suitable range of experiences.

1

The grounded theory model proposes that eco-emotional experiences are initiated by lived ecological disruption, such as air pollution, heat, and environmental degradation, rather than via abstract knowledge of climate change. These disruptions are then interpreted through culturally embedded meaning systems, including religious practices, agricultural rhythms, and place-based identity, and are further shaped by structural inequalities, such as socioeconomic positioning and perceived political inaction.

Eco-emotions emerge not as discrete states but as dynamic configurations, in which anxiety, anger, sadness, grief, hope, and helplessness coexist and fluctuate. Perceived agency operates as a key interpretive dimension influencing emotional trajectories. Where agency is experienced as viable, distress may be channelled into problem-focussed or meaningfocussed coping. Where agency is constrained, coping shifts towards strategies that regulate distress through psychological distancing, including fatalism, resignation, and disengagement. Coping processes recursively reshape how ecological disruptions are interpreted over time.

This framework situates eco-emotions within the intersection of ecological change, cultural meaning-making, and structural inequality, offering a contextually grounded account of their emergence and regulation.

1. Introduction

Nepal and India face disastrous consequences due to human-induced environmental degradation, with climate change exacerbating food, water, and energy insecurity in both nations [1, 2]. While action is being implemented to address these challenges, primary adaptation strategies focus on physical and economic adaptation, leaving mental health and wellbeing a gap in policy and practice considerations. For example, climate change adaptation policies in both countries (i.e., India adopted the National Action Plan on Climate Change, 2018; Nepal adopted the National Climate Change Policy, 2019 and National Adaptation Plan, 2021) lack consideration of mental health and wellbeing aspects of the climate and ecological crises. Existing scholarship increasingly highlights the limitations of adaptation frameworks that privilege technological interventions in isolation, and call for greater attention to social, cultural, and emotional dimensions of climate change [3, 4].

Without incorporating mental health into adaptation planning, efforts to build resilience risk being partial and less effective, as psychological well-being is integral to individuals' and communities' capacity to adapt and recover [5, 6]. Therefore, investigating how individuals experience eco-emotions and cope would highlight the challenges they face related to the climate and ecological crises, allowing the implementation of strategies focusing on mental health and wellbeing to assist in adaptation and resilience-building.

Hulme argues that grasping the nature of climate and therefore climate change is a complex, multifaceted task that necessitates engagement with the cultural narratives shaping perceptions of climate [7]. In South Asian contexts, such narratives are embedded in socio-economic realities, development trajectories, and locally grounded understandings of environmental change [4]. This makes it essential to study how people not only experience climate and ecological impacts but also interpret and emotionally respond to them. Of particular interest is the belief in fatalism or karma, which is deeply engrained in the cultural and religious fabric of South Asian societies that functions as a crucial meaning-making and emotion-focussed coping strategy in collectivistic contexts such as Nepal and India [8] [9] [10].

Investigating eco-emotions through the lens of fatalism may provide a novel understanding of how it serves to regulate emotional responses to the climate and ecological crises in collectivistic settings.

1.1. Eco-emotions and coping

The term "eco-emotions" can be defined as "affective phenomena which are significantly related to the climate crisis" [11], including a range of emotions related to an individual's perception, attitudes, beliefs, and concerns of climate and/or ecological crises [12]. Research indicates younger generations are most vulnerable to mental health and wellbeing impacts of the climate and ecological crises [13, 14], necessitating the need to investigate eco-emotional responses in this population. Research on coping with ecoemotions has shown how coping is related to individual's experiences of eco-emotions, from engaging in actions and pro-environmental behaviours [15] [16] [17] [18], to adaptive and maladaptive coping [15, 19, 20].

Currently, there are a limited number of studies that have investigated eco-emotions in an Indian context, and none in Nepal. One found most people are "extremely worried" or "very worried" about climate change and 74% worried about climate change having an impact on daily functioning [21]; in contrast, others using a standardised scale of climate anxiety suggest that Indian populations do not experience high levels of climate-anxiety [22, 23]. This inconsistency may reflect the limitations of standardised instruments in capturing culturally situated eco-emotional experiences in non-Westernised contexts. Furthermore, it underscores the need for qualitative approaches that can comprehensively explore how eco-emotions are understood, expressed and psychologically regulated in South Asian contexts.

To our knowledge, no study has yet qualitatively explored lived-experiences related to the climate and ecological crises faced by young adults in Nepal or India. Understanding how they perceive the climate and ecological crises and how they experience and understand eco-emotions will help contextualise quantitative exploration.

2.1. Constructivist Grounded Theory

Constructivist grounded theory (CGT) aims to construct theory that arises from the researcher's interpretation of the data in context rather than an actual representation. Theory is constructed based on the "historical, social, and situation conditions of its productions" [24]. The lived-experiences of the participants are given centre stage and they are allowed to express themselves without prejudice. The researcher's positionality, values and associations are acknowledged and addressed appropriately as well, in order to facilitate research in a manner where theory is constructed and grounded in the data collected.

Finally, CGT involves 'focused coding'; where the researcher actively chooses which codes are most significant to carry on with towards theoretical conceptualisation [24]. Therefore, CGT allows exploration of lived-experiences of the population in relation to eco-emotions; and provides a structure for theory construction in a population where eco-emotions have not been qualitatively explored.

2.2. Participants and Recruitment

A purposive, stratified sampling matrix was established prior to data collection with a target sample size of N = 24 participants. This fixed structure was strategically designed to ensure sufficient input from participants in demographics (biological sex and socioeconomic class) thought to potentially affect responses to the research questions. The sample was taken from one urban centre in each of Nepal and India, with equal numbers in each country recruited from the urban-poor and urban-middle-class. Equal number of participants based on biological sex were recruited, as the literature suggests sex differences in emotional responses to the climate and ecological crises [25].

Data collection and concurrent qualitative analysis proceeded until the crosscomparative matrix was fully populated. To satisfy the criteria for thematic rigour, data saturation was evaluated retroactively within this sample. The Primary Investigator (P.I.) verified that later interviews across each demographic level yielded no novel codes, themes, or structural properties. This confirmed that the 24-participant sample provided sufficient depth needed to fully develop and saturate the generated grounded theory model.

Interviews were conducted in English, Nepali or Hindi, depending on the preference of each participant. Interview data in Nepali and Hindi was translated into English as part of the coding and analysis by the P.I. The anonymised transcripts in the original languages were retained. Informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection and verbal informed consent was obtained prior to the interview. Participant recruitment and Interviews began on 1 November 2023 and concluded on 18 March 2024.

In Nepal, the sample was recruited from the Kathmandu Valley, the largest urban area in Nepal, where the majority of citizens reside, generally due to familial ties, or as migrant workers pursuing economic opportunities that are lacking in other parts of the country. Kathmandu Valley has a large population of young adults seeking job opportunities, with the overall population being approximately 3.1 million [26]. In India, the sample was similarly recruited from urban centres in Delhi national-capital-region, one of the largest populated areas in the world with over 31 million individuals, and a population density of over 11320 persons per sq. km [27]. It has a large population of young adult and migrant residents. Local fieldworkers assisted in recruitment, especially harder-to-reach-participants from the urban poor demographic. In addition to recruitment, fieldworkers also assisted in providing technological support and internet access to some urban poor participants without existing connectivity, enabling online interviews.

The urban-poor was an important demographic to include in this study, as they are disproportionately affected by climate and ecological crises, and often lack the resources required to adapt, cope, or build resilience [28]. Purposive sampling was used to ensure representation of these perspectives, rather than a disproportionate representation from easier-to-recruit middle-classes. Participants from urban-poor demographics were recruited from areas commonly identified as 'slums' in Kathmandu and Delhi national-capital-region to ensure representation from lower socioeconomic groups. To enhance potential transferability of findings, the definition of urban-poor was guided by United Nations Program on Human Settlements (UN-HABITAT) [29].

Definition of urban-middle-class was guided by Roman [30] and López-Calva and Ortiz-Juarez [31] who identify low risk of falling back into poverty as a key criterion for middle-class status. According to latest estimates, the middle-class in India stands at 66 million [32]; Nepal has an estimated 22% of its population in 2010-2011 residing within the middle class category [33].

2.3. Procedure

An interview guide sought to ask questions relevant to the research question, whilst allowing participants to share what they wanted, enabling data to emerge organically through interactions (S2 file). Pilot interviews informed the adequacy of the guide. Participants took part in an online audio-recorded 1:1 interview lasting approximately 90 minutes and completed a brief demographic questionnaire, related to participants' age, biological sex, level of education, and current employment status. Four rounds of six interviews were conducted, aligning with grounded theory's iterative design [24, 34]. Insights from earlier rounds shaped the questions and probes used in later interviews.

The recordings were transcribed as soon as possible, with identifiable information redacted and/or anonymised. The audio recording(s) were then permanently deleted.

2.4. Reflexivity

The P.I.'s research interests were shaped by a strong nature-connection and universalist values [35]. Their personal background -being from Nepal and having spent significant time in India -fostered a firsthand understanding of the lived realities of environmental challenges in the region. The P.I. was cognisant of the power asymmetries inherent in the researcher-participant relationship and critically examined their own position within these dynamics. Additionally, they acknowledged the privileges associated with their familial history of socioeconomic and political influence within South Asian societies. To mitigate the effects of these biases and power imbalances, the P.I. engaged in methodological self-consciousness [34] and memo-making to capture researcher reflections [24].

2.5. Credibility checks

Credibility checks were consistent with grounded theory research [24, 34]. To enhance interpretive rigour, investigator triangulation was conducted with second and third authors, who provided continuous feedback regarding generation of categories and development of theory. Additionally, participant checking (member checking) was employed iteratively throughout the analysis phase to ensure emergent themes and categories accurately reflected the participants' lived-experiences and perspectives. The P.I. also maintained a reflexive journal which helped the researchers to engage in consistent reflexive practice to reduce bias and its impact. Resonance, as mentioned by Charmaz [24] was maintained by incident-related coding, and in-vivo coding that ensured closeness to data and participants' lived-experiences. Interview transcripts, a reflexivity journal, and memos contributed to an audit trail of the various steps taken in the entire research process.

3.1. Core theoretical proposition

Eco-emotional responses emerge through a recursive, culturally embedded process in which lived ecological disruption is interpreted through meaning systems and systemic 12 212 213 214 215 inequalities. These interpretations generate differentiated emotional configurations that shape coping repertoires and, over time, may lead to chronic ecological distress. The following stages describe this sequential process.

3.2. Stage one: Lived ecological disruption as emotional catalyst

Across interviews, participants demonstrated awareness of system-level environmental change, often articulated through formal or English-language terminology such as "climate change" or "global warming." However, abstract scientific constructs rarely elicited strong emotional intensity. Prakash reflected: "How we came to understand climate change was through English as a medium... a direct translation might not make sense... the term itself I feel doesn't get through."

In contrast, when participants described direct tangible experiences (extreme heat, air pollution, flooding, agricultural disruptions) their narratives shifted in affective intensity, as shared by Aashish: "the air quality one it scares me... it really scares me."

Similarly, Ravi described recurrent floods in his village: "Now... there are floods every year and they cause a lot of damage... Last year, many people's fields and crops in our village were destroyed."

This patterned distinction, observed across interviews, indicates that eco-emotions were activated primarily through lived and embodied disruptions rather than through abstract environmental knowledge alone. Ecological change became affectively salient when intruded into everyday routines, health, agriculture, or mobility.

3.3. Stage Two: Culturally embedded appraisal

Environmental disruptions did not automatically produce uniform emotional responses; rather, emotional responses took shape within culturally embedded interpretive frameworks. Participants interpreted ecological change through culturally situated frameworks that included agricultural rhythms, religious practices, intergenerational continuity, and place-based identity. For instance, Srijana described distress when delayed monsoons interfered with rice planting rituals: "if it is not on time, then it will be very bad."

Similarly, Rita shares her anger over ongoing degradation of sacred (natural) entities: it feels like these are just words by people [deifying and worshiping rivers and other natural bodies]... if you truly believe in this, why can't people keep the rivers clean?

Why can't people think twice or even 100 times before polluting the Ganga." Ravi noted rising temperatures altering Chatt Puja: "it's no longer cold. It used to be earlier but now it is hot even during the puja time." In Nepal, participants described glacial melt not simply as environmental degradation, but as erosion of spiritual and national identity. Neeti expressed anger regarding emissions from industrialised nations, and the disproportionate impacts being faced: "if I have to suffer the punishment for the things that I haven't done... then I feel that it's unfair. So if the emissions from a factory... emissions that are being done somewhere else and because of that our mountains are melting… I really feel angry" These accounts indicate that ecological change was frequently experienced as disruption to cultural continuity and collective identity. Environmental degradation was therefore narrated as not only physical loss but as cultural destabilisation. Across interviews, emotional intensity increased where environmental instability intersected with culturally meaningful practices or symbols.

3.4. Stage Three: Structural positioning and systemic inequalities

Participants frequently contextualised environmental harm within broader narratives of political inaction, corruption, global inequity, and socioeconomic marginalisation. Garima shared her frustrations at perceived government betrayal when political or institutional systems were seen as enabling environmental degradation in the name of development: in Nepal environmental extraction and exploitation etc. happens with political protection.

So even though you know about all of this, you can't do anything about it" Similarly, Shyam shared his anger at the inaction of the Delhi government in addressing the deteriorating air quality: "Delhi is the world's most polluted city! But they are doing nothing... they are selfish and only care about themselves" Perceived structural positioning influenced emotional configuration. Where participants framed themselves as structurally constrained, eco-helplessness and frustration intensified, as elaborated by Gita: "It feels really bad that we live in such an environment.

We should also get to live in a clean environment! So that we can live well and be happy... It doesn't feel good even thinking about all this, because we can't do anything."

Where responsibility was attributed to powerful institutions or global actors, ecoanger emerged as morally directed, as articulated by Prakash: "I feel very strongly when I'm there in [UK city], their sky is so richly blue and the air quality is so good and I feel like these (expletive)... because of them our country is in the state it is currently… these people came and did whatever they wanted to... and now they are living like this in their own country."

Structural inequalities shaped both the interpretation of ecological disruption and the configuration of emotional responses.

3.5. Stage Four: eco-emotional configuration

Rather than describing singular emotional states, participants articulated layered and fluctuating emotional constellations. Eco-anxiety coexisted with eco-anger; eco-sadness intertwined with eco-hope; distress alternated with motivation. Rahul articulates this dichotomy while explaining how hope and anxiety are entwined within his narrative: "it makes me feel scared… like an existential dread... it becomes like this ecological anxiety as such, about the nature and all becomes integrated with our existential anxiety… that nothing matters.

That the environment will also go away we will also go away that nothing is going to be left on earth... and again, I say the hope has to be there. The hope has to be continuously built... only when you keep this hope you try to make efforts… with these small efforts coming from a large number of people I think change will be there. So hope is one factor I try to never let go of."

Similarly, Diya shared her experience of mixed emotions when thinking about the future: "I mean its mixed emotions.

There's a bit of helplessness… there's an acknowledgment of my privilege for sure, there's also anger because umm you know, even though I'm privileged I'm not that privileged that I'll be able to guard myself from every consequence and yea, definitely a bit of hope… maybe [laughs] who knows. Let's see if that hope actually works out or not… I mean, that's our reality so we have to deal with it and cope, so yea."

These findings suggest that eco-emotions operate as dynamic configurations rather than discrete psychological categories. Emotional responses were relational, context-sensitive, and temporally fluid.

3.6. Stage Five: Perceived agency as regulatory dimension

Perceived agency emerged as a critical interpretive dimension influencing emotional trajectories. Where participants expressed belief in collective action, technological progress, or political reform, eco-hope coexisted alongside distress. For example, Laxmi shared the aspirations repeatedly stated by multiple participants of the benefits that "development" would bring: "Everyone wants facilities, everyone wants development right?

And it feels like all of the suffering we are experiencing and witnessing is the result of this development... everything has a positive and a negative side to it. I feel it's like that only because in order to get something, you need to be willing to lose something as well."

Sita shares the power of collective actions, while encouraging others to engage in community-based pro-environmental behaviours: "If a lot of people start adopting these kinds of behaviours then it'll be so good for the environment!

It'll be positive for the society as well if people would just think about these things and at least try to do some of these things."

Conversely, when participants perceived limited capacity to influence systemic forces, eco-helplessness intensified: "Then after the anger there is also a sense of helplessness.

That what can an individual like me really do about it? so yes, it will be anger and helplessness." (Aashish).

"but what can a person like me do? Nothing can be done. And when I think about such things and feel such things, I don't feel like talking or interacting with anyone... This is how I feel due to all of this... I didn't say these just for the sake of saying this, this is how I truly feel. Because I feel like I am a direct victim of all of these things." (Shivani).

Agency therefore operated not as a fixed individual trait but as a shifting interpretive stance that modulated emotional intensity and direction.

3.7. Stage Six: Coping as recursive re-appraisal

Although the findings of the preceding studies describe multiple coping strategies, the analysis revealed an underlying process shaping how these responses emerged. Coping with eco-emotions was not simply an individual psychological choice; rather, it reflected a contextually situated appraisal of agency within structural and cultural constraints. This process is therefore conceptualised as coping under structural and cultural constraint, rather than as purely individual emotional regulation. An individual's appraisal of these constraints actively shapes the trajectory of their subsequent behavioural and emotional strategies.

Participants first appraised the degree of control they possessed over ecological stressors. Where individuals perceived relatively higher personal agency (often facilitated by socioeconomic privilege) coping tended to take problem-focused or meaning-focused forms.

These included purchasing air purifiers, modifying consumption behaviours, engaging in activism, or cultivating constructive hope. Such strategies allowed participants to transform eco-distress into purposeful action, reinforcing a sense of coherence and moral alignment:

It makes me feel connected… for example I'm not someone who's able to see climate change and stuff in a you know, experiential perspective too much. But when I do these kinds of behaviours [pro-environmental behaviours] then I feel like I am able to have a better connect with nature... Makes you feel good... you are able to act responsibly and contribute in some possible way." (Rohan) "Air purifiers are a big thing. Air purifiers are needed in every household I feel... this friend of mine just suggested it... And honestly it makes a difference… like I feel like I can breathe better." (Shiva). "I want to use my activism for awareness and advocacy and policy lobbying… I try to deal with all of the frustration, anger, anxiety, and fear that I feel by transforming it in a positive way with the work that I do." (Neeti). However, where agency was perceived as limited (due to economic precarity, systemic corruption, or the scale of environmental degradation) coping shifted toward strategies that regulated distress through psychological distancing. These included fatalism, resignation-based acceptance, rationalisation of development harms, emotional disengagement, and technocentric optimism. Rather than reflecting apathy, these responses functioned as protective mechanisms that conserved emotional resources in contexts where structural change felt unattainable. Fatalism and resignation-based acceptance emerged as distinct yet overlapping mechanisms. Fatalism externalised responsibility to divine will or fate, offering culturally embedded emotional buffering. Resignation-based acceptance, in contrast, reflected a pragmatic acknowledgement of limited personal or systemic agency, often accompanied by emotional withdrawal or normalisation. Both strategies reduced anticipatory anxiety, but through distinct meaning-making processes: "the point is also that we cannot be too serious about things like this [air pollution]... if you believe that your health is bound to deteriorate, then this creates a fear in your mind... you feel tension and fear gets ingrained in your mind. So I believe that don't think too much and just work. Whatever happens will be seen." (Ravi) "to be honest I don't really think about these stuff on a daily basis… I try to separate the dust and pollution and everything from the actual global warming stuff… so this is my personal thing... And you don't want to really believe the news and articles about all of this… bottom line is that there's a lot of distractions available so it's easy to avoid certain topics." (Dilip). "I mean [sighs] whatever will happen will happen we'll tolerate it. Whatever will happen is in God's hands and nature is mother nature so I hope it won't be too bad. Whatever will happen we'll tolerate it. We'll have to tolerate it right?" (Rita). "You can't even do anything about this, so what's the point of thinking about it? Whatever will happen in the future will happen, we'll see when it happens." (Srijana) Importantly, personal values shaped coping trajectories. Participants oriented toward universalist or self-transcendent values (used here and elsewhere as interpretive lenses rather than empirically measured constructs) were more likely to channel eco-anger and anxiety into pro-environmental action, whereas those aligned with achievement or power values more frequently employed rationalisation or future-oriented technocentric optimism. Values thus influenced not only behavioural responses, but the emotional architecture of coping itself: "I feel like responsibility is on people like me who's had the privilege of education to certain degrees… the onus comes on them to stand up in such situations." (Prakash). "I feel that if I use crackers then what example am I setting for my own (future) children and my students and kids in our area? So first I have to change myself then only I can educate the kids not to use crackers because I know how harmful it is… this had an impact on me and I decided to not use crackers and I educate kids to not use crackers and I try my best to convince other people to not do it too" (Ram). "But you know, because humans are the ones who are inventing everything and are already thinking about their survival and everything. There will be… technology... I think so there will be somewhere or somehow somewhat a method, technology or something." (Neha).

Coping, therefore, must be understood relationally: situated at the intersection of structural constraint, cultural worldview, perceived agency, and value orientation. Coping reshaped appraisal processes, which in turn influenced subsequent emotional responses.

The process was recursive rather than linear. The use of the term "re-appraisal" here does not refer to cognitive reappraisal as conceptualised within individual-level emotion regulation models. Rather, re-appraisal is used in a broader interpretive sense to describe how participants continuously re-interpret ongoing ecological disruption in relation to shifting perceptions of agency, cultural meaning systems, and structural constraint. The process described is socio-culturally embedded and contextually situated, rather than a discrete intrapsychic strategy.

3.8. Integrative theoretical synthesis

The findings converge to advance a recursive, culturally embedded, and structurally situated process model of eco-emotional experience. Eco-emotions did not arise solely from awareness of environmental degradation, nor are they reducible to individual psychological reactions. Rather, they emerge when lived ecological disruption is interpreted through culturally meaningful frameworks and situated within broader systems of structural inequality. This grounded theoretical framework thus situates eco-emotions within the intersection of ecological change, cultural meaning systems, structural inequalities, perceived agency, and value orientation. It advances an explanatory model that accounts not only for the emergence of eco-emotions, but for their regulation, transformation, and potential chronicity within contexts of uneven environmental vulnerability. 4. Discussion

This study investigated the emotional responses (eco-emotions) to the climate and ecological crises in young adults in Nepal and India, and how they coped with their ecoemotions. Drawing on a constructivist grounded theory approach, the narratives of 24 participants (12 each from Kathmandu valley and Delhi national-capital-region) revealed contextually embedded eco-emotions and coping strategies, shaped by intersecting cultural, structural, and socioeconomic factors.

4.1. Contextual eco-emotions and language

Eco-emotional responses among participants were primarily driven by contextual factors. Direct ecological degradation elicited far more intense emotions than abstract, vicarious awareness of climate change. Daily exposure to these impacts heightened emotional salience, giving rise to eco-emotions intertwined with participants' material and social realities. These experiences interacted with temporal orientations: current environmental harms were frequently interpreted as precursors to a more precarious future, particularly among those who perceived limited capacity to alter their living conditions [36] [37] [38]. Eco-emotions were thus not merely cognitive responses to anticipated threats but were grounded in a lived sense of constrained agency and escalating vulnerability.

Language is critical for understanding emotional responses to the climate and ecological crises in non-Westernised contexts. Although terms like 'climate change' and 'global warming' are generally recognised, their meanings often remain abstract and disconnected from everyday lived-realities. In contrast, tangible localised experiences like erratic monsoons or rising temperatures elicited eco-emotional responses more firmly grounded in participants' daily lives. This underscores the importance of linguistic and narrative framing in shaping how ecological impacts are perceived and felt, reinforcing the politics of language as essential to climate justice and equity [39]. Consistent with psycholinguistic literature, concreteness and proximity in language shape emotional salience and memory encoding [40].

The disjuncture between local realities and dominant climate discourses highlighted the larger epistemic challenges in the field, where certain forms of scientific or technical knowledge are privileged over situated, experiential, or culturally embedded understandings [3, 41, 42]. Such hierarchies risk delegitimising the emotional and psychological dimensions of ecological change for Global South communities, reinforcing climate injustice [4, 43, 44]. By demonstrating how eco-emotions emerge from daily encounters with environmental precarity and structural inequity, this study underscores the need for a more pluralistic and contextsensitive understanding of mental health impacts within climate and ecological scholarship and policy.

Cultural idioms of distress [45] further suggest that emotional states may be expressed indirectly, particularly where open discussion of personal distress is less normative. In the Nepalese transcripts, paralinguistic cues like tone, pacing, and emphasis conveyed emotional intensity even without explicit emotion words. Bikash's description of environmental degradation for development, for instance, was marked by rising vocal pitch and emphatic repetition, suggesting anger and frustration. In such contexts, attending to tone, affective intensity, and indirect expressions offers a valuable means of interpreting emotional experiences [46].

4.2. Socio-cultural situatedness and constrained agency

Eco-emotional experiences were deeply socio-culturally situated, shaped by locally embedded understandings of emotion, worldviews, and culturally normative emotionregulation strategies. What initially appeared paradoxical was that resignation was often narrated in the language of acceptance. Whereas Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) conceptualises acceptance as an agentic, adaptive process [47], resignation-based acceptance was characterised by disengagement and learned helplessness in response to perceived absence of systemic support and constrained personal agency.

This coping was rooted in the broader framework of socioeconomic mobility, in which individuals prioritise livelihood and stability over ecological considerations, enacting what might be described as strategic acquiescence -a form of resignation permitting emotional detachment from seemingly intractable problems while pursuing immediate, attainable goals [48, 49]. Constrained agency was often exacerbated by perceptions of state apathy and systemic corruption, which undermined trust in collective or policy-level solutions [50]. Here, acceptance is entangled with resignation not as an absence of coping, but as a contextually adaptive strategy. This reframing invites a broader understanding of acceptance that accounts for sociopolitical and structural constraints, rather than solely individualistic or agency-driven.

Rather than a binary of adaptive versus maladaptive, Aldao [51] advocates understanding emotional regulation as flexible and context-dependent. Accordingly, participants' disengagement, denial, and emotional numbing (conventionally labelled as 'maladaptive') can be interpreted as situationally appropriate responses that manage overwhelming emotions in the absence of viable alternatives. Where individuals had little access to resources, power, or policy influence, these behaviours enabled them to navigate daily challenges without being immobilised by anxiety or despair. This speaks to the importance of recognising structurally induced resilience, where coping is not about 'overcoming' adversity but managing it within one's environmental limits. Understanding these behaviours as functional coping under constraint offers a more compassionate, contextually grounded approach to mental health and ecological adaptation in the Global South.

4.3. Role of fatalism and collective coping

Fatalism emerged as a significant coping strategy, particularly among participants from low-income or marginalised backgrounds. Many described climate impacts as beyond their individual control, attributing outcomes to fate, divine will, or broader societal forces.

This pattern is especially salient in collectivistic contexts, where externalising responsibility to community, government, or spiritual belief systems offers psychological protection [52].

Here, fatalism functioned not as apathy, but as a culturally embedded, potentially adaptive coping response [53], providing emotional distance from distressing events and reducing the cognitive burden of personal responsibility in situations marked by uncertainty and limited personal agency.

The Western emphasis on self-determination may exacerbate eco-emotions by placing the burden on the individual, thus necessitating the development of personalised coping mechanisms [52, 54]. In contrast, collectivistic frameworks may offer more socially embedded strategies, including fatalism, that alleviate individual distress. Understanding fatalism through social support systems and cultural orientations -particularly in disaster adaptation and response -provides a more nuanced perspective on its functional role [55, 56], warranting further investigation into whether, and when, fatalistic beliefs may operate as a culturally congruent form of coping [57, 58]. This resonates with broader critiques in global mental health literature cautioning against universalising Western models of agency and mental health [59].

The coexistence of fatalistic and other forms of functional coping forms emphasises the need to address the mental health impacts of the climate crisis in ways that account for cultural norms and social realities. Such responses appear insufficiently recognised in current urban Nepali and Indian policy landscapes, a gap that risks deepening existing exclusions and reproducing climate injustice.

A broader socio-ecological perspective highlights how the social determinants of mental health intersect with accelerating environmental stressors. Structural inequalities, limited access to mental health resources, and socioeconomic precarity exacerbate mental health impacts of ecological change [60, 61]. As anthropogenic disasters and socioeconomic instability increase, negative eco-emotions are likely to surge globally, particularly among vulnerable populations with constrained adaptive capacity [62]. For young adults, perceived powerlessness in the face of global inaction has already been linked to psychological distress [63], underscoring the urgency of integrating culturally attuned mental health frameworks into climate adaptation and resilience polices [64].

4.4. Growth-oriented development and techno-optimism

A consistent theme concerned the perceived tension between economic development and environmental protection. As both India and Nepal are Low and Middleincome Countries (LMICs), participants frequently viewed infrastructural and economic development as an urgent national priority, even at the expense of ecological wellbeing.

These narratives must be situated within the historical context of colonial extraction and uneven development, which continues to shape public imaginaries of progress and modernity [43, 44]. Viewed thus, the prioritisation of growth is not a contradiction but a rational response in societies still working to secure basic socio-economic needs.

Participants' accounts revealed a form of development dissonance: many expressed strong negative eco-emotions while simultaneously endorsing growth-oriented policies perceived as a necessary evil for improving collective living conditions. One interpretation applies Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs theory [65, 66] at the societal level. Although based on individualistic values and theorised at the individual level, it may help explain why populations emphasise economic stability and infrastructural improvement before attending to the climate and ecological crises [67]. From this perspective, development is foundational to broader wellbeing. Rather than reflecting a lack of environmental care, endorsement of growth polices reflects system justification [68]: Individuals feel structurally compelled to defend growth-oriented models as viable mechanisms for securing physical safety and collective survival.

This complexity was further shaped by techno-optimism-the belief that technological innovation or scientific advancement will ultimately resolve environmental challenges.

Research suggests techno-optimism can reduce cognitive dissonance, allowing individuals to maintain ecological concern while assuming large-scale solutions will emerge without significant personal or societal sacrifice [69] [70] [71]. In this study, techno-optimism appeared to provide a psychological buffer, enabling acceptance of environmental decline while sustaining hope in development-driven futures.

Techno-optimistic beliefs have also been associated with reduced perceived personal responsibility, shifting mitigation and adaptation onto governments, corporations, or technological systems [72, 73]. By mitigating immediate distress, such beliefs may inadvertently diminish public urgency and reduce collective demands on institutional actors [74, 75].

These beliefs align with growth-oriented capitalist logics that frame prosperity as dependent on continuous expansion, a narrative embedded in postcolonial development discourse and reinforced by the institutionalisation of economic growth as a global benchmark of success [76]. However, techno-optimism does not operate uniformly. Recent evidence indicates that both techno-pessimism and techno-optimism can correlate positively with proenvironmental behaviours, whereas "techno-fix" attitudes (belief in simple technological solutions without systemic change) are negatively associated with them [77]. This distinction is critical: techno-optimism grounded in critical awareness rather than passive faith may offer a productive narrative for motivating environmental engagement. Framing climate communication to harness constructive techno-optimism without reinforcing complacency or deflecting responsibility could be valuable in urban South Asian contexts. Nonetheless, overreliance on technological solutions risks obscuring the socioeconomic and structural drivers perpetuating ecological challenges [3, 4], and may reproduce the very systems contributing to degradation [71].

Addressing these crises therefore requires solutions extending beyond technological aspiration to confront deeper political, economic, and social inequities [41]. Only by integrating technological possibilities with structural transformation can equitable, durable responses be achieved.

4.5. Values, meaning-focussed coping, and constructive hope

Participants' values significant shaped how they processed eco-emotions. Those aligned with universalist or self-transcendent values (e.g. environmental concern, social justice) more often reported strong eco-anxiety, eco-anger, and eco-sadness. However, these emotions frequently motivated action such as climate activism and lifestyle changes. Neeti and Garima for example, described transforming eco-anger and eco-anxiety into positive action through youth engagement and policy advocacy, reflecting meaning-focussed coping. Others, such as Rohan, felt more connected and purposeful through small-scale proenvironmental behaviours. These accounts reflect Ojala's [15] distinction between constructive eco-hope, which fosters adaptive coping and avoidant eco-hope, based on denial and disengagement.

Garima's reflection is particularly illustrative: despite her urban-middle-class background, she highlights the inequities in environmental advocacy, noting that proenvironmental behaviours are often a privilege inaccessible to poorer communities. Her critique reframes environmentalism as a site of social inequality, where pro-environmental choices may be inadvertently imposed in ways that disregard lived realities.

Participants identifying with achievement or power-based values expressed fewer eco-emotions and more often downplayed environmental concerns, sometimes accompanied by technocentric optimism that reduced the need for present-day emotional engagement. It should be noted, however, that these values-based reflections resonate with existing literature [35], and were employed in this study as interpretative conceptual tools rather than formally assessed constructs. Taken together, the findings suggest that values do not merely influence attitudes but shape the very emotional architecture of coping.

Understanding this relationship can inform value-based interventions that align belief systems with behavioural intentions, supporting resilience and meaning-making in the face of climate distress.

4.5. Structural vulnerability among urban-poor populations

The disproportionate emotional and material burden borne by urban-poor participants highlights the intersection of climate vulnerably and social inequality. The lack of agency they perceived in daily life was reflected in their eco-emotions and emotional regulation, illustrating how urban-poor populations face disproportionate climate-related challenges that will intensify with rising extreme weather events [28]. As migrant populations increasingly move to urban centres in Nepal and India [78, 79], these populations will likely face increasingly severe climate and ecological challenges unless systems are established to support their adaptation and resilience.

4.6. Strengths and limitations

This study represents one of the first in-depth qualitative investigations of ecoemotions and regulatory strategies within the Nepalese and Indian contexts. The findings underscore the need for culturally situated and context-sensitive adaptation strategies, challenging the continued dominance of policy frameworks rooted in Global North assumptions. By centring lived-experiences in the Global South, this research contributes to broader debates on climate justice and equity, particularly in relation to populations that face disproportionate climate impacts [62].

Conducting surveys online limited the reach for participants from the poorest of the urban-poor demographic. Given the widespread adoption of mobile internet services among the target population that justified online data collection [80, 81], the digital divide likely excluded some from the poorest urban-poor demographic.

Additionally, the study did not explore participants' caste or community affiliationssocial identifiers that hold deep historical and structural significance in South Asian societies.

While this methodological choice was made to maintain a well-defined research scope, it is acknowledged that caste and community-based experiences may significantly shape how eco-emotions are felt and how coping unfolds. Future research would benefit from explicitly engaging with these constructs to deepen understanding of how environmental stressors interact with social stratification in the region.

Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable groundwork for further inquiry and supports a growing call for culturally situated, equity-informed approaches to climate and mental health research in the Global South [12, 62, 82].

4.7. Directions for future research

Expanding the scope of the current study to include other urban centres across South Asia would help strengthen the contextual understanding of eco-emotions and coping in diverse settings. Whilst findings from this study may transfer to other similar urban centres, there are a wide range of cultural, socio-economic and geographic differences across these nations and the region. Future research could include rural communities to further our understanding on the eco-emotions experienced in South Asian settings. Crossregional comparisons may reveal both shared and distinct patterns in how environmental concerns and emotional responses are experienced and managed.

Finally, there is scope for quantitative inquiry to complement and deepen these qualitative insights. Quantitative evaluation of the levels of eco-emotions experienced would be important in representative samples. Currently, only levels of climate/eco-anxiety have been measured in a South Asian context [22, 23, 50]. Expanding this to evaluate levels of eco-anger, eco-sadness, etc. and associations with mental health outcomes would help develop understanding of the eco-emotions experienced and their mental health impacts.

Future work could explore the relationships between personal values and coping strategies, the role of fatalism, and the specific pathways through which eco-emotions influence behavioural and psychological outcomes. Additionally, exploring relationships between proenvironmental behaviours and eco-emotions would enhance theoretical clarity. A wider role for quantitative research could help determine the prevalence and effect size of any effects, relationships, or group differences suggested from the qualitative data. Together, these approaches would provide a stronger empirical foundation for understanding the interplay between values, eco-emotions, and coping strategies. This mixed-methods approach would strengthen the empirical foundations for interventions aimed at promoting climate resilience and mental wellbeing in culturally relevant ways.

5. CONCLUSION

This study examined emotional responses to climate and ecological crises among young adults living in urban contexts in Nepal and India. The study contributes to a more comprehensive and culturally situated understanding of eco-emotions, addressing significant gaps in a literature that has been dominated by Global North perspectives. It has provided in-depth insights into how individuals in Kathmandu and the Delhi national-capital-region emotionally experience climate and ecological crises and how they cope with associated eco-emotions. These findings foreground the role of lived-experience, cultural context, and meaning-making processes in shaping eco-emotional responses.

By moving beyond Global North-centric frameworks in the study of eco-emotions, this study demonstrates the value of culturally grounded, interdisciplinary approaches for capturing the complexity of eco-emotional experiences and informing equitable adaptation strategies. The research provides a foundation for future work aimed at refining theoretical models of eco-emotions, expanding empirical coverage across diverse populations, and translating emotional insights into meaningful policy and practice responses in the context of accelerating ecological and climate change.

Environmental change becomes emotionally salient when it intrudes upon embodied routines, collective rituals, agricultural rhythms, and place-based identity. These disruptions are appraised through cultural meaning systems and filtered through perceptions of political inaction, global injustice, and socioeconomic constraint. The resulting emotional responses are not singular states, but dynamic constellations in which anxiety, sadness, anger, hope, and helplessness coexist and fluctuate. Perceived agency functions as a central regulatory dimension within this process. Agency operates not as a fixed trait, but as an interpretive stance shaped by values, privilege, and structural positioning. Where agency is experienced as viable (through collective action, behavioural modification, or moral responsibility) distressing eco-emotions may be channelled into constructive coping. Where agency is experienced as constrained, coping shifts towards psychological distancing strategies that regulate distress under conditions of limited structural change. Coping, in turn, reshapes appraisal processes. Meaning-focussed reframing, behavioural engagement, resignation, or fatalism influence how subsequent ecological disruptions are interpreted and emotionally shaped. The process is therefore recursive rather than linear: appraisal shapes emotion; emotion shapes coping; coping reshapes appraisal.
