Ethics Approval Check

Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, and it was present:

Half of participants were told which language each sign belonged to, whereas the other half received no such information.

Yet, WEIRD participants are typically familiar with the cultures and societies in which these languages are used, i.e. the US and France.

Furthermore, if the aesthetic evaluations in the informed participants are influenced by sociocultural associations, they are predicted to correspond to the participants’ cultural preferences: participants who prefer French over US-American culture are predicted to evaluate LSF as more aesthetically appealing, whereas participants who prefer US-American over French culture are predicted to evaluate ASL as more aesthetically appealing.

In contrast, uninformed participants are predicted to give similar ratings to ASL and LSF because they cannot draw on sociocultural associations linked to these languages.

Taking these insights into consideration, we aimed to explore four related questions: (i) whether the participants are able to infer the valence of signs from their manual components; (ii) whether this perceived valence correlates with perceived aesthetic appeal; (iii) whether the participants see iconic potential in the observed signs; and (iv) whether perceived iconicity is related to their ability to infer valence.

Participants were randomly assigned to an informed group and an uninformed group.

In the informed group, participants were informed by a flag (i.e. the US-American or French flag) in the top left corner of the video which language each sign belonged to (Figure 1).

Before the experiment, participants completed a questionnaire that asked whether they knew any sign language and how frequently they encountered sign language in their daily lives.

During the appeal and valence phase, participants were not informed about the meanings of the signs they were rating.

After viewing each clip, participants provided their ratings, answering the questions “How appealing do you find the look of the sign?”

Participants were instructed to watch each clip only once and to provide intuitive judgments.

After completing all ratings, participants filled in a post-experiment questionnaire.

After completing the experiment, there was a short debriefing and participants were thanked for their participation.

We recruited participants from across Europe, and in each of the two experimental groups we obtained a balanced distribution of native language backgrounds: around 25% Germanic, 25% Romance, 25% Slavic, and 25% other language families.

A further inclusion criterion was that participants had no prior knowledge of or experience with sign languages.

These participants were excluded from the analysis.

All of our participants were hearing.

After applying these exclusion criteria, the final sample consisted of 93 participants in the informed group (female: n = 39, male: n = 51, other: n = 3; mean age = 32.83 ± 9.46; language background: Germanic: n = 26, Romance: n = 25, Slavic: n = 22, other: n = 20) and 102 participants in the uninformed group (female: n = 48, male: n = 52, other: n = 2; mean age = 35.89 ± 11.21; language background: Germanic: n = 34, Romance: n = 26, Slavic: n = 22, other: n = 20).

The participants were recruited via the crowdsourcing platform Prolific (prolific.com, 2025) and received £3.50 for their participation.

The study protocol was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee at the University of Vienna (reference number: 01314) and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Simply knowing which language was being evaluated was thus sufficient to shape aesthetic judgements, even though participants had no prior experience with either language.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Aesthetic evaluations of languages depend not only on the linguistic signal but also on language-external factors, notably familiarity with a language and the sociocultural associations triggered by recognising it. For spoken languages, these factors are hard to separate, because languages evoking strong cultural associations are usually also familiar. We used sign languages to isolate the influence of sociocultural associations, testing hearing nonsigners who were unfamiliar with American (ASL) and French (LSF) Sign Language but familiar with the associated cultures. Participants rated 64 signs (32 ASL, 32 LSF; half positively, half negatively valenced) for aesthetic appeal, perceived valence and, once given the meaning, perceived iconicity. Half of participants were told which language each sign belonged to, whereas the other half received no such information. Appeal ratings for ASL and LSF differed only when the languages were indicated: the more participants liked French relative to US-American culture, the more appealing they found LSF relative to ASL, and vice versa. No such pattern emerged without this information. Sociocultural associations can thus shape aesthetic evaluation of a language independently of direct linguistic experience. Additionally, although participants did not know the signs' meanings, positively valenced signs were rated as more positive and, particularly in ASL, more appealing than negatively valenced signs. This suggests that aesthetic and affective evaluations of signs are closely linked. Finally, more iconic signs received valence ratings more closely aligned with their actual valence. To our knowledge, this is the first extension of (phon)aesthetic research to sign languages.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1. Phonaesthetics

Language is not only a means of communication but also an object of aesthetic evaluation. In everyday discourse, speakers and listeners frequently describe languages with terms such as "beautiful", "pleasant", or "melodic", but also "harsh", or "unpleasant". These descriptions indicate that linguistic forms can evoke affective responses beyond their communicative function (Nemestothy et al., 2025). Such aesthetic evaluations can have important social consequences, since they may shape attitudes towards speakers, and may influence processes such as language learning, intercultural communication and social interaction more generally. Consequently, in recent years, aesthetic evaluations of language have also received increasing scientific attention. This has given a renewed momentum to the field of phonaesthetics, which investigates how people perceive and evaluate the aesthetic qualities of languages and linguistic patterns (Nemestothy et al., 2025; Crystal, 1995).

A central question in phonaesthetic research is what people evaluate when they judge the aesthetic appeal of a language. On the one hand, aesthetic evaluations may reflect properties of the linguistic signal itself, including its phonetic and prosodic characteristics (Reiterer et al., 2020; Kogan & Reiterer, 2021; Matzinger et al., 2021). On the other hand, they may also be influenced by language-external factors, such as familiarity with the language or sociocultural stereotypes associated with it (Anikin et al., 2023; Nemestothy et al., 2025). Because these different influences typically co-occur in natural language perception, disentangling their relative contribution remains one of the main challenges in understanding the aesthetic evaluation of languages.

Previous research has shown systematic differences in the perceived aesthetic appeal of languages. For example, Reiterer et al. (2020) and Kogan and Reiterer (2021) found that Romance languages, particularly French and Italian, consistently received higher appeal ratings than, for example, German and several Slavic languages -a pattern they termed the "Latin Lover Effect". They also identified several phonetic and prosodic characteristics that were common among highly rated languages, including high sonority levels, a high proportion of vowels, "CV" syllable structures (Reiterer et al., 2020), flat prosodic contours, and fast speech tempo (Kogan & Reiterer, 2021). Studies using unfamiliar natural languages (Kogan et al., 2026), fantasy languages (Mooshammer et al., 2024) and experimentally constructed languages (Matzinger et al., 2021; Matzinger & Košić, 2025) likewise suggest that characteristics inherent to the linguistic signal can influence aesthetic evaluations, although the reported effects are generally modest. For example, Matzinger et al. (2021) showed that phrase-final lengthening, a prosodic feature in which the final syllables of words are produced with a longer duration than preceding syllables, increased the perceived appeal of artificial words, but only slightly.

There is growing evidence that language-internal properties alone are not sufficient to explain aesthetic evaluations. Reiterer et al. (2020) and Kogan and Reiterer (2021) noted that many of the languages evaluated in their studies, including those that contributed to the "Latin Lover Effect", were at least somewhat familiar to the participants, who were predominantly from WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) Central European backgrounds. Thus, the evaluated languages were likely not only evaluated in terms of their intrinsic appeal but also associated with existing cultural knowledge, personal experiences, and stereotypes that may have influenced their ratings (Nemestothy et al., 2025). The importance of such external influences is further underscored by Anikin et al. (2023). In a large-scale study that included a broad range of mostly non-WEIRD languages and participants from diverse linguistic backgrounds, they found no consistent evidence that some languages were universally perceived as more aesthetically appealing than others. Moreover, none of the phonetic features they investigated, including the presence of specific phonemic classes and the overall size of the phonetic repertoire of a language, showed a reliable correlation with pleasantness ratings. Together, these findings suggest that aesthetic evaluations of languages are not determined solely by their phonetic and prosodic properties but are substantially influenced by external factors that become available when listeners recognize languages.

The present study aims to investigate these language-external influences in more detail. We distinguish between two conceptually distinct forms of language-external influence: a) linguistic familiarity, i.e. familiarity with the language itself and b) sociocultural associations linked to the language. Linguistic familiarity refers to direct experience with the linguistic system, for example through language learning, media exposure, travel, or other forms of contact with a language (Giles & Coupland, 1991). In contrast, sociocultural associations arise from the broader knowledge and beliefs associated with the cultures, nations, and social groups that a language represents. This distinction is consistent with the framework proposed by Nemestothy et al. (2025), which differentiates between three different influences on aesthetic appeal: inherent value (cf. Bourhis et al., 1979; Van Bezooijen 2002), imposed norms (cf. Giles & Niedzielski, 1998), and personal or emotional influences. Within this framework, inherent value corresponds to language-internal factors, whereas imposed norms and personal or emotional influences represent different forms of language-external factors. Linguistic familiarity largely corresponds to personal or emotional influences, whereas sociocultural associations largely correspond to imposed norms, although the categories proposed by Nemestothy et al. (2025) are slightly broader and encompass additional influences (e.g. personal influences also include factors such as a person's musicality or personality type). Even though linguistic familiarity and sociocultural associations frequently co-occur, they reflect distinct mechanisms and may influence aesthetic evaluations in different ways.

One explanation for how familiarity with a language can affect aesthetic preferences can be found in the mere-exposure effect, which describes the tendency to develop more positive evaluations of stimuli simply because they have been encountered repeatedly (Zajonc, 1968; Bornstein & Craver-Lemley, 2022). This effect, which has been demonstrated, for example, in the domain of music and visual art (Madison & Schiölde, 2017; Leder, 2001), is thought to arise because repeated exposure increases processing fluency, i.e. the ease with which a stimulus can be perceived and processed by the brain (Reber et al., 2004; Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009; Winkielman & Cacioppo, 2001; Dragojevic & Giles, 2016). Processing fluency is closely related to predictability: through repeated exposure, stimuli become easier to anticipate, which facilitates their cognitive processing and creates a sense of mental ease, comfort, and safety (Reber et al., 2004; Winkielman et al., 2012). In the context of language, familiar sound patterns may receive higher aesthetic evaluations because they are processed more fluently. Importantly, familiarity effects do not necessarily require the conscious recognition of a language. Similarities between a target language and the listener's native language, in such terms as phonotactic probabilities (cf. Vitevitch & Luce, 1999) or prosodic patterns, may also contribute to familiarity-based evaluations, even when listeners are unable to identify the language itself (Mooshammer et al., 2024).

Previous experiences with a language may also create positive affective associations that go beyond perceptual processing. For example, learning a language may be associated with feelings of competence, achievement, or personal development (Dörnyei, 2009; Mercer & Williams, 2014). Similarly, successful communication, meaningful interactions, or personally relevant experiences when using a particular language can positively influence aesthetic evaluations of that language (Giles & Coupland, 1991; Kristiansen, 2009). Finally, recognizing a language during an experiment may influence evaluations, as recognition can evoke positive feelings of success and competence (Zajonc, 1968; Winkielman et al., 2006).

In contrast, recognizing a language may also activate sociocultural associations that are distinct from direct experience with the linguistic system itself. This is because languages function as important markers of social and cultural identity. At a collective level, their recognition can trigger the access of culturally shared knowledge and beliefs about groups represented by a language. Such beliefs are shaped by relations of power as well as historical, political, economic, and societal processes that influence how groups are positioned within a society (Nemestothy et al., 2025; Busch, 2019). Consequently, listeners may associate a language with prestige, status, sophistication, or modernity, even though such evaluations may ultimately reflect evaluations of the social group associated with the language rather than the language itself (Edwards, 2009; Garrett et al., 2003). These sociocultural associations may influence language evaluations even in the absence of substantial experience with the language itself. Simply recognizing the cultural match of a language may be sufficient to evoke such associations. For example, the common perceptions of the German language as harsh and orderly, and Italian as erotic and soft may reflect the broader stereotypes that portray German culture as disciplined and strict and Italian culture as romantic and elegant (Kogan & Reiterer, 2021; Nemestothy et al., 2025; cf. Giles & Niedzielski, 1998). Through education, media, and public discourse, these shared beliefs become reinforced and form part of broader language ideologies that shape language perception (Busch, 2019).

Sociocultural associations are not limited to socially shared beliefs and stereotypes. They may also develop at an individual level through personal experiences with the cultures, countries, and social groups associated with a language. Positive experiences, for example through friendships, or exposure to food, lifestyle, music or other traditions of a particular linguistic group, may create favourable associations (Nemestothy et al., 2025). Thus, languages may be evaluated more positively not only because of shared cultural associations but also because they are connected to personally meaningful experiences and positive memories of the cultures where they are used.

Although linguistic familiarity and sociocultural associations are conceptually distinct, they are closely intertwined during natural language perception and are therefore difficult to disentangle empirically. For example, Reiterer et al. (2020) estimated that "familiarity with a language" accounted for approximately 40% of the variance in phonaesthetic ratings (cf. Nemestothy et al., 2025). However, in their study, familiarity was assessed through language recognition. As a consequence, this measure likely captured not only previous experience with the linguistic system itself but also the sociocultural associations that recognition automatically activates. Thus, it remains unclear to what extent previous findings attributed to familiarity actually reflect direct linguistic experience with a language, sociocultural associations, or a combination of both.

A major obstacle to disentangling these two language-external factors is methodological. A straightforward way to experimentally isolate the influence of sociocultural associations would be to present participants with languages that are unfamiliar to them, while manipulating explicit information about the languages' identity. Under these conditions, any difference in aesthetic evaluations between informed and uninformed participants could be attributed primarily to the activation of sociocultural associations rather than to prior linguistic experience. However, implementing such a design with spoken languages is difficult. For example, in WEIRD populations, languages that evoke strong sociocultural associations, such as French, Italian, or German, are typically also familiar and readily recognized (de Mareüil et al., 2005; Vieru et al., 2011). Conversely, languages that are sufficiently unfamiliar to prevent recognition usually evoke only weak sociocultural associations, as participants often have little knowledge of the cultures and social groups they are linked to (Gumperz, 1979). This makes it difficult to separate the effects of linguistic familiarity from those of sociocultural knowledge.

To overcome this limitation, in our study, we expand (phon)aesthetic research to sign languagesfoot_0 . Familiarity with and recognition of sign languages are generally limited amongst the hearing population (Pasikowska-Schnass, 2018). Thus, testing the appeal of sign languages in hearing individuals allows us to experimentally manipulate the knowledge that people have about the identities of the languages they are evaluating and, in turn, to control the activation of sociocultural associations -independently of linguistic familiarity. Specifically, we examine American Sign Language (ASL) and French Sign Language (LSF). It is not known how many hearing individuals from WEIRD populations are familiar with these specific sign languages; we do know, however, that this familiarity is limited within Deaf communities themselves (Mitchell & Young, 2023) and thus can also be expected to be low amongst hearing individuals. Yet, WEIRD participants are typically familiar with the cultures and societies in which these languages are used, i.e. the US and France.

We hypothesize that explicit knowledge about a language's identity, and the sociocultural associations it evokes, influence the aesthetic evaluations of that language (H1). Specifically, participants who are informed about whether they are rating ASL or LSF are hypothesized to show different appeal ratings than participants who are unaware of the languages' identities. Furthermore, if the aesthetic evaluations in the informed participants are influenced by sociocultural associations, they are predicted to correspond to the participants' cultural preferences: participants who prefer French over US-American culture are predicted to evaluate LSF as more aesthetically appealing, whereas participants who prefer US-American over French culture are predicted to evaluate ASL as more aesthetically appealing. In contrast, uninformed participants are predicted to give similar ratings to ASL and LSF because they cannot draw on sociocultural associations linked to these languages.

If explicit knowledge about the languages' identities does not influence aesthetic evaluations (H0), this would suggest that sociocultural associations contribute little to language preferences and that previously reported language-external effects for spoken languages may instead reflect familiarity and prior experience with the language itself.

1.2. Valence and iconicity

In addition to the main question of how linguistic familiarity and sociocultural associations influence perceived aesthetic appeal, the present study addressed a secondary question. We asked whether participants could infer the semantic valence of signs from their form, particularly from their manual components, and whether aesthetic evaluations correlated with this perceived semantic valence. Previous research has suggested a possible relationship between the meaning of linguistic forms and their aesthetic appeal, but this relationship has not yet been directly investigated (Crystal, 1995). For example, Crystal (1995) anecdotally asked people to list the words that sounded most beautiful to them and concluded that most of the words they listed had positive meanings. Our aim was to explore this relationship more systematically in sign languages and to examine whether valence was also correlated with aesthetic appeal when participants did not know the meanings of the signs but could just infer their valence from their form.

A growing body of research suggests that semantic valence, i.e. the positive versus negative dimension of meaning, is not always encoded arbitrarily in linguistic form, but can instead be iconically motivated both in the sounds of spoken language and in the spatial forms of sign. In spoken language, Thorndike (1946) offered one of the earliest attempts to quantify this link by compiling lists of English words with positive and negative meanings and examining the sounds that occurred in them. He found that certain phonemes, such as /ʒ/, occurred disproportionately in positive words, whereas others, such as /ʌ/, were more common in negative ones (as cited in Sidhu, 2024). Other studies have also identified associations between sounds and affective meanings. For example, Adelman et al. (2018) found that the duration of initial phonemes predicted the valence of words, with longer and shorter initial phonemes predicting positive and negative valence, respectively. Aryani et al. (2018) found that unvoiced consonants and sibilants were associated with negative valence, while voiced consonants were associated with positive valence. Recent work further suggests that sonorants and front unrounded vowels are associated with positive meanings, whereas obstruents and back rounded vowels are associated with negative meanings (Dorsi et al., 2026 (preprint); Körner & Rummer, 2022). Yu et al. (2021) investigated potential explanations for the links between particular phonemes and valence, and proposed that the vowel /i/ is iconically tied to positive valence because its articulation recruits the zygomaticus major muscle involved in smiling, whereas /ʌ/ is associated with negative valence through its resemblance to negative facial affect. Thus, although the specific iconic form-valence correspondences vary across studies, the literature suggests that phonological form can systematically bias the perceived valence of words.

In a tonal language, Zheng et al. (2025) found that Mandarin lexical tone sequences systematically bias emotional ratings, such that "higher pitch level, wider pitch range, and upward pitch contour direction are likely associated with positive valence" (2025, p. 397), a pattern that the authors link to the broader iconicity of pitch in emotional prosody rather than to any inherent meaning of the tones themselves.

Crucially, this valence-iconicity relationship is not confined to the auditory-vocal channel but also occurs in the visual-spatial modality. Yap et al. (2014) showed that the same "Good is Up / Bad is Down" metaphor that shapes pitch-valence associations in Mandarin (Zheng et al., 2025) also structures the vertical movement of signs in three unrelated signed languages. Analyzing American Sign Language, French Sign Language, and British Sign Language, they found that "on average, signs with upward lexical movements were the most positive in valence, and signs with downward movements the most negative," with non-vertical signs falling in between (Yap et al., 2014 (Yap et al., , p. 1812)). Because this pattern held across genetically unrelated sign languages and paralleled findings in the tonal domain of Mandarin, the authors argue that "implicit space-valence metaphors are encoded in the forms of words in both signed and spoken languages" (Yap et al., 2014 (Yap et al., , p. 1808)), constituting a previously unrecognized source of non-arbitrariness in the lexicon that holds across modalities. These findings are particularly relevant to the present study because they suggest that semantic valence may be associated with perceptible properties of a sign's form, rather than being accessible only through knowledge of the sign's conventional meaning.

Iconicity, the direct, motivated resemblance between a linguistic form and its referent, is far more pervasive in sign languages than in spoken languages and has become a central object of study in its own right, rather than a marginal exception to linguistic arbitrariness (Perniss et al., 2010). Sign languages provide particularly rich opportunities for form-meaning correspondences because iconicity can arise from several components of a sign, including manual and non-manual ones. Sign languages routinely encode positive and negative meaning through the co-occurrence of a manual sign, which has components such as handshape, location or movement, with non-manual markers such as facial expression, head tilt, or body posture (Perniss et al., 2010). In Polish Sign Language (PJM), for example, the sign glossed GOOD is articulated with an "OK" handshape accompanied by a head nod, a non-manual marker that carries connotations of approval within Polish culture, whereas the sign glossed BAD is articulated with a "letter-X" handshape accompanied by a furrowed brow and a head shake, a non-manual marker conventionally associated with disapproval. This manual-non-manual pairing parallels patterns reported for valence marking in ASL and LSF (Yap et al., 2014). Independent of non-manual marking, the manual articulation itself appears to be organized in part around the conceptual metaphor. The ASL signs STRONG and WEAK illustrate this pattern: STRONG is produced with an upward movement and tensed musculature, whereas WEAK is produced with a downward movement and limp fingers. Other signs appear to draw on a distinct, more direct form of iconicity: the ASL sign SCARY, for instance, is produced with a trembling hand movement that visually resembles bodily shivering (see: spreadthesign.com, 2025). These observations suggest that valence in sign languages may be conveyed not only through conventionalized non-manual marking but also through iconic and metaphoric properties of the manual articulation itself: a form-meaning correspondence that should, in principle, be recoverable by naïve sign raters with no knowledge of the target sign language.

Previous research on meaning perception suggests that certain aspects of the meaning of unfamiliar signs can, to some extent, be perceived from their form alone, although exact meaning identification is generally limited. Sehyr and Emmorey (2019), for example, found that hearing non-signers correctly guessed the meanings of around 10% of ASL signs, with more iconic signs showing higher semantic transparency and greater guessing accuracy. Their participants could correctly guess the meanings of some unfamiliar signs when presented with neutral facial expressions and no mouthings, and existing gestural knowledge facilitated the interpretation of novel signs. Similarly, Thompson et al. (2010) found that British Sign Language (BSL) signers responded differently to different iconic signs in a phonological handshape-judgment task. Thus, when a sign is iconic, its manual form appears to play an important role in the activation or retrieval of its meaning. However, the potential of the manual component to provide cues to meaning has not yet been fully explored, especially in the context of semantic valence.

Taking these insights into consideration, we aimed to explore four related questions: (i) whether the participants are able to infer the valence of signs from their manual components; (ii) whether this perceived valence correlates with perceived aesthetic appeal; (iii) whether the participants see iconic potential in the observed signs; and (iv) whether perceived iconicity is related to their ability to infer valence.

Potential links between valence and aesthetic value have, to our best knowledge, not been researched in sign languages.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Experimental conditions and procedure

We conducted an online experiment in Labvanced (Finger et al., 2017), in which participants rated video recordings of signs along three dimensions: perceived appeal, perceived valence, and perceived iconicity. Half of the signs were from American Sign Language (ASL) and half from French Sign Language (LSF). Within each language, half of the signs had positive valence and half had negative valence.

Participants were randomly assigned to an informed group and an uninformed group. In the informed group, participants were informed by a flag (i.e. the US-American or French flag) in the top left corner of the video which language each sign belonged to (Figure 1). In the uninformed group, participants received no information about the languages' identities. Before the experiment, participants completed a questionnaire that asked whether they knew any sign language and how frequently they encountered sign language in their daily lives.

The experiment consisted of two phases. In the first phase, participants rated the signs' appeal and valence, and in the second phase, they rated the signs' iconicity. During the appeal and valence phase, participants were not informed about the meanings of the signs they were rating. During the iconicity phase, however, the meaning of each sign was displayed in the corner of the video clip (Figure 1, right). Between the two phases, participants could optionally take a self-timed break of up to two minutes. After viewing each clip, participants provided their ratings, answering the questions "How appealing do you find the look of the sign?" (for appeal), "Do you think the meaning of this sign is positive or negative?" (for valence), and "How iconic do you find the sign?" (for iconicity) on a continuous scale from 1 to 100.1 represented the lowest rating ("not appealing at all", "very negative", "not iconic at all") and 100 represented the highest rating ("very appealing", "very positive", "very iconic"). Participants were instructed to watch each clip only once and to provide intuitive judgments. The clips were presented in random order.

As we assumed that participants would not be familiar with the concept of iconicity, they received a brief explanation of iconicity before the second rating phase. This explanation included an example, and the rating scale was explained to ensure that participants understood the task. We did not provide a more detailed explanation of the term "appeal", as formal definitions would likely not reduce its inherent subjectivity or prevent variation in individual interpretations (Aleem et al., 2019; Vashi & Quay, 2015).

After completing all ratings, participants filled in a post-experiment questionnaire. This questionnaire included demographic questions concerning age, gender, country of residence, and language background, as well as questions about the participants' attitudes towards French and US-American culture ("How much do you like French/US-American culture?). These attitudes were assessed separately on continuous scales ranging from 1 ("not at all") to 100 ("very much"). After completing the experiment, there was a short debriefing and participants were thanked for their participation. In total, the study took approximately 30 minutes to complete.

2.2. Participants and groups

Given that we expected relatively small effects, we aimed to recruit 100 participants for each of the two experimental groups (informed and uninformed). The study was designed to approximate the typical WEIRD participant samples that are commonly used in research on the phonaesthetics of spoken languages (e.g. Kogan & Reiterer, 2021), in order to facilitate comparability with findings on the perceived appeal of spoken languages. Thus, we aimed for a sample that was broadly WEIRD but still sufficiently diverse in terms of linguistic background.

We recruited participants from across Europe, and in each of the two experimental groups we obtained a balanced distribution of native language backgrounds: around 25% Germanic, 25% Romance, 25% Slavic, and 25% other language families. The "other" category included participants with other European language backgrounds as well as participants living in Europe whose native languages were not necessarily European. In the final sample, this group comprised participants with a wide range of native languages, including Albanian, Arabic, Chinese, Estonian, Finnish, Greek, Hungarian, Japanese, Persian, Tagalog, Urdu, Welsh, Bengali, Hindi, Indonesian, Latvian, Lithuanian, Swahili, Turkish, and Thai. To minimize potential biases related to being part of the target cultures, we excluded participants born in France or the United States, as well as participants currently residing in France. Participants residing in the United States were not included by design, as recruitment was restricted to Europe.

A further inclusion criterion was that participants had no prior knowledge of or experience with sign languages. This ensured that any aesthetic judgement elicited during the experiment reflected responses to the visual properties of the stimuli or the cultural manipulations, rather than to the signs' semantic content. Although this requirement was specified in the recruitment instructions, some participants indicated in the pre-experiment questionnaire that they either knew sign language or encountered sign language regularly. These participants were excluded from the analysis. The final sample included only participants who reported no knowledge of sign language and who indicated that they encountered sign languages in daily life "never" or "rarely". All of our participants were hearing.

After applying these exclusion criteria, the final sample consisted of 93 participants in the informed group (female: n = 39, male: n = 51, other: n = 3; mean age = 32.83 ± 9.46; language background: Germanic: n = 26, Romance: n = 25, Slavic: n = 22, other: n = 20) and 102 participants in the uninformed group (female: n = 48, male: n = 52, other: n = 2; mean age = 35.89 ± 11.21; language background: Germanic: n = 34, Romance: n = 26, Slavic: n = 22, other: n = 20). The participants were recruited via the crowdsourcing platform Prolific (prolific.com, 2025) and received £3.50 for their participation. The study protocol was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee at the University of Vienna (reference number: 01314) and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.3. Stimuli

The two sign languages selected for this study were American Sign Language (ASL) and French Sign Language (LSF). ASL and LSF belong to the same language family and are historically related, as ASL developed in part from LSF, following the introduction of LSF to the United States by Laurent Clerc in the early 19th century (Power, 2022). Despite this common structural ancestry, the two have since diverged into distinct, fully autonomous linguistic systems (Woodward, 1978; Schlehofer, 2016; Supalla & Clark, 2015). Their genealogical relationship makes ASL and LSF a principled pair for comparison, as it provides some degree of structural comparability while still treating them as distinct languages.

The stimulus set comprised 64 signs, with 32 signs from each language. The same 32 concepts were represented in ASL and LSF, but the corresponding signs differed in their manual articulation across the two languages. Half, i.e. 16, of the signs in each language had positive and half had negative semantic valence, based on the norms reported by Warriner et al. (2013; see Table 1). To select the signs for the study, we proceeded through the positively valenced words on Warriner et al.'s (2013) list, starting with the first items and selecting nouns for which the corresponding ASL and LSF signs differed in their manual articulation. We then selected the corresponding antonyms, provided that these also had distinct ASL and LSF realizations.

The videos were created by a hearing Polish Sign Language (PJM) user who, following an extensive training, recorded all ASL and LSF signs in one sitting, using one Sony HDR CX-405 camera positioned opposite the signer. All vocabulary items were sourced from Spread the Sign (spreadthesign.com, date of access: 15.5.2025), a large multilingual online dictionary for sign languages, created by the European Sign Language Center (ESLC, 2026). The signer's face was blurred in each video using the deface command-line Python tool. This was done to control for the potential influence of facial appearance and facial expressions on aesthetic evaluations and to focus the stimuli on the manual form of the signs. The videos were then clipped to show individual signs and edited to include or omit the language-identifying flags and descriptions of the semantic content of the signs. The resulting videos were approximately 5 seconds long.

2.4.1. The influence of language knowledge and cultural preference on appeal ratings

To test whether the aesthetic perception of signs was influenced by language knowledge, i.e. the participants' awareness of which language they were rating, and cultural preference, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model. The dependent variable was participants' appeal ratings of the displayed signs. The fixed effects of primary interest were Group (informed vs. uninformed), Language (LSF vs. ASL), and Culture preference, as well as their interactions.

Valence of the stimuli (positive vs. negative) and trial number were included as covariates to control for stimulus-related properties and potential order effects. Valence was also of theoretical interest, as we expected differences in perceived appeal between positively and negatively valenced stimuli. Culture preference was operationalised as a continuous variable based on participants' self-reported ratings of their attitude towards French and US-American culture in the postexperimental questionnaire. Specifically, for the measure of culture preference, US-American liking scores were subtracted from French liking scores. Thus, positive values indicate a preference for French culture and negative values indicate a preference for US-American culture.

Initially, we specified a maximal random-effects structure that included random intercepts for participants and signs (i.e. meanings), as well as random slopes for Language and Valence within participants and a random slope of Language within signs. This approach was used to account for repeated-measures dependencies in the data and to reduce the risk of inflated Type I error rates (Barr et al., 2013; Schielzeth & Forstmeier, 2009). This model did not converge and showed signs of overparameterisation, as indicated by a singular fit. We therefore simplified the random-effects structure by sequentially removing random slopes until model convergence and a non-singular fit were achieved. We retained the random intercepts for participants and signs.

This resulted in the final model: Appeal Rating ~ Group * Language * CulturePref + Valence + Trial_Nr + (1 | Participant) + (1 | Sign). The uninformed group, ASL, and negative valence were set as reference levels in the model. Overall, the sample size of the model was 12,480 data points (195 participants, each rating 64 signs). The model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation for final parameter estimation. We used a likelihood ratio test to test the overall significance of the full model as compared with a null model comprising only the control predictors (i.e. valence and trial number) and the random effects (R function anova; Dobson, 2002). For this comparison, both models were refitted using maximum likelihood estimation.

To further examine the significant three-way interaction between Group, Language, and Culture preference, we conducted follow-up simple slopes analyses (Aiken et al., 1991). Specifically, we estimated the effect of Culture preference on ratings separately for each combination of Group (informed vs. uninformed) and Language (LSF vs. ASL). These analyses were implemented using estimated marginal trends as implemented in the emtrends function from the emmeans package in R (Lenth & Piaskowski, 2026), which allows the estimation of regression slopes for continuous predictors within mixed-effects models.

2.4.2. The influence of valence on appeal and perceived valence ratings

To examine more closely how semantic valence influenced the participants' evaluations of the signs and whether this influence differed between ASL and LSF, we conducted two additional mixed-effects analyses.

The first analysis used appeal ratings as the dependent variablefoot_1 . The fixed effects included Valence (positive vs. negative), Language (LSF vs. ASL) and their interaction as main effects, as well as Group, Culture preference, and trial number as covariates. The final model was specified as: Appeal Rating ~ Valence * Language + Group + CulturePref + Trial_Nr + (1 + Valence | Participant) + (1 | Sign). The inclusion of a by-participant random slope for Valence, alongside random intercepts of participant and sign, improved the model fit and did not result in convergence problems or singularity.

The second analysis used participants' valence ratings as the dependent variable. The fixed-effects structure mirrored the appeal model and included Valence, Language, and their interaction as fixed effects, and Group, Culture preference, and trial number as control variables. The final model was specified as: Valence Rating ~ Valence * Language + Group + CulturePref + Trial_Nr + (1 | Participant) + ( 1 | Sign). An initial model including a byparticipant random slope for Valence resulted in singularity. Thus, only random intercepts of participant and sign were included.

The overall contribution of Valence, Language and their interaction was assessed via likelihood-ratio tests that compared the full models to respective null models that only contained the control predictors (i.e. Group and Culture preference) and the random effects (R function anova; Dobson, 2002).

Additionally, we assessed the relationship between appeal and valence ratings. To do that, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model that used the participants' appeal ratings as the dependent variable and the perceived valence ratings as a fixed effect. We also added the control variables Valence, Language, Group and Culture preference, as well as random intercepts for participants and signs. The final model was specified as: Appeal Rating ~ Valence Rating + Valence + Language + Group + CulturePref + (1 | Participant) + (1 | Sign).

2.4.4. General information

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2). The models were created using the lmer function from the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). For all models, we visually inspected the residuals plotted against fitted values as well as a Q-Q plot. This indicated that the assumptions of normally distributed and homogeneous residuals were fulfilled and that there were no obvious deviations from normality or homoscedasticity.

3.1. The influence of language knowledge and culture preference on appeal ratings

A comparison of the full model to the null model containing only Valence, trial number, and random effects indicated that the inclusion of Group, Language, and Culture preference significantly improved model fit (χ²(7) = 16.29, p = .023). This suggests that the experimental predictors explained additional variance in the appeal ratings beyond the control variables. The model explained a small proportion of variance at the level of fixed effects (R²m = 0.015), while the model including random effects accounted for substantially more variance (R²c = 0.209). This indicates that substantial variability in the appeal ratings was driven by betweenparticipant and between-stimulus differences rather than the experimental predictors alone.

The critical test of our hypotheses was whether language knowledge influenced appeal ratings and whether this effect differed between LSF and ASL depending on cultural preference. This was supported by a significant three-way interaction between Group, Language, and Culture preference (Table 2). More specifically, the interaction showed that in the informed group, and when the language was LSF, cultural preference (i.e., a preference for French culture) had a positive influence on the appeal ratings. To further quantify this interaction, we conducted follow-up simple slopes analyses for each Group and Language combination. These analyses confirmed that in the informed group, the effect of Culture preference differed significantly between ASL and LSF, with opposite directional trends across the two languages. For LSF, higher cultural preference values (i.e., stronger preference for French culture) were associated with higher appeal ratings, whereas for ASL, lower cultural preference values (i.e., stronger preference for US-American culture) were associated with higher ratings. In contrast, in the uninformed group, neither LSF nor ASL showed a reliable association between Culture preference and ratings. Pairwise comparisons further confirmed that the slopes differed significantly between ASL and LSF within the informed group (estimate = -0.084, SE = 0.023, z = -3.72, p = .001), whereas no significant differences emerged in the uninformed group (Figure 2). Our model also revealed a significant main effect of Valence (Table 2), which indicates that participants give higher appeal ratings to positively valenced than to negatively valenced signs. This suggests that affective properties of the signs may influence aesthetic judgements, even when participants are not knowledgeable of the meanings of the signs they are evaluating. This will be explored in more detail in the next section.

3.2. The influence of valence on appeal and perceived valence ratings

For both the appeal and the valence ratings, the inclusion of Valence, Language and their interaction significantly improved model fit relative to a null model excluding these terms (appeal ratings: χ²(3) = 132.14, p < .001; valence ratings: χ²(3) = 172.17, p < .001). The model for appeal explained a small-to-moderate proportion of variance (R²m = 0.021, R²c = 0.217) and the model for valence explained a slightly higher proportion of variance (R²m = 0.039, R²c = 0.258). Across both models, this indicates that Valence and Language contributed meaningfully to explaining the variability in both appeal and valence judgments. The model for appeal ratings (Table 3) revealed a significant main effect of Valence, which indicates that positively valenced signs received higher appeal ratings than negatively valenced signs -even though participants were not informed about the valence of the signs (Figure 2). In addition, the model showed a significant interaction between Valence and Language, which indicates that the effect of Valence on appeal ratings differed between ASL and LSF. More specifically, the valence of signs influenced aesthetic judgments more strongly in ASL, so that the positive valence of a sign had a stronger positive effect on appeal ratings for ASL compared to LSF (Table 3). A comparable pattern emerged for perceived valence ratings. The model (Table 4) revealed a significant main effect of Valence, indicating that -even though participants were not informed about the meanings of the signs -positively valenced signs were rated as more positive than negatively valenced signs (Figure 3). Again, this effect was moderated by Language, as indicated by a significant interaction effect of Valence and Language. Similar to the results for appeal, the difference in perceived valence between positive and negative signs was more pronounced for ASL than for LSF, suggesting that the participants' sensitivity to valence cues varied across languages (Table 4). The model also indicated a small but significant effect of trial number, meaning that participants evaluated signs as slightly more negative as the experiment progressed.

Visual inspection of the confidence intervals (Figure 3) further indicated that the separation between positive and negative signs was more pronounced for perceived valence ratings than for appeal ratings. For example, in LSF, the confidence intervals for positive and negative signs were clearly separated in the valence ratings, whereas they overlapped in the appeal ratings. In our linear mixed-effects model examining the relationship between appeal and perceived valence ratings, higher perceived-valence ratings were associated with higher appeal ratings. Specifically, a 10-point increase in perceived valence was associated with an estimated 4.3point increase in perceived appeal (Table 5). 4. Discussion

We asked whether hearing non-signers' aesthetic evaluations of signs are influenced by their attitudes towards the cultures associated with two sign languages, ASL and LSF, and whether the semantic valence of unfamiliar signs can be perceived from their form alone. Cultural preference influenced appeal ratings only when participants knew which language they were evaluating: when language identity was disclosed, signs from the language associated with the preferred culture were judged more appealing. When it was withheld, cultural preference had no effect. Also, we found no evidence that signs from either language were intrinsically more appealing. Moreover, although participants did not know what the signs meant, positively valenced signs were rated as both more positive and more appealing. Exploratory analyses further indicated that signs perceived as more iconic elicited ratings more strongly aligned with their semantic valence (cf. Yap et al., 2014). We first discuss the role of language knowledge in aesthetic judgement, then the aesthetic appeal of sign languages more generally, and finally how valence may be conveyed by sign form.

4.1. Sociocultural associations in phonaesthetic perception

Our results show that explicit knowledge of a language's identity can influence its perceived aesthetic appeal. Participants who knew whether they were evaluating American Sign Language (ASL) or French Sign Language (LSF) gave different ratings from those who were not informed, and these differences followed cultural preferences: among informed participants, a stronger preference for French culture went with higher appeal ratings for LSF, and a stronger preference for US-American culture with higher ratings for ASL. Among uninformed participants, no corresponding difference emerged. Simply knowing which language was being evaluated was thus sufficient to shape aesthetic judgements, even though participants had no prior experience with either language. Although such sociocultural influences have been suggested before (e.g. Giles & Niedzielski, 1998; Anikin et al., 2023), they have been hard to separate experimentally from other language-external factors. In particular, when recognition-based measures have been used to evaluate the influence of language-external factors, it has remained unclear if aesthetic reactions towards a language arose from sociocultural associations that became activated when the language was recognized or from prior direct experience with the linguistic system and the environment where it is used, such as through language learning or travelling. By disentangling these influences, the present study shows that sociocultural associations can affect aesthetic evaluations independently of linguistic familiarity. Because the proposed mechanism, the activation of culturally shared knowledge once a language is identified, is not specific to the signed modality, it is plausible that this finding will extend to spoken languages. This does not mean, however, that other factors play no role. The relative influence of language-internal factors and of other language-external factors, such as familiarity, remains to be explored (cf. Nemestothy et al., 2025). Our paradigm offers a basis for testing familiarity experimentally: participants' exposure to one of the languages could be manipulated beforehand, for example through passive viewing or through more active learning (memorizing, recognizing or producing signs). Comparing such conditions would show whether, and to what extent, familiarity changes perceived appeal, and would help to distinguish simple perceptual exposure from active engagement with the linguistic system.

Other personal and emotional factors also deserve further exploration, particularly given the large perceiver-level variability in our study, which parallels observations from other studies, such as Kogan et al. (2026). Previously studied individual factors in spoken languages included listener characteristics such as personality (Winkler et al., 2023), age (Kogan et al., 2026) and musicality (Nemestothy et al., 2025; Kogan et al., 2026), as well as speaker characteristics such as voice quality (Reiterer et al., 2020) and speech intelligibility (Giles & Niedzielski, 1998; Van Bezooijen, 2002). Further individual characteristics of both perceivers and producers remain to be explored in the future.

A further refinement concerns how cultural preference is measured. We used a coarse measure, asking how much participants like a culture as a whole. This lumps together factors that may be evaluated very differently, such as cultural production (e.g. music, literature, film, cuisine), heritage and landscape (e.g. architecture, landmarks, scenery), the people and their way of life, and the political system and its history. Future studies could assess these aspects separately, since the sociocultural associations triggered by a language may draw more on some than on others. The effect may also be amplified for cultures that provoke particularly extreme or polarized reactions in the public.

Finally, our findings underline once more that research on the intrinsic appeal of languages should minimise language recognition as far as possible (cf. Nemestothy et al., 2025). For languages familiar to many potential participants, one option is to create artificial languages that model the phoneme inventory, phonotactic probabilities, phonological neighbourhoods or other distributional properties of real languages. This approach retains relevant characteristics of the linguistic signal but also prevents participants from identifying individual words or the language itself, as the stimuli consist of novel nonsense words.

However, this approach has an inherent limitation: preserving the linguistic characteristics of interest entails some similarity to the source language, so participants may still perceive the artificial language as "sounding like" a real one, in line with findings on fantasy languages built from natural-language characteristics (Mooshammer et al., 2024). Artificial languages may thus reduce explicit recognition effects but not necessarily implicit, subconscious ones.

4.2. The aesthetic appeal of sign languages

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic extension of (phon)aesthetic research to sign languages. The finding that hearing non-signers rated positively and negatively valenced signs differently in terms of appeal, discussed in more detail in the next section, suggests that signs are targets of differential aesthetic evaluation. Because participants did not know what the signs meant, these evaluations cannot rest on semantic knowledge and may reflect, at least in part, properties of the signs' visual form. This opens a new avenue for research on the aesthetic perception of language beyond the spoken modality. The present study should be regarded as a first step towards establishing sign languages as a domain for (phon)aesthetic research rather than as a comprehensive account of sign-language aesthetics. Several limitations and directions follow.

First, because we did not systematically manipulate or control the formal properties of the signs, we cannot say which of them contribute to appeal. Possible language-internal sources of aesthetic variation include the phonological parameters of handshape, location, orientation and movement (speed, direction, path), which future studies could vary across otherwise matched signs. For the same reason, the absence of an overall difference in appeal between ASL and LSF should be interpreted cautiously: our signs were not selected to test differences between the languages and may not represent the full range of characteristics of either of them. Determining whether whole sign languages differ in their aesthetic appeal would require a larger and more systematically controlled sign set and, for cross-linguistic comparison, a broader range of sign languages. Whereas spoken-language research can draw on standardized texts such as The North Wind and the Sun (Reiterer et al., 2020), to our knowledge, no comparable material exists for sign languages; narratives elicited with Frog, Where Are You? (Mayer, 1969) may offer a partial alternative.

A second limitation for making conclusions about the appeal of sign languages as such concerns the nature of the stimuli. We deliberately focused on manual components and excluded non-manual ones such as facial expression, eyebrow position, mouth gestures, body posture and body lean. This controlled the visual information presented, but non-manual components are integral to natural signing and carry substantial prosodic information (Sandler, 2010; Wilbur, 1999). Thus, a comprehensive account of sign-language aesthetics must consider both manual and non-manual components and how they jointly contribute to perceived appeal. Likewise, the aesthetics of a sign language as a whole cannot be inferred from isolated signs. Longer stretches of signing would allow the investigation of the temporal organisation or transitions between signs, which may have functions analogous to prosody in spoken languages.

A further limitation concerns the signer: all signs were produced by a single hearing, non-native signer, who had been trained in both languages. This ensured comparable proficiency across ASL and LSF, but it does not rule out that individual idiosyncrasies in articulation, or deviations from native production, influenced the ratings. Stimuli from several native signers of each language would address this.

An important next step would be to investigate aesthetic evaluations by native or experienced signers, including Deaf signers. Their extensive experience with signing may lead to different evaluations from those of people with little or no experience with sign languages, as they may attend to properties of signs that are less salient to sign-language-naïve participants (Morford et al. 2008). It would also be interesting to test whether familiarity with a particular sign language leads to more positive evaluations of that language than of others. Such an effect could reflect greater familiarity with the linguistic system itself or potential sociocultural associations, as suggested for spoken languages. Such work would clarify whether recognition effects generalize across modalities.

4.3. Perceived valence

Our results suggest that non-signers were able to infer the semantic valence of the signs in our stimulus sample from their manual form alone: positively valenced signs were perceived as more positive than negatively valenced signs and also received higher appeal ratings. These effects were stronger for ASL than for LSF, which may indicate that cues to valence are more available or salient in ASL, but may equally reflect the particular set of signs used. Exploratory analyses showed that perceived iconicity modulated valence ratings: more iconic signs received more extreme ratings in the direction of their semantic valence. Because we removed nonmanual components, a channel through which affect is normally conveyed (Massaro & Egan, 1996), our results may even underestimate non-signers' ability to detect valence; at the same time, they show that the manual components alone carry valence-relevant information.

A particularly intriguing finding is that perceived valence and aesthetic appeal were closely linked, even though participants did not know what the signs meant and could only infer their valence from form. However, the correlational association between perceived valence and appeal does not establish the causalities behind it: participants may have found signs appealing because they perceived them as positive, or perceived them as positive because they found them appealing. Notably, the differences between positively and negatively valenced signs were larger for perceived valence than for appeal. This is to be expected, as valence is precisely the dimension along which the two sets of stimuli were defined. The pattern is also compatible with a sequence in which participants first estimate valence and then base their appeal judgement partly on that estimate, making the appeal difference an attenuated reflection of the valence difference. This remains speculative, however, since the same pattern would arise if both judgements drew, to different degrees, on a common underlying source. One candidate is processing fluency: more easily processed stimuli tend to be judged as both more aesthetically pleasing (Reber et al., 2004) and more positive (Winkielman & Cacioppo, 2001), so fluency differences across signs could produce the correlation without either judgement directly shaping the other. Fluency alone could not, however, explain the effect of the signs' actual semantic valence, unless our positive and negative signs happened to differ in processing ease, a possibility that our design cannot rule out.

Processing fluency also complicates the interpretation of the iconicity effect. Our design cannot establish whether iconicity influenced valence and appeal judgments directly, or indirectly by allowing participants to arrive at a meaning more quickly or confidently, which in turn shaped their evaluations. Iconicity has been shown to facilitate lexical processing in signers (Thompson et al., 2010; Vinson et al., 2015), and more iconic signs may similarly be easier for non-signers to interpret. Any effect we attribute to the semantic transparency of iconic signs could therefore also reflect differences in processing ease. Future work could address this by collecting response times or confidence ratings in a meaning-guessing task alongside appeal and valence ratings, so that the effect of iconicity on evaluation could be separated into a component mediated by ease of interpretation and a direct, form-driven component.

The finding that valence could be inferred from form alone, and more strongly for more iconic signs, is consistent with an embodied or conceptual metaphor account of meaning construction (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980). On this view, abstract evaluative concepts such as "good" and "bad" are grounded in recurring bodily and perceptual experience: upward position and movement are associated with positive affect and downward with negative affect (Casasanto & Dijkstra, 2010; Meier & Robinson, 2004), and approach-and avoidance-oriented movements show comparable associations (Chen & Bargh, 1999). Because sign articulation is spatial and kinetic, signs may instantiate such valence-space mappings more directly than spoken phonology can, as work on metaphor in sign languages suggests (Taub, 2001). This account risks being invoked post hoc for any observed form-valence pairing, and our design cannot distinguish it from a more cautious explanation in which iconicity alone is sufficient to leak semantic content. Disentangling them would require stimuli in which iconicity and the direction of embodied valence mappings are manipulated independently.

As noted above, our stimuli were isolated, decontextualized signs produced without the non-manual components, such as facial expression, eyebrow position, mouth gestures, and body lean, that carry substantial prosodic information in natural signing (Sandler, 2010). With regard to valence, our results may therefore underestimate non-signers' ability to detect valence in signs, as a channel through which affect is normally conveyed was removed. At the same time, the fact that participants detected valence from manual components alone indicates that the hands themselves carry valence-relevant information. In natural signing, however, manual and non-manual components operate together, and future research should examine how they jointly contribute to the perception of valence.

A final point concerns the generalizability of our findings across sign languages. ASL and LSF are historically related and share a considerable proportion of their lexicon (Woodward, 1978), so they are not independent samples of sign language. Whether non-signers can detect valence in unrelated sign languages remains open and could be tested with languages outside the French Sign Language family, such as BSL or German Sign Language (DGS). Such replications would show whether the valence effects are specific to the ASL/LSF pair or reflect sign-language perception more generally.

5. Conclusion

The main contribution of this study is to isolate the effect of sociocultural associations on the aesthetic perception of languages and to separate it from other language-external factors such as exposure and familiarity. We showed that sociocultural associations have a considerable effect on how appealing languages are perceived and thus need to be taken into account, or experimentally controlled, when investigating the aesthetic appeal of languages. This also bears on how attitudes towards languages and their speakers arise: judgements that appear to concern a language may in fact reflect attitudes towards the culture associated with it, which is relevant for understanding prejudice based on, or attributed to, language.

Secondly, this study is, to our knowledge, the first extension of (phon)aesthetic research to sign languages, and it establishes them as a promising target for future (phon)aesthetic work. We showed that signs carry different aesthetic values and that this is linked to the perception of the signs' valence. This link between aesthetic and affective responses, which is available even to perceivers who do not know the language, is particularly intriguing, and future work should disentangle the causal relationship between the two.

On a speculative note, the link between appeal and valence may also be relevant for language evolution. Appealing forms may initially have been produced for their own sake, and the appeal of forms may thus have driven the emergence of a larger and richer repertoire that was only gradually linked to meanings. When a form's appeal matches its valence, this could have made the learning of form-meaning pairings more efficient and may have also boosted the reuse of such forms (cf. Imai & Kita, 2014). Given that aesthetic preferences need not be adaptive (Prum, 2012) and evolutionary accounts of aesthetics are notoriously difficult to falsify, we therefore offer this framework as a candidate perspective for future research rather than a conclusion our data support directly. own. While our primary aim in this study is to use sign languages to investigate language-external influences on the aesthetic evaluation of spoken languages, we also provide a first exploratory investigation of sign language aesthetics, an area that has, to our knowledge, not yet been systematically studied and warrants further research.

This analysis addressed a theoretically distinct question from the preceding model (see 2.4.1.), which focused on the influence of sociocultural cues, particularly Culture preference and knowledge of language identity, on appeal ratings. Here, the main question was whether valence influences appeal ratings and whether this effect differs by language. We therefore constructed a separate model using the same fixed-effects structure as the corresponding analysis of perceived valence below, which allowed us to directly compare the effects of valence on appeal and perceived valence ratings.

Interestingly, Figure2suggests that positively valenced signs from ASL were perceived as more iconic than negatively valenced signs from ASL and signs from LSF. However, an initial model including a three-way interaction with Language showed that this interaction was non-significant, which indicates that the influence of iconicity on valence perception was overall comparable across ASL and LSF. These results indicate that, regardless of language, perceived iconicity plays a role for valence judgements, with more iconic signs eliciting more extreme ratings consistent with their semantic valence.
