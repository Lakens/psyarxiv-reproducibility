What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Parents of children in organized sports have become increasingly involved in their children's sport journeys and there are growing concerns about their behaviors when attending their children's competitions. One potential driver of these negative behaviors is the inclusion of the child in the parent's self-concept. In this study, based on the Model of Excellencism and Perfectionism (MEP), we examined if levels of excellencism and perfectionism in sports parents were associated with different indices of parental investment in a child's sport, including the inclusion of the child and their child's sport in the self-concept, child-invested contingent selfesteem, self-based pride, and social comparison-based pride. We recruited 330 parents with a teenager participating in organized sports (Mparents = 43.28 years old; SDparents = 6.48 years).

Results of a confirmatory factor analysis supported the two-factor structure of the Scale of Perfectionism and Excellencism (SCOPE) with parents. Results of multiple regression showed that excellencism positively predicted identification with the child, child-invested contingent self-esteem, self-based pride, and social comparison-based pride. In contrast, perfectionistic standards were positively associated with inclusion of the child in the self-concept, inclusion of the sport of the child, and child-invested contingent self-esteem, while negatively predicting selfbased pride. In all, perfection strivers are more identity invested in their child's sport journey compared to excellence strivers. Overall, the results of the study supported our hypothesis that perfection strivers are at a higher risk of overidentifying with their children who compete in youth sports.

On the one hand, perfectionistic concerns are consistently associated with adjustment difficulties and symptoms of psychopathologies, (Hill et al., 2025). Because these outcomes are linked to the same construct (i.e., perfectionism), the contrasting findings associated with standards and concerns are puzzling.

These inconsistent results for perfectionistic standards have also led some researchers to question the construct validity of this dimension of perfectionism. Gaudreau (2019) introduced the concept of excellencism to formally distinguish the pursuit of excessively high personal standards from the pursuit of high but reasonable ones. In the MEP, perfectionistic standards are defined as the "tendency to aim and strive toward idealized, flawless, and exceedingly high standards in a relentless manner" (Gaudreau, 2019, p. 200), while excellencism is defined as the "tendency to aim and strive toward very high yet attainable standards in an effortful, engaged, and determined yet flexible manner" (Gaudreau, 2019, p. 200). Overall, the MEP proposes a novel bidimensional model of personal standards to shed light on controversies surrounding perfectionistic standards.

Recent MEP research provided a new way to evaluate whether perfectionism is beneficial (i.e., associated with positive consequences over and above excellencism), unneeded (i.e., being unrelated to positive consequences over and above excellencism), or harmful (i.e., associated with negative consequences over and above excellencism; Gaudreau et al., 2024). Research in sport has shown that once distinguished from excellencism, perfectionistic standards are associated with tolerance toward cheating and doping in sports (Gaudreau, Boileau, et al., 2025; Gaudreau & Schellenberg, 2022), controlled motivation (Boivin et al., 2025), fear of failure (Gaudreau et al., 2021), lower positive affect (Schellenberg et al., 2026), lower savoring and enjoyment (Gaudreau et al., 2024). As for excellencism, it is associated with less positive attitudes toward cheating and doping in sports (Gaudreau, Boileau, et al., 2025; Gaudreau & Schellenberg, 2022) , autonomous motivation (Boivin et al., 2025), subjective sport achievement (Gaudreau et al., 2021), well-being (Schellenberg et al., 2026), savoring and enjoyment (Gaudreau et al., 2024). Overall, when distinguished from excellencism, there is little evidence to suggest that perfectionism is beneficial. Perfectionism rather appears unneeded and harmful.

Sport Fans and Identifying with Sport Objects

It is common for sport fans to identify with sport objects such as teams, athletes, sports, coaches or even stadiums (Wann et al., 2025). Interestingly, little research has been conducted on feeling psychologically connected to an athlete (Wann et al., 2025). Yet the youth sport context presents itself as an ideal context in which to study this phenomenon. In recent years, parents of children in organized sports have become increasingly involved in their children's sport (e.g., Bonavolontà et al., 2021; Stefansen et al., 2018). There is also progressively more attention that is directed toward parents in youth sport competitions (e.g., Brodeur et al., 2023; Dorsch et al., 2015; Goldstein & Iso-Ahola, 2008) because they sometimes display inappropriate behaviors on the sidelines. Unfortunately, this has a negative influence on the experience of the athletes competing, other spectators, and even officials (Webb & Knight, 2024). The way parents behave in the youth sport context probably has a lot to do with how much place their child takes in their self-concept. More importantly, we believe perfectionism and excellencism may also play a role.

Perfectionism, Excellencism, and Identification with One's Child in Sports

There is some indirect evidence to suggest that perfection strivers may overidentify with their children. Previous research has found parental perfectionism to be associated with overparenting (e.g., Segrin et al., 2020), transferring one's ambitions onto one's children (Nguyen et al., 2025), psychological control (e.g., Smith et al., 2017), as well as authoritarian and protective parenting, among others (Yıldız et al., 2020). Parents who engage in such behaviors treat their offspring as an extension of themselves rather than as a separate entity which is an indirect manifestation of overidentification with the child. Thus, we could expect parents higher in perfectionism to display stronger levels of inclusion of the child in their selfconcept.

As for parents who strive for excellence, they are likely to include their child in their self, but potentially less so than perfection strivers. Previous research has found that excellence strivers are better than perfection strivers when it comes to balancing different activities at school and in their sport (Gaudreau et al., 2021). Other research has found that those who pursue perfection are more likely to make sacrifices that put all their eggs in the same basket for the pursuit of their goals than those who strive for excellence (Pétrin-Pomerleau et al., 2025). These results suggest that excellence strivers are adaptable and that they can make place for multiple objects in their identity and life. This could make excellence strivers less likely to overidentify with their child.

Research has shown that in close relationships, it is normal and even adaptive to incorporate the other person in one's sense of self (e.g., Aron et al., 1992). This also applies to the parent-child relationship where parents often make their child a part of who they are. Furthermore, some parents of children in organized sports may also include their child's sport in their self-concept. However, we believe that identifying with the sport of one's child to a high extent could be an indication of overidentification with the child. Parents of a child in organized sports are supporters of their child's sport journey rather than competitors themselves. Therefore, if parents define themselves strongly through their child's sport, they could exaggeratedly identify with their offspring. Thus, identifying with the sport of one's child could be considered an indirect indicator of overidentification. We could expect perfection strivers to include their child's sport significantly more in their self-concept compared to excellence strivers. This would suggest that perfection strivers are at a higher risk of overidentifying with their child.

Inclusion of the Child in the Self-Concept and the Meaning Attached to their Achievement

Research on sport fans has shown that team identification leads some fans to interpret their team's successes and failures as their own (Wann et al., 2025). Similarly, some parents have a tendency to base their self-worth on their offspring's achievement or failures (Wuyts et al., 2015). Parents with a stronger need to prove that they are worthy as a person may emphasize their child's accomplishments in hopes of being perceived more favorably by others. This is commonly referred to as child-invested contingent self-esteem (Wuyts et al., 2015). This projection of the self into one's child's activities appears particularly relevant for competitive sports where children may be compared to one another based on their performances. Previous research has found a positive relationship between perfectionistic standards and child-invested contingent self-esteem (Wuyts et al., 2015). Parents higher in perfectionism believe that their child's achievements and failures influence how worthy of a person they are. However, previous research did not distinguish between perfectionism and excellencism. It is, thus, necessary to revisit these previous results through the MEP.

On a similar note, it is common for parents to take pride in their children's accomplishments. Pride has a "highly social nature" in that the triumph of others can "confirm or enhance personal worth" (Lazarus, 1991, p. 271). In his cognitive-relational theory of emotions, Lazarus (1991) defines the core relational theme of pride as the "enhancement of one's egoidentity by taking credit for a valued object or achievement, either our own or that of someone or group with who we identity". Including the child in the self helps explain why parents are often vicariously proud of their child's success. However, not all parents emphasize the same criteria to define their child's success (e.g., White et al., 1992). Pride is a goal-congruent positive emotion, highly contingent on what individuals prioritize in their goal system (Lazarus, 1991).

For example, self-based pride results from taking pride in one's child's effort and improvement over time; that is, when they are performing better than they were in the past (Tracy & Robins, 2007). When their child makes effort and improves, excellence strivers could be more likely to be proud than perfection strivers. Excellencism may be linked to self-based pride because effort and self-improvement are controllable and align with a growth mindset (Gaudreau, Racine, et al., 2025), mastery goals (Chreim et al., 2026), and social development goals (Pétrin-Pomerleau et al., 2025). Self-based pride can be more authentic because it raises pride without triggering the quest for power and superiority (e.g., Tracy & Robins, 2007). Conversely, people can experience more hubristic or socially constructed pride when they outperform others (Buechner et al., 2018).

Perfection strivers are hypercompetitive (Gaudreau et al., 2024) and pursue goals through which they want to outperform others (Chreim et al., 2026) and demonstrate their social value (Pétrin-Pomerleau et al., 2025) and superiority (Andrade et al., 2025). When their child outperforms other kids, perfection strivers could be more likely to be proud than excellence strivers. Yet, in sports, the name of the game is winning. Given that their child is potentially more included in their self, excellence strivers could be more likely to be proud than nonexcellence/nonperfection strivers when their child improves and/or outperforms others in sports.

This Study

We had two objectives in this study. First, we wanted to test the construct validity of the two-factor SCOPE in a sample of sports parents and assess its suitability in this population.

Second, we aimed to assess how excellencism and perfectionism are associated with parental tendencies to overidentify and overly derive a sense of self-worth based on their children engagement in sports. We selected five identity-related indicators to be investigated alongside excellencism and perfectionism: (a) inclusion of the child in the parents' self, (b) inclusion of the child's sport in the parent's self, (c) child-invested contingent self-esteem, (d) experience of pride when one's child makes effort and improves (i.e., self-based pride), and (e) experience of pride when one's child outperforms other kids in sports (i.e., socially constructed pride). We had a guiding hypothesis that perfectionism would be associated with indices of overinvestment, but we did not formulate specific hypotheses. Our goal was to explore how each characteristic of the parents' identity is specifically associated with excellencism and perfectionism.

Method Participants

Using G*Power, we estimated the sample size required to detect the average effect in psychology studies (r = .16, f square = .026; Bosco et al., 2015). We aimed for a statistical power of 80% (two-tailed; p < .05) in analyses with two predictors. The analysis suggested a sample of 305 participants. We aimed to recruit 350 participants because we expected some missing data, inattentive responding, and outliers.

We recruited a sample of 351 parents of a teenager competing in organized sports on Prolific Academic. Parents could participate in this study if they were (a) 18 years old or older, (b) were the parent or stepparent of at least one child, (c) if their child competed in an organized sport against teams or individuals, and (d) if the child was between 11 and 19 years old (i.e., born between 2007 and 2014). We applied the latter criteria because of potential differences in the parent-child relationship in childhood compared to adolescence. Participants received a compensation of 1.50£ for their participation. This study was approved by the research ethics committee of the (Masked for review). The parent's age ranged from 28 to 63 years old (M = 43.28 years old; SD = 6.48). Most (63.8%) identified as female and 34.5% identified as male (1.7% missing). Ethnic background, social-economic status, and occupation can be found in the Supplementary Materials. Parents responded to the questionnaires referring to their child who spends the most time competing in organized sports. The children's age ranged from 7 to 25 years (M = 13.84 years; SD = 2.08 years), 60.7% identify as male, 39.0% as female, and 0.3% as non-binary. The number of years they have been competing in their sport, hours of practice per week during the competitive season, and level of competition are detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

Design and Measures

Prolific users were invited to respond to a 10 to 12 minutes survey hosted on Survey Monkey. After providing their informed consent, participants answered demographic questions and completed a series of self-reported scales. Participants answered other measures not analyzed in this study.

Scale of Perfectionism and Excellencism

We used the 10-item version of the Scale of Perfectionism and Excellencism (SCOPE; Gaudreau et al., 2022) to measure excellencism (e.g., "As a person, my general goal in life is to be a competent person") and perfectionism (e.g., "As a person, my general goal in life is to be a perfect person"). The participants responded to the questionnaire on a scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (totally). The SCOPE's two-factor structure, the reliability of its subscales, and the validity of its scores have been supported (Gaudreau et al., 2022). Internal consistency (i.e., McDonald's Omega) for the excellencism (ω = .909) and perfectionism (ω = .974) subscales was excellent in this sample.

Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale (IOS Scale)

The Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale (Aron et al., 1992) was used twice to measure the extent to which parents have included their child and their child's sport in their self-concept. This scale displays seven Venn diagrams with increasingly more overlap between the self and another object (e.g., one's child). For exploratory purposes, we added three additional diagrams (i.e., 8, 9, 10) to complete the continuum. Results were identical across the 7 and 10-point versions (see Tables S4-S5 and Figure S2 for results using the 10-point versions). Validity evidence supports the discriminant, convergent, and predictive validity of its scores, as well as its test-retest reliability (Aron et al., 1992).

Child-Invested Contingent Self-Esteem Scale

Participants responded to the 15-item Child-Invested Contingent Self-Esteem Scale (Wuyts et al., 2015). They were asked to report the extent to which they agreed with each item (e.g., "My child's success in sport causes me to feel proud of myself") on a scale from 1 (not at all agree) to 7 (totally agree). Exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis have shown that a 1factor solution explains between 40% and 44% of the variance and fits the data well. The scale's internal consistency and the convergent validity of its scores are also supported (Wuyts et al., 2015). Internal reliability was excellent (ω = .943) in this study.

Self-Based and Social Comparison-Based Pride

To measure self-based and Social comparison-based pride in parents, we adapted items from a pride measure (Buechner et al., 2018). We used four items to measure self-based pride (e.g., In general, I have felt proud of the effort my child puts into their sport) and four others to measure social comparison-based pride (e.g., In general, I have felt proud that my child is a better athlete than others). They were answered on a scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always).

Internal consistency of the self-based pride (ω = .890) and social comparison-based pride subscales (ω = .937) was good 1.

Preliminary analyses

We performed data cleaning in SPSS, but our main analyses were performed in Mplus 9.0 and R 4.6.1. We removed participants whose children were younger than 11 (n = 4) or older than 19 (n = 2). To evaluate the fit of the model, we used the following fit indices: the chisquare test (𝜒 ! ), the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Given that fixed cutoff values to fit indices cannot be generalized to all models, we decided to generate cutoff values tailored to the characteristics of our model using the Dynamic Fit Index (DFI) approach by McNeish and Wolf (2023). Implementing a simulation algorithm, the dynamic R package (Wolf & McNeish, 2023) generates levels of fit index values (i.e., number of first-order factors minus one) against which you can compare the values obtained for your model (for a tutorial, see Boivin et al., 2026). At each level, the algorithm adds a misspecification to the model: a cross-loading of the (next available) item with the smallest primary loading to the (next available) factor with highest construct reliability (McNeish & Wolf, 2023). Therefore, depending on where our model stands against these cutoff values, we can get an idea of the magnitude of misfit in the model. Given that the SCOPE (Gaudreau et al., 2022) is comprised of two factors, there is only one misspecified path added to the model and only one level against which we can evaluate the fit of our model. We generated DFI cutoffs for a categorical multi-factor CFA in R.

Discussion

The MEP is a recent addition to the sport psychology literature. Past studies found that athletes' personal standards can be differentiated based on whether they focus on excellence or perfection (e.g., Schellenberg et al., 2026). This study was the first-ever to provide empirical support for the distinction between excellencism and perfectionism in a sample of parents. A confirmatory factor analysis showed that SCOPE scores fit a two-factor model. Regression analyses showed that excellencism and perfectionism of parents were differentially linked to their propensity to excessively include their child in their own sense of self. Overall, these results supported the construct validity of the SCOPE and offered novel insights into the complex ways personal standards can shape how parents incorporate their child into their self-concept.

Key Findings and Interpretations

Results of CFA on the SCOPE (Gaudreau et al., 2022) using dynamic fit cutoffs (McNeish & Wolf, 2023) supported its two-factor structure in a sample of parents. Particularly, the fit of the model to the data surpassed the dynamic cutoffs tailored to the SCOPE, and the two-factor model fit significantly better than a one-factor model. This means that parents do observe and understand the distinction between striving for perfection and striving for excellence. Consistent with previous validation work on the MEP (e.g., Gaudreau et al., 2022), the perfectionism and excellencism subscales were highly and positively correlated and the strength of the latent correlation did not suggest multicollinearity. The internal consistency of the perfectionism and excellencism subscales was excellent. This is strong support for the construct validity of the SCOPE in parents.

Perfectionism was a positive and statistically significant predictor of the inclusion of the child in the self-concept, meaning that perfection strivers score significantly higher on this variable than excellence strivers. Taken alone, this finding could be interpreted as perfection strivers being more involved and engaged parents which is considered positive for child development (e.g., Grolnick & Lerner, 2023). However, the positive relationships of perfectionistic standards with inclusion of the sport of their child and child-invested contingent self-esteem outline the need for more cautious interpretation. Perfection strivers define themselves through their child's sport to a significantly higher extent than excellence strivers.

They also feel as though their self-esteem depends more on their child's successes in sport.

Importantly, the biggest difference found between perfection and excellence strivers in this study was on child-invested contingent self-esteem (d = 0.986, p < .01). These findings suggest that perfection strivers excessively incorporate their child into their self-concept.

Further supporting this point, in a supplemental analysis (see Table S3 in the Supplementary Materials), we found that inclusion of the sport of the child into the parent's sense of self explained additional variance in child-invested contingent self-esteem over and above the inclusion of the child in the self. This indicated that parents who identify strongly with both their child and the sport of their child are the ones most likely to base their self-esteem on their child's achievement. In this study, perfectionistic standards were a positive predictor of both inclusion of the child and the sport of the child. Perfection strivers were also the ones who reported the highest levels of child-invested contingent self-esteem. Therefore, based on these results, we can conclude that perfection strivers display some overidentification with their child.

Our results are consistent with previous research which has reported a positive relationship of perfectionistic standards with psychological control (e.g., Smith et al., 2017), parental overprotection (e.g., Segrin et al., 2020), transferring one's own unfulfilled ambitions onto one's child (Nguyen et al., 2025), as well as authoritarian parenting (Yıldız et al., 2020) -variables which are also indicative of overinclusion of the child in the parent's self-concept.

We found excellencism to be a positive and significant predictor of the inclusion of the child in the parent's self-concept, meaning that excellence strivers score significantly higher on this variable compared to nonexcellence/nonperfection strivers. This likely means that excellence strivers are more engaged in their relationship with their child (Aron et al., 1992). Importantly, excellence strivers seem better able to maintain clearer self-child boundaries because they do not identify significantly more with the sport of their child than nonperfection/nonexcellence strivers. Yet excellencism positively predicts child-invested contingent self-esteem, meaning that those who pursue excellence believe that their self-esteem depends more on their child's sport successes than nonperfection/nonexcellence strivers. Still, the difference between excellence and nonperfection/nonexcellence strivers on child-invested contingent self-esteem is much smaller (d = 0.379, p < .01) than the one between perfection and excellence strivers on this variable (d = 0.986, p < .01). Once again, it seems as though excellence strivers can better balance their child in their identity compared to perfection strivers. The higher child-invested contingent self-esteem in excellence strivers compared to those parents with lower standards might partly reflect the fact that nonperfection/nonexcellence strivers are less achievement driven (Gaudreau, 2019; Gaudreau et al., 2022). As potential support for this interpretation, we found that nonexcellence/nonperfection strivers are less likely to experience pride (compared to both excellence and perfection strivers) when their child improves or display effort just as much as when they outperform other kids in sport.

Unfortunately, lower engagement toward the child can have negative consequences for child development (e.g., Grolnick & Lerner, 2023). In all, our pattern of results suggests that excellencism reflects a more balanced pattern of investment toward their child compared to perfectionism and non-excellencism/non-perfectionism.

According to Lazarus (1991, p. 272), success is the object of pride rather than its cause.

As he eloquently pointed out, the fact that "we take credit for success -it is ours and it expands our identity -is the cause, which is what pride is all about". Observing that excellence strivers are more likely to experience pride than perfection strivers when their child improves and makes effort indicates that defining aspects of their identity were enhanced by the perceived value of their child's achievement. "In feeling proud we have lived up to or even gained as measured against a personal or social standard to which we aspire" (Lazarus, 1991, p. 272). As they value self-based criteria of success, excellence strivers are well positioned to be proud of their child and expand their own sense of worth as they raise an offspring that exhibits goal-congruent values of learning, mastery, growth, and adaptability. Perfection strivers are less likely to value and expend their identity based on self-based criteria of success. Contrary to expectations, excellence and perfection strivers did not differ significantly in their propensity to report pride in their children when they outperformed others. They were both significantly higher than nonexcellence/nonperfection strivers. In sport, athletes compete against each other and show up with the goal of winning the game. Winning is ingrained into the fabric of sports, so much that it creates a strong situation in which outperforming others acts as a shared value -a social normamong many sports parents who have a strong need for achievement. This potential explains why outperforming others is an object that makes both perfection and excellence strivers proud of their child when participating in their sport.

Limitations

First, the scale we used to assess how much parents include their child or their sport in their self-concept does not clearly distinguish between identification and overidentification. As a result, we cannot determine the cutoff at which scores reflect overidentification. Still, we believe our results for inclusion of the sport of the child and child-invested contingent self-esteem provide some support for the idea that perfection strivers overidentify with their child. Given these issues, we suggest that future research move beyond the quantity of identification and instead examine its quality such as whether it is balanced and flexible or so dominant that it conflicts with important aspects of the parent's identity. This would be a more direct test of our hypothesis and would require less inferences and extrapolation.

Second, we did not assess behaviors of parents when attending their child's sport competitions. Although perfectionism is associated with higher levels of inclusion of the child in the self-concept, we do not know if perfectionistic standards are associated with problematic behaviors in parents on the sidelines (e.g., verbal aggression, boasting behaviors, intimidation, etc.). This should be investigated in future research.

Third, we used a cross-sectional design meaning that we cannot infer that perfectionistic standards or excellencism cause the inclusion of the child or the sport in the parent's selfconcept, child-invested contingent self-esteem, self-based pride, or social comparison-based pride. Experimental designs that manipulate standards would be necessary to test for such a hypothesis. In future research, longitudinal designs would be necessary to test for the directionality of the effect, but also to rule the possibility that an unmeasured variable is responsible for the relationships observed in this study. However, recruiting parents of children in organized sports is both difficult and challenging. One strength of our study is our large sample size and the composition of the sample which included parents of teenagers competing at all levels of competition. This strength makes our study a good foundation for future longitudinal work. Finally, future research might want to replicate our results in young children (i.e., 6 to 9 years old) participating in sports rather than teenagers.

Conclusion

This study was the first to examine excellencism among parents of children involved in organized sports. CFA supported the two-factor structure of the SCOPE, indicating that the distinction between excellencism and perfectionism applies to parents and extending previous sports studies that focused on athletes (e.g., Schellenberg et al., 2026). Perfection strivers had a harder time drawing a line between themselves and their child's sport and seemed to overidentify. Future research should examine whether this makes them more likely to display negative behaviors when attending their child's sport competitions than excellence strivers, who can more neatly separate their sense of self from their child's.
