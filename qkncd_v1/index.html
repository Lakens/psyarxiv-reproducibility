Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Children ages 5-10 years, adolescents ages 12-15 years, and adults viewed interpersonal (individual vs. individual) and intergroup (group vs. group) conflicts with ambiguous perpetrator intent.

Participants perceived more intentionality and expected harsher responses in intergroup conflicts than in interpersonal conflicts; this effect strengthened within childhood and between age groups.

Greater intentionality was attributed to group perpetrators than individual perpetrators which accounted for harsher response expectations in intergroup relative to interpersonal conflicts.

Different conceptions of interpersonal and intergroup conflict resolution emerge early in development and increasingly diverge across the lifespan.

[div-01]

Conflicts pervade social interactions across development. From the playground to the office to the battlefield, conflicts foment distrust, cause emotional, financial, and physical harms, and threaten the social bonds necessary for thriving relationships and cooperative societies (Bar-Tal, 2011; Lewicki et al., 2021). Conflict resolution is therefore required to ameliorate these harms and sustain cooperative relationships (Laursen et al., 2001; Taylor, 2020). However, successfully resolving conflict is challenging because it involves complex social processes like forgiving, restoring justice, and rebuilding trust (McAuliffe et al., 2025; McLaughlin et al., 2024; Reinders Folmer et al., 2021). Conflict resolution is also difficult because conflicts are multifaceted and complex. They can vary in topic (e.g., resources, ideology, status), participants (e.g., siblings, friend groups, political parties), and scale (e.g., interpersonal, local, domestic, international), among other dimensions (Bar-Tal, 2011; Lewicki et al., 2021). Therefore, effective conflict resolution requires first identifying the most impactful elements of conflicts and investigating how those elements affect conceptions of and responses to conflict.

Interpersonal vs. Intergroup Conflicts

One approach to characterizing different types of conflict has been to distinguish between interpersonal and intergroup conflicts. In this framework, interpersonal conflicts concern character traits or personal preferences, while intergroup conflicts concern group membership (Killen et al., 2013; Stephan, 2008; Verkuyten, 2022). For example, a conflict would be interpersonal if one coworker insulted another coworker because of their rude behavior, but it would be intergroup if it was because of their religion. While apology, forgiveness, or punishment can help resolve interpersonal conflicts, intergroup conflict resolution must also address group membership (Böhm et al., 2020). This framework identified conflict content as critical for understanding conflicts and has generated more targeted prejudice reduction interventions (e.g., Confer et al., 2025; Killen et al., 2022).

Beyond conflict content, however, interpersonal and intergroup conflicts also differ in that they have different types of perpetrators and victims (Enright et al., 2016; Wildschut & Insko, 2007). In this framework, interpersonal conflicts are between an individual perpetrator and an individual victim, whereas intergroup conflicts are between a group perpetrator and a group victim. For example, if one coworker insulted another coworker, even about their religion, the conflict would be interpersonal because it has an individual perpetrator and an individual victim. But, if a group of coworkers insulted another group of coworkers, even about their rude behavior, the conflict would be intergroup because it has a group perpetrator and a group victim. Independent of conflict content, defining conflicts by their actors provides an orthogonal lens through which to understand and evaluate the differences between these conflicts (Wildschut & Insko, 2025; Van Lange & Bergmann, 2026).

Individual and Group Perpetrators and Victims

The type of perpetrator and victim (i.e., individual or group) likely impacts conceptions of conflict and its resolution. Individuals and groups are different; they have different cognitive, affective, and social capacities (Enright et al., 2016; Van Lange & Bergmann, 2026). An individual has one mind and consults only their own thoughts and feelings when evaluating, initiating, or responding to conflicts. In contrast, a group comprises many individuals, each with their own mind and emotions, making conflict more complex for groups to navigate and harder for observers to attribute mental states (Van Lange & Bergmann, 2026). These differences matter because they may impact perceptions of intent and severity which are important in shaping conceptions of conflict (Ames & Fiske, 2013; Amir et al., 2021; Wildschut & Insko, 2007). For example, individual perpetrators could be perceived as more intentional because they act independently so their intent is concentrated, whereas groups require coordination, so their intent is diffused among group members (Cooley et al., 2017). Alternatively, group perpetrators may be perceived as more intentional because unified collective action requires intentional coordination between all group members, whereas an individual does not coordinate with others, so their actions may be more likely to be deemed accidental relative to a group (Waytz & Young, 2012).

Further, relative to an individual victim, harming a group may be perceived as especially severe because more people are harmed (Riggs & Kalish, 2016; Singer, 2015). In contrast, relative to a group, harming an individual might be considered more severe because they are more vulnerable than a group (Nordgren & McDonnell, 2011). Thus, whether a perpetrator or victim is an individual or group likely impacts perceptions of and responses to interpersonal and intergroup conflicts, with downstream effects for conflict resolution.

Developing Conceptions of Interpersonal and Intergroup Conflicts

A developmental approach is particularly well-positioned to examine how conceptions of interpersonal and intergroup conflicts may differ. From a young age, children begin developing the capacity for conflict resolution (Laursen et al., 2001; Nasie et al., 2016). Like adults, children respond to conflicts by forgiving, punishing, and establishing trust, and they anticipate more positive outcomes after forgiveness and more negative outcomes after punishment or inaction (Amir et al., 2021; Kinzler et al., 2011; McAuliffe et al., 2025; Loustau et al., 2024; McLaughlin et al., 2024). Children as young as 5 years old reject stereotypes as the basis for social exclusion and rectify group-based inequalities (Elenbaas et al., 2016; Killen & Stangor, 2001). Children also expect more trust after forgiveness than adolescents or adults (McLaughlin et al., 2024).

These findings underscore that conceptions of conflict are present in childhood and develop with age.

relevant for differentiating between interpersonal and intergroup contexts. Specifically, theory of mind and intent attribution emerge in childhood and strengthen into adolescence and adulthood (Buon & Margoni, 2025; Dumontheil et al., 2010; Guazzelli Williamson et al., 2025; Killen et al., 2011). If, as argued, perspective taking and intent perceptions operate differently for individual and group perpetrators, we would expect to see distinct developmental patterns for interpersonal and intergroup conflicts. Exploring interpersonal and intergroup conflicts as these cognitive abilities emerge can test our hypotheses and provide insight into the developmental processes underpinning responses to these conflicts.

Children also develop complex group cognition throughout childhood (Liberman et al., 2017). Infants stare longer at ingroup than outgroup members, exhibiting the precursors of intergroup cognition (Fogiel et al., 2023). With age, children increasingly understand whether individuals belong to a group, perceive different levels of entitativity between different groups, and distinguish between groups and collections of individuals (Plötner et al., 2016; Svirydzenka et al., 2010). They also make different inferences about larger and smaller groups, perceive status differences, and use group membership information to make inferences about social preferences (Heck et al., 2022). Because the antecedents of complex group cognition emerge in childhood, children can likely differentiate between individual and group perpetrators and victims, which may in turn lead to distinct developmental trajectories for conceptions of interpersonal and intergroup conflicts. However, this has not yet been demonstrated empirically, and how conceptions of and responses to interpersonal and intergroup conflicts develop remains an open question.

Present Study

We investigated the development of conceptions of and responses to interpersonal and intergroup conflicts in children, adolescents, and adults. Participants viewed third-person interpersonal (individual perpetrator and victim) and intergroup (group perpetrator and victim) transgression vignettes. They also viewed vignettes with a group perpetrator and an individual victim as well as with an individual perpetrator and a group victim. This design allowed us to examine differences between interpersonal and intergroup conflict, isolate the independent effects of perpetrator type and victim type, and detect interactions between our manipulations. Data Analytic Plan

We first compared our model to a null model to demonstrate that our hypothesized predictors improved model fit (Forstmeier & Schielzeth, 2011). To examine the effects of perpetrator type, victim type, and age group on each dependent measure, we ran linear mixedeffects models with the dependent variable as the response term (continuous), perpetrator type (categorical: individual, group), victim type (categorical: individual, group), age group (categorical: child, adolescent, adult), their interactions, gender, and vignette order as predictors with subject and vignette content as random effects. All contrasts were conducted using this model, and post-hoc tests used the multivariate t (MVT) p-value correction method for multiple comparisons, which does not assume that contrasts are independent. Degrees of freedom were calculated using the Satterthwaite method. Severity was reverse-coded for all analyses. While we preregistered running separate models for each age group, running one model reduced experiment-wise error by minimizing the number of models and post-hoc contrasts. All hypotheses were tested using this model except for the within-childhood age-related effects.

To analyze within-childhood age differences, we ran linear mixed-effects models for each dependent measure with the dependent variable as the response term (continuous), perpetrator type (categorical: individual, group), victim type (categorical: individual, group), age (continuous), their interactions, gender, and vignette order as predictors with subject and vignette content as random effects. Age was calculated as the difference between participation dates and participant birthdays. We did not preregister analyses treating age as continuous across all age groups because we expected most of the developmental change to occur in childhood. We were unable to collect adolescent and adult birthdays.

To test whether intent and severity mediated the relations between our manipulations and responses to conflict, we ran three multigroup structural equation models: intent as sole mediator, severity as sole mediator, and a parallel mediation model. The models used full information maximum likelihood estimation with cluster robust standard errors to account for the within-subjects design and cluster randomized vignette order.

DISCUSSION

Beginning in childhood, conflicts are common across a range of social relationships.

Maintaining these relationships requires effective conflict resolution, which in turn, involves identifying and responding to the most important elements of a particular conflict. This study proposed that evaluating conflicts by their perpetrators (individual vs. group) and victims (individual vs. group) provided a new lens for investigating how conflicts are conceptualized and responded to. Participants evaluated and responded to intergroup conflicts (group perpetrator and group victim) more harshly than interpersonal conflicts (individual perpetrator and individual victim), and this pattern increased with age. Notably, these were differences in degree as opposed to direction, suggesting that intergroup conflicts are considered more extreme than, but not radically distinct, from interpersonal conflicts.

E i i i iconflicts were largely driven by the perception that group perpetrators are more intentional than individual perpetrators. Intent perceptions explained, across all age groups (forgiveness and trust approached significance for children), why participants responded more harshly in intergroup conflicts. Notably, intent was a stronger and more consistent mediator than severity for all age groups, indicating that intent, rather than severity, was more impactful when evaluating conflicts. Alternatively, fewer severity effects may have been detected because the severity measure had relatively low variability on its six-pointscale (SD = .77; SDchild = .94; SDadolescent = .77; SDadult = .68). However, there were still significant effects for severity in adolescents and adults, increasing the likelihood that severity was genuinely less impactful than intent in shaping conceptions of conflict.

While not independently impactful, victim type interacted with perpetrator type in adolescence and adulthood such that conflicts with a group perpetrator and an individual victim were viewed as most extreme. These findings may have occurred because individual victims were perceived as more vulnerable than group victims, with the power imbalance between perpetrator and victim amplifying perceptions of intent and severity (Nordgren & McDonnell, 2011). Given that these effects were not observed in children, the ability to coordinate information about perpetrators and victims and consider the vulnerability of individual victims may emerge later in development. Separately, when there was a group victim, children, with age, perceived more intent and expected less trust, illustrating that while children increasingly become sensitive to victim type, they interpret its impact in the opposite direction from adolescents and adults. Instead, children may more simplistically infer that harming a group victim is worse because more people are harmed (Riggs & Kalish, 2016; Singer, 2015).

Together, these findings highlight that perpetrator type and, to a lesser extent, victim type, shape perceptions of conflict and expected responses to conflict.

Why is More Intent Attributed to Group Perpetrators?

While differences emerged because participants viewed group perpetrators as more intentional than individuals, the reasons for this effect remain unclear. One possibility is that, as hypothesized, if a group acts collectively, it might be assumed that group members coordinated, leading to higher intent attributions. Participants may also reason that collective action is unlikely to occur by chance and that if groups act together, it is likely that they acted purposefully. While not tested directly, these explanations provide potential cognitive mechanisms for the observed developmental change. Developing Theory of Mind, perspectivetaking, and quantitative reasoning skills may enable children and adolescents to understand the complex dynamics of group cognition and to think probabilistically about collective action, leading them to perceive group actors as more intentional (Crimston et al., 2023; Killen et al., 2011). Research examining the relation between cognitive development and conflict resolution could shed light on these findings.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Our findings apply to well-defined groups of a few people, but they may not hold for larger more amorphous groups like ethnic or political groups.

For a friend group or army unit, the actors are clearly defined, but for ethnic or political groups it is unclear who is acting or being acted upon. Similar differences may exist between interpersonal conflicts and conflicts involving these types of groups, but this study was not designed to address that question. Implications

This study documents that children have distinct conceptions of and responses to interpersonal and intergroup conflicts, underscoring that conceptions of conflict resolution emerge early in development. Second, given that intergroup conflicts were approached more harshly than interpersonal conflicts, intergroup conflicts may be perceived as harder to resolve.

Especially considering the ambiguously intentional context, this belief may unnecessarily lead to a skepticism that intergroup conflicts can be resolved, perhaps revealing a bias. Finally, with age, children perceived more intent and expected less trust. However, children still perceived less intent and expected more forgiveness and trust than adolescents and adults, independent of condition. Whether this is because children give more benefit of the doubt, are still developing cognitively, or are more naive, conflict resolution may be easier to facilitate in childhood. Taken together, this study presents initial evidence that intergroup conflict resolution interventions might be especially impactful in childhood.

Conclusion

From the playground to the battlefield, conflict is a part of life. Investigating conceptions of conflict, how they are responded to, and, by extension, how they can be resolved is key to understanding this important aspect of the human experience. This study provides evidence that the type of perpetrators and victims in a conflict impacts conceptions of and responses to conflict. Because of these differences, interpersonal and intergroup conflicts may require slightly different approaches for their resolution to account for elevated perceptions of intent for group perpetrators. Given that conceptions of conflict and its resolution begin to emerge in childhood, conflict resolution interventions may be especially effective if implemented as these conceptions are forming.

Norming Study Information

During stimuli creation, we needed to develop eight ambiguously intentional transgression vignettes to show all four conditions twice without repeating vignette content.

Participants were N = 184 adults (Mage = 41.3, SDage = 12.6, 57.1% male, 42.4% female) recruited using Prolific. To minimize perceived differences between the vignettes, we created language for 37 potential scenarios. The language was displayed without images. We then normed them across 8 dimensions: intentionality, severity, morality, responsibility, weirdness, punishment, forgiveness, and commonness, each on a sliding scale from 0-100 with higher scores indicating greater magnitude on that dimension. Each participant rated 10 vignettes on these dimensions in a randomized order. For each dimension, we ran a one-way ANOVA with the dimension as the outcome and the vignettes as the predictor. We then ran all post-hoc contrasts with tukey corrections and selected groups of vignettes which did not differ significantly.

Given our interest in creating ambiguously intentional vignettes, we first normed on intentionality and selected vignettes closest to the neutral mid-point of the scale. Overall, participants thought the transgressions were not very intentional (M = 35.4, SD = 30.3). The ANOVA was significant F(36, 1803) = 8.04, p < .001. The group of vignettes which were closest to the neutral mid-point and did not significantly differ were: painting, art, diorama, carrots, kites, tomato plant, birdhouse, flower, science, lego, sculpture, games, snowmen. The intentionality ratings for this group ranged from 38.4 (painting) to 59.5 (snowmen).

We then took these vignettes and normed them on severity to minimize perceived differences in the severity of the transgressions. Overall, participants thought these transgressions were not very severe (M = 40.2, SD = 27.9). The ANOVA was significant F(12, 651) = 14.09, p < .001. The group of vignettes which did not significantly differ were: games, kites, flower, tomato plant, lego, birdhouse, carrots, diorama. The severity ratings for this group ranged from 28.8 (games) to 39.9 (diorama).

After norming for intentionality and severity, only eight vignettes remained, and these were used for the study. The diorama vignette was changed to use "school project" instead of "diorama" to be more accessible to children. These eight vignettes did not differ on morality (F(7, 400) = 0.83, p = .567), responsibility (F(7, 400) = 0.46, p = .862), punishment (F(7, 400) = 1.03, p = .408), or forgiveness, (F(7, 400) = 0.37, p = .919). There was a significant difference in weirdness (F(7, 400) = 2.44, p = .019) such that ruining a birdhouse was deemed more weird than ruining boardgames, but neither scenario was considered particularly weird. There were also significant differences in commonness (F(7, 400) = 10.97, p < .001), but these differences were not particularly relevant to our design and were controlled for with condition randomization across vignette.

In addition to the variables described in the main text, the adolescent and adult surveys contained two more dependent measures: forgiveness deservingness and character intent. Similarly, while the intent variable measured participants' own perceptions of intent, character intent measured participants' beliefs about the victims' intent perceptions. These measures were presented in a randomized order alongside the other dependent measures. These measures were not included in the child survey to minimize the protocol length and because they patterned similarly to the forgiveness and intent measures.

Differences between forgiveness and forgiveness deservingness and between intent and character intent

Using Welch's independent samples t-tests, we compared differences between forgiveness and forgiveness deservingness and between intent and character intent. Participants believed perpetrators deserved more forgiveness than the perpetrators were likely to receive (t(5801.70) = 12.27, p < .001), while participants also perceived less intent than they believed the victims perceived (t(5777.10) = -16.77, p < .001). This was true both for adolescents (tforgiveness(2703) = 8.66, p < .001; tintent(2644.90) = 10.36, p < .001) and adults (tforgiveness(3095.80) = 8.69, p < .001; tintent(3128.50) = 13.29, p < .001) (Figure S6). These findings suggest that thirdperson judgements and perceptions are less extreme than individuals' perceptions of actors' perspectives in a transgression.
