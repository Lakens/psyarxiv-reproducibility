Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Ninety percent of the world's children live in Low-/Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), but relatively little is known about children's mental health risk in these settings.

Using a genetically informative twin-family design, we estimated parent-child associations for mental health symptoms (MHSs), indirect effects through specific risk/protective factors, and how parental MHSs impact genetic/environmental influences on child MHSs in Nigeria.

We recruited 320 twin pairs (aged 3-5 years) and both parents in two Nigerian towns.

Participants were assessed for MHSs (emotional/behavioural symptoms in children; anxiety, depressive and Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder symptoms in parents), and selected risk/protective factors (adverse home environments, adverse childhood experiences, poor physical health; and religiosity, parental warmth and prosociality). We used structural equation modelling to test parent-child MHS associations, and mediation by risk/protective factors. We estimated latent genetic (A), shared environmental (C) and individual-specific environmental (E) influences on child MHSs, and how parent MHSs impact these influences using univariate twin models.

Child MHSs were significantly associated with MHSs in both parents. The strongest indirect effects for parent-child MHS associations were through adverse childhood experiences and diminished parental warmth. Others indirect pathways involved poor physical health and diminished prosociality. Latent A and C influences each explained 22-29% of the individual differences in children's MHSs. Independent of A influences, C influences were impacted by parents' MHSs.

Our findings indicate that potentially modifiable environmental pathways are important for understanding parent-child MHS associations for very young children in a LMIC, and these may inform intervention design. Larger and more rigorous genetically informative designs are needed to replicate and extend our findings.

Introduction

Up to one in every six children in the world experiences significant mental health (including neurodevelopmental) symptoms (MHSs) (Kieling et al., 2024). MHSs in early childhood (up to age eight years; UNICEF, 2026) often persist into adulthood (Harris et al., 2024; Solmi et al., 2022) and adversely affect childhood and adult physical health, social and academic/occupational outcomes (Copeland et al., 2021), and consequently national economies (Lizheng et al., 2019). Considering that the developing brain is more sensitive to risk exposures and interventions (Burkhouse & Luby, 2026; Lupien et al., 2009; Nelson et al., 2025), research is better directed at early childhood. During this period, MHSs cluster into emotional and behavioural symptoms which differentiate into depression/anxiety, conduct/aggression-related and neurodevelopmental disorders over development (Achenbach, 1978; Leno et al., 2022; Liu, 2004; Liu et al., 2011). The burden of childhood MHSs is potentially higher in Low-/Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) where 90% of the world's children and adolescents reside (Kieling et al., 2011). However, in LMICs, especially in West Africa, childhood MHSs are underrecognised, under-resourced and under-researched (Weine et al., 2020; Wilson & Patel, 2026; Yu et al., 2023). Projections that two out of every five children in the world will be living in Africa by 2050 (Li, 2014) indicate an urgent need for childhood mental health research in African LMICs.

Parental MHSs are one of the strongest risk factors for childhood MHSs (Jami et al., 2021). Beyond transmitted genetic risk (Rice, 2022), parental MHSs can create adverse environments that independently increase MHSs risk in children (Eley et al., 2015; Jami et al., 2021; Rutter & Quinton, 1984). These environmental pathways include adverse home environments (characterised by domestic violence and dysfunction; Ehrensaft & Cohen, 2012; Herba et al., 2016; Manczak et al., 2017; Onigbinde et al., 2025), and negative parent-child interactions including physical and emotional abuse and neglect/inadequate parental care (Chen, 2023; Herba et al., 2016). In LMICs, diminished nurturing may be further associated with an increased likelihood of recurrent acute illnesses, or worse outcomes of chronic conditions in children (Pierce et al., 2020; Weobong et al., 2015). Diminished protective factors are another under-investigated pathway (Padaigaitė-Gulbinienė et al., 2025) and these include warm parent-child interactions (Bowes et al., 2010), children's protective prosocial behaviours (Byrne et al., 2023; Malti & Speidel, 2024) and religiosity, which is high in many LMICs (Patel et al., 2008).

Additional gaps in childhood mental health research in LMICs include a differential focus on older children (Aladro et al., 2022), which excludes early childhood. Fathers are also typically excluded, which is critical considering that households are often socioeconomically dependent on men in LMICs (Okafor et al., 2022). Paternal involvement in childhood MHSs research and intervention may be beneficial in LMICs (Jeong et al., 2023; Kalembo et al., 2012; Ladur et al., 2021). Finally, childhood mental health research in LMICs often focuses on isolated associations with individual risk factors (e.g., Charak et al., 2021; Rieder et al., 2019), with little research incorporating multiple domains of risk, including genetic influences.

Understanding the joint roles of multiple risk/protective factors in parent-child MHS associations in LMICs can inform intervention priorities in these contexts.

To bridge these gaps, we recruited a genetically informative sample of parents and preschool twin children in Nigeria and investigated: i. associations between paternal and maternal MHSs (depressive, anxiety and Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder [ADHD] symptoms) and child MHSs (behavioural; and emotional symptoms); ii. mediation of parentchild MHS associations by measured risk (household chaos, intimate partner violence, adverse childhood experiences and children's poor physical health)/protective (parental warmth, parents' religiosity and children's prosocial behaviour) factors, and iii. overall genetic and environmental influences on childhood MHSs, and how parental MHSs impact these. We hypothesised positive parent-child MHS associations and indirect effects through measured risk and diminished protective factors. We expected genetic and environmental influences on children's MHSs and that parent MHSs would impact these influences.

Methods

Sample

The sample comprised 320 households, each comprising parents and a complete set of twins aged 3-5 years, recruited from two towns in South-Western Nigeria (Ile-Ife and Ilesa) as part of a pilot study to test the feasibility of twin-family research (Oginni et al., 2025).

Recruitment has been described elsewhere (Oginni et al., 2024; 2025).

Questionnaires

All study questionnaires (Supplementary material) were translated into Yoruba using the backward and forward translation method with additional input from a Community Advisory Group to ensure cultural acceptability (Oginni et al., 2025). Single questions were used to assess the ages and sex of the children and parents. Children's zygosity was assessed using a direct question to mothers ('Are your twins identical or non-identical?') and observation of physical characteristics, which had an accuracy of 90-91% for monozygotic and same-sex dizygotic twins when compared with the twins' photographs (Oginni et al., 2025).

Each parent completed questionnaires assessing child-and parent-/family-based MHSs and risk/protective factors. For each questionnaire, responses to individual items were summed, and higher scores indicated higher levels of the constructs assessed.

Child-based variables

Mental health symptoms

Behavioural and emotional symptoms were assessed using mother-reports of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; Goodman, 1997). Given the inconsistent factor structure of the SDQ in non-Western countries (Brown et al., 2025; De Vries et al., 2018; Stevanovic et al., 2015), we conducted exploratory factor analyses to identify the domains of children's MHSs in the sample. Consistent with previous reports from Nigeria (Stevanovic et al., 2015), we identified three domains: behavioural (including ADHD) symptoms (items 2, 10, 12, 15, 22, 21R, 25R; Cronbach's alpha=0.60), emotional symptoms (items 3, 5, 6, 8, 13, 16, 19, 24, 18R; Cronbach's alpha=0.46) and prosociality (items 1, 4, 9, 17, 20, 7, 14 Cronbach's alpha=0.57). The Cronbach's alpha for behavioural symptoms is consistent with other research (Riglin et al., 2013), and the overall low values are consistent with previous findings when the SDQ is used among younger children (Theunissen et al., 2013; Toseeb et al., 2022).

Child-based risk factors

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) were assessed using 5 questions selected from the World Health Organisation Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (World Health Organization, 2018). Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Never) to 3 (Many times). Cronbach's alpha was 0.91.

Childhood physical health was assessed using 7 questions, each rated 'No' (0) or 'Yes' (1), assessing neonatal health conditions and treatments, longstanding health conditions, recurrent illnesses/treatments common in Nigeria (e.g., malaria and diarrhoea), and hospital admissions for other reasons. A composite score was created with higher scores indicating poorer physical health.

Protective factors

Parental warmth was assessed using 7 items from the Parents and Feelings Questionnaire (Deater-Deckard, 2000). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale with scores ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (Strongly disagree); negatively phrased items were reverse-scored. The Cronbach's alpha was 0.70. Obstetric complications were assessed using questions that rated the presence of serious physical health problems, use of prescribed medication and hospital admissions, any use of alcohol or tobacco during each trimester of pregnancy; preterm birth, prolonged labour (lasting longer than 24 hours, Bakker et al., 2024), unsupervised and/or instrumental delivery.

Mental health symptoms

Depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9, Kroenke et al., 2001) and the 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 questionnaire (GAD-7, Spitzer et al., 2006), respectively. Each item was scored on a 3-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). Risk factors

Intimate partner violence was assessed using the 4-item HARK questionnaire (Sohal et al., 2007) which screens for physical, emotional and sexual abuse, with each item scored 0 (No) or 1 (Yes). The Cronbach's alpha in the present study was 0.62 in both parents.

Household chaos was assessed using 14 items from the 15-item Confusion, Hubbub and Order Scale (CHAOS) (Matheny Jr et al., 1995), with each item scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Very much like your own home) to 4 (Not at all like your own home).

Protective factors

Religiosity was assessed using the 6-item Religiosity Orientation Test which rates the frequency of religious behaviours in Nigeria (e.g., attending services and praying; Idehen, 2001). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Daily/frequently) to 5 (Never). Cronbach's alpha was 0.78 for fathers and mothers.

Preliminary Analyses

Data preparation

Data cleaning was carried out using SPSS (version 29); further data preparation and structural equation modelling were carried out in Lavaan (Rosseel, 2014) and OpenMx (Neale et al., 2016) in R using Maximum Likelihood estimation (see Supplementary materials for further details). Phenotypic analyses

Descriptive analyses, correlations and mediation

The study variables were summarised using proportions and measures of central tendency. Inferential analyses (t-and Chi-square tests) were used to compare father and mother variables whilst reporting effect sizes (Cohen's d and Cramer's V). We estimated bivariate correlations between child MHSs, parental MHSs and other latent factors. Additionally, we estimated cross-twin correlations for behavioural/emotional symptoms across monozygotic and dizygotic twin pairs. Higher monozygotic compared to dizygotic cross-twin correlations would suggest additive genetic influences; but if nearly equal, would suggest shared environmental influences (Rijsdijk & Sham, 2002).

Next, we specified structural mediation models to identify indirect pathways linking parent and child MHS factors through risk/protective factors. First, we preliminarily specified separate single mediator models with each parent's MHS factor as a separate predictor, behavioural/emotional symptoms as separate outcomes, and each risk/protective factor as a mediator (Figure 1a). Secondly, we incorporated all the risk/protective factors as joint mediators in single models with each parental MHS factor and behavioural/emotional symptoms as a separate predictor and outcome, respectively (Figure 1b). This was to identify independent mediation paths for paternal and maternal MHSs separately. Thirdly, we incorporated both parental MHS factors as joint predictors in single models with behavioural/emotional symptoms as separate outcomes (Figure 1c). This was to test mediation pathways while adjusting for both parents' MHSs. We computed mediation effects as products of the standardised coefficients of the indirect paths (a and b, Figure 1a), and statistical significance was determined using Likelihood-based confidence intervals of the product which are robust for structural equation models (Falk & Biesanz, 2015). For ease of presentation, we reported the parsimonious multiple mediator models (non-significant paths dropped) in the main results and report the full models in the Supplementary material (Supplementary Table S9).

Biometric analyses

We incorporated the twin structure of the children's data into separate univariate twin models to estimate additive genetic (A), shared (C) and individual-specific (E) environmental influences on child behavioural/emotional symptoms (Rijsdijk & Sham, 2002). A influences index the sum of genetic influences across the whole genome; C influences, those that make twins similar to one another; and E influences, those that make twins different from one another, including measurement error (Rijsdijk & Sham, 2002; Willoughby et al., 2023). The design assumes identical (monozygotic) twins are genetically identical while non-identical (dizygotic) twins are, on average, 50% genetically similar; that both sets of twins are similarly impacted by their shared environment, and that mate selection among parents was random (Rijsdijk & Sham, 2002; Willoughby et al., 2023). In a final step, we incorporated both parental MHS factors into the separate univariate twin models for behavioural/emotional symptoms (Purcell & Koenen, 2005) and inspected for changes in the A, C and E estimates to indicate how these influences were impacted by parental MHSs.

Analyses were not preregistered due to the exploratory nature of the study.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate pathways between parental and childhood MHSs in sub-Saharan Africa using a genetically sensitive family-based design.

Paternal and maternal MHSs were significantly associated with childhood MHSs (behavioural and emotional symptoms), as were specific risk factors (adverse home environments indexed by intimate partner violence and household chaos, adverse childhood experiences [ACEs] and children's poor physical health) and diminished protective factors (parental warmth and prosociality). In the final models incorporating both parents' MHSs and all the risk/protective factors for each childhood MHS, ACEs and low prosociality mediated the associations with both childhood MHSs. In addition, children's poor physical health and low parental warmth further mediated the associations with emotional symptoms. Finally, biometric analyses indicated moderate additive genetic and shared environmental influences on childhood MHSs, and parental MHSs impacted on shared environmental influences on childhood MHSs independent of genetic influences.

Mediation of the associations between childhood and parental MHSs

Consistent with existing research, childhood MHSs were significantly associated with paternal and maternal MHSs (Jami et al., 2021; Scarlett et al., 2024). ACEs mediated the associations between childhood MHSs, and both paternal and maternal MHSs, as has been previously demonstrated in China and the United States (Brown et al., 2022; Chen, 2023).

ACEs had the largest indirect effects for behavioural symptoms and the second largest effects for emotional symptoms. Thus, our findings suggest ACEs as an important common pathway linking parental and children's MHSs in a LMIC (Rowell & Neal-Barnett, 2022).

Diminished prosociality further mediated the associations between maternal MHSs and both childhood MHSs, consistent with emerging evidence (Byrne et al., 2023). This pathway may reflect the developmental association between prosociality and emotion regulation (Malti & Speidel, 2024) which is transdiagnostically associated with behavioural and emotional symptoms (Compas et al., 2017). However, further research is needed to corroborate our finding in other LMIC contexts.

Mediation pathways involving diminished paternal and maternal warmth and children's poor physical health were unique to emotional symptoms. Diminished parental warmth had the largest indirect effects for the associations between emotional symptoms and parental MHSs. Our finding extends evidence from LMICs by highlighting the importance of paternal warmth in early childhood (Osborne & Ahinkorah, 2024). This contrasts with the cultural stereotype of fathers' roles solely as providers in these contexts (Nambile Cumber et al., 2024). Consistent with findings from HICs (Bayer et al., 2011; Pierce et al., 2020), poor physical health further mediated the association between maternal MHSs and emotional symptoms. Thus, independent of ACEs, maternal MHSs in LMICs may be associated with childhood emotional symptoms through reduced nurturing and increased vulnerability to recurrent acute illnesses or suboptimal adjustment to/recovery from chronic conditions.

Notably, the above mediation paths were independent of confounding by socioeconomic indices and obstetric complications which had smaller associations with childhood MHSs. An important negative finding was the absence of significant mediation by adverse home environments (household chaos and intimate partner violence). The attenuation of the indirect effects of adverse home environment in the single versus multiple mediation models suggests the possibility that its associations with childhood MHSs are further mediated by the other risk/protective factors. Similarly, religiosity was not a significant mediator, though it was positively associated with maternal and childhood MHSs. Although the positive associations contrast with the characterisation of religiosity as a protective factor (Laird et al., 2011), they may reflect possible masking by ongoing stressors (Braam & Koenig, 2019; Osundina et al., 2017).

Genetic and environmental influences on childhood MHSs

Consistent with research from HICs (e.g., van der Valk et al., 2003), we demonstrated genetic and environmental influences on MHSs in early childhood. Our finding is also consistent with that from a LMIC (Zheng et al., 2016), though among older children; and may reflect factors in both the family and wider social context in LMICs.

We extended knowledge by demonstrating that parental MHSs impact on the shared environmental influences on child MHSs independent of genetic influences in our LMIC sample of preschool children and their parents. This finding is consistent with existing evidence from HICs (Ahmadzadeh et al., 2021; Jaffee et al., 2002; Jami et al., 2021). However, our approach was unable to detect genetic contributions to parent-child MHS associations (Purcell & Koenen, 2005). Shared environmental influences on parent-child MHS associations may also be biased by shared rater variance (Van der Valk et al., 2001) whereby estimates can be inflated or diminished when parents unintentionally rate twins as being more similar to or different from each other respectively (Simonoff et al., 1998).

Implications for policy

In demonstrating shared environmental influences on parent-child MHS associations in this Nigerian sample, similar to HICs, our findings suggest the importance of elucidating environmental causal pathways in parent-child MHS associations in LMICs. Possible pathways include physical health and responsive caregiving which are incorporated in the World Health Organisation's Nurturing Care Framework for Early Childhood Development (ECD) (World Health Organization et al., 2018). We further highlight the importance of fathers' responsive caregiving roles in a LMIC, and potential benefits of targeting them in child mental health and ECD interventions as are being designed in Nigeria currently (Hammanyero, 2026). We also highlight prosociality as a potential intervention target in a LMIC (Byrne et al., 2023; Kubzansky et al., 2023). However, more genetically informative child mental health research is needed in LMICs using larger samples and more rigorous designs capable of testing causal pathways in these settings.

Strengths and Limitations

Given the dearth of childhood mental health research in LMICs (Wilson & Patel, 2026; Yu et al., 2023), our study fills a critical research gap. We consolidated on this by incorporating information from both parents (Zecchinato et al., 2025) and simultaneously investigating multiple risk/protective factors to assess the relative importance of these factors.

Using genetically informative data, we provide preliminary estimates of genetic and environmental influences on early childhood MHSs in a LMIC setting; and explored how these influences impact on parent-child MHS associations.

Despite these strengths, our findings need to be interpreted considering the following limitations. Although our absolute sample size was large (1230 adults and children), adjusting for clustering reduced this to 320 household units (and twin pairs), which may be underpowered to detect small effects (including estimates of A and C influences on child MHSs) as evidenced by wide confidence intervals. Our statistical approach may overestimate shared environmental influences on parent-child MHS associations because parental MHSs in this study were obligatorily shared by each twin pair (Purcell & Koenen, 2005), and mothers rated both their own and their children's MHSs. Our use of cross-sectional data precludes causal inference which is important given emerging evidence of bidirectional causal associations between child and parent MHSs/risk/protective factors (Ahmadzadeh et al., 2019; Bright et al., 2026). These limitations may be overcome in future LMIC research by combining larger samples with more advanced biometric genetic designs. These designs can detect bidirectional causal effects in parent-child associations using cross-sectional data while controlling for confounding by genetic risk transmission (McAdams et al., 2018).

Conclusion

Using a sample of children and both parents in a LMIC, we identified risk (adverse childhood experiences and poor physical health) and diminished protective factors (prosociality and parental warmth) as mediators of cross-sectional parent-child MHS associations during early childhood. We further provided (to our knowledge) the first preliminary estimates of genetic and environmental influences on MHSs during early childhood. Our findings suggest that parent-child MHS associations reflect shared environmental influences.

What's known?

 Parental mental health symptoms (MHSs) are strong risk factors for childhood MHSs.

 Little is known about these pathways in Low-/Middle-Income Countries (LMICs).

What's new?

 Using a genetically informative design in a Nigerian sample, we identified key risk (adverse childhood experiences and children's poor physical health) and diminished protective (prosociality and parental warmth) factors that partly explained parent-child MHS associations.

 The shared environment is an important component of parent-child MHS associations in our LMIC sample, independent of genetic influences.

What's relevant?

 We highlighted key risk and protective factors relevant for understanding childhood MHSs in a LMIC.

 Fathers should be included in childhood MHS research/interventions in LMICs.

 Future childhood MHS research in LMICs should use larger genetically informative samples to allow causal inference.
