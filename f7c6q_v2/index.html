Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Existing research into the link between teachers' emotions and the quality of their lessons has generally relied either on students' or teachers' ratings of instructional quality and drawn on teacher samples from homogeneous cultural contexts. The present study addressed these limitations by employing data from the multi-national Global Teaching InSights Study, in which 652 mathematics teachers in eight countries in East Asia, Europe, and South America self-reported their experiences of enjoyment, anger, and anxiety about teaching, and expert raters assessed instructional quality along the three established dimensions of classroom management, social-emotional support, and instructional support. We used fixed-effects linear models to obtain estimates for the covariation between self-reported trait emotions and observer-rated instructional quality dimensions. For social-emotional support and teacher anger / teacher anxiety, links were negative and significant; for social-emotional support and teacher enjoyment, links were positive but did not reach statistical significance. These effects were largely homogeneous across the international samples, as exploratory analyses revealed. For classroom management and instructional support, effects were negligible in size, and specifically teacher enjoyment-instructional support links were highly internationally heterogeneous. These findings provide essential insights into how teachers' internal emotional experiences translate into their outward instructional behaviors, and the nuances brought about by varying assessment approaches for instructional quality. The observed cross-country variability may spark future cross-national, cross-cultural research in this domain.

1 Introduction

Great lessons inspire, poor lessons bore and annoy-and some teachers deliver great lessons far more often than others. The crucial role of instructional quality for students' learning is wellestablished (Hattie, 2003 (Hattie, , 2023;; Ruiz-Alfonso et al., 2020), as is the fact that it varies substantially (Mullis et al., 2020). A deeper understanding of the sources of differences in instructional quality across teachers may prove decisive in the quest to improve the quality of instruction worldwide.

Prior research shows that instructional quality is closely linked with a wide array of cognitive, moti-vational, emotional, attitudinal, and contextual factors. For example, one area which past research (Baumert et al., 2010; Fauth et al., 2019; Kunter et al., 2013) has consistently emphasized concerns teachers' different types of knowledge, i.e., content knowledge, pedagogical knowledge, and pedagogical content knowledge (Shulman, 1987). Above and beyond such cognitive factors, affective aspects like motivation also have been shown to play a role regarding how teachers approach teaching and to what degree they succeed (Baumert & Kunter, 2013; Blömeke et al., 2018). In this context, teachers' emotions have started receiving increased research attention in the last two decades (Frenzel et al., 2021; Sutton & Wheatley, 2003). Since emotions are among the most salient determinants for human cognition and action (Clore & Schiller, 2016) and teachers' emotions have been shown to play a pivotal role for various classroom processes (Burić, 2020; Frenzel et al., 2009 Frenzel et al., , 2021)), this represents fertile ground for additional research. If teacher emotions can explain differences in the quality of teaching, this would have important implications for teacher education and training. There is initial empirical evidence of links between teachers' emotional experiences and various facets of their instructional behavior (e.g., Burić & Frenzel, 2023; J. Chen, 2019a J. Chen, , 2019b;; Frenzel et al., 2009; Hagenauer & Hascher, 2018; Lazarides et al., 2021; Russo et al., 2020; Saunders, 2013; Seitz et al., 2015; Trigwell, 2012; Zhang et al., 2019), which so far exclusively used student-or teacher-reports of instructional quality, and was limited in the cultural scope, predominantly sampling western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic (WEIRD; see Henrich et al., 2010) countries. The present study seeks to add to this literature. The specific research question addressed by our study is whether teacher self-reported experiences of enjoyment, anger, and anxiety are systematically correlated with observerrated instructional quality, across a broad and diverse range of eight countries on three continents: Chile, the People's Republic of China, Colombia, Germany, Japan, Mexico, Spain, and the United Kingdom. This is also intended to help answer the question whether pleasant teacher emotions broadly correlate with higher instructional quality and unpleasant ones with lower instructional quality. For this, we utilize data from the Global Teaching InSights Study (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2020c). The Global Teaching InSights Study represents the first major international study to systematically collect data on secondary school mathematics teachers' self-reported classroom emotions as well as video-based observer ratings of instructional quality (Opfer, 2020a).

1.1 Conceptualizing Teacher Emotions

In line with contemporary conceptualizations of emotions, we view them as multi-component phenomena comprising affective, cognitive, motivational, expressive, and physiological components which have their origin in appraisals of stimuli or situations (Ekman, 1999; Pekrun, 2006; K. R. Scherer, 2005 K. R. Scherer, , 2009)), a framework which has been commonly applied to teacher emotions in past research (Burić & Frenzel, 2019; Frenzel et al., 2016; Sutton & Wheatley, 2003).

More specifically, in line with Frenzel et al. (2021), we view teacher emotions as "evaluative reactions, encompassing various psychological and physical subsystems that are uniquely embedded in the specific events and social contexts teachers encounter in their profession" (p. 251) which then induce certain emotion-specific action tendencies depending on that evaluation (Frijda et al., 1989; K. R. Scherer, 2005). The triggers of teacher emotions in the mathematics classroom fit well into the pattern of teacher emotion triggers described by Frenzel (2014), including students' learning and achievement, engagement and motivation of students, instructional effectiveness, relationships with students, as well as support and recognition from parents and supervisors (Martínez-Sierra et al., 2022). The body of research on teacher emotions has also clearly shown that teachers vary in their tendencies to experience different emotions: Some habitually experience stronger and more frequent teaching enjoyment, some tend to react with anger more often and more intensely than others during teaching, yet others experience anxiety during teaching more frequently and intensely (Frenzel et al., 2016).

Existing literature on discrete teacher emotions has commonly focused on teacher enjoyment, teacher anger, and teacher anxiety. Teacher enjoyment and teacher anger are the most commonly reported positive and negative teacher emotion, respectively (Burić & Frenzel, 2019; Frenzel et al., 2015 Frenzel et al., , 2016;; Jiang et al., 2021; Sutton, 2007), and teacher anxiety has traditionally received considerable research interest in part due to its high relevance for teachers' well-being (Chang, 2013; Frenzel et al., 2016; Sinclair & Ryan, 1987) and the historic focus on teacher anxiety in the field of teacher emotion research (Frenzel et al., 2016; Sinclair & Ryan, 1987). The Global Teaching InSights Study measured these three teacher emotions using self-report measures of teacher-specific, trait-like teaching enjoyment, anger, and anxiety (Mihaly et al., 2021; Praetorius et al., 2020).

1.2 Conceptualizing Instructional Quality

Assessing the links between teacher emotions and instructional quality is far from straightforward; one reason for this is that instructional quality is a hotly debated, highly complex phenomenon. Classroom teaching takes place in a multi-faceted social environment, and researchers disagree on the exact scope of the term "instructional quality" (Charalambous & Praetorius, 2023). In the present contribution-in line with the Global Teaching InSights Study-we adopt a view that has gained theoretical consensus across different strands of instructional quality research and has received extensive empirical support: It rests on the idea that instructional quality resides in deep structures of teaching, which can be conceptualized in separate dimensions.

One of the well-established strands of instructional quality research coined in the German research community posits that instructional quality can best be described through the three dimensions classroom management, student support, and cognitive activation (Klieme et al., 2001; Praetorius et al., 2018). In the United States, Pianta et al. (2008) proposed a similar structure and developed the Classroom Assessment Scoring System (CLASS) as a tool for external instructional quality ratings. In this framework, features of instructional quality are separated into the dimensions of classroom organization (mapping on classroom management), socialemotional support (mapping on student support), and instructional support. Instructional support maps on, but also goes beyond cognitive activation, as it additionally entails instructionally supporting techniques such as scaffolding and internal differentiation (Kleickmann et al., 2020).

First, classroom organization contains a set of more or less overt practices which teachers use to avoid disruptions and ensure student attentiveness (Bell, Castellano, & Klieme, 2020). Effective teachers reach this goal by reacting appropriately to disruptions, by implementing efficient routines, and by monitoring the students' actions at all times (Bell, Castellano, & Klieme, 2020; Pianta & Hamre, 2009; Praetorius et al., 2018). Accordingly, this was measured in the Global Teaching InSights Study by noting how teachers handled disruptions, organized classroom routines, and monitored their classroom (Bell, Castellano, & Klieme, 2020).

Second, social-emotional support consists of teacher practices related to respect, encouragement, warmth, and support for struggling students (Bell, Klieme, & Castellano, 2020; Pianta & Hamre, 2009). Social-emotional support allows students to feel safe in their learning environment, which encourages them to take risks, e.g., proposing solutions they are not quite sure about (Hattie, 2023; Sharma, 2015). Accordingly, in the Global Teaching InSights Study, social-emotional support was determined by observing teachers' behaviors related to respect, encouragement, warmth, and support for struggling students (Bell, Klieme, & Castellano, 2020).

Third, the dimension of instructional support covers all content-related aspects of instructional qual-ity (Pianta & Hamre, 2009). In the Global Teaching InSights Study, it is simply referred to as "instruction," and the present paper will follow that terminology. It is further separated into four subdimensions: "Discourse" covers aspects like the quality of explanations given by the teacher, facilitating learning through appropriate questions, or whether students speak sufficiently often compared to the teacher. "Student cognitive engagement" concerns the question whether students are actively engaged in trying to solve a problem using multiple different approaches while understanding the necessary processes and procedures. "Assessment of and responses to student understanding" relates to whether teachers manage to entice students to convey their thoughts and whether teachers are then able to dynamically adapt their teaching to students' present understanding. Finally, "quality of subject matter" describes how well teachers convey logical connections and patterns in a clear and understandable way (Bell, Schweig, et al., 2020).

Of the aspects of instructional quality, instruction is the one most closely linked to the subject and content of a lesson, whereas the factors that define classroom organization and social-emotional support are considered more generic (Dreher & Leuders, 2021; Praetorius et al., 2014). High-quality mathematics instruction requires that teachers possess broad and deep conceptual knowledge of mathematics to be able to explain or illustrate concepts in different ways that make sense to students (Baumert et al., 2010). These teachers should then be able to teach mathematics in a rich way (including multiple representations, explicit links between these representations, and high-quality explanations), dynamically use students' ideas and misconceptions, lead activities that are connected to the relevant mathematical concepts, teach mathematics equitably to all students (Learning Mathematics for Teaching Project, 2011), present mathematics procedures clearly, and demonstrate the use of relevant mathematical tools like calculators in an instructive way (Charalambous & Litke, 2018). Accordingly, the specific topic of a mathematics lesson influences how teachers configure their instruction (Kunter et al., 2013). Thus, in order to ensure the best possible comparability, the Global Teaching InSights Study covered lessons that focused on the same topic in all classrooms across all participating countries, namely introduction to quadratic equations (Klieme & Reiss, 2020).

Instructional quality is notoriously difficult to measure. Researchers often use student or teacher ratings; however, students have difficulty determining if a lesson is cognitively engaging in a way that is conducive to learning, and they often cannot differentiate between overwhelmingly difficult lessons and cognitively engaging ones (Fauth et al., 2020; Kunter & Baumert, 2007; Sigurjónsson et al., 2022). Teachers, on the other hand, can suffer from selfrepresentation bias that may lead them to overstating the quality of their own teaching (Kopcha & Sullivan, 2007). Furthermore, when studies derive both information on teacher emotions and information on instructional quality from teachers' responses, this issue is compounded by single-source bias (Frenzel et al., 2021; Podsakoff et al., 2003). In such cases, it becomes unclear which share of the association between instructional quality and various teacher emotions is derived from a genuine correlation and which share is due to the common source of the information on both constructs (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

An alternative approach is to have a trained third party observe a lesson and rate its quality. Advantages of external ratings, compared to teacher or student reports, include not only the absence of self-representation bias or sympathy halo effects (Feistauer & Richter, 2017; Röhl & Rollett, 2021), but also optimized conceptual validity and measurement specificity through the use of structured rating manuals (see, e.g., Bell, 2020) and the professional knowledge of external raters regarding instructional quality (Kunter & Baumert, 2007), especially compared to laypeople as raters, whose assumptions about what good teaching entails can skew results (see Flink et al., 1990). At the same time, external raters can typically only rate a relatively small number of lessons, which can limit overall validity (Praetorius et al., 2014; Weston et al., 2021); the expense and effort inherent to the method limits attainable sample sizes (Praetorius et al., 2014; Weston et al., 2021); and the fact that even expert raters are susceptible to various sources of distortion, such as rater drift (Casabianca et al., 2015; Gitomer et al., 2014). Therefore, different sources of information on instructional quality like expert ratings and student ratings capture different facets of instructional quality, rather than one approach being unambiguously superior (Fauth et al., 2020; Kunter & Baumert, 2007).

1.3 Associations between Teacher Emotions and Instructional Quality

Affective science suggests that emotions, cognition, motivation, and behavior are closely intertwined.

Accordingly, in her model on causes and effects of teacher emotions, Frenzel (2014) posits that the emo-tions teachers experience influence their cognition, motivation, and behavior and thus shape the quality of their teaching through a range of psychological mechanisms, while teachers' behaviors influence student behaviors and outcomes, which in turn influence their emotions (see also Burić & Frenzel, 2023; Frenzel et al., 2021). While data used in the present study do not allow for an analysis of causal directions, the theoretical foundations for current research into teacher emotion-instructional quality links, with the reciprocal model at their center, make certain assumptions about directions of influence.

Grounding our research in relevant theory thus requires the inclusion of causal assumptions that will not be empirically tested by the present study.

One mechanism through which teacher emotions likely affect their teaching behavior is that of cognitive processing styles. According to the "Broadenand-Build Theory," pleasant emotions positively impact individuals' cognitive flexibility and creativity (Fredrickson & Branigan, 2005; Sugawara & Sugie, 2021), which are both of great importance in the complex and unpredictable classroom environment. This implies that teachers who habitually experience pleasant emotions during teaching should have access to more instructional strategies and adapt better to different situations during their lessons (Frenzel, 2014; Frenzel et al., 2021). Conversely, managing unpleasant emotions like anger and anxiety imposes a cognitive load penalty, leaving less working memory capacity free to fulfill the varied requirements of teaching a lesson (Plass & Kalyuga, 2019). This is particularly true since open displays of teachers' anger and anxiety are both commonly considered unprofessional (Hagenauer et al., 2016), meaning that teachers must commit cognitive resources to downregulating their emotional expression when they feel angry or anxious. Moreover, worrying associated with anxiety can be expected to result in reduced cognitive control, which makes it harder for an anxious person to quickly shift their attention between different tasks and stimuli (Beckwé et al., 2014)-a key requirement for teachers to successfully manage their classrooms and support student learning.

Additionally, emotions have a motivational component (K. R. Scherer, 2005), and this associated motivational tendency predicts how much time and effort individuals invest in fulfilling tasks (Halbesleben & Bowler, 2007; Plass & Kalyuga, 2019). It is therefore also a reasonable proposition that repeated experiences of pleasant emotions during teaching fuel teachers' willingness to invest time and effort in lesson preparation, while frequent and intense expe-riences of anger or anxiety ought to coincide with avoidance of teaching-related activities outside of school, hence reducing lesson preparation quality and time.

Above and beyond those proposed effects from emotions on instructional quality, Frenzel and colleagues also emphasize the systemic nature of classroom processes which involves close and reciprocal interconnections between all actors' behaviors (Frenzel et al., 2009 (Frenzel et al., , 2021)). Hence, efficient and successful teaching, bringing about few disturbances and high student engagement, can be expected to positively impact teachers' confidence and self-efficacy levels and in turn promote positive emotions like enjoyment, whereas delivering instruction of poor quality undermines teachers' confidence and sense of control over teaching activities and can therefore give rise to experiences of negative emotions such as anger and anxiety (for initial empirical evidence on such reciprocal effects between instructional quality and teacher emotions in Germany and Croatia, see Burić & Frenzel, 2023). Overall, there are compelling theoretical reasons for assuming that high quality instruction will coincide with heightened levels of teacher enjoyment, while poor quality instruction will be linked with elevated levels of teacher anger and anxiety. However, the existing evidence for such links between teacher emotions and instructional quality is still scarce. With the present study, we set out to add to this evidence by exploring those links using observer ratings of instructional quality across several countries world-wide, using the Global Teaching InSights data (OECD, 2020c).

1.4 Considerations on Country and Cross-Cultural Comparisons

The topic of quadratic equations was chosen in the context of the Global Teaching InSights Study because it showed the highest overlap between countries regarding curriculum implementation and age of the students (Klieme, 2020). However, differences in countries' educational systems still lead to some systematic differences between countries, which are depicted in detail in Table A4 in the Appendix. All countries except for Colombia have compulsory mathematics curricula on the national or state level. A tertiary education is required for secondary mathematics teacher qualification in all countries, though the specific implementation differs. The most salient difference is related to the grade level at which the introductory lessons on quadratic equations were located across the different countries in the sample:

While most countries introduce quadratic equations in grades 8 or 9, Chile does so only in grade 10 or 11 (McCaffrey, 2021; Opfer et al., 2020). Accordingly, Chinese and Spanish students in the sample were on average less than fourteen years old, while Chilean students were on average older than sixteen (Opfer et al., 2020). This raises the question whether instructional quality is sufficiently comparable across countries and cultures. Deep structures of instructional quality in mathematics are applicable across countries worldwide (Blömeke et al., 2016), the same broad dimensions of instructional quality benefit student achievement no matter the country (Kyriakides et al., 2013), and dimensions of instructional quality show metric measurement invariance (Herbert et al., 2022; R. Scherer & Nilsen, 2016; Senden et al., 2023). Instructional quality also shows comparable correlations with teacher variables like teacher experience and self-efficacy across countries (Blömeke et al., 2016). In the context of the Global Teaching InSights Study, a comprehensive effort was undertaken to harmonize international and country-specific views of instructional quality based on a broad literature review and input from national experts to ensure the observation framework was applicable to teaching in all participating countries and cultures (Bell, Klieme, & Praetorius, 2020).

The Global Teaching InSights Study focused on teachers not only from eight different countries, but also three continents with different cultural backgrounds. Given that, the additional question arises whether and to what degree emotions can be expected to be functionally equivalent across cultures and countries and whether and to what degree the emotions teachers experience in class can thus be assumed to covary with their teaching behavior in similar ways across cultures and countries. The question of culture-specificity versus universality of emotions is contentious within broader affective science (Barrett, 2016; Barrett et al., 2025; De Leersnyder et al., 2021; Ekman, 2018; Keltner et al., 2019; Mesquita et al., 2016). Additionally, there is evidence for crosscultural differences in emotion display rules, with implications for how culturally appropriate individuals from different cultures deem it to outwardly express the emotions they experience (Matsumoto, 1990; Safdar et al., 2009). In the context of investigating correlates of academic emotions, the most developed view is perhaps that of relative universality (Pekrun & Goetz, 2023), which holds that the ways distinct emotions affect (and are affected by) classroom processes are largely universal, though the strength of such correlations may well be culture-dependent. This maps onto the assumption of repertoire equivalence (Fontaine & Breugelmans, 2021), according to which emotions are understood to entail comparable action tendencies across cultures, while the way they manifest in observable action can be affected by culture-specific factors.

This assumption of relative universality is in line with cross-cultural studies like those by Alonso- Arbiol et al. (2011), Maitner et al. (2022), and She et al. (2017), which demonstrated similar behavioral tendencies for anger and fear across cultures. Regarding teacher emotions specifically, evidence remains scarce, although Chinese results on the connection between teacher emotions and instructional quality (J. Chen, 2019b) imply similarities to findings from Europe (Burić & Frenzel, 2023).

Beyond this, Wang et al. (2023) explicitly tested for moderating effects of "Western" vs. "Non-Western" samples in their meta-analysis on teacher emotion regulation-well-being links and did not find substantial differences regarding the correlation between emotion suppression and well-being among teachersfoot_0 . This counters the idea that teachers from cultures that require stronger suppression of emotion expression might suffer from a cognitive load penalty, compromising their potential to provide high-quality instruction. Finally, research has shown comparable links between teacher motivation and instructional quality (Nalipay et al., 2023) as well as between teacher self-efficacy and instructional quality (Blömeke et al., 2016; Zee & Koomen, 2016) across cultures, bolstering the case for cross-cultural similarities of the links between teachers' affective traits and their instructional quality.

Following this reasoning, we propose that the degree to which teachers from different cultures and contexts report enjoyment, anger, and anxiety during teaching may well differ across cultures and contexts, and the frequency and qualitatively manifested manner of showing certain teaching-quality-related behaviors may well vary across the various countries sampled in the Global Teaching InSights Study. However, the general direction of links between teacher emotions and their instructional behavior should be similar across those countries. Specifically, we propose that across countries, teachers' enjoyment should be positively linked with several facets of teaching quality, while teacher anger and anxiety should show negative links.

At the same time, we do not exclude the possibility that the strengths of these generally positive and negative links may vary across different cultural contexts. For example, it is conceivable that (classroom) cultures characterized by higher power distance across teachers and their students may be marked by stronger links between teacher anger and classroom management, as teachers in such cultures more strongly externalize their anger in terms of disciplining behaviors (which in turn may not even be perceived as particularly controlling by the students in these cultures, e.g. Zhou et al., 2012). In turn, it is also conceivable that teacher-reported anger shows weaker links with observer-rated teaching behaviors in cultures where display rules make the outward expression of anger culturally inappropriate; teachers may internally experience anger but not show corresponding outward behavior which observers could perceive and rate according to its impact on instructional quality.

For the specific context of the present study, it is important to note that "culture" is not equivalent to "country." For the countries sampled within the Global Teaching InSights Study in particular, it is challenging to systematically map them on different cultural frameworks, such as the classic individualisticcollectivistic distinction (Beugelsdijk & Welzel, 2018; Hofstede, 2001; Minkov & Kaasa, 2022). Specifically, Germany and the United Kingdom would generally classify as individualistic and China, Chile, Mexico, and especially Colombia as collectivistic, with Japan and Spain situated in the middle (Hofstede, 2001; Minkov, 2018; Pelham et al., 2022).

Furthermore, assigning entire countries to such rough cultural groups remains controversial (R. Fischer & Schwartz, 2011; Leung & Cohen, 2011; Smith et al., 2017 Smith et al., , 2021)), as does the factorial validity of the individualism-collectivism dimension (Taras et al., 2014). Hence, we proposed that the Global Teaching InSights Study's international, unprecedentedly large teacher sample is highly suitable to test for the general direction of teacher emotion-teaching quality links and therefore the overall functional role and theoretical plausibility of teacher emotions as decisive factors for classroom dynamics. However, it did not seem particularly suitable to substantiate any main or moderating effects of culture. For these reasons, we focused our directed hypotheses on general expected patterns across the full Global Teaching InSights sample, while planning to addition-ally explore potential differences between countries in an open-ended manner.

1.5 The Present Study

In exploring potential links between teachers' selfreported emotions and observer-rated instructional quality, this study addresses important gaps in existing research: A lack of outside observer perspectives and a lack of cross-national analyses. First, past studies have commonly assessed instructional quality via teachers' or students' reports. As explained previously, this approach does not capture all aspects of instructional quality equally well. It is worth noting that it is by no means trivial to assume that subjective, internal emotional experiences as reported by teachers indeed manifest themselves in terms of behaviors implying higher or lower levels of instructional quality as observable to external one-time "visitors" of a classroom. Yet the theory provided above on cognitive, behavioral and motivational implications of emotional experiences provides compelling grounds for assuming this.

Second, previous studies concerning the link between teacher emotions and instructional quality have usually been limited to a single country or conducted on a per-country basis, whereas both instructional quality and teacher emotions are highly relevant phenomena in classrooms all over the world. This study takes advantage of data from eight different countries on three continents, covering a range of both industrialized and emerging countries from the Global Teaching InSights Study (OECD, 2020c), which represents the first major international study to collect data on secondary school mathematics teachers' self-reported classroom emotions as well as observer ratings of instructional quality based on classroom videos (Opfer, 2020b).

Based on our previous considerations on relative universality of teacher emotions and cross-national / cross-cultural comparability of broader dimensions of instructional quality, we suspected that we would find similar effects for each country in the sample, but we allowed for the possibility of significant differences. Exploring in detail the degree to which the relationships between teacher emotions and facets of instructional quality potentially vary is therefore an additional benefit of this study. In view of the relatively large overall teacher sample size of the Global Teaching InSights Study, the present study has substantial potential for advancing our under-standing of teacher emotions, instructional quality, and their interrelations.

Analyses of existing datasets like ours may invite data-dredging or "fishing expeditions" for significant correlations, or suspicions of such behavior (Nosek & Lakens, 2014). Even without malice, excessive flexibility during the process of analyzing pre-existing datasets can easily introduce false positive findings (Simmons et al., 2011). Therefore, we conceptualized this study as a secondary registered report, which aids in minimizing such issues (Nosek & Lakens, 2014).

2 Research Questions and Hypotheses

The overarching research question for the present study is whether and to which extent teacher emotions in mathematics classrooms worldwide are linked to observable facets of instructional quality. We hypothesized that teachers' self-reported enjoyment would be positively associated with observers' ratings of classroom management (H1.

1), socialemotional support (H1.2) and instruction (H1.3), as well as the sub-dimensions of instruction, which in the Global Teaching InSights Study were discourse (H1.3.1), student cognitive engagement (H1.3.2.), assessment of and responses to student understanding (H1.3.3), and quality of subject matter (H1.3.4). Conversely, we expected that teachers' self-reported anger (H2.1, H2.2, H2.3, H2.3.1, H2.3.2, H2.3.3, and H2.3.4, respectively) and anxiety (H3.1, H3.2, H3.3, H3.3.1, H3.3.2, H3.3.3, and H3.3.4, respectively) would be negatively associated with those facets of instructional quality. We additionally explored whether the strength of each of these correlations varies significantly between countries. Due to the limited theoretical basis, we refrained from stating hypotheses of either equivalence or specificity.

4.2 Sample and Procedure

The Global Teaching InSights Study sampled a total of N = 652 teachers from eight countries: Chile, China, Colombia, Germany, Japan, Mexico, Spain, and the United Kingdom (Opfer, 2020a) who taught mathematics at secondary schools of any level outside of special education (McCaffrey, 2021). Of those, 47% identified as female, and 61% had ten years or more of teaching experience (Opfer, 2020a). Table 1 shows the per-country breakdown of the demographic data.

In each country, the mathematics lesson unit in which the topic of quadratic equations was first formally introduced marked the focal time of data collection. Efforts were undertaken to draw a random sample of teachers from each country, i.e., drawing a random sample from all suitable secondary schools and then drawing a random sample from all eligible teachers within a school (McCaffrey, 2021). Teachers were eligible if they were currently teaching a class where the topic of "introduction to quadratic equations" would be covered in the ongoing school year. Random sampling across the country was achieved in Colombia, whereas in Chile, China, Mexico, and the United Kingdom, random sampling took place, but only from schools within parts of the country. Germany, Japan, and Spain did not use a random sampling procedure (Yoo & Propp, 2021). The sampled teachers participated together with their target class where the topic of quadratic equations was introduced in the same school year.

Two lessons covering the introduction of quadratic equations were videotaped; the choice of lessons was determined based on teachers' and videographers' schedules and availability (Yoo & Propp, 2021). The research design additionally involved two sets of self-report surveys for teachers. One was administered before the lesson series on quadratic equations started (referred to as pre-questionnaire in the study); it involved a range of scales on general classroom routines and teacher attitudes, including their self-reported emotions which were used for the present analyses. The second questionnaire was administered after the lesson series on quadratic equations had ended (referred to as post-questionnaire in the study); it pertained specifically to this particular lesson series. These data were not used in the present analyses. As an international study, the Global Teaching InSights Study employed equivalent procedures in all eight participating countries (Opfer, 2020a).

4.3 Measures

4.3.1 Teacher Emotions Scales

Teacher enjoyment, anger, and anxiety while teaching the target class were measured using the Teacher Emotions Scales (Frenzel et al., 2016) in the teacher pre-questionnaire of the Global Teaching InSights Study. Each emotion was assessed using four items with four-point Likert scales from1 = strongly disagree to4 = strongly agree (Mihaly et al., 2021). For example, an item for enjoyment is "I often have reasons to be happy while I teach these students," for anger "I often feel annoyed while teaching these students," and for anxiety "I am often worried that my teaching isn't going so well in this class" (OECD, 2020a; see Appendix, Table A1 for all items). Reliability for all scales was good in the original validation study for the Teacher Emotions Scales, with 𝛼 = .72 to .90 for teacher enjoyment, 𝛼 = .80 to .89 for teacher anger, and 𝛼 = .70 to .89 for teacher anxiety (Frenzel et al., 2016). The language of the questionnaire was Spanish in Chile, Colombia, Mexico, and Spain, Chinese in China, German in Germany, Japanese in Japan, and English in the United Kingdom (Opfer, 2021). English and German language versions were provided to the OECD by the original scale authors; the corresponding OECD country managers were responsible for translating the scales into Spanish, Chinese, and Japanese.

To determine scale reliability across countries for the Global Teaching InSights Study, which only reported per-country reliabilities, we accessed the data in a limited way (see Researcher Integrity Statement). In line with recommendations by McNeish (2018), we calculated McDonald's 𝜔 in place of Cronbach's 𝛼. This yielded moderate to good (Shrout, 1998) reliability for each scale, with 𝜔 = .83 for teacher enjoyment, 𝜔 = .74 for teacher anger, and 𝜔 = .74 for teacher anxiety.

4.3.2 Video Analysis of Instructional Quality

Classroom management, social-emotional support, and instruction were assessed using expert ratings of video-recorded mathematics lessons (Bell, Castellano, & Klieme, 2020; Castellano & Bell, 2021). To obtain component scores, an observation coding system was developed and iteratively refined over two years prior to the main study. This included theoret-ical and empirical input from all participating countries (Pons, 2020). Rating was undertaken by specifically trained external raters. To ensure consistent and accurate rating of the lessons, a small group of master raters (between two and four for each participating country) underwent formal in-person training together before returning to their respective country, where they used centrally developed training materials to train local raters (Bell, 2020). On average, there were around 15 raters in each country, varying from 7 in Japan to 25 in Chile (Castellano & Bell, 2021). Regular calibrations comparing rater scores with master rater scores were employed to ensure consistency and address possible rating drift (Castellano & Bell, 2021).

Table A2 of the Appendix contains all items on instructional quality used in our study along with their descriptions according to Castellano and Bell (2021). Three items were used to judge classroom management, two for social-emotional support, and eleven for the instruction dimension (Castellano & Bell, 2021): The classroom management component score is calculated from the items "routines," "monitoring," and "handling disruptions" scores, and the social-emotional support domain score is calculated from the items "respect" and "encouragement and warmth." For instruction, an overall instruction score is calculated as the average of all eleven items. In addition, subdomain scores are built for instruction, denoted as "discourse" (comprising 3 items), "quality of subject matter" (comprising 2 items), "student cognitive engagement" (comprising 3 items), and "assessment of and responses to student understanding" (comprising 3 items) (see Appendix, Table A2; OECD, 2020b). Each item was rated by the observers on a four-point Likert scale (Castellano & Bell, 2021).

To derive overall per-teacher scores for each scale, lessons were segmented in 16-minute chunks to reduce cognitive demand for the raters (Bell, 2020); two lessons per teacher were recorded, each of which was partitioned, on average, into three to four segments. Afterwards, two raters scored each item for each chunk. Subsequently, scores of both raters for each chunk were averaged, then those per-chunk scores were averaged across all chunks, resulting in a single score per item for each teacher. Finally, higherorder scores for the scales were calculated as mean scores from the item scores-e.g., the "classroom management" score was calculated as the mean of the items "routines," "monitoring," and "handling disruptions" (Castellano & Bell, 2021).

Since the Global Teaching InSights Study itself only reports per-country reliabilities, we again accessed the data in a limited fashion (see Researcher Integrity Statement) to calculate across-country internal consistencies for each scale and subscale. Reliability for classroom management was moderate at 𝜔 = .63, as was reliability for social-emotional support at 𝜔 = .60. Reliability for instruction overall was good at 𝜔 = .83. As for the subscales of instruction, discourse (𝜔 = .68), student cognitive engagement (𝜔 = .66), and assessment of and responses to student understanding (𝜔 = .73) were all moderate, whereas quality of subject matter (𝜔 = .38) was not acceptable. Therefore, hypotheses regarding this sub-dimension (H1.3.4, H2.3.4, and H3.3.4) were excluded from the study, and quality of subject matter was generally excluded from analyses.

Our approach to power analysis for the single-slope model was a Monte Carlo simulation, similar to the method suggested for mixed-effect models by Hox et al. (2018). We defined a fixed-effects linear model to determine the correlation between each teacher emotion and each dimension of instructional quality, using one teacher emotion each as the predictor, one dimension of instructional quality as the criterion, and the countries as dummy-coded clustering variables. 4.5.2 Literature Review to Determine Expectable Effect Sizes for Emotion-Instructional Quality Links

For our literature review, we used standardized slopes (𝛽) or zero-order correlations (r) as measures of effect size and only considered bivariate associations. Existing literature on the association between teacher emotions and various aspects of instructional quality indicates a pattern of small to large effects with a fairly wide spread of potential effect sizes (see Appendix, Table A3), which may be due to the differences between the exact constructs being measured as well as methodological variations.

Notably, all sources in our literature review used either student or teacher ratings of instructional quality, making all estimations of effect sizes for observer ratings only approximations, since external observers may well measure somewhat different constructs compared to what teachers and students would report (Fauth et al., 2020). Previous literature linking teacher emotions with instructional quality has not so far employed observer ratings of instructional quality, but has usually relied on student ratings, making these studies quite different from the proposed analysis of the present study. While existing research does not provide sufficient information to conclude whether observer ratings of instructional quality should correlate with teacher emotions as much as student ratings do, a useful parallel is research linking teacher self-efficacy with instructional quality: In their meta-analysis, Klassen and Tze (2014) found comparable overall effect sizes for student ratings of instructional quality, at a median of r = .20 for student ratings and r = .21 for observer ratings. The number of studies using student ratings within that meta-analysis was low, but Toropova et al. (2019) again found a similar effect for student ratings. Thus, for a construct that is fairly closely related to teacher emotions conceptually and that relies on teacher self-reports in its measurement, effect sizes for student reports and observer ratings are comparable, bolstering the estimates for our power analysis. In conclusion, we consider the parameters extracted from existing literature on teacher emotion-instructional quality links informative for estimating plausible effect sizes for our novel exploration of the correlation between teacher emotions and observer-rated instructional quality-despite the fact that previous studies have relied on instructional quality ratings by teachers or students.

For teacher enjoyment, there exists an overall pattern of moderate effect sizes. The median effect size reported for teacher enjoyment in the studies under consideration (see Appendix, Table A3) is 𝛽 = .38. Effect sizes are somewhat larger when it comes to indicators of classroom management, which are all at moderate-to-large or large, with a median of 𝛽 = .61. However, there are fewer results available for classroom management than for the other dimensions. Effect sizes are mostly at least moderate for socialemotional support, with the sole exception of a very small effect (Hagenauer & Hascher, 2018) found for autonomy support; the median is 𝛽 = .28. Overall, finding moderate to large effect sizes for enjoyment and all instructional quality dimensions appears likely.

6.2 Teacher Emotion-Instructional Quality Links: Exploring Effects of Culture

When further scrutinizing these observed variabilities of the emotion-instructional quality links, one noticeable pattern we identified was the comparatively larger apparent effects in European countries compared to South American and East Asian nations. Following up on this, we conducted an additional exploratory analysis by arranging countries along an individualism-collectivism axis, using their individualism score extracted from waves 6 and 7 of the World Values Survey (Haerpfer et al., 2022; Inglehart et al., 2014) by Minkov and Kaasa (2022), and explored whether there was a systematic link between this score and each of the emotion-instructional quality links for the three main dimensions of overall instructional quality. We used the metafor package for R (Viechtbauer, 2010) to conduct meta-regression analysis on the individual regression coefficients across all countries, and tested whether individualism was a significant moderator. Compared to simply correlating regression coefficients with individualism, this approach allowed us to (1) account for standard errors and (2) apply the Hartung-Knapp modification for small numbers of effects (Jackson et al., 2017). We analyzed this for all three teacher emotions and the three superordinate dimensions of instructional quality. This showed a significant effect of individualism on the link between teacher enjoyment and classroom management, F(1, 6) = 8.73, p = .03, and teacher anger and classroom management, F(1, 6) = 8.41, p = .03, a tendency that is also clearly visible in Figure 3. Both the positive association between teacher enjoyment and classroom management and the negative association between teacher anger and classroom management increased in magnitude, the higher the corresponding country's individualism score: For one standard deviation of individualism, the associated change of the regression slope for teacher enjoyment was 𝛽 = 0.09, and the change of the slope for teacher anger was 𝛽 = -0.08.

7 Discussion

In our study, we aimed to analyze whether teachers' self-reported enjoyment, anger, and anxiety during teaching and observer-rated aspects of instructional quality are linked in accordance with the reciprocal model on causes and effects of teacher emotions (Frenzel, 2014; Frenzel et al., 2021). We utilized data from the international Global Teaching InSights Study and derived effects across all countries. The highly plausible theoretical underpinnings rooted in general affective science and Frenzel's reciprocal model as well as results from prior studies employing teacher or student ratings of instructional quality both suggested a high likelihood of finding moderately strong effects. However, we have-among our confirmatory analyses-only found one single statistically significant predictive effect that holds across all countries in the sample: A negative association between teacher-reported anger and observer-rated social-emotional support. Teacher-reported anxiety, too-while being excluded from our confirmatory analyses-showed a negative significant link with observer-rated social-emotional support. Teacherreported enjoyment showed a trend towards positive correlation with observer-rated social-emotional support with a similar absolute effect size as the negative effects for anger and anxiety, but this effect did not reach statistical significance. 4Overall, the present study showed that the more anger and, at least from an exploratory perspective, the more anxiety teachers reported feeling when teaching the target class, the less the external observers found those teachers to demonstrate emotionally and socially supportive behaviors. Notably, these effects only became fully evident when controlling for country-level differences in teaching quality ratings in the multiple-regression approach and were partially masked in the overall correlative findings. This highlights the importance of considering country-level differences in a large cross-national sample, such as the Global Teaching InSights Study. We also observed a statistically non-significant effect indicating that teachers' enjoyment may correlate with higher levels of observed social-emotional support, which in our opinion warrants further scientific inquiry in future research.

What teachers reported to feel on the inside indeed seems to reflect in their outward relational behavior, but not in their didactical or classroommanagerial behavior, as judged by external observers. This finding that social-emotional support stands out as the one dimension of instructional quality that is most clearly linked with teachers' emotions makes sense conceptually: The overall emotional tone of the classroom as well as the level of negativity (including anger) present are both aspects of this dimension (Hamre et al., 2013). Raters in the Global Teaching InSights Study used the quantity and severity of negative interactions in the classroom as well as shared warmth between teachers and students as indicators for social-emotional support (OECD, 2020b), both of which are reasonably linked with teachers' inward experiences of enjoyment and anger. The Global Teaching InSights Study's third indicator for social-emotional support focused on students' risk-taking and their propensity to freely share their work or ask questions. Links with such classroom events and teachers' emotional experiences makes sense in view of existing research, such as Frenzel et al. (2016), who found that teachers' enjoyment correlates positively and teacher anxiety negatively with their acceptance of students' errors, which ought to influence students' preparedness to risk being wrong or asking a "stupid" question.

Importantly, though, except for these consistent but small teacher emotion-social-emotional support behavior links, our findings otherwise amounted to null-findings regarding the teacher emotion-instructional quality links. This contrasts with previous research (see Appendix, Table A3) generally finding moderate effects of teacher emotions across several instructional quality dimensions and contexts. Below, we provide three possible explanations for this.

7.1 Potential Explanation 1: Prior Research Overestimated Effects

The uniqueness of the present study lay in its assessment approaches: This is the first study which linked teacher emotions assessed via self-report with instructional quality assessed via expert ratings of instructional quality based on classroom video recordings. Teachers' emotional self-reports reflect their internal experiences of emotions during teaching, which may well diverge from how these emotions-and their consequences-become visible on the outside. Classroom display rules, variability in teacher emotional expressiveness, and emotion regulation effectiveness may explain those discrepancies (Frenzel et al., 2025; Matsumoto, 1990; Safdar et al., 2009; Taxer & Gross, 2018; Wang et al., 2023). As such, the present null-findings for effects of teacher emotions on their externally judged instructional quality (except for social-emotional support) may be explained by the potential fact that teachers on average are sufficiently successful at regulating their emotions, hiding or suppressing their negative experiences in class, to deliver decent levels of instructional quality irrespective of how they feel on the inside (see also Wang & Frenzel, 2025, regarding the double-edged function of surface acting among teachers). Consequently, there is a possibility that previous studies not employing observer scores may have overestimated the effect of teacher emotions on instructional quality. Prior research which used teachers as the data source for both their own emotions and instructional quality may well have suffered from some effect overestimation due to singlesource bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

Further, even studies that used students as observers-hence circumventing single-source bias-may have delivered over-estimated effects as students may be subject to psychological distortions such as halo effects (Röhl & Rollett, 2021): They may thus rate enthusiastic and joyful teachers more highly across a number of dimensions (also referred to as "educational seduction" or "Doctor Fox effect", see Naftulin et al., 1973, which, however, has been more or less refuted in more recent literature: see Frenzel et al., 2019; Peer and Babad, 2014). Finally, there is also a possibility that existing research is affected by publication bias and thus studies which-like this study-had previously found statistically insignificant results may exist but have never been published.

7.2 Potential Explanation 2: Prior Research Measured Different Aspects of Instructional Quality

Besides their respective potential for distortions, teachers, students, and external observers also perceive essentially different aspects of instructional quality (Fauth et al., 2020; Kunter & Baumert, 2007). Thus, there is another reasonable explanation for our unexpected null findings: It is possible that aspects of instructional quality of which teachers are more aware or which students notice more acutely are more tightly linked to teacher emotions. This link may, conversely, be much weaker for aspects of instructional quality which outside observers notice. This hinges on the idea that each of these data sources have their own unique perspective on instructional quality with its own specific validity (Fauth et al., 2020; Kunter & Baumert, 2007). It is also possible that teacher emotions interact more closely with aspects of instructional quality that become visible over a longer time frame (see Praetorius et al., 2014). The two lessons covered by the Global Teaching InSights Study may have been too short a time frame to capture certain facets of instructional quality (according to our findings, likely from the domains of classroom management and instruction) that are more visible in teacher or student ratings.

7.3 Potential Explanation 3: The Current Study Underestimates Effects

Finally, it is very plausible that distortions in the Global Teaching InSights Study's measurements masked existing effects. For one, the raters for the Global Teaching InSights Study were not present during the lessons but judged instructional quality based on classroom videos, and the impression of a lesson derived from a recording is substantially different-and potentially less accurate-than what a live observer would perceive (Curby et al., 2016). The framing of a video restricts both what raters can see and hear (Wyss et al., 2023). This may have, for example, distorted raters' ratings of classroom management by altering their impression of how noisy the classroom was at a given moment, or relevant events may have happened out of frame. It must also be acknowledged that the Global Teaching InSights data have clear psychometric limitations. Interrater reliability between raters as well as agreement between raters and master raters were not especially high across the domains (Castellano & Bell, 2021), and while our analysis of across-country reliabilities showed sufficient internal consistency, the same cannot be said for all scores within individual countries for both instructional quality ratings (Castellano & Bell, 2021) and teacher emotion self-reports (Mihaly et al., 2021). Per-country reliabilities for instructional quality ratings were overall only small to moderate (Castellano & Bell, 2021). Of particular note is that the Global Teaching InSights Study found very little variation in classroom management scores in the Chinese sample (Castellano & Bell, 2021), raising questions as to the reliability and validity of the measurement instrument within that subsample.

Regarding the Teacher Emotions Scales, in Spanish-speaking countries, the internal consistencies were also quite low, specifically for teacher anger, which was lowest in Mexico at 𝛼 = .58, and teacher anxiety, which was lowest in Colombia at 𝛼 = .49 and also unsatisfactory in Spain at 𝛼 = .59. For teacher enjoyment, reliability was satisfactory for all countries (Mihaly et al., 2021). This might imply that the OECD-produced Spanish translation had some flaws, or that translating emotional content from English into Spanish bears particular challenges.

Developing internationally applicable, objective, reliable, and valid observation instruments and processes is notoriously difficult (Bell et al., 2015), as is the creation of multi-language questionnaires that are applicable across different linguistic contexts and cultures (Dong & Dumas, 2020; J. Fischer et al., 2019; International Test Commission, 2017). Our study may therefore well be underestimating the true effects. Hence, future improvements to instructional quality measurements in high-fidelity observational studies like the Global Teaching InSights Study may eventually reveal stronger effects than the ones obtained in the present study.

Sample characteristics between the countries were also notably different (see Appendix, Table A4): For example, average student age was 13.78 years in Spain compared to 16.58 years in Chile, which, in addition to cultural differences, likely limited betweencountry comparability. This variability in sample characteristics may be a contributing factor for the large intra-cluster correlations (see Supplement, table S6), which increased standard errors and thus p-values in this study considerably. Additionally, sampling bias is present in the Global Teaching In-Sights Study's dataset, with only Colombia achieving a fully representative national sample of secondary school mathematics teachers and Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom all relying on convenience sampling.

7.4 Interpretation of Exploratory Findings

A second key distinction of the present study compared to its predecessors was its diverse international teacher sample. Our exploratory investigation of per-country effects revealed, first, that the as-sumption of relative universality (Pekrun & Goetz, 2023) largely held up: We did not observe the variance across countries to be statistically significant. The direction of effects was largely equivalent across countries and in line with theory, though it must be stressed that this purely exploratory observation should not be mistaken for a significant, confirmatory result. Nevertheless, we also observed a large degree of heterogeneity and a couple of notable exceptions with counter-expected directions of effects. Those exceptions included Colombia, where teacher enjoyment negatively predicted classroom management; Japan, where teacher enjoyment negatively predicted assessment of and responses to student understanding; Spain, where anxiety positively predicted the same sub-dimension; and Germany, where teacher enjoyment negatively predicted instruction and especially the student cognitive engagement sub-dimension, while anger positively predicted instruction and its sub-dimensions. Since the focus of this paper was not on per-country effects and these values were derived from exploratory analyses, we refrain from speculating about these particular exceptions in detail.

Further, our last exploratory analyses highlighted an intriguing tendency: There was a significantly stronger positive link between teacher enjoyment and classroom management and a significantly stronger negative link between teacher anger and classroom management in more individualistic (Hofstede, 2001; Minkov & Kaasa, 2022) countries.

Both methodological and substantive factors may have contributed to this finding. For instance, the scales originally developed in one culture may not demonstrate the same psychometric characteristics when 'imported' into another culture (F. F. Chen, 2008), potentially also compromising opportunities to detect cross-scale covariation in the 'importing' culture. On a more substantial note, a possible explaining factor could be stricter emotion display rules within more collectivistic cultures (Safdar et al., 2009), which may have mitigated some potential positive effects of expressing enjoyment as well as potential negative effects of expressing anger on observed classroom management.

While this should only be considered a preliminary exploratory result for the purposes of this registered report, it certainly provides an intriguing angle for future research. There are also clearly a myriad of differences between the eight countries in this specific sample that may likewise explain the heterogeneity of the effects. These range from systemic differences between their educational systems (see Table A4) to economic factors to cultural factors that are more specific and multi-faceted than the unidimensional collectivism-individualism axis. Therefore, future research into the role of cultural differences regarding the teacher emotions-instructional quality link may benefit from assessing teachers of different cultural backgrounds who work inside comparable institutional frameworks, e.g., by choosing teachers from a culturally diverse country such as the United States (see Leung & Cohen, 2011).

7.5 Future Directions

Future research into the links between teacher emotions and instructional quality across national contexts may benefit from focusing on the socialemotional support dimension in particular, as it showed the closest links in our analyses. Clearly, there is a need for larger sample sizes; specifically to explore, on a more fine-grained level, the ways teacher emotions interact with classroom contexts and cultural contexts in their effects on instructional behavior. This is a challenging, but potentially fruitful endeavor that may, for instance, benefit from mixed-methods approaches in combination with large-scale international educational assessment projects.

8 Conclusions

Instructional quality remains an intriguing and highly relevant field of research, but its assessment is subject to numerous theoretical and methodological idiosyncrasies. Teacher emotions share these attributes. This complexity is again increased by taking the international and intercultural view. In our study, we took a step towards investigating the interrelations between self-reported teacher emotions and expert ratings of instructional quality, using a large international sample of teachers. We found a significant negative effect for the link between teachers' negative emotions (anger and anxiety) and social-emotional support. We also found a nonsignificant trend for a positive effect of teacher enjoyment. For the dimensions of classroom management and instruction, there were no significant effects and negligible effect sizes. This large percentage of insignificant and null effects puts this study into stark contrast with previous findings that had relied on more homogeneous samples and teacher or student ratings of instructional quality. Our findings and deliberations may serve as springboards for future cross-national, cross-cultural research into teacher emotions, instructional quality, and their interrelatedness.
