How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Scientific collaboration, often measured through co-authorship, has grown in many disciplines and is frequently linked to increased citation rates. However, it remains unclear which disciplines have seen the greatest growth in collaboration and how this growth relates to article-level metrics.

Moreover, little is known about how team demographics (e.g., h-index, gender, number of institutions) and team size interact with regard to research impact. Understanding these dynamics is essential given their influence on hiring, tenure, and the broader scientific ecosystem. Using metadata from Elsevier's International Center for the Study of Research (ICSR) Lab, we examined collaboration trends across disciplines and their associations with citation outcomes.

Team Science: An Examination of Factors Associated with Citation Metrics

The number of co-authors on a project (i.e., team size, proxied here by the length of the authorship list) has been increasing in many fields of study, such as biomedical sciences (Papatheodorou et al., 2008), medicine (Weeks et al., 2004), and psychology (Zafrunnisha & Reddy, 2010). Notably, the number of co-authors is one factor that may be positively associated with article metrics (e.g., citation rates, Larivière et al., 2015; Wuchty et al., 2007), although the extent of this relationship may vary by field (e.g., physics, Hinnant et al., 2012). Within coauthored papers, additional characteristics of the authorship team may be related to these metrics.

Author diversity, broadly speaking, has been associated with positive outcomes such as publication in high-impact factor journals, rates of citations, and funding (Freeman & Huang, 2015; Nichols, 2014). It is important to note that author diversity can be conceptualized along a continuum of factors such as gender, physical location (e.g., United States, Brazil), institutional affiliation, discipline, and rank. Evidence suggests the impact of international collaboration, rank, and gender on article metrics in fields such as sport science research (Wang et al., 2015), physics (Hinnant et al., 2012), and psychology (Geraci et al., 2015), respectively. Despite the benefits of having diversity among co-authors for scientific productivity and impact, co-author teams tend to be homogenous (e.g., within a cancer research center, Fagan et al., 2018). For example, when looking at gender, authors exhibit a tendency to work with others of the same gender, a finding that is consistent over time (Whittington et al., 2023). Thus, recommendations for scientists to diversify or broaden their co-author networks may not be effective without careful consideration of team size and composition. Consistent with this, simply enlarging a collaboration team or seeking well-connected collaborators is not always the best strategy for research performance, and the best approach depends on a scholar's own research variety and goals (Zhu et al., 2021).

The available research paints an incomplete picture of co-authorship trends and their relationship to citation metrics. Landmark studies have documented the growth of team size and its association with citations over long time spans (e.g., 1900 spans (e.g., -2011 spans (e.g., , Larivière et al., 2015;; 1955 -2000 , Wuchty et al., 2007)), and the gender gap in authorship has been studied across countries, disciplines, and career stages (Huang et al., 2020; Larivière et al., 2013). However, these literatures have largely developed separately, and less is known about how team size and several features of team composition (gender, the h-index of the first and last authors, and the number of institutions and countries represented) are jointly related to article-level citation metrics within and across disciplines in a single, contemporary, article-level dataset. Understanding co-author team composition is therefore critical, given previous research showing that, despite the benefits of co-authorship diversity, people tend to work with those similar to them. As such, this project aims to first establish trends in co-authorship team size within disciplines across time and then explore how the composition of these co-authorship teams is related to citation metrics. The research questions guiding these aims are as follows: 1a.) What is the pattern of team size distribution within various disciplines over time, 1b.) is team size related to article impact?, and 2.) What characteristics of team composition have the strongest association with article metrics? Question 1. What is the pattern of team size distribution within various disciplines over time, and is team size related to article impact?

Initiatives, calls, and funding opportunities for collaborative research (from both private and public/government sectors) have been increasing in recent years, with some arguing that large teams (i.e., co-authors) are necessary for addressing modern societal questions and problems (Samarasekera, 2009). Concurrently, various disciplines have reported changes in coauthorship trends (Papatheodorou et al., 2008; Wuchty et al., 2007). Discipline-specific investigations have identified co-authorship trends that differ by subdiscipline. For example, over approximately 30 years the average number of authors roughly doubled in four major musculoskeletal science journals (from 3.67 to 7.3 authors per article) (Russell et al., 2019), whereas team sizes in mathematics have remained small, rising from an average of 1.10 authors per paper in the 1940s to 1.63 in the 1990s (Grossman, 2002). Among the social sciences, a more consistent pattern of increased co-authorship emerged, although specific changes vary by subfield (De Meuse et al., 2014; Gaunt, 2014; Hunter & Leahey, 2008; Kozlowski et al., 2017; Rath & Wohlrabe, 2016). This suggests that needs and norms within various disciplines may influence how quickly and to what extent authorship trends change and solidify. Because the current state of the research focuses on constructs like time period (e.g., eleven years of one journal, Abu and Verma (2019)), a single publication such as one handbook (De Meuse et al., 2014), and geographical region (Leifeld et al., 2017) in isolation, examining co-authorship trends among multiple disciplines concurrently would afford a more holistic overview of the state of team science.

Examining co-authorship trends across time and disciplines alongside citation metrics will also facilitate a deeper understanding of the conditions under which co-authorship is associated with impact. For example, among publications indexed in the annual volumes of the Web of Knowledge™ in 2007, single-author papers received an average of 2.91 citations while multiauthor papers exhibited an average of 5.39 (Thijs et al., 2015) (using a three-year citation window and excluding publications assigned only to the social sciences or to arts and humanities); this difference was consistent when examining citation averages from 1998 to 2007. Further probing this relationship, it appears that the number of citations increases with the number of authors, up to a saturation point of approximately 20 authors. This non-linear relationship between team size and impact, which has also been described as an inverted U in cross-domain research (Zhu et al., 2021), suggests that other factors, such as the composition of the team, may contribute to the observed benefits when co-authored teams come together. We emphasize that team size is treated here as a descriptive feature of the authorship list; our analyses are observational and cannot establish that adding authors causes higher impact.

Question 2. What characteristics of team composition have the strongest association with article metrics?

When thinking about the composition of organizations, including teams, scholars have documented both positive and negative effects of diversity (van Dijk et al., 2012; van Knippenberg, 2024). On the one hand, through team diversity members gain more insight about the task through varied perspectives. On the other hand, dissimilarities can create tension (Williams & O'Reilly, 1998). The extent to which teams take advantage of the diversity of their members through discussion and the exchange of information may depend on characteristics of individual members that facilitate either open-mindedness or social categorization and intergroup bias (Homan et al., 2008; van Knippenberg et al., 2004). Simulation research has also found that the relationship between diversity and team performance is curvilinear (an inverted U) (Palmer, 2006). The Categorization-Elaboration Model (CEM, van Knippenberg et al., 2004) integrates these perspectives: diversity (whether based on social categories or on differences in information and expertise) can stimulate the elaboration of task-relevant information, which benefits performance, but categorization processes can undermine that benefit (see also van Knippenberg, 2024).

The CEM provides a conceptual rationale for examining the present variables together.

Gender is a social-category characteristic of the team; the number of institutions and countries represented index affiliation-based (and likely information-based) diversity; and the h-index of the first and last authors indexes the status and expertise that authors bring to the team. Team size sets the number of people among whom these differences can be distributed, and is therefore both a covariate for and a context of the composition variables (indeed, institution and country counts cannot exceed team size). We selected these variables because they are the author-level characteristics available at scale in the Scopus metadata, and we treat them as correlates of citation metrics rather than as causal mechanisms. This research on team diversity must also be considered alongside sociocultural research about how members of certain groups have historically been treated in the sciences.

Generally speaking, despite increases in women's representation in academia, women still are underrepresented when it comes to publishing co-and solo-authored papers (Jagsi et al., 2006). In addition to authoring a smaller proportion of published work, research suggests women are also less likely to hold prominent authorship positions (Rigg et al., 2012; West et al., 2013).

For example, women are underrepresented regarding publishing articles in specific areas of medicine (Cushman, 2022; Süßenbacher et al., 2017), STEM fields (e.g., mathematics, Mihaljević-Brandt et al., 2016), and social sciences (Bonham & Stefan, 2017; Mihaljević-Brandt et al., 2016), although the extent of these differences vary by subfield (e.g., computational biology, as compared to biology in general, Bonham & Stefan, 2017). Interestingly, women are less likely to collaborate with previous co-authors compared to men (Zeng et al., 2016); although it is not clear why this is the case, it could have important implications for their careers as novel teams tend to produce higher-impact work (Katzenbach & Smith, 2003).

Furthermore, women are less likely to receive credit for their contributions to empirical and scholarly work (Ross et al., 2022). A growing body of research suggests that contributions by women to peer-reviewed publications are often perceived as less valuable than those authored by men. After controlling for the year of publication, publication outlet, focus, theoretical framework, methodology, tenure status, and institutional affiliation, women were still cited less than men (Maliniak et al., 2013). Relevant to this manuscript is the body of research examining the association between gender and citation rates. Previous work has noted that in specific fields, men are less likely to cite women's work and work authored by women typically receives fewer citations overall (Cameron et al., 2016; Hutson, 2002). This difference in the likelihood of accumulating citations could influence a researchers' impact (Rezek et al., 2011), which is often considered important for career advances.

One commonly used measure of a scholar's output and impact is the "h-index" (Hirsch, 2005). The h-index combines the number of articles published with the number of citations received for those articles. This approach attempts to balance high-impact articles with the number of articles published. Specifically, the h-score is the number of articles (X) that receive X citations. The h-index is one of the most widely used measures of a scholar's impact (Crespo & Simoes, 2019), although it has also been criticized, for example for its dependence on field and career length and for behaving inconsistently when comparing scholars (Bornmann & Daniel, 2007; Waltman & van Eck, 2012). Fields such as psychology have observed gender differences pertaining to h-indices that favor men. Women professors have a significantly lower h-index than men, with a difference of approximately four points. After controlling for the year of first publication, gender shares approximately 2.6% of the variance with h-index (Geraci et al., 2015). This effect was examined relative to mean salaries for full professors, and practically translated to an astounding effect of $13,100 a year difference between men and women (Geraci et al., 2015).

Notably, the calculation of h-indices may be particularly biased by self-citations and years without publication. Higher rates of citations received by men were partially explained by high rates of self-citation which altered scores up to 14 extra h-index points. Women also engaged in self-citation, although less so, and only impacting their h-index by up to 5 points (Cameron et al., 2016). It is important to note that there is evidence of increasing equity between genders in certain fields; in international relations, for example, the gap between men's and women's manuscript submissions and publications is narrowing, and there appears to be no significant relationship between gender and citation count (Østby et al., 2013). Although there are alternatives to the h-index that account for self-citations (e.g., the b-index, Brown, 2009), the hindex remains popular, and we use it here as one available (and imperfect) indicator of an author's prior visibility rather than as a measure of scholarly quality.

Given the arguments regarding the necessity of scientific collaboration and large international teams to address modern societal questions and problems (Samarasekera, 2009), it may be intuitive that institutional and geographical diversity is on the rise. One source notes an increase in Psychology articles that have the presence of both a U.S. author and at least one international co-author (Kliegl & Bates, 2011). Similar increases in the fraction of internationally co-authored papers (i.e., ratio of co-authored articles with international institutions to the total number of co-authored articles with domestic and international institutions) were noted in other scientific disciplines, such as mathematics, biology, medicine, and engineering (Coccia & Wang, 2016). However, while it appears most fields are increasing international engagement, applied fields (especially medical sciences) have higher rates of growth (Coccia & Bozeman, 2016).

Collaboration involving those outside of one's home institution and country may significantly impact article metrics, which may be one reason for the increasing rates of international collaboration. For instance, for UK institutions indexed in the Science Citation Index between 1981 and 1994, collaboration within one's home university or country resulted in a small increase in average impact (0.75 citations) whereas collaboration outside of one's home country resulted in a more moderate increase (1.6 citations, Katz & Hicks, 1997). Indeed, both well-established and young institutions benefit from international collaboration as evidenced by a citation per paper increase when the authorship team is international (Khor & Yu, 2016). This benefit is corroborated by the observation that international co-authorship publications are over-represented in the ESI global top 10 % highly cited publications relative to all journal publications from the same institution (Khor & Yu, 2016). Indeed, article impact in terms of citations per paper seems to be greatest for those manuscripts with multiple domestic and international collaborators, and the smallest for those with no institutional collaboration.

However, this trend may not be consistent across fields, journals, and/or countries, and warrants further investigation (e.g., physics, Hinnant et al., 2012; Rousseau & Ding, 2016). For example, international collaboration generally increases citation rates in the field of sports science, but the extent of this effect varies by which countries are collaborating (Wang et al., 2015). Collaboration between the United States and Canada, for instance, produced a mean citation rate greater than the domestic mean observed citation rate of each country respectively, whereas collaboration between the United Kingdom and Greece produced a mean citation rate that was lower than the domestic mean observed citation rate of each country respectively. Furthermore, when examining the benefits of international collaboration on citation count for American authors publishing in the top three interdisciplinary journals (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences [PNAS], Nature, and Science), there is an increased citation rate for international collaboration when compared to American-only authored papers (Rousseau & Ding, 2016). It is important to note that this effect was the result of a significant increase in citation rate for international papers published in PNAS only. When examining each journal independently, no benefit of international collaboration was found for Science or Nature. Given the accelerated growth of international collaboration across various fields over time (Coccia & Bozeman, 2016; Walsh & Maloney, 2007), it is increasingly important to understand the influence of this diversity on article metrics.

The majority of work examining team characteristics and article metrics have focused on a specific field (Hinnant et al., 2012), and/or a limited/restricted number of team characteristics (e.g., size, Wuchty et al., 2007). Empirical data comparing the impact of diverse/multiple team characteristics on article metrics within a given field is limited (Grossman, 2002; Hinnant et al., 2012; Nabout et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015; Weeks et al., 2004). Along a similar vein, empirical data comparing the impact of team characteristics on article metrics across fields is also limited (for notable exceptions, Larivière et al., 2015; Wuchty et al., 2007). Comparing outcomes within and across fields can allow for an increased understanding of these fields' scientific culture.

Practically, understanding factors associated with an article's impact can be important for researchers' outreach, as well as planning for the job market and tenure review.

Method Ethics Information

All of the data for this manuscript is drawn from peer-reviewed journals. All of the extracted meta-data from the individual publications are de-identified in the database. As such, per US Federal Guidelines, this research is not subject to human ethics review boards ( §46.101, (b) (4)).

Design

This study was an archival data investigation into scholarly publication records using Elsevier's curated database Scopus. This study was conducted through access to Elsevier's International Center for the Study of Research Lab (ICSR; https://www.elsevier.com/icsr). The ICSR is designed to support researchers in their empirical study of meta-science on large datasets using the Databricks platform running Apache Spark architecture for big-data analyses. Through ICSR, researchers can access full publication metadata from Scopus including article metrics, author(s), and affiliation information. The data snapshot used was ICSR version v008 (snapshot dated 9 May 2023), and all citation counts reflect citations accrued up to that date. The unit of analysis was the journal article. For each article, we used the publication year, the ordered list of authors (and each author's inferred gender, h-index, and affiliations), the journal's ASJC subject classification, and citation counts. Scopus contains over 80 million entries from 1788 to present (Boyle & Sherman, 2006); we restricted the data as described in the Sampling Plan below. All variables extracted from this dataset are described below.

Article characteristics included the year of publication, number of authors (team size), and discipline. Team size was operationalized as the number of authors on the article's authorship list; this is a common proxy for the size of the team that produced the work, but it is not strictly synonymous with it (e.g., it excludes acknowledged contributors). Discipline was defined using the All Science Journal Classification (ASJC; https://service.elsevier.com/app/answers/detail/a_id/15181/supporthub/scopus/) with the five global subject area codes (Health, Physical, Life, Social Sciences, and Multidisciplinary). 1 Because the ASJC is assigned at the level of the journal, an article takes the classification of the journal in which it was published. The Multidisciplinary category therefore reflects the type of journal (e.g., broad-scope outlets) and not the disciplinary makeup of the author team. Journals carrying more than one ASJC code were counted in each of the corresponding areas, so an article could contribute to more than one subject-area model; the overall models counted each article once. Articles whose journals fell outside the four named areas were classified as Multidisciplinary. Metric characteristics included citation count (the number of times an article was cited within the current data snapshot, including self-citations) and five-year citation count (the number of citations received within five years of publication, as supplied in the ICSR fieldweighted citation impact table; this is the raw count, not the field-normalized ratio). For the fiveyear citation models we included only articles published before 2017, so that every article had a complete five-year window. 1 We originally planned on using the 27 subfields within the ASJC. However, we updated our preregistration to use only five of the larger subfields due to computational constraints.

Variables.

Author characteristics included affiliation country, affiliation/institution, h-index, gender, and gender ratio. We transformed affiliation countries into a count variable of the number of unique countries. Similarly, we transformed affiliation/institutions into a count of the number of different affiliations across authors. We used the h-index of the first and last authors for each article, with and without self-citation. Author position was used as recorded; we did not assume that the last author is the most senior member of the team (see Limitations). Last, Elsevier has noted that their inferred gender column has a 97% accuracy, and we used the inferred gender of the first and last authors for each article. From this variable, we additionally calculated the gender ratio (number of inferred male / total authors) as a single variable for each article.

While Scopus includes articles from 1788 to present, we limited our sample to journal articles published from 1970 until the date of the data snapshot. Scopus indicates that articles from 1970 to present include reference information, which was required to calculate several of the above mentioned variables. We originally planned to include all eligible articles from 1970 forward. Due to computational constraints, we randomly sampled 25% of eligible articles during this time span to constrain the run time of our code and the memory necessary to calculate h-index. Articles were sampled by simple random sampling (fixed seed), so the sample is expected to be representative of the eligible population, and with several million articles per subject area the sampling error is negligible. However, we did not compare the sample with the full population, nor repeat the sampling, so we cannot demonstrate representativeness across publication years or subject areas; this is a limitation. It is important to note that we did not calculate h-index on the randomly sampled dataset. These values were calculated on the full data and then merged with the randomly sampled data to ensure h-indices were appropriate for authors on the randomly sampled data.

Sampling Plan.

Given the large size of the Scopus dataset, all analyses were expected to achieve the required 95% power. Analyses using the pwr library (Champely et al., 2017) indicated that the planned regressions had the ability to detect effects at R² < .00001 at 95% power using a 95% confidence interval. Because such tiny effects are detectable, statistical significance is uninformative here; we therefore emphasize the magnitude of the effects (the slopes and R 2 ) when interpreting the results.

Power.

No values reached this criteria for the five subject areas. In our analyses, a linear trend was the main focus of interest, and we did not note any non-linear trends. Nearly all scatter plots indicated heteroscedasticity, and team size and citation counts are discrete, non-negative, and highly right-skewed (with many uncited articles), which are conditions under which count models (e.g., Poisson or negative binomial, possibly zero-inflated) are often preferred. We retained ordinary least squares linear models for three reasons: (a) they were specified in our preregistration; (b) with samples of this size, inference about the slopes does not depend on the normality of the residuals; and (c) the slopes are directly interpretable as the expected difference in citations per additional author (or per one-unit difference in an author characteristic), which we use throughout. However, the standard errors and confidence intervals from these models should be interpreted cautiously given the heteroscedasticity, and the linear models may misrepresent the conditional mean at the extremes of the distribution. We did not run negative binomial, zeroinflated, or other count-data models, and we no longer have access to the data to do so; this is a limitation of the present work. Please see Appendix A for further details.

The large sample size was expected to indicate all predictors significant at the traditional p value criterion. For individual predictors, we used the 95% confidence intervals of the slope to indicate if the effect was non-zero (i.e., the confidence interval did not include zero rounded to three decimals). Because significance was expected for even trivial effects, we interpreted the size of the slopes and of R² rather than significance alone.

The difference between effects was determined by examining the confidence intervals of R² to three decimal places calculated with the MBESS library (Kelley, 2022). If they did not overlap, we stated that they were different.

Appendix A includes all exact values for reference. Discussion Question 1. What is the pattern of team size distribution within various disciplines over time, and is team size related to article impact?

The first research question in this paper addressed the pattern of team size distribution within various disciplines over time and the relationship between team size and article impact.

Our findings indicate a consistent increase in team size across disciplines, corroborating previous research on the expansion of scientific collaboration (Larivière et al., 2015; Wuchty et al., 2007).

The trend was most pronounced in the physical sciences. This is consistent with reports of substantial rises in team size in other natural science fields, such as musculoskeletal science (Russell et al., 2019) and biology (Nabout et al., 2015), over the past few decades, although those studies did not examine physics. Notably, the social sciences exhibited the lowest median number of authors per manuscript, a trend that may reflect long-standing disciplinary norms favoring single or small team authorship (Henriksen, 2016).

Similarly, while growth was seen in the social sciences, this discipline had the lowest median number of authors per manuscript. In psychology, for example, this may be due to the long periods of solo-author dominance, followed by a rise in teams of two and, recently teams of three (at least based on specific journal metrics, Kozlowski et al., 2017). Henriksen (2016) notes that co-authorship has grown in the social sciences between 1980 and 2013, but the median number of authors per article remains low relative to other disciplines. Interestingly, Paul-Hus et al. (2020) explain that while co-authorship remains low in the social sciences, when taking acknowledgments into consideration, the total number of contributors more closely resembles that found in other disciplines. Looking at authorship and acknowledgment trends concurrently may represent an important area of future research that illuminates authorship norms among various disciplines and guides conversations about crediting. Despite this variation, the broader increase in co-authorship aligns with the necessity of interdisciplinary and large-scale collaborations to address complex scientific challenges (Samarasekera, 2009). These trends may be influenced by funding opportunities favoring collaborative efforts, the need for diverse expertise in tackling intricate research questions, and the growing complexity of modern scientific inquiry.

Our analysis also found a positive association between team size and article impact, as measured by citation counts, consistent with previous findings that collaborative works tend to garner more citations (Thijs et al., 2015; Wuchty et al., 2007). These data are observational, and the association should not be read as team size driving citations. It may partly reflect characteristics that co-occur with larger teams, such as disciplines in which both team size and citation counts are high; our by-discipline analyses show that the association was present within each of the five broad subject areas, but these areas remain coarse (e.g., large-team fields such as particle physics and genomics are grouped with smaller-team fields), so residual disciplinary differences cannot be ruled out, and finer-grained disciplinary controls could change the estimates. One potential explanation for the association is the increased dissemination of collaborative research through multiple authors' networks, leading to broader recognition and citation. Larger teams may also bring more collective expertise, which could improve the robustness and credibility of findings (Larivière et al., 2015), and larger teams have more opportunities for self-citation, which would inflate citation counts because our citation outcomes included self-citations. We also did not account for journal quality (e.g., impact factor, CiteScore, or SJR); articles in more prestigious journals tend to be cited more, and larger or more diverse teams may be more likely to publish in such journals, so journal quality is a plausible confounder of the associations reported here. While the relationship between team size and citations appears generally positive, it is not necessarily linear. Prior studies have suggested that citation benefits plateau beyond a certain threshold (e.g., approximately 20 authors), indicating diminishing returns for excessively large teams (Sharma, 2021; Zhu et al., 2021). We modeled team size as a linear term, so we could not evaluate this possibility directly. Future research should explore potential moderators, such as discipline-specific authorship norms and the role of team leadership in fostering impactful collaborations.

Question 2. What characteristics of team composition have the strongest association with article metrics?

Gender composition was associated with citation rates, though the associations varied by discipline. We caution against interpreting these patterns as an effect of author gender on citing behavior. Readers frequently cannot infer authors' gender (e.g., from initials or unfamiliar names), and researchers generally cite work for its relevance, novelty, methodological quality, or venue. A more plausible reading is that gender is correlated with other factors that we did not model, such as career stage, institutional prestige, funding, journal quality, topic, and finer-grained disciplinary differences, as well as with disciplinary norms regarding authorship position, scientific networks, and systemic inequities in academia. Consistent with prior research, our results indicate that male-dominated authorship teams tended to receive fewer citations in social sciences and health sciences, whereas in some disciplines, particularly the life sciences, male first authors were associated with higher citation counts (Maliniak et al., 2013; West et al., 2013). Prior studies that did account for some of these factors have suggested that gender biases in citation practices may persist, with work authored by women under-cited in some fields, particularly fields historically dominated by male researchers (Cameron et al., 2016); our models cannot test that explanation. Because the gender variable was inferred rather than self-reported, and because effect sizes were small, these results should be treated as descriptive.

In the life sciences, papers received more citations when there was a female author in the last place and male authors in the first spot. These findings may reflect the various authorship norms for life sciences subdisciplines, wherein the weight of one's contributions is associated with particular authorship positions. In all other instances, male-authored papers received more citations. This is compatible with previous research that highlights the systematic devaluation of women's peer-reviewed contributions and research that is considered feminine (Ni et al., 2021) or that focuses on women's issues (Mirin, 2021).

Gender.

Another possible explanation for the observed disparities is the tendency for women to engage in broader collaboration networks, which may enhance the visibility of their work (Zeng et al., 2016). However, systemic barriers, including inequitable crediting of contributions and biases in authorship order conventions, may still limit women's perceived impact (Ross et al., 2022). Moreover, prior research suggests that gender gaps in citation rates may be exacerbated by self-citation practices, as men are more likely to cite their previous work, thereby inflating their citation metrics (Hutson, 2002). Because our citation outcomes included self-citations, this pathway cannot be separated from other explanations here.

Our findings highlight the citation advantages associated with institutional and geographic diversity. Collaboration across institutions and national borders was positively associated with citation counts, consistent with prior studies indicating that international co-authorship is associated with greater article visibility and impact (Katz & Hicks, 1997; Khor & Yu, 2016). These associations may not reflect an independent effect of collaboration. They could instead reflect unmeasured factors, such as larger grants, participation in international consortia, publication in higher-impact journals, or institutional prestige, that make both multi-institution authorship and higher citation counts more likely. Notably, articles in multidisciplinary journals and collaborations spanning multiple countries were associated with the highest citation rates. Note that "multidisciplinary" is a characteristic of the journal, not of the authorship team, so this result speaks to publication venue rather than to team composition. The pattern is compatible with the increasing recognition of global, cross-disciplinary perspectives in addressing scientific challenges (Coccia & Wang, 2016). These results are also compatible with the argument that international collaborations may facilitate access to diverse expertise and resources, potentially enhancing research quality and its subsequent impact (Kliegl & Bates, 2011).

Institution and Country.

Beyond increased citation impact, international collaborations can also contribute to a more comprehensive and innovative approach to research questions by integrating diverse methodological perspectives and theoretical frameworks (Coccia & Bozeman, 2016). The exchange of ideas across different scientific traditions and institutional structures can lead to the development of novel insights and more rigorous analytical approaches (Wang et al., 2015).

Additionally, researchers engaged in international partnerships may benefit from greater access to funding opportunities, as many governmental and private organizations prioritize collaborative, multinational research initiatives (Walsh & Maloney, 2007). However, challenges such as differences in institutional policies, language barriers, and variations in research infrastructure may also shape the effectiveness of these collaborations. Future research should examine how these structural and logistical factors impact collaboration outcomes and whether specific disciplines are more adept at leveraging international partnerships for research advancement.

The positive association between h-index and citation counts was evident across all disciplines, with stronger effects observed for last authors compared to first authors. This finding is compatible with the idea that researchers with established publication records tend to attract more citations, potentially due to their reputation and extensive academic networks (Geraci et al., 2015). Interestingly, the relationship between h-index and citation count was more pronounced when self-citations were excluded, which is consistent with, but does not demonstrate, high-impact researchers benefitting from external validation rather than selfreferential practices (Leimu & Koricheva, 2005). This highlights the importance of distinguishing between genuine academic influence and self-reinforcing citation behaviors when assessing scholarly impact. Additionally, scholars with higher h-indices may have greater access to institutional resources, mentorship opportunities, and visibility in high-impact journals, further h-Index.

compounding their influence on citation metrics. Future research should explore how variations in h-index across disciplines and career stages affect long-term scholarly impact.

The limitations of h-index as a universal metric must also be acknowledged. It does not account for field-specific citation norms, and early-career researchers and those publishing in niche areas may be at a disadvantage. In addition, we used the h-index of the last author as an indicator of an experienced author, but authorship conventions differ across disciplines (e.g., alphabetical ordering or other author-order practices), so the last author is not necessarily the most senior member of the team, and the first/last author comparison may be biased in fields that do not follow the first/last convention. Alternative bibliometric indicators, such as the i10-index or g-index, may provide complementary insights into scholarly impact by addressing some of these limitations. Moreover, as open-access publishing continues to rise, its potential impact on citation disparities linked to h-index should be explored further.

Conclusion and Future Directions

In summary, our study documents the growing prominence of scientific collaboration and its association with citation metrics. Team size and institutional and international diversity were positively associated with citation counts, and gender representation also showed varied associations with citations that differed by discipline. These associations were small in magnitude: the models explained, at most, about 7% of the variance in citations, and the author variables explained only a fraction of that. Statistical significance was assured by the size of the dataset, so the practical size of the effects is what matters for interpretation.

Limitations. The analyses are observational, so the associations cannot be interpreted causally. We used linear models for count outcomes that are skewed and heteroscedastic; used five broad subject areas that hide large differences in collaboration practices within them; did not control for journal quality, career stage, institutional prestige, or funding; and included self-citations in the citation outcomes. The h-index is a contested measure, and last-author position is an imperfect proxy for seniority. Gender was inferred rather than self-reported, and we analyzed a 25% random sample of eligible articles. Because we no longer have access to the ICSR data, we could not present the distributions of team size and citations beyond summary statistics and capped boxplots, report descriptive statistics for the author variables, or run additional models.

Any of these may account for part of the observed associations.

Implications. Given the small effect sizes and the likelihood of confounding, these findings do not support using team size, team composition, or author h-index as stand-alone indicators in research evaluation, funding allocation, hiring, or promotion decisions; such uses risk rewarding correlates of citation counts rather than the quality or contribution of the work.

They are better suited to describing how collaboration practices differ across fields and to informing institutional policies that remove barriers to inclusive collaboration (for example, by supporting cross-institution and international partnerships and by examining how credit is assigned in teams) without presuming that such collaboration will in itself raise citation counts.

Future directions.

Future research should test these relationships with models suited to count data (e.g., negative binomial), include interactions among team size, time, and composition variables, add journal-level and career-stage controls, and use finer-grained subject classifications. Additionally, exploring the intersectionality of gender, institutional affiliation, and career stage could provide a more nuanced understanding of how different factors are jointly related to research visibility and impact. Finally, the recent adoption of large language models (LLMs) in research and writing may be changing how researchers collaborate and how teams are formed, for example by changing the amount of work that can be done by small teams or by lowering barriers across languages and disciplines. Whether team sizes and collaboration patterns have shifted since the widespread availability of LLMs is an empirical question that our data, which end at the data snapshot date, were not designed to address; the trends reported here provide a pre-LLM baseline against which such changes could be evaluated in future team science research. As the scientific enterprise continues to evolve, ensuring equitable and inclusive collaboration practices will be essential.
