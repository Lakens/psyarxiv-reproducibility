Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.08 2 (1) = 5.3, p = 0.08) (all p-values corrected using the Bonferroni method).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Contemporary accounts of social category learning propose that children build social categories from experience. Despite the central role of experience in these accounts, we still know relatively little about how children learn social categories from their environment. Theories in other domains of developmental science have benefited from empirical work characterizing the structure of children's experience; this work suggests that children's environments contain regularities that shape developmental outcomes by structuring the information available for learning. We propose that a similar approach can advance theories of racial category learning: characterizing the regularities present in children's environments is critical to understand how racial category representations are formed. Using U.S. children's books as a tractable, developmentally-relevant source of input to children, we employ corpus-analytic methods to examine two input properties known to influence category learning: frequency and contextual structure. We find that characters from minoritized racial groups are less frequent than their base-rate in the population (Study 1) and that there are systematic differences in the contextual themes associated with different racial groups (Study 2). Together, these findings suggest that children's books contain distributional regularities along dimensions known to shape category representations.

More broadly, by characterizing the structure of children's experience, this work contributes to a larger effort in developmental science to better understand the environmental regularities from which children learn.

Characterizing the input to racial category learning: Evidence from corpus analyses of children's books It is well documented that children around the world develop preferences and make assumptions about people based on characteristics associated with racial groups 1 , including physical features, culture, religion, and/or language (Farago et al., 2015; Hirschfeld, 1995; Hughes et al., 2023; Levy & Hughes, 2009; Quintana, 1998; Raabe & Beelmann, 2011). The development of these preferences and assumptions is believed to start with the learning of racial categories (Averhart & Bigler, 1997; Barrett & Davis, 2008; Bigler & Liben, 2007; Doyle & Aboud, 1995; Katz et al., 1975; McGlothlin & Killen, 2005); therefore, understanding how children learn these categories and how this learning can be modified by experience is a critical part of supporting children's healthy socio-emotional development (Durlak et al., 2011; Marcelo & Yates, 2019; McCall et al., 2022; Skinner-Dorkenoo et al., 2023; Waxman, 2021). As we will review in more detail below, contemporary accounts suggest that racial categories are learned from repeated experience with patterns or regularities in the environment. For example, in the United States, children's experiences are likely to include racially segregated neighborhoods and schools, lack of racial diversity among people in leadership positions, and stereotyped media depictions (Hughes & Watford, 2022; Roberts & Rizzo, 2021; Salter et al., 2018). 1. Early learning of racial categories

It is well-documented that during infancy and early childhood children begin to attend to and differentiate people based on characteristics associated with multiple socially-relevant dimensions -including those typically associated with racial groups (e.g., skin color, facial features, hair texture, language, and/or religious markers; for reviews, see Aboud & Skeerry, 1984; Katz, 2003; Quintana, 1998; Raabe & Beelmann, 2011; Waxman, 2021). Research with U.S. samples shows that while newborns start with no visual preferences for specific racial groups (Kelly et al., 2005), in the first few months of life infants start to use differences in skin tone as a basis for forming perceptual categories and start to prefer faces that match the race of their caregivers (Kelly et al., 2007; Waxman, 2021). By the time White infants are 9 months of age, they begin to lose the ability to differentiate both within and between Asian and Black faces (Kelly et al, 2007; Quinn et al., 2016; Sugden & Marquis, 2017). In other words, before their first birthday, White infants have formed a distinction between White faces and faces of people from minoritized racial groups, while failing to differentiate faces within those minoritized racial groups 2 . 2 In the context of the U.S., we will use the term 'minoritized racial groups' to collectively refer to Asian, Pacific Islander, or Asian American people; biracial and multiracial people; Black or African American people; Indigenous people; Latine, Hispanic, or Latin American people; and Middle Eastern or Arab people. We use 'minoritized racial groups' to collectively refer to these groups both for ease of reading and because this term is becoming more commonly used in the social and health sciences while also highlighting the active processes of structural racism that disadvantage those people (Sabik & Versey, 2023; Wingrove-Haugland & McLeod, 2022). We will refer to people of European ancestry who are phenotypically White (commonly referred to as White, European American, or Caucasian people) as 'White' or the 'dominant racial group' (Devos & Banaji, 2005; Mack & Palfrey, 2020).

In early childhood, U.S. children continue to show evidence of using characteristics associated with racial groups to differentiate people, such as using skin tone to decide who is 'nice' (Byrd et al., 2017; Clark & Clark, 1947). Starting at age 3 and throughout childhood, U.S. children -both White and racially minoritized children -show social preferences towards White people, for example, when evaluating potential for friendship or inferring intentions in ambiguous situations (Aboud & Skerry, 1983; Hughes et al., 2023; Katz, 2003; Killen et al., 2010; McGlothlin & Killen, 2005; Raabe & Beelmann, 2011; Rizzo et al., 2022a; Waxman, 2021). The development of these social preferences is understood to stem from children's representations of racial categories (Averhart & Bigler, 1997; Barrett & Davis, 2008; Bigler & Liben, 2007; Doyle & Aboud, 1995; Katz et al., 1975; McGlothlin & Killen, 2005).

Critically, these learning trajectories are shaped by children's social contexts (Bar-Haim et al., 2006; Hagerman, 2018; Sangrigoli et al., 2005; Rizzo et al., 2022b). Infants raised in racially homogenous settings show a stronger own-race face preference at 3 months of age and attend less to other-race faces at 6 months of age relative to infants who experience significant cross-racial contact (Bar-Haim et al., 2006; Ellis et al., 2017), with experimental evidence showing that own-race preferences can be reduced with increased exposure to other-race faces (Anzures et al., 2012; Heron-Delaney et al., 2011). In childhood, 4-to 11-year-old children from racially diverse communities show lower levels of majority-group preferences relative to children from racially homogeneous communities (Pauker et al., 2016; Rizzo et al., 2022b) and 7-10 year-old White children attending racially homogenous schools in the United States show a pro-White preference when interpreting ambiguous evidence, whereas White and Black children attending racially diverse schools do not show this preference (McGlothlin & Killen, 2010). Together, this evidence suggests that the early learning of racial categories is experience-dependent.

Children growing up in social contexts like the U.S. are likely to experience a lack of racial diversity among people in positions of authority such as teachers and doctors, be exposed to media that promotes racial stereotypes, or live in neighborhoods or attend schools that are racially homogeneous (Hughes et al., 2023; Hughes & Watford, 2022; Roberts & Rizzo, 2021; Salter et al., 2018). Current perspectives suggest that repeated exposure to these patterns leads children to encode social categories and use them to make inferences about people (Barrett & Davis, 2008; Bigler & Liben, 2007; Hirschfeld, 1995; Hughes et al., 2023; Lei et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2017; Roberts & Rizzo, 2021; Qian et al., 2025; Quinn et al., 2021). Yet, despite the central role of experience in these accounts, we still know little about how children learn racial categories from the regularities in their environment.

Work across developmental science shows that characterizing the regularities in children's environments is a critical step towards building better developmental theories. For example, theories of language learning have a long history of integrating insights from empirical work documenting the statistics in child-directed language to refine theory and to identify targets for intervention (Anderson et al., 2021; Hart & Risley, 1995; Hoff, 2003; Suskind, 2016; Rowe, 2012 : Rowe & Snow, 2020). We propose that this approach is equally beneficial for theories of social category learning: characterizing the regularities present in children's environments is a critical part of understanding how racial category representations are formed and how to modify them. Based on well-established findings on input features that influence category learning, we hypothesize that there will be systematic differences in children's experiences with racial groups in both their frequency and distributional contexts; we review this literature in the next section.

2. Category learning and representational change

From birth and throughout the lifespan, humans are remarkable at encoding the statistical structure of their environment, and this ability supports a wide range of cognitive and social processes (Fiser & Lengyel, 2022; Isbilen & Christiansen, 2022; Romberg & Saffran, 2010; Ruba et al., 2022; Shapiro & Turk-Browne, 2015). In particular, a large literature converges on frequency and distributional contexts as two critical features of the input that influence the familiarity and accessibility of categories, the features associated with those categories, and how they are differentiated. Across domains, learners are sensitive to how often entities occur (Arnon, 2015; Carvalho et al., 2021; Ellis, 2002; Kruschke, 1996; Lavi-Rotbain & Arnon, 2021; 2022; 2023; Yang & Worthy, 2025) and the contexts in which they occur (Bullinaria & Levy, 2007; Gómez, 2002; Günther et al., 2019; Hamilton & Gifford, 1976; Jones et al., 2017; Jones & Mewhort, 2007; Landauer & Dumais, 1997; Reeder et al., 2013; Rhodes et al., 2012; Richie & Bhatia, 2021; McClelland & Rogers, 2004; Vlach & Sandhofer, 2011), using frequency and distributional contexts to form expectations about category structure and category membership. Over time, these statistical regularities contribute to the formation of category representations by highlighting which distinctions are predictive and which are not (Bigler & Liben, 2007; McClelland & Rogers, 2004; Raviv et al., 2022 : Shufaniya & Arnon, 2022; Vales et al., 2020a). Through these processes, environmental regularities shape how categories are represented, the features learners associate with categories, and how categories are differentiated from one another.

Sensitivity to statistical structure, however, can also produce inaccurate representations when environmental regularities are misleading. For example, children develop misconceptions about scientific concepts such as the shape of the Earth and produce overregularization errors such as "mouses" and "goed" (Berko, 1958; Carey, 1985; Elman, 2001; Hanley et al., 2016; Vosniadou & Brewer, 1992; Waller et al., 2024). These inaccurate representations arise, at least in part, from children extracting statistical regularities from their environment. For instance, everyday experience provides little evidence that the Earth is a sphere orbiting the sun, leading children to infer that the Earth is flat (Vosniadou & Brewer, 1992). Similarly, because English input contains many plural forms ending in -s and past tense forms ending in -ed, children generalize these patterns to produce forms such as "mouses" and "goed" (Elman, 2001; Plunkett & Marchman, 1993; Waller et al., 2024).

We propose that learning racial categories is another domain in which the statistical structure to which children are exposed may give rise to inaccurate representations. Systematic differences in the frequency and distributional contexts associated with different racial groups would lead children to represent racial groups as increasingly dissimilar, thereby contributing to the development of racial bias (Averhart & Bigler, 1997; Barrett & Davis, 2008; Bigler & Liben, 2007; Doyle & Aboud, 1995; Katz et al., 1975; McGlothlin & Killen, 2005). If so, then we would expect children to encounter White individuals more frequently than individuals from minoritized racial groups (Study 1) and to systematically encounter different racial groups embedded in different distributional contexts (Study 2).

As a proxy for children's experience, we focus on children's picture books. We focus on children's books because they are ubiquitous in the lives of young U.S. children -many families read to their children daily since birth (Patel et al., 2024; Young et al., 1998) and educators often use picture books in preschool and kindergarten classrooms (Kemple et al., 2016). Children's books expose children to people, historical events, cultural practices, and everyday activities (Adukia et al., 2023; Aronson et al., 2018; Bishop, 1990; Lewis, 2022), providing children with repeated exposure to patterns in who is represented, how frequently different groups appear, and the contexts in which they are depicted. Although books are just one of the many experiences through which children likely acquire knowledge about social categories, we follow prior work (e.g., Clerkin et al., 2017; Montag et al., 2015; Lewis et al., 2022; Sugden et al., 2014) in using a tractable, developmentallyrelevant sample of children's environments to examine the structure of their experiences.

3. Study 1: Frequency

If children's input does contribute to learning racial categories, then we would expect to see that characters from minoritized racial backgrounds should be underrepresented relative to White characters in children's books. To evaluate this question, it is important to define what is meant by 'underrepresented'. One might argue that, because of the demographics of the United States, it would be expected that most characters in children's books available in the U.S. would be White. According to 2022 estimates, 58.9% of the total U.S. population identifies as White non-Hispanic (US Census); we will evaluate our findings relative to these estimates.

Existing work suggests that characters from minoritized racial groups are less common in children's books relative to characters from the majority group (Bramlett-Solomon & Roeder, 2008; Chung, 2019; Cooperative Children's Book Center, n.d.; Koss, 2015; Koss & Paciga, 2020; Roberts & Rizzo, 2021). Yet, the magnitude of these differences varies considerable across existing studies. For example, some studies report that 25% or less of the book corpus analyzed depicts characters from minoritized racial groups (e.g., Hughes-Hassell et al., 2009; Iwamoto, 1996; Lee, 2017), whereas this figure approaches or surpasses 50% in other studies (e.g., Edmonds, 1986; Hughes-Hassell & Cox, 2010; McDaniel, 2020). This makes it challenging to evaluate our frequency hypothesis from existing individual studies. Therefore, we will for the first time synthesize existing corpus studiesfoot_0 through a meta-analysis of proportions (Lipsey and Wilson, 2001; Wang & Liu, 2016); this meta-analytical technique provides an aggregate estimate of frequency across corpora and allows for a more precise test of our frequency hypothesis. Because existing studies differ in which racial groups were reported, we adopted a coding scheme that contrasted minoritized characters with White characters. Although this approach necessarily obscures important differences among minoritized groups, it is methodologically necessary so that consistent estimates can be extracted across corpora.

3.1 Method

3.1.1. Literature Search.

We conducted a literature search following the PRISMA approach (Liberati et al., 2009) (see Figure 1). We searched Google Scholar and Scopus between March and June 2022 using the following terms: ("character" OR "protagonist") AND ("child" OR "children") AND ("book" OR "picture book" OR "literature") AND ("race" OR "ethnicity") AND ("diversity" OR "representation"). This search retrieved 826,000 results in Google Scholar and 4 results in Scopus, making exhaustive screening infeasible; therefore, we screened the abstracts of the first 500 Google Scholar results and all 4 Scopus results (N = 504).

Additional sources (N = 17) were identified through related-publication searches, reference lists, and our own data (reported in the Supplemental Materials, S1). As shown in Figure 1, abstract screening of these 521 sources, using the inclusion criteria described below, identified 40 potentially eligible sources for full-text review. Fulltext screening resulted in a final sample of 20 sources that satisfied all inclusion criteria and were included in the data extraction step (these are indicated by an asterisk (*) in the Reference section).

Sources were eligible if they: (1) were peer-reviewed journal papers, theses, or proceedings (book sources were excluded because they are not primary sources); (2) analyzed children's books that were available to children in the United States (we did not include analyses of textbooks, television programs, or materials intended for adolescents or adults); (3) coded the race of human characters -including White characters and at least one other racial group -and reported sufficient information to determine character countsfoot_1 ; (4) were written in English.

3.1.2. Data extraction.

First, for each paper, we extracted the authors, title, and year of publication. This information was used to create a shortened citation (e.g., "Crisp et al., 2016") to be used in figures. Next, to calculate effect sizes, for each study we extracted the number of human characters that belonged to minoritized racial groups, the number of human characters that belonged to the majority racial group, and the total number of human characters. These data were extracted from the text, table, figures, or a combination of these, typically found in the Results section or the supplemental materials. Due to different studies employing different coding schemes (e.g., coded the presence of animal and/or inanimate characters; included an "indeterminate" or "unknown" category), we first extracted the number of characters belonging to either the dominant racial group (i.e., indicated in the paper as White, European, or Caucasian) or to a minoritized racial group (i.e., characters coded as Asian, Biracial or Multiracial, Black or African American, Latine, Middle Eastern or Arab, Native American, Person of color, or Racial/Ethnic minority). The sum of the count of the two categories was entered as the total number of human characters. Using this sum as the denominator when calculating the proportions of characters from minoritized racial groups and the dominant racial group ensured that the proportions were comparable across all studies included. Note that in some cases (e.g., when additional categories such as animal or inanimate character were coded or when multiple characters per book were coded) this might result in the percentages reported in the meta-analysis differing from those reported in the source and/or the total number of characters in our analysis differing from the total number of books reported in the paper.

For each paper we additionally coded the following variables: (i) which characters were coded (only one protagonist, main characters, all characters); (ii) the publication year or range of publication years of the books coded in that source; (iii) how many books were coded; and (iv) how the books were selected for inclusion (award-winning and/or best-selling status; time period; school libraries). These variables are reported in the Supplemental Materials (S2). Finally, to address the possibility that there could be overlap in book titles across the papers included in the meta-analysis -which could create unknown interdependencies in the datawe additionally coded the degree of overlap in book titles across the sources for which this information was available; this variable was used to conduct the sensitivity analysis reported in the Supplemental Materials (S2).

3.1.3. Analytical Approach.

A random effect meta-analysis was performed to estimate the pooled frequency of characters from minoritized racial groups. Confidence intervals for individual studies were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method and the between-study variance was estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird technique. Data were pooled using the inverse-variance method and the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to calculate an overall proportion. A value of 0.411 was used to specify the null hypothesis (i.e., that there is no underrepresentation of characters from minoritized racial groups relative to U.S. demographics). This number was obtained by calculating the complement of the proportion of the U.S. population who identifies as White non-Hispanic (58.9%; U.S. Census Bureau) (i.e., 1 -0.589 = 0.411).

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed visually using a forest plot and quantified using the I² statistic; heterogeneity was considered to be high if I² was greater than 75% (Higgins et al, 2003). The meta-analysis was performed using R software (R Core Team, 2022) and the package "meta" (Balduzzi et al., 2019). All data and code are openly available (osf.io/vhnpm/? view_only=76182da197e14dce87b73d433cb433a2). This study was not preregistered.

3.2. Results

The forest plot in Figure 2 depicts the estimated frequency and 95% confidence intervals of each study included in the data analysis, sorted by the precision (i.e., the inverse of the variance) of each study; the total pooled estimate is presented at the bottom. As can be seen, there is considerable variability across studies, with the proportion of characters from minoritized racial groups ranging from 6% to 61%. Notably, the large majority of studies (16 out of 20) reports numerical estimates of characters from minority racial groups that are below the U. There was evidence for heterogeneity in effect sizes (I² = 97%; 95% CI: [96.0%; 97.5%]), suggesting that the overall point estimate might be moderated by additional variables. The accepted practice is that a quantitative analysis of moderators includes at least 10 studies per moderator (Littell et al., 2008); due to the small sample of studies, we instead conducted (i) a qualitative analysis of studies whose 95% confidence intervals were farthest away from the confidence interval of the summary effect size in either direction and (ii) a leave-one-out analysis to ensure that outliers did not bias the point estimate -these analyses are presented in detail in the Supplemental Materials (S2). First, the qualitative analysis suggests that the number of books selected for inclusion and how the books were selected for inclusion might contribute to variation in the observed estimates (see Supplemental Materials S2). We note that, as with any analysis of moderators, these trends are descriptive and cannot be taken as causal evidence (Cuijpers, 2016; Littell et al., 2008). Second, there was no evidence from the leave-one-out analysis that any source had an influential effect on the overall pooled point estimate. Finally, to address the possibility that different sources could include many of the same book titles, we recalculated the meta-analytical frequency estimate including only sources for which we are certain there is no or low crosssource overlap; those analyses, reported in detail in the Supplemental Materials (S2), showed that the degree of overlap was very small and unlikely to influence the meta-analytical frequency estimate.

3.3. Summary: Frequency of racial groups in children's books

Taken together, the results of this meta-analysis suggest that characters from minoritized racial groups are underrepresented in children's books relative to their base-rate in the U.S. population. As of 2022, an estimated 41% of the U.S. population belongs to minoritized racial groups, but our aggregate estimate suggests that only 25% of characters in children's books are people from minoritized racial groups. By synthesizing evidence across existing corpus studies, this meta-analysis provides the first aggregate estimate of the frequency with which racial groups are depicted in children's books, allowing a more precise test of frequency differences.

4. Study 2: Distributional contexts

In Study 2 we tested the hypothesis that there are systematic differences in the distributional contexts surrounding the depiction of racial groups in children's books. Existing evidence suggests that children may encounter systematically different information about characters from different racial groups through the narratives in which those characters are embedded. For example, a content analysis of 218 books marketed to infants and toddlers showed that when characters from minoritized racial groups were depicted, the books still centered White characters (Hughes-Hassell & Cox, 2010). Similarly, analyses of children's books depicting Asian (Rodríguez et al., 2018) and Latine (Mendoza & Reese, 2001) characters show that these characters are often presented in stereotypical ways.

Systematic differences in content were further documented in a thematic analysis of 1,037 books depicting characters from minoritized racial groups published between 2008 and 2015 (Aronson et al.; 2018).

This analysis suggested that different racial groups were associated with different thematic contexts; for example, Black characters were likely to be depicted in books telling stories about oppression and injustices, while Asian and Latine characters were likely to be depicted in books about cultural practices (Aronson et al., 2018). Here we expand this existing work in two important ways. First, we use a larger corpus of books and include a wider range of publication years relative to prior work -both important for the generalizability of the findings. Second, we use a measure of mutual information to quantify associations between racial groups and thematic contexts while accounting for differences in the base rates of both thematic contexts and racial groups within the corpus. This allows us to identify whether particular thematic contexts are disproportionately associated with specific racial groups beyond what would be expected from their overall frequencies alone.

As we describe below, we analyzed a comprehensive collection of children's books featuring characters from minoritized racial backgrounds in which each book has been categorized according to its central narrative theme (Aronson et al., 2018). We treated these themes as an operationalization of context -under the assumption that characters appearing in books with the same theme are embedded in more similar contexts than characters appearing in books with different themes. If different racial groups are systematically associated with different distributional contexts, thematic context and racial group should be statistically non-independent.

4.1. Method

>Method

4.1.1. Book Collection.

We searched the Diverse BookFinder corpus (diversebookfinder.org) to document the number of books depicting characters from minoritized racial backgrounds in each thematic context.

Because the collection systematically catalogs books according to both racial group and thematic context, it provides a unique resource for examining the association between these variables. The collection in the Diverse BookFinder includes all literature for children and youth featuring characters from minoritized backgrounds published in the US since 2002. We restricted the corpus to "Picture books" (using the "Form" filter) and used the "Central Theme Categories" filter to obtain the count of books depicting characters of each racial group in each theme. We also restricted the analysis to six racial groups and five themes that showed the most variability across racial groups in prior work (Aronson et al., 2018; Laboy et al., 2023) foot_2 ; we excluded categories that were either infrequently represented or showed little variation across groups because such categories contribute limited information about associations between racial group and thematic context. The data reported here reflect the collection as of February 2025, including 4,403 books. Given that children's beliefs about racial/ethnic groups can also include religious markers (e.g., Taylor et al., 2020; Van der Straten Waillet & Roskam, 2012; Zamit et al., 2024), in the Supplemental Materials (S3), we present an additional analysis that includes religious groups.

4.1.2. Themes and Racial Groups.

The Diverse BookFinder employs the coding scheme developed by Aronson et al. (2018). Coders are experts in children's literature (e.g., librarians, professors, teachers) and trained over a 9-week period to classify the racial group and the theme of books. Each title is additionally reviewed by an additional trained coder before being added to the collection. Additional details about the coding procedures are provided by Aronson et al. (2018) and on the Diverse BookFinder website.

The five themes -i.e., contexts -included in our analysis are: Any child, Centering Culture and Identity, Cross-group, Folklore, and Oppression. Books in the Any Child category feature narratives about everyday topics and adventures in which the racial group of the character is not central to the narrative (e.g., 'Jabari Jumps' by Gaia Cornwall). By contrast, books in the Centering Culture and Identity category feature characters in which racial identity is central to the narrative (e.g., 'Bilal Cooks Daal' by Aisha Saeed). Books in the Cross Group category depict interactions between racial groups (e.g., 'A New Kind of Wild' by Zara Gonzalez Hoang).

Books in the Folklore category feature aspects of tradition, cultural rituals, or values of a group of people (e.g., 'The Orphan and the Polar Bear' by Sakiasi Qaunaq). Finally, books in the Oppression category depict groupbased injustice and struggles for justice (e.g., 'Separate is Never Equal' by Duncan Tonatiuh). We treated these thematic categories as a proxy for the semantic contexts in which characters are depicted. Under this operationalization, books assigned to the same thematic category are assumed to provide more similar contextual information than books assigned to different categories. Although prior work already suggests that young children are sensitive to thematic differences in narratives (Aronson et al., 2016; Cameron et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2025; Gasser et al., 2022), future research will be needed to determine how children learn from the thematic categories examined here.

The six racial groups included in the analysis are: Asian/Pacific Islander/Asian American; Biracial/Multiracial/Mixed Race; Black/African/African American; First/Native Nations/American Indian/Indigenous; Latine/Hispanic/Latin American; Middle Eastern/North African/Arab. For ease of reading, these groups will be shortened in the text and in figures as follows: "Asian", "Multiracial", "Black", "Native Peoples", "Latine", "Middle Eastern".

4.1.3. Analytical Approach.

As typical of naturally-occurring data, the levels of the two variables analyzed here show unequal base rates. For example, some racial groups are represented more frequently than others (e.g., Black characters are more common than Latine characters), and some thematic contexts are more common than others (e.g., Centering Culture and Identity books are more common than Oppression books); this makes raw counts difficult to interpret. Therefore, to analyze the patterns of conditional dependencies in the book collection, we applied a measure of mutual information (Pointwise Mutual Information, PMI; Fano, 1961) to the contingency table of racial groups and themes using the pmi function from the svs package (Plevoets, 2015) in R (R Core Team, 2022). PMI corrects for the unequal base rates by quantifying the extent to which a specific group-theme pairing occurs more or less frequently than would be expected from the overall frequencies of the group and theme alone. Conceptually, PMI compares the observed frequency of a specific racial group-theme pairing (e.g., Black-Oppression) to the frequency that would be expected if racial group and theme were statistically independent. A PMI score of 0 indicates independence; positive values indicate that a pairing occurs more frequently than expected under an assumption of independence and negative values indicate that it occurs less frequently than expected under an assumption of independence. To facilitate interpretation, we focus on pairings with PMI scores in the highest and lowest quartiles of the distribution (see red and dark blue cells in Figure 3); these pairings are the strongest positive and negative departures from statistical independence and therefore provide the clearest evidence of systematic associations between racial groups and thematic contexts.

4.2. Results

Figure 3 depicts a heatmap of the PMI scores calculated on the contingency table of racial groups and thematic contexts; the red and dark blue cells depict PMI scores at the top and bottom quantiles of the distribution of PMI scores, respectively.

Overall, different racial groups appear to be more strongly associated with certain themes. Specifically, Black characters dominate the Oppression theme (PMI=0.64), Native People (PMI=0.75) and Latine (PMI=0.47) characters are strongly associated with the Centering Culture and Identity theme, and Middle Eastern (PMI=1.55) and Asian characters (PMI=0.92) are likely to be portrayed in the Folklore theme.

Finally, there are also strong associations between Multiracial characters and the Any Child theme (PMI= 0.98), as well as between Black (PMI=0.29) and Middle Eastern/Arab (PMI=0.26) characters and the Cross Group theme -though the last two were weaker relative to the other themes positively associated with each group.

The lowest scores -by hypothesis, the pairings that are least likely to be experienced by U.S. children -offer us further insight into the distributional contexts. Middle Eastern (PMI=-1.95) and Native Peoples (PMI=-0.80) were unlikely to be portrayed in books with the Any Child theme (recall that these racial groups were also most frequently depicted in the Folklore and the Centering Culture and Identity themes, respectively).

Latine (PMI=-0.96), Multiracial (PMI=-0.95), and Asian (PMI=-0.90) characters were unlikely to be depicted in books about Oppression. Finally, Multiracial (PMI=-1.78), Native People (PMI=-1.26), and Black (PMI=-1.1) characters were unlikely to be portrayed in books with Folklore messages.

4.3. Summary: Distributional contexts in children's books

Study 2 suggests that, even when the relative frequencies of each group and contextual theme are considered, there are systematic differences pertaining to how often certain racial groups and thematic contexts are likely to occur -or not occur -together. First, Black characters were disproportionately associated with books about Oppression, while Asian, Latine, and Multiracial characters were underrepresented in this thematic context.

Second, Middle Eastern/Arab and Native Peoples were disproportionally depicted in Folklore or Centering Culture and Identity books, with these same groups being unlikely to be depicted in Any Child books. Third, Latine and Asian characters were disproportionally depicted in Folklore or Centering Culture and Identity books. While it is important for children to learn about cultural histories and practices (White & Wanless, 2019), the systematic co-occurrence of particular racial groups and thematic contexts may provide children with distributional evidence that certain groups belong with certain kinds of narratives. To the extent that children are sensitive to these distributional regularities, such patterns may contribute to the learning and differentiation of racial categories.

5. General Discussion

The studies reported here aimed to characterize the structure of experience from which children may learn racial categories. Drawing on theories of category learning, we focused on two properties of experience that are known to influence category formation and representational structure across domains: frequency and contextual structure. Consistent with our predictions, Study 1 showed systematic differences in the frequency with which racial groups are depicted in children's books, such that characters from minoritized racial groups were underrepresented relative to their prevalence in the U.S. population. Study 2 showed systematic differences in distributional contexts, such that different racial groups were systematically associated with different thematic contexts. Together, these findings suggest that children's books contain regularities that could plausibly support the learning of racial categories. In what follows, we discuss the implications of these findings for theories of racial category learning, consider how characterizing children's input may inform efforts to modify developmental trajectories, and outline directions for future research.

5.1. Frequency and contextual structure as statistical regularities in children's experience

The ability to learn from the statistical distribution of data is one of the most well-established findings in developmental science. From birth, infants are remarkably adept at learning the distribution of events in their environment, and this ability is thought to underlie infants and children's ability to segment words from speech, acquire visual categories, establish word-to-object mappings, and learn to recognize emotions (Fiser & Lengyel, 2022; Isbilen & Christiansen, 2022; Romberg & Saffran, 2010; Ruba et al., 2022; Shapiro & Turk-Browne, 2015).

Contemporary social learning theories similarly propose that children's learning about social categories is shaped by the regularities they observe in their environments (Bigler & Liben, 2007; Hirschfeld, 1995; Hughes et al., 2023; Hughes & Watford, 2022; Roberts & Rizzo, 2020), but while we now know much about the trajectory of this learning, we know significantly less about the structure of the experience from which children learn these categories. A motivation for this work was the observation that theories in many areas of developmental science have been refined by careful characterization of the input available to learners (Clerkin et al., 2017; de Barbaro & Fausey, 2022; Hart & Risley, 1995; Franchak et al., 2026; Hoemann et al., 2026; Montag et al., 2015; Pereira et al., 2026; Sugden et al., 2014; Yu & Smith, 2012), while theories of racial category learning have largely developed without comparable descriptions of the relevant regularities children experience in everyday life (though see Bigler & Liben, 2006; Cikara et al., 2022; Rhodes & Mandalaywala, 2017; Rizzo et al., 2026; Ward & Bridgewater, 2023). By examining children's books as one measurable source of developmentally-relevant input, the present studies provide evidence that children's experience includes regularities both in the frequency with which racial groups are encountered and in the contexts in which they are encountered.

Research on category learning shows that category representations are shaped by the frequency with which exemplars are encountered and by the distributional contexts in which they appear (Arnon, 2015; Bullinaria & Levy, 2007; Carvalho et al., 2021; Ellis, 2002; Kruschke, 1996; Landauer & Dumais, 1997; McClelland & Rogers, 2004). The present findings suggest that both sources of information vary systematically across racial groups in children's books. From a statistical learning perspective, these regularities constitute potential learning signals: repeated exposure to these patterns may provide information about which racial groups are common and about which narratives and experiences tend to co-occur with which groups; in turn, this input may provide distributional information that differentiates racial groups from one another in children's mental representations. In other words, if children encode these regularities, they may gradually accumulate different expectations about different racial groups. In this way, frequency and contextual structure may be dimensions of experience through which everyday experiences contribute to the formation and differentiation of racial category representations.

More broadly, we believe that characterizing the statistical structure of children's experiences, guided by what is known from the categorization and representational change literatures, should become a central component of research on racial category learning, much as analyses of child-directed language have become central to theories of language development (Anderson et al., 2021; Hart & Risley, 1995; Hoff, 2003; Suskind, 2016; Rowe, 2012 : Rowe & Snow, 2020). An implication of this perspective is that explaining the development of racial categories requires understanding not only the representations children possess, but also the statistical regularities from which those representations are learned. By identifying systematic differences in both the frequency and contextual structure of racial representations in children's books, the present studies provide a step toward specifying the environmental inputs that may give rise to children's emerging beliefs, expectations, and attitudes about racial groups. A shift toward studying the structure of children's experiences, guided by insights from the categorization and representational change literatures, has the potential to advance a more mechanistic understanding of how ow everyday environments contribute to the emergence of racial categories -and, potentially, racial biases -in early childhood.

5.2. Implications for modifying children's experiences

Importantly, understanding the structure of children's experience also has the potential to allow us to change the learning trajectory of racial categorization, and thus interrupt the development of racial biases. If children's representations of racial groups are shaped by the statistical regularities they encounter, then characterizing those regularities may help identify features of experience that can be targeted for intervention -a critical goal towards improving all children's well-being and reducing inequalities (Marcelo & Yates, 2019; Skinner-Dorkenoo et al., 2023; Waxman, 2021).

The present studies were not designed to test interventions. At the same time, if frequency and contextual structure are important components of the input from which children learn racial categories, then these same features of experience may be promising targets for future intervention efforts. Therefore, one implication is that interventions may be most effective when they modify the same dimensions of experience that contribute to category learning: if frequency and contextual structure play a role in shaping racial category representations, then interventions that alter these properties of children's experience may promote greater perceived similarity across groups. Based on the results reported here, one hypothesis is that children's representations of racial groups should become more similar if different racial groups are encountered with more comparable frequencies and if thematic contexts are distributed more evenly across groups. Importantly, these two factors likely to not operate independently; for example, in the context of book exposure, merely increasing the frequency of exposure to racially diverse characters might not be sufficient to increase intergroup similarity in the absence of more uniform distributions of thematic contexts. The contribution of each factorand their interaction -will be critical to determine in future empirical work. It also remains an open question whether exposure to a more uniform distribution (both of frequency and of contexts) would be sufficient to alter category representations shaped by prior experience, or whether one would need to oversample from the other end of the distribution -that is, to increase exposure to groups and to group-theme pairings in the inverse of the frequency documented in the present studies. Distinguishing among these possibilities will require experimental and longitudinal work tracking changes in children's representations.

5.3. Open questions

The evidence reported here leaves several important questions for future work. First, it will be important for future work to determine if the statistics observed in children's books generalize to other aspects of children's experiences and to other social groups. The present studies focused on books because they provide a tractable, developmentally relevant source of input and because existing work suggests that the structure of experience observed here is likely not restricted to books or even to media (Bramlett-Solomon & Roeder, 2008; Hughes & Watford, 2022; Roberts & Rizzo, 2021; Salter et al., 2018; Tukachinsky et al., 2015; Vargas & Verstynen, 2025). Nevertheless, future work should directly examine the structure of other aspects of children's experience with racial categories and whether comparable patterns emerge for other social categories (e.g., Lewis et al., 2021).

Second, it will be important to document whether these patterns remain stable or change over time - Third, it will be important to directly relate individual variation in experience -whether naturally occurring or experimentally manipulated -to changes in children's representations of racial categories. These will be critical to develop better theoretical models of experience-dependent development of racial categories, as well as to develop better intervention tools. We would be remiss to not mention the importance of using valid and reliable measures to assess changes in racial categories and social preferences; existing media-based intervention studies have often relied on measures with unknown psychometric properties (Aboud et al., 2012), limiting the conclusions that can be drawn about developmental change.

Finally, future work should consider how specific experiences generalize to new instances. For example, Study 2 suggested that some racial groups were associated with similar thematic contexts; does such a pattern of input similarity result in those specific groups becoming more similarly represented? Black characters were also the most common group in the corpus examined in Study 2; does exposure to one (or a few) minoritized racial groups lead to generalization of attitudes to other minoritized racial groups? Changes in representational structure can result from both increasing and decreasing similarity among existing internal representations of categories (e.g., Vales et al., 2020b). Accordingly, a mechanistic account of racial category learning will require a deeper understanding of how specific properties of children's experiences shape the representational similarity and differentiation of social groups.

S1 -Racial diversity in a corpus of contemporary popular U.S. picture books [see Study 1]

Award-winning status has been a common process of selecting books that are likely to be encountered by many U.S. children (e.g., Adukia et al., 2021; Koss, 2016; Moffet, 2016). These awards typically span a wide range of years and include books published as early as the early 1900's. Estimates of racial representation in children's literature going back to at least 1985 suggest a modest increase in published books about characters from minoritized racial groups in the last decades (CCBC, n.d.). Thus, it is possible that corpora that include only award-winning books spanning a wide range of years are not the most accurate representation of the books to which U.S. children are currently exposed. In existing work, when other metrics of popularity -such as bestselling status -have been taken into account, the corpus also included story and chapter books marketed to older children (Balkaran & Roberts, 2019). One study documented the racial diversity in U.S. children's board books (i.e., those marketed to infants and toddlers), but the titles were selected for inclusion based on year of publication (Hughes-Hassell & Cox, 2010). Thus, to date there is limited documentation of the degree of underrepresentation in books that are likely to be encountered by the youngest U.S. children. To address this gap and complement existing data, we conducted a content analysis of racial diversity in a corpus of 100 contemporary popular U.S. picture books (Montag et al., 2015); we then included this new dataset in a meta-analysis of racial diversity in U.S. children's books (i.e., Study 1 in the manuscript).

S1.1. Corpus description and coding approach.

We used the book titles in the corpus developed by Montag et al. (2015). This corpus includes 100 children's picture books targeted to ages 0-5 published until 2014 that are popular in the United States (e.g., best sellers on amazon.com; often circulated in libraries and included in librarian-recommended lists), and thus are books that young U.S. children are likely to encounter. We compiled the year of publication, author name, and illustrator name for each of these 100 titles, and coded them according to the categories described below. The books in the corpus were published between 1893 and 2014, with the distribution centered in 1991 (SD=25.4) (see Figure S1 below). This aligns with trends reported in prior work that some titles remain popular for many years after publication (see Adukia et al., 2023; Koss and Paciga 2020; Smith, 2013).

Two coders independently coded all titles according to the following categories, described in more detail in each section below: Main character of a book; Presence of human behaviors; Race of the Author and Illustrator. This independent coding was used to calculate the interrater reliabilities that are reported for each category. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion between the two coders and the final consensus was included in the final data for analysis.

S1.2.1. Coding.

To identify the main character of each book, the coders took into consideration the title, illustrations, synopsis of the book, and the book pages. If the main character of a book -the character that is present in most pages and is the target of the plot -was considered human, their race was coded as 'dominant' (i.e., White) or 'minoritized'. If no main character could be identified, the coders used 'undetermined' (1% of the corpus). The coders reached 91% agreement on this category.

S1.2.2. Results.

To examine the distribution of main characters in the corpus, we compared in an omnibus analysis the number of books in each of the main character categories ('dominant racial group', 'minoritized racial group', 'animal', and 'object'); this allows us to document if the categories were equally distributed in the corpus.

We followed up on this omnibus analysis by sequentially comparing the categories based on their frequency (i.e., sequential pairwise comparisons from the most to least frequent character category); this analysis also included a planned test of the hypothesis that White characters would be more frequent than characters from minoritized racial groups -which we tested by directly comparing the frequency of these two categories. Because of the multiple pairwise comparisons conducted in this analysis, we adjusted the p-values using the Bonferroni method.

As can be seen, the categories are not evenly distributed in the corpus. Specifically, more than half of the books in the corpus (66%) depict nonhuman characters, with 56% of the books including an animal as the main character and 10% of the books in the corpus including an object as the main character. With regards to human characters, 31% of the characters in the corpus were members of the dominant racial group, while 2% of the characters in the corpus were members of a minoritized racial group. S1.3. What behaviors are depicted in the corpus of contemporary popular books?

The analysis reported in the previous section showed that animal main characters were the most common category in the corpus of popular books analyzed. Many forms of media for children include nonhuman characters (such as animals or objects) depicted performing actions typically associated with humans (e.g., wearing clothes, driving a vehicle, having friendships; Casey et al., 2021; Goldstein & Alperson, 2020; McCabe et al., 2011). While fantasy and make-believe are ubiquitous components childhood in Western societies (Hofferth & Sandberg, 2001), if children's picture books are more likely to depict nonhuman characters performing human actions than human characters belonging to minoritized racial groups, this may have consequences for children's emerging understanding of race-based hierarchies. For White children, such statistics may increase children's tendency to anthropomorphize animals and hinder recognition of the shared humanity among people from all racial and ethnic backgrounds. For children belonging to minoritized racial groups, not seeing characters like themselves may result not only in the internalization of racial hierarchies but may also result in limited opportunities for the development of positive self-identity (Koss, 2015; Pérez Huber, Camargo Gonzalez & Solórzano; 2020; Pérez Huber & Solorzano, 2015). To examine whether such trends were present in this corpus of popular children's picture books, the presence of human behaviors was coded for the main characters in the corpus.

S1.3.1. Coding.

To code this category, the coders took into consideration the title, illustrations, synopsis of the book, and the book pages. The main characters were coded as whether they frequently displayed human behaviors (e.g., wearing human clothes, living in a house, driving a vehicle, using human language, having friendships), nonhuman animal behaviors (e.g., laying eggs, eating grass, living in a burrow), or both (when both types of behaviors were present; e.g., talking and laying eggs). Books in which characters displayed other kinds of behaviors or for which the coders were unable to code this category with certainty from the information available were coded as 'other' (1% of the corpus). The coders reached 70% agreement on this category.

S1.4. What is the racial makeup of creators in the corpus of contemporary popular books?

Prior work suggests that creators who are members of minoritized racial groups are more likely to create diverse content (e.g., Casey et al., 2021). At the same time, creators who belong to minoritized racial groups are underrepresented in children's literature (Cooperative Children's Book Center, n.d.; Koss, 2015; Mabbott, 2017). As such, we also documented the race of the author and illustrator of the books in this corpus to examine if similar trends were present.

S1.4.1. Coding.

The author and the illustrator of each book were coded as either belonging to the 'dominant' (i.e., White) or 'minoritized' racial group. The coders followed the coding scheme suggested by the Cooperative Children's Book Center (n.d.), including looking at the author or illustrator's biographies, websites, interviews, association memberships (e.g., Asian/Pacific American Library Association), and family background. The two coders reached 99% agreement on this category.

S1.4.2. Results.

The overwhelmingly majority of the creators (authors and illustrators) of the books in the corpus was White (98%), with only 2% of all creators being a member of a minoritized racial group, ! 2 (1) = 161, p < 0.0001. Such a small number of creators belonging to minoritized racial groups precluded any further analysis of trends.

S.1.5. Summary

Taken together, these results suggest that these popular contemporary picture books overrepresent White characters. The results further suggest that animal main characters are not only the most frequent category, but also that these animal characters are likely to be depicted performing behaviors typically associated with humans (e.g., a pigeon driving a bus or a dinosaur having a bedtime routine). Future work can more directly examine what such regular experience with books depicting anthropomorphized animal characters (but not with characters belonging to minoritized racial groups) may be teaching young children about the social and racial hierarchies entrenched in the United States.

S2. Meta-analysis of frequency in U.S. children's books: Qualitative description of potential moderators, leave-one-out analysis, and sensitivity analysis of title overlap [see Study 1]

The meta-analysis of proportions reported in Study 1 found evidence of between-study heterogeneity. Such variation is typically examined by performing a quantitative moderator analysis. However, the accepted practice is that a quantitative analysis of moderators should include at least 10 studies per moderator (Littell et al., 2008), which is not feasible in this meta-analysis. Therefore, due to the small number of sources available in this meta-analysis, we examined the heterogeneity in effect sizes in two ways: through a qualitative description of the sources whose confidence intervals were furthest away from the confidence interval of the pooled point estimate and through a leave-one-out analysis. We note that, as with any analysis of moderators, these trends are descriptive and cannot be taken as causal evidence (Cuijpers, 2016; Littell et al., 2008).

First, we examined the sources whose confidence intervals were furthest away from the confidence interval of the pooled point estimate (i.e., the sources at both ends of the distribution) as it pertains to the following four dimensions: which characters were coded; the publication year or range of publication years of the books coded in that source; how many books were coded; and how the books were selected for inclusion in that source. Four sources had an estimated number of characters from minoritized racial groups that were at the lower end relative to the pooled estimate: Adukia et al., 2021; Crisp et al., 2016; Iwamoto, 1996; and Blinded for review.

Three of these four sources coded only protagonists or multiple main characters (Crisp et al., 2016; Iwamoto, 1996; Blinded for review) and one source coded all characters (Adukia et al., 2021). Three sources spanned a wide interval of publication years (min: 1893; max: 2019; Adukia et al., 2021; Iwamoto, 1996; and Blinded for review); this information was not available for Crisp et al., 2016. Two of these sources included more than 1,000 books (Adukia et al., 2021; Crisp et al., 2016) and the other two included 100 or less (Iwamoto, 1996; Blinded for review). Three of these sources selected books for inclusion based on award-winning status or other measures of popularity (Adukia et al., 2021; Iwamoto, 1996; Blinded for review). One source selected books for inclusion by sampling 11 school libraries in a single state (Crisp et al., 2016), which might still be a proxy of popularity (the titles of the books included are not provided, preventing us from directly assessing this possibility).

Two sources had an estimated number of characters from minoritized racial groups that were at the upper end relative to the pooled estimate : Edmonds, 1986; Mann-Boykin et al., 2016. These sources coded only protagonists or multiple main characters (Edmonds, 1986; Mann-Boykin et al., 2016). Books included in the source spanned about 50 years ending in the late 1970's for one source (1928 -1974; Edmonds, 1986); this information was not available for the other source (Mann-Boykin et al., 2016). Both sources included fewer than 1,000 books; Mann-Boykin et al. (2016) included 70 books and Edmonds (1986) included 952 books. One source selected books for inclusion based on publication over a specific time period (Edmonds, 1986), and the other source selected books for inclusion by sampling 10 ten early childhood classroom libraries in a single state (Mann-Boykin et al., 2016); it is important to note that the selection process in Mann-Boykin et al. (2016) intended to oversample books that included characters from minoritized racial groups, likely contributing to the fact that this is the source that estimates the largest proportion of characters from minoritized racial groups.

Second, to investigate whether any sources had a disproportionate effect on the pooled point estimate, we conducted a leave-one-out analysis; this analytic approach (i) fits the random-effects model leaving out one study at a time and calculates the pooled point estimate without each study at each iteration, and (ii) identifies 'influential' cases by considering the difference between the pooled point estimate with and without each study (Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010). The results from this analysis suggest that the point estimate does not seem to change as a result of excluding each study, with no study being identified as influential according to the thresholds discussed in Viechtbauer, & Cheung, 2010; across all iterations, the pooled point estimate ranged only from 0.25 to 0.28 (M=0.25; SD=0.007). Thus, there was no evidence that the pooled point estimate depended strongly on particular sources.

S3. Representation of racial/ethnic groups including religious groups [see Study 2]

Because children's social categories can include religious markers (e.g., Taylor et al., 2020; Van der Straten Waillet & Roskam, 2012; Zamit et al., 2024) and because the distinction between ethnic and religious groups is not well defined, we conducted a follow-up to the analysis reported in Study 2 that additionally includes the three most frequent groups under the "Religion" tab in the DiverseBook Finder database. This added an additional 585 books to the analysis, for a total of 4,988 books. The data reflect the collection as of February 2025. All aspects of the analytic approach were the same as reported in the main manuscript. We would like to note two features of the dataset that are important for contextualizing the results. First, these three categories combined (i.e., Christian, Jewish, Muslim) account for a small proportion of the total number of books in this analysis (12%).

Second, the books in the DiverseBook Finder collection are selected for inclusion based on featuring characters from minoritized racial/ethnic groups and then additionally tagged if aspects of religious groups are includedand therefore, these likely do not accurately represent all children's books depicting religious groups. Because of these two features of the dataset, the results reported here should be viewed as in need of future corroboration.
