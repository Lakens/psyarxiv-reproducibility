Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Full text

Abstract

Although neurodivergent people can achieve high levels of academic and vocational training in the same professions as others, they remain un-and under-employed; this is in part due to the "wickedness" of the problem. To address the complexity we convened a group of neurodivergent lived experience and employment experts to 1) define which problems in neurodiversity and skilled employment are most urgent to address; 2) create actionable strategies with potential leverage for generating the desired employment outcomes. We used qualitative methods from the systems thinking field: Soft Systems Methodology, futures visioning and SWOT analysis, and Group Model Building (system dynamics). The workshops resulted in identifying intervention leverage between organizational psychology constructs of employee wellbeing, workplace climate, and mutually beneficial workplace relationships, in conjunction with a new disability stress construct, rather than specific skills building or remediation of disability characteristics. Reaching a future in which neurodivergent people love their jobs will take education, resources, champions from the outside and inside, universal access, paradigm shifts, infrastructure, and recognition of individual and social complexity. We recommend examining the organizational psychology literature for adaptable interventions; focusing on peer support; leveraging emerging technology to manage complexity; and supporting supervisor behavior change based on authentic understanding.

Introduction

Problem Formulation

Although neurodivergent (Autistic, ADHDer, autDHDer, Touretter, Dyslexic, Mad, etc.) people can achieve high levels of academic and vocational training in the same range of professions as others, they remain un-and under-employed even compared to people with other disabilities, particularly in skilled jobs (i.e., jobs requiring more than a few weeks of training to master). Exact statistics for the U.S. remain elusive for reasons including the aggregation of disability type and job level in national reporting and a historic lack of research on neurodivergent adult employment. However, based on aggregate statistics 1, 2 ; qualitative information from people with lived experience 3, 4 ; studies from comparable countries outside of the U.S. 5 ; and, discourse in both the business 6 and neurodiversity communities 7 , there is strong reason to believe that skilled employment imbalances exist.

In recent decades, a plethora of programs have emerged to address these imbalances; however, solutions have had limited impact, particularly in skilled settings outside of information technology. Typing "neurodiversity employment" into a search engine will produce hundreds of neurodiversity at work programs, articles on neurodivergent advantages in technology, tips for employers, and academic centers dedicated to the issue. However, broad imbalances persist in skilled settings even as these interventions proliferate. The reasons for this are multiple, including 1) a focus on unskilled jobs; 2) when skilled, a focus on technology jobs in wellresourced corporations leaving out the majority with training, interest, or talent in any other industry; 3) a focus on autism that excludes other forms of neurodivergence or multiply disabled autistic people; 4) tying programs to services when many fall through service gaps, particularly if they do not have intellectual disability or are managing their mental health conditions; 5) the conceptualization and direction of programs and services from outside the neurodiversity community; and, 6) the assumptions of a homogenous view of neurodivergence. Despite the potential value of existing solutions for the subpopulations they serve, these solutions, in their current state, are insufficient even in aggregate to address the broader problem.

There is good reason for the narrow scope and large number of solutions. Neurodiversity and skilled employment, like many problems in policy and public health, is a "wicked problem"-it has such a high degree of complexity that it lacks clarity both in terms of its formulation and its solution 8 . Dimensions of complexity include the 1) dynamics of intense social stigma, 2) heterogenous nature of the population, 3) interdisciplinary nature of the domain, 4) evolving context of employment, and 5) high variance in real-world employment settings. Narrowing the scope (e.g., to unskilled positions for transition-age autistic youth receiving services, or to highly controlled partnerships with technology corporations) is a way to reduce the complexity and make the problem less wicked-albeit at the cost of neglecting the majority of the problem space and population. The proliferation of narrow solutions is symptomatic of both struggles with complexity and the characteristic of wicked problems that whomever defines the problem and they do so determines its possible solutions 9.

Purpose of Research Question

To address the complexity of the problem and further our long-term aim of improving skilled employment outcomes for neurodivergent people, we took a systems thinking and communityengaged approach using methods designed for wicked problems. Our project had two objectives: 1) to define which problems in neurodiversity and skilled employment are most urgent to address and their desired outcomes/goals for intervention and 2) to create actionable strategies with potential leverage for generating the desired employment outcomes.

Methods

Participatory and Systems Thinking Approaches

This project used a Participatory Action Research (PAR) 10 approach, in which 1) all members of the research team are also members of the research's target population, and 2) the research should generate change. Specifically, it promotes lived experience as knowledge that is just as valid as academic expertise, and it employs the systematic inquiry of science to create realworld change that benefits the neurodiversity community and population.

Systems thinking is an approach to holistic inquiry with roots in pragmatism and operations research during World War II 11, 12 . As a reaction to the non-mechanistic (i.e., unpredictable, heterogeneous, non-physical) and deeply nonlinear (i.e., feedback-driven) nature of complex socio-technical systems-which makes them difficult or impossible to understand and control using traditional reductionist science-systems thinking is a way to work with complex phenomena without breaking them into component parts. It is a means to, essentially, "see both the forest and the trees" 13 . As skilled employment for neurodivergent people is a massively complex domain, this project uses systems thinking methodologies for holistic inquiry.

Qualitative Approach

We used three qualitative methodologies from the systems thinking field to define the problem and identify outcomes: Soft Systems Methodology, futures visioning, and Group Model Building. Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) generates action plans to address problems in situations where both the problem and its solutions are ill-defined 12 . It is completed collaboratively by a small group of stakeholders, and comprises seven steps which can be used independently or in various combinations 12 . The steps are 1) determine a scope for the problem; 2) express it with a rich picture; 3) formulate "root definitions," or statements of purpose that describe the problem space and transformation in the format of "what to do, how to do it, why to do it;" 4) build a conceptual model of activities to achieve the transformation; 5) compare the model to the real world; 6) identify desirable and feasible actions; and 7) create a plan for action. Futures visioning 14 is a futures thinking method designed to 1) frame the past and present; 2) imagine desirable futures; and 3) reconcile where we are with where we want to be (i.e., the futures vision). For the first two futures visioning activities, we imagined we had achieved the transformations in the root definitions (from the SSM), and then answered the questions: 1.

What have we achieved? 2. Who are our stakeholders and how have they benefited from what we've done? 3. What are we most pleased about? 4. What have we put in place to make sure we can sustain over time? 5. How are we measuring progress and success? 6. Is there anything we still need to do?

7. What are the challenges we face now? For the third futures visioning activity we conducted a SWOT (Strength, Weakness, Opportunity, Threat) Analysis, using the categories and themes from the futures visioning as input 14.

System dynamics is the study of feedback and control, and its methodologies focus on modeling complex, nonlinear systems in holistic (i.e., non-reductionist) ways that can enhance understanding and capacity for intervention in these situations. 11 Group Model Building (GMB) generates collaborative qualitative system dynamics models called Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs). The method enables experts with no training in system dynamics to develop CLDs with the facilitation of skilled modelers 15.

The first two phases of GMB are 1) identifying the problem to model and 2) planning the workshop with facilitators. The third phase comprises: 1.

Brainstorming the "nouns" or ideas that function as nouns involved in the problem. 2. Identifying feedback relationships among nouns to create "seed" CLD structures to serve as starting points for group discussion. 3. Introducing feedback thinking (i.e., thinking in terms of simultaneous mutual influences rather than linear cause and effect) to the expert group. 4. Generating the completed CLD structures from the seed structures through group discussion. 5. Editing the structures into a cohesive model and validating the model by comparing it to real-world data or known theory. 6. Qualitatively analyzing policies and interventions.

Research Paradigm

Our paradigmatic lens for workshop analyses combined aspects of constructivism with a critical realist ontology 16 , critical 17 , and transformative paradigms 18.

Researcher Characteristics and Reflexivity

The project used a participatory action research approach 10 with seven experts (including the Principal Investigator, DMR) who 1) have lived experience of neurodivergence and neurodiversity culture; 2) have professional expertise relevant to employment; 3) represent demographic heterogeneity (race, gender, geographic region, industry, education). Our collective neurodivergence includes autism, ADHD, learning disabilities, psychiatric disabilities, sensory processing disabilities, and mobility disabilities. Our collective professional expertise includes employee resource group leadership, disability employment policy, peer support, employee management, product accessibility, clinical practice, academic research, industrial process analysis, and disability service systems across technology, research, arts, policy, academic, and health industries. Our PAR group collaborated with two systems science PhD students, KA and JC, and our research assistant PI who provided facilitation and support for the Group Model Building workshops and the causal loop diagramming. WW, an experienced modeler with an outside perspective, assisted with CLD analysis.

Context

This study took place in the U.S. and focused on U.S.-based employment systems for reasons of feasibility.

Sampling and Ethical Considerations

While this study was a systematic inquiry (i.e., research), it did not involve human subjects or population sampling.

Data Collection Methods

We collected data in 8 remote workshops on Zoom: 1) Soft Systems Methodology steps 1 and 2; 2) Soft Systems Methodology step 3; 3) Futures visioning; 4) SWOT analysis; 5) GMB, which also served as SSM step 4, 6) initial leverage identification; 7) SSM step 5 using the final GMB output; and 8) SSM steps 6 and 7 using an Ease/Benefit matrix. DMR facilitated all workshops except the rich picture workshop, led by an expert in visual facilitation, and the GMB workshop, which KA, JC, and PI facilitated. For all workshops, the team provided information verbally, in text chat, and, when applicable, by writing on shared whiteboards and documents.

For workshop 1, the visual facilitator created a live record of our discussion by drawing on whiteboards. They prompted the team first to discuss the current situation of neurodivergence at work, and then to discuss the ideal situation.

For workshop 2, we collected data during the workshop in a shared document structured with the CATWOE (Client, Actor, Transformation, Worldview, Owner, Environment) framework 12 . The document remained open after the workshop for team members to add additional ideas.

For workshops 3 and 4, we collected data live on shared whiteboards that DMR set up with the futures vision questions and the SWOT grid.

For workshop 5, the GMB workshop, DMR introduced the team to feedback thinking. We then developed the models through group discussion. KA facilitated the discussion while JC updated the structures on a Miro board and PI took additional notes on ideas to integrate into the structures after the meeting. Workshop 6 focused on identifying initial leverage. We collected data through live discussion on a Zoom whiteboard formatted with headings for each high-level category of leverage in Meadow's leverage framework: physical events, information/patterns of behavior, social/system structure, and mental models/paradigm 19 . Workshop 7 presented the edited, verified CLD, updated the original root definitions based on what we learned from our system dynamics modeling, and performed an initial analysis of actions that could trigger leverage based on the model through discussion of several starter theories of change synthesized by DMR. This provided the conceptual model for SSM step 5. We collected data through discussion on a FreeForm board. Workshop 8 presented a draft logic model based on the theories of change, areas of leverage, and additional brainstorming, to create an Ease/Benefit Matrix of potential solutions ("ease" being similar to "cost" but more holistic in scope). We collected data through live discussion on a Zoom whiteboard formatted as an Ease/Benefit Matrix.

Data Processing, Analysis, and Trustworthiness

For the rich picture, the visual facilitation included frequent check-ins to verify the pictures correctly captured the discussion (i.e., data collection, processing, analysis, and trustworthiness occurred simultaneously).

For workshop 2, DMR used the information in the shared CATWOE document and rich pictures to create a first draft of the root definitions. This involved following the SSM procedure for casting the data into the format of "Do P by Q to achieve R," where PQR answers the questions, "What? How? Why?" or "do what by how to achieve the why." This defines the transformation as a purposeful activity. DMR presented the draft root definitions to the team, who memberchecked 20 and refined them in a shared document and through feedback during a meeting. DMR edited the root definitions later based on what we'd learned from our examination of dynamics and leverage, which the group again member-checked and refined.

For workshops 3 and 4, DMR conducted a content analysis 21 of the discussion to synthesize it into categories and themes and member-checked them with the group for trustworthiness 22 . We removed one category from the SWOT content analysis-strengths related to the visibility of disability in employment-after our examination of dynamics and leverage revised our assumptions about it (it has dynamics that can lead to worse outcomes and othering which make its role both ambiguous and less impactive than the items we retained).

System dynamics modeling requires verification to be rigorous, and after the GMB and leverage workshops 5-7, DMR located relevant literature and real-world data with which to triangulate, adjust, and reconcile the CLDs, as described in Sterman 23 . The verification process with existing literature allowed us to organize our many structures and leverage points under existing occupational theories. We also used member-checking with the group periodically during model development and to confirm the final models presented here.

Futures Visioning

In our desired future , Neurodivergent people love their work! Employees and their communities are healthy. There is a culturally ingrained, holistic, and accurate understanding of neurodivergence and the needs of neurodivergent employees, which also extends to other employee characteristics. Higher-order impacts emerge in innovation, policy, practice, and paradigms that benefit the public.

Reconciliation Between Present and Desired Future

Education

We need to move beyond awareness campaigns, mandatory disability trainings, and public service announcements. Neurotypical people need to learn more about neurodivergent processing, skill sets, and behavior, as well as how to have better empathy for neurodivergent people. Organizational leaders and the public need to better understand the benefits of a workforce that includes both neurodivergent and neurotypical employees. Educational resources need to be clear, accurate, include neurodivergent perspectives, and target a variety of audiences (e.g., management, co-workers, service providers, clinicians, the public). While neurodivergent people's lived experiences are foundational to education, those who already carry a substantial burden, especially those with additional lived experiences, should not be asked, nor expected, to educate, and we need to be prepared to recognize and diffuse pressure for them to do so.

Resources and funding

To create educational resources, we need more open conversations across stakeholders that center neurodivergent voices and priorities. We need the federal, industrial, and foundational entities that provide funding and resources to understand, develop, and carry out the steps needed to create the transformations. Consideration of both systems-level and individual-level resource distribution needs to happen when allocating funding and resources. For example, by distributing money and resources to neurodivergent people to meet short-term needs, while supporting research programs to develop, test, and implement long-term solutions. These resources must reach, or at least benefit, neurodivergent people, not just the institutions that hold them.

Champions from the outside

Given how far we remain from the vision despite recent progress, we need champions from outside the neurodiversity community, especially with systems-level power. This includes buy-in from organizational leadership; champions in policy, service systems, research, education, and clinical practice; and allies who advance the priorities of the community. These champions need to use their power directly to advocate for change and indirectly to create opportunities for neurodivergent people.

Champions from the inside

We need to continue advancing neurodivergent people into positions of power within employment systems who are willing and able to do the extra work of ambassadors. This includes more neurodivergent people in politics, organizational leadership, management, employment services, research, and education.

We need to use the internal strengths of our community to provide peer mentorship and peer support for and at work. To do that, we need to recognize both the substantial value of peer support and mentorship, and the extra burden of being a peer support specialist or mentor. We need to act on that recognition by providing essential support and improved financial compensation to peer workers and better understand and address peer burnout.

We need to hear from more voices within our community-including those with overlapping characteristics and those most impacted-to guide external and internal champions and provide more comprehensive community leadership.

Universal access and accessibility

There are likely many neurodivergent workers in skilled positions who do not know they are neurodivergent or do not have a diagnosis. Even for those with a diagnosis, the barriers to requesting accommodations may be too high. Universal access that includes adjustments for neurodivergent processing has the potential to meet the needs of these employees. Employment outcomes might also improve for neurotypical employees who would benefit from the same adjustments (i.e., "the curb cut effect" for communication, sensory processing, and cognition rather than mobility), though access should not be predicated on this.

When implementing universal access, we must attend to nuances and under-served access needs. For example, we need to preserve systems for supported decision-making, provide pathways to communication for all, be responsive to conflicting accommodations, and not be dogmatic about the ways neurodivergence can express itself.

Paradigm shifts

How close we are to our vision depends on whether socio-political climates keep improving for neurodiversity framing and acceptance or keep deteriorating. This moment is unusual in that loud signals are happening in both directions.

We need to continue looking for creative ways to shift paradigms toward acceptance. For example, by seeking systems that interact with neurodiversity and employment and are signaling a stronger push toward improvement, such clinical practice, clinical education, and research. Another is by navigating ideological minefields and echo chambers to collaborate meaningfully with people with conflicting paradigms, incorporating their ideas without compromising the vision. For example, by learning why some employers are uncomfortable hiring neurodivergent employees or asking people who wish to be cured of their neurodivergence for input on employment service interventions.

Lastly, supporting paradigm shifts that value workers more or give workers more power (e.g., employee-owned business models) could help support transformations for neurodivergent workers too.

Infrastructure

We need holistic coordination between statewide and local service systems, systems navigation, education and training systems, and human resource systems. The current silos between systems hurt neurodivergent people in multiple ways, from arguing who pays for someone who is both autistic and bipolar to failing to create an effective or sustainable transition between higher education support systems and employment support systems. Service gaps need filling for all neurodivergent people. Further, we need to analyze and modify employment policies and programs, even those designed to be helpful, for ways they might undermine successful employment of neurodivergent people. For example, those that perpetuate sub-minimum wages, or that make it impossible for skilled people to work without loss of critical services.

Recognizing individual and social complexity

We also need to remove silos between neurodivergence and other demographic factors involved in employment imbalances. Neurodivergent people have the same range of characteristics as neurotypical people, some of which can create better opportunities while others can accumulate barriers over time (e.g., socio-economic status, geographical location, race, documentation status, gender, sexuality, co-occurring disability, religion, etc.). We need to attend to within-group employment imbalances and employee needs while we attend to between-group (i.e., neurodivergent-neurotypical) imbalances. We need to recognize that some neurodivergent individuals are closer to our vision than others and include everyone as we move toward the future.

Objective 2: Create actionable strategies with potential leverage for generating the desired employment outcomes

System Dynamics Model (Causal Loop Diagram) and SSM Step 5

Figure 3 shows the model after synthesizing our structures and verifying them versus literature. CLDs are read by following the arrows around loops. A positive arrow means the variables influence each other in the same way; a negative arrow that they influence each other in opposite ways. Loop labels indicate the essence of the loop, its direction, and a polarity-does it create a Reinforcing loop which exhibits exponential behavior or a Balancing loop which exhibits asymptotic behavior? Variables external to loops but influencing them are shown with a light, dotted line.

Feedback impacting disability pressure

Two external variables-workplace culture gap and socio-political climate regarding neurodivergence-influence disability pressure. Workplace culture gap is the difference between the norms of a workplace and the needs of a neurodivergent worker. For example, in a company with a history of valuing and rewarding neurotypical social behavior, the gap would be large, and disability pressure also large. However, a workplace culture where social activities are optional and whimsy encouraged might have a smaller gap, and disability pressure would also be smaller. These variables are important but difficult to predict or impact, as they are entrenched (workplace culture) or volatile (socio-political climate).

R1: How do I feel?

This reinforcing loop describes the feedback between disability pressure and workplace climate. As a vicious cycle, it describes the situation in which as pressure increases so do negative feelings about work, which increases pressure. As a virtuous cycle, it describes a situation in which as workplace climate improves, pressure goes down, and the employee has an improved perception of their workplace.

B1: Can I sustain?

This balancing loop describes the feedback between disability pressure and adjustments to person-job-organization fit. The greater the pressure, the more adjustment needed; the more adjustments, the lower the pressure. The ability to adjust, however, is regulated by the accessibility capacity of the person, job, and organization, which limits what is possible.

B2: Do I belong?

This balancing loop (long dashes) describes the feedback between person-joborganization fit, workplace climate, and disability pressure. Providing adjustments to improve fit improves climate, which reduces pressure and the number of adjustments needed. It's regulated by both the workplace culture gap and socio-political climate influences on disability pressure, and the accessibility capacity on person-job-organization fit. How much an employee's needs are accommodated impacts workplace climate and the amount of disability pressure they are under at work. A sense of belonging or workplace community is more likely when the employee feels supported by measurable adjustments to meet their needs.

Feedback impacting employee wellbeing

Employee wellbeing is regulated by the external variable of the services gap. This includes not just services related to employment, but other services the employee needs to be well in their life. For example, assistance with instrumental activities like housekeeping and shopping, access to transportation, and therapy. The more unmet needs an employee has, the worse their wellbeing, regardless of how well the job is going. This limits even the best workplace dynamics; however, it also suggests how employment outcomes might be improved through indirect means.

Feedback impacting workplace innovation and productivity B3: Productivity, stability, and innovation.

This balancing loop (blue/lighter shaded) involves the variables of mutually beneficial relations, disability pressure, person-job-organization fit, and employee wellbeing. Improved relations reduce disability pressure and improve job fit, which improves employee wellbeing. Healthy employees are more capable of having healthy, mutually beneficial relations with their supervisors, co-workers, and overall job tasks. The better the relations, the less the disability pressure, the better the person-job-organization fit, and the better the employee health. These are known factors in innovation, productivity, and job stability (see Discussion). The workplace culture, socio-political climate, accessibility capacity, and services gap, however, regulate how far this dynamic can reach.

Feedback impacting overall employment outcomes R² Employment outcomes. This long outside reinforcing loop (bold) around the entire structure involves the variables of disability pressure, workplace climate, employee wellbeing, and mutually beneficial relations. If acting as a vicious cycle leading to poor employment outcomes, increased disability pressure leads to decreased workplace climate and poorer employee wellbeing. Poorer wellbeing leads to less mutually beneficial relations, which increases disability pressure. Eventually, a breaking point comes and the employee cannot sustain. If acting as a virtuous cycle, lower disability pressure leads to a better workplace climate, increased employee wellbeing, and increased mutually beneficial relations, which lowers disability pressure and leads to successful employment.

Leverage Identification, Change Models, Plan for Action -SSM Steps 6-7

We identified the major points of leverage within disability pressure, workplace climate, employee wellbeing, and mutually beneficial relations. Minor points of leverage exist in accessibility and person-job-organization fit. Although policy had been a major part of our GMB session and structures, we ultimately placed it outside of the system due to its capriciousness and surface impact (i.e., it influences the external variables of workplace culture, socio-political climate, accessibility capacity, and services gap but rarely creates deep structural change resilient to policy reversals or environmental changes). The following are the highest impact solutions or plans for action from our ease/benefit analysis, in order of ease.

• Peer mentor and peer support resources, both static and live, including content related to employee wellness, positive psychology, and strategies for managing difficult work situations.

• Accommodation decision and solution aids based on functional needs and sensitive to workplace culture, climate, industry, job characteristics, and employee characteristics, including disclosure risk.

• Supervisor behavior change tools for supporting neurodivergent employees, targeted toward supervisors motivated to change (e.g., adapting the VSST Intervention for veterans 24 ).

• Troubleshooting and solution aids for employee-job-organization fit based on functional need and sensitive to workplace culture, climate, industry, job characteristics, and employee characteristics including disclosure risk.

• Work pragmatics translation tools that assist with interpretation, scripting, and communicating with neurotypical supervisors, co-workers, or clients and is sensitive to the workplace culture, climate, and nature of the industry and job, and a reverse translator for neurotypicals to understand neurodivergent employees.

Discussion

Integration, Contributions, and Transferability

Taking a participatory action research and systems thinking approach to the problem of employment imbalances for skilled neurodivergent people resulted in a focus on transforming employee wellbeing, workplace environments and climates, disability pressure, and enhancing workplace innovation rather than specific skills building or remediation of disability characteristics. Doing this will take education, resources and funding, champions from the outside, champions from the inside, universal access and accessibility, paradigm shifts, infrastructure, and recognition of individual and social complexity. Actionable courses of intervention include increased focus on interactive and passive peer support and mentoring that targets the areas of transformation; leveraging emerging technology to support accommodations, job fit, and workplace communication including in situations where disability disclosure is not feasible; and supporting supervisor behavior change based on authentic understanding neurodivergence at work rather than through policy demands. Neurodivergent people must be involved in developing these solutions from a holistic perspective. This includes activities surrounding solution building, as defining the problem, designing the solution, and determining evaluation and assessment criteria.

The areas of transformation in our root definitions map to known multidimensional constructs in the literature. From the organizational literature, employee wellbeing includes health and safety both within and outside the workplace 25 ; the dimensions proposed by Attipoe 26 in particular, reflect our discussions of the interconnected facets of physical health, mental health, emotional health, job satisfaction, work/life balance, and overall quality of life.

Workplace climate, the perception of the work environment both individually and collectively, can be conceived as an outward expression of workplace culture, the ingrained norms of the workplace 27 . While workplace climate theory is still unfolding, the climate constructs of leader support and facilitation, role stress and harmony, job challenge and autonomy, and workgroup cooperation, warmth, and friendliness described in James, Choi, Ko, McNeil, Minton, Wright, Kim 28 and those in the workplace climate scale developed by Patterson, West, Shackleton, Dawson, Lawthom, Maitlis, Robinson, Wallace 29 map well to our discussions. While culture takes a long time to change because of its roots in historical norms, climate is more amenable to short-term intervention and, over time, can shift the paradigms of culture by reinforcing new norms.

The disability pressure construct is not directly present in the literature, but it maps in part to the model proposed by Meyer 30 . Meyer's model shows how general stressors, unfair treatment based on personal characteristics, and the expectation of that unfair treatment can lead to poor health outcomes. Simultaneously, it shows that sense of community and identity related to those same characteristics is protective at both individual and community levels, which can lead to good health outcomes. The model is 1) unidirectional rather showing feedback; and 2) not specific to disability. We are currently developing a feedback model of disability pressure.

Critically, employee wellbeing, workplace climate, person-job-organization fit, and heterogeneity of workplaces are all known to influence productivity and innovation. 26, 27, [31] [32] [33] That these known constructs and relationships emerged organically from our workshops should, on one hand, be no surprise, as neurodivergent people are engaging in the same occupational systems as the general population. However, the development of interventions from this literature to improve employment outcomes for neurodivergent people is sparse, and interventions in companies that target these constructs are not improving overall outcomes for neurodivergent employees. This suggests that there is a large, unexplored literature to draw from in intervention development, while known interventions from this literature likely need adaptation to be effective for neurodivergent employees.

Notably, our work did not define the problem or its solutions in terms of the types of individuallevel behaviors most often targeted by disability employment interventions and policies; for example jobseekers not behaving typically enough to pass a job interview, or businesses not having sufficient financial incentive to hire difficult employees. Instead, we defined the problem in terms of breakdowns in the interconnected dimensions that drive broader employment outcomes for employees, businesses, and society. For example, inadequate support for neurodivergent employee wellness, or supervisors unaware of how to create the kinds of relationships with their neurodivergent employees that lead to increased innovation and productivity. This suggests a different set of actions for solution.

While neurodivergent people are increasingly included in employment research, to our knowledge no research to date has explicitly grounded itself in neurodivergent epistemology and defined the problems in employment outcomes fresh from that center, rather than by building on the existing work of neurotypical researchers. Our findings have more in common with organizational interventions than with disability interventions, which both provides evidence of transferability of our ideas and a suggestion that taking an organizational approach rather than a medicalized approach could open new avenues for effective intervention. Most importantly, our work shows the importance of centering the field in both the lived experience and professional expertise of neurodivergent people.

Peer support, peer mentoring, and participation in peer communities are both protective factors and effective interventions. The substance use and early psychosis literature, in particular, has shown robust connections between peer engagement and improved outcomes 34 , and peer interventions are starting to be used within occupations that have a strong sense of community, like first responders 35 . Our work identified peer engagement as a key point of leverage for improving employment outcomes. Despite this, peer support is less valued than other therapies, as evident by extreme pay imbalances (a peer support specialist in 2025 makes on average $20 per hour in Oregon, while an occupational therapist makes an average of $50).

Acknowledging the equivalence of academic education and lived experience, when combined with insight into how that experience interacts with surrounding systems, could help increase the perceived value of peers clinical practice.

Taking a systems thinking approach enabled a high-level and long-term view of how individual, organizational, and environmental components influence overall employment outcomes.

Limitations

We conducted this research in the U.S. when the environment surrounding disability and employment is out of equilibrium, including an abrupt erosion of disability rights protections and policies and unusual levels of economic uncertainty. However, we feel our findings and recommendations would have applied prior to the current disequilibrium. The U.S. focus limits the transferability of findings to other countries. While our expert group included multiple kinds of neurodivergence and personal characteristics, we did not have a full range of expertise; future work should also engage people with lived experience of Tourette's, OCD, and other common forms of neurodivergence, as well as with people who experience a broader range of demographic characteristics (e.g., age, geographic location, etc.).

Recommendations

 Explore adapting evidence-based interventions that target employee wellbeing, workplace climate, and employer/employee relationships for neurodivergent workers.  Conduct efficacy research on tools the neurodiversity community has created for itself (e.g., Goblin Tools for pragmatics translation, https://goblin.tools)  Explore disability pressure and the role it plays in employment outcomes and wellbeing.  Involve people with lived experience in defining intervention outcomes and developing interventions.  Encourage interdisciplinary collaborations with organizational psychologists and others with expertise in business dynamics outside of medical and disability fields.

 Invest in business development to help entrepreneurs incorporate neurodivergent talent into their workforce from the start, as well as to support neurodivergent entrepreneurs who can model best-practices in neurodiversity-friendly business development.  Find ways emerging technology can support solutions that are not tied to specific services, industries, or diagnoses; in particular, artificial intelligence may help manage heterogeneity in ways that were previously not possible.  Support peer support specialists and peer strategies to lessen employee burden, reduce disability pressure, and promote employee wellbeing.  Explore structural solutions that are not reliant on policy.  Research why existing programs aren't moving outcomes, including assessments of scalability and focus (e.g., how can small, siloed programs that hit many of our recommendations such as Aspiritech (https://aspiritech.org) scale to reach a larger neurodivergent population and outside of technology).

Conclusion

To reduce imbalances for neurodivergent people in skilled employment, we recommend taking a systemic and disability-led approach to defining the problem and locating its many better solutions. This project takes a step back from the current chaos to use systematic inquiry designed for situations of high complexity to define the problems that most urgently need addressing, determine what they might look like if resolved, understand the current state of the system that gives rise to them, identify the leverage within the system that could effectively generate change, and develop a holistic, data-informed, plan for action. Critically, this project leverages the native innovation and expertise of the neurodiversity community itself to point toward the north star of, "We love our jobs!"
