To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 7 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

INTRODUCTION

Inhibitory control is a fundamental component of executive functioning that enables individuals to suppress dominant or prepotent responses in favour of contextually appropriate alternatives. Although inhibitory control is often studied as overt motor responses, it also operates on internally generated representations, suppressing memories, thoughts, and verbal associations (Diamond, 2013). One such process is semantic inhibition, which involves inhibiting highly activated verbal or conceptual representations to allow the selection of contextually appropriate alternatives (Burgess & Shallice, 1996; Cervera-Crespo & González-Alvarez, 2017). Impairments in semantic inhibition have been reported across neuropsychiatric conditions characterized by impaired cognitive control (Wang et al., 2013). Consequently, understanding the neural mechanisms supporting semantic inhibition may provide valuable insights into forms of inhibitory dysfunction that extend beyond the motor domain.

Inhibitory dysfunction has long been implicated in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) where it may contribute to the characteristic persistence of intrusive thoughts and repetitive behaviours (Norman et al., 2019). Most evidence, however, comes from motor paradigms, such as the Stop-Signal and Go-NoGo tasks, which predominantly capture impairments in response inhibition, that have been closely linked to compulsive tendencies (Bannon et al., 2002; Norman et al., 2019). Neuroimaging studies using these paradigms have consistently implicated dysfunction within cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits in OCD (Pauls et al., 2014; Tomiyama et al., 2022; van Velzen et al., 2014). Altered recruitment of the presupplementary motor area (preSMA) during inhibitory control has been reported in patients with OCD and, importantly, in their unaffected first-degree relatives (de Wit et al., 2012). These observations suggest that abnormal preSMA function may reflect a candidate neural marker of vulnerability to obsessive-compulsive symptomatology rather than a consequence of the clinical disorder itself.

The predominance of motor paradigms leaves an important question unresolved. Typically, the symptoms of OCD include not only repetitive actions but also persistent thoughts, images, doubts, and urges. Studying the suppression of verbal and conceptual representations offers a means of testing whether inhibitory alterations associated with obsessive-compulsive tendencies extend beyond the motor domain.

Consequently, the Hayling Sentence Completion Task (HSCT) is particularly relevant for assessing semantic inhibition in OCD. The task contrasts completion of a sentence with its expected ending with suppression of that dominant completion in favour of an unrelated response. Difficulty in suppressing a strongly activated sentence completion word may be related to the difficulty to disengage from habitual or intrusive cognitive content. Behavioural studies have reported altered Hayling-task performance in OCD (Doolub et al., 2023; Van der Linden et al., 2005), and electrophysiological evidence has also linked semantic inhibition to obsessive-compulsive tendencies (Iyoki & Kawasaki, 2019). However, the neural substrates of semantic inhibition in relation to OCD symptomatology remain poorly characterized.

OCD is not an all-or-none diagnostic category. Instead, obsessions and compulsive tendencies vary along a continuum in the general population (Abramovitch et al., 2013). Moreover, several of its core features, such as compulsive behaviors and rumination, are increasingly conceptualized as transdiagnostic processes that cut across multiple psychiatric disorders (Fullana et al., 2010; Gillan et al., 2016). Therefore, complementary to the study of diagnosed OCD, a dimensional approach can be an effective research strategy by examining whether individuals differing in obsessive-compulsive traits also vary inhibitory processing. Studying participants with lower and elevated trait levels also reduces confounding by medication, illness duration, and previous treatment. This approach is particularly informative when the aim is to identify candidate mechanisms of vulnerability rather than consequences of established disorder.

Within the supplementary motor complex, the preSMA and SMA proper are anatomically adjacent but differ in their connectivity and functional organization. The preSMA occupies a more rostral position and has been particularly implicated in higher-order aspects of action control, including response selection and the inhibition of prepotent responses, whereas SMA proper is more closely associated with motor-related processes (Nachev et al., 2008). This functional distinction is paralleled by differences in connectivity, with the preSMA showing prominent connections with prefrontal and anterior cingulate regions, whereas SMA proper is more strongly associated with posterior parietal and motor circuitry (Lioumis et al., 2025). These differences make the distinction between preSMA and SMA proper particularly relevant when examining forms of inhibitory control that extend beyond the motor domain.

The present study used an fMRI adaptation of the HSCT to investigate the neural correlates of semantic inhibition in individuals with elevated obsessive-compulsive traits. Complementary whole-brain and anatomically defined region-of-interest analyses in the preSMA and adjacent SMA proper were performed. We expected inhibition trials to produce greater behavioral cost and stronger preSMA recruitment than initiation trials. We further examined whether inhibition-related modulation in the preSMA and SMA proper differed between lower and elevated obsessive-compulsive traits. In this way, the study tested whether neural alterations associated with obsessive-compulsive tendencies extend from motor inhibition to the control of prepotent semantic representations.

METHODS

Participants

Recruitment for the fMRI experiment started with an online screening protocol implemented via Google Forms, which was responded to by 963 participants. All of them were native Spanish speakers, undergraduate students from the University of La Laguna (Spain). The initial screening battery included the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) (Sanz Fernández et al., 2003), the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) (Lobo et al., 2002), and the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) (Vega-Dienstmaier et al., 2002) to assess depressive symptoms, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive traits, respectively. The BDI-II and HAM-A were used to exclude participants with elevated depressive or anxiety symptoms, respectively. Participants were selected on the basis of Y-BOCS scores to obtain groups differing markedly in obsessive-compulsive symptom severity. Individuals scoring between 8 and 10 (mild range) were selected for the control group, whereas those scoring between 22 and 28 were considered for the high-OCD-trait group. This selection was intended to provide groups with contrasting levels of obsessive-compulsive traits rather than to establish clinical diagnostic categories. Participants were excluded if they reported any psychiatric diagnosis within the past two years or current use of psychotropic medication. Additional exclusion criteria included contraindications for MRI scanning, such as the presence of orthodontic appliances, metallic implants that could not be safely removed, or severe claustrophobia. Only participants who were both native Spanish speakers and right-handed were included.

The final sample comprised 40 participants, with 20 individuals in the high-OCD-trait group and 20 in the control group. The control group comprised 10 men and 10 women, whereas the high-OCD-trait group comprised 6 men and 14 women. The mean age in the high-OCDtrait group was M = 21.45 years (SD = 6.14), and in the control group M = 22.65 years (SD = 4.76). No significant differences were found between groups in age (Welch's t(35.77) = 0.69, p = .494) or sex distribution (Fisher's exact test, p = .333).

Prior to participation, all individuals provided written informed consent in accordance with the procedures established by the Servicio de Resonancia Magnética para Investigaciones Biomédicas at the University of La Laguna (https://www.ull.es/servicios/segai/normas-de-uso-investigaciones-biomedicas/).

The experimental study was approved by the local Ethics Committee of the University of La Laguna (registry number: CEIBA2023-3250), and all procedures complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS)

The Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) is widely regarded as the gold standard for assessing the presence and severity of obsessive-compulsive symptoms in clinical and research contexts (Goodman, Price, Rasmussen, Mazure, Delgado, et al., 1989; Goodman, Price, Rasmussen, Mazure, Fleischmann, et al., 1989). The scale consists of 10 items, grouped into two subscales (obsessions and compulsions) each comprising five items that evaluate time spent, interference, distress, resistance, and control over symptoms.

Responses are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 4 (extreme symptoms), yielding a total score from 0 to 40, where higher scores indicate greater severity of OCD symptomatology. Interpretation of Y-BOCS scores typically follows established cutoffs: scores of 0-7 indicate subclinical symptoms, 8-15 mild symptoms, 16-23 moderate symptoms, 24-31 severe symptoms, and 32-40 extreme symptoms. A Spanish-language version of the Y-BOCS has demonstrated good reliability and validity for the assessment of obsessive-compulsive symptom severity (Vega-Dienstmaier et al., 2002).

Hayling Sentence Completion Task

A modified version of the Hayling Sentence Completion Task was implemented to assess semantic inhibitory control during magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). The task comprised 80 unique incomplete sentences presented auditorily. Each sentence was presented twice, once in each of the two response conditions, yielding 160 total trials. A colored frame indicated whether participants should select a contextually appropriate completion (green; initiation) or inhibit that response and select an unrelated word (red; inhibition). Three candidate completions were presented visually. The complete set of sentence frames and response alternatives is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The 80 incomplete sentences were selected from a standardized set of 142 Spanish HSCT sentences (Jan et al., 2024). To determine the candidate completions, an independent normative study was performed with 30 participants, who did not take part in the fMRI experiment. These participants were presented with each incomplete sentence and asked to provide the most appropriate completion. Contexts for which the same completion was produced by over 96% of participants (e.g., words with a cloze probability of 1 or near to 1), were retained to serve as the "correct" response. For each sentence frame, two additional options were created: an "unrelated" word with no semantic or phonological association with the correct completion, and a word semantically or phonologically "related" to it. The related option was included to increase the difficulty of the choice in semantic inhibition trials. All three options were grammatically compatible with the sentence context, and matched for word class, gender and number.

The options were selected to be comparable in word length, lexical frequency, familiarity and imageability according to norms from the Spanish EsPal database (Duchon et al., 2013), as shown in Table 1. Design and Procedure

The fMRI experiment employed an event-related design comprising 80 Initiation and 80 Inhibition trials. The 160 trials were presented in two independently randomized sets of 80 trials, each containing 40 trials per condition. The positions of the correct, related, and unrelated response options were randomized across trials. Each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 1 s, followed by the auditory presentation of an incomplete sentence. Each auditory file lasted 3.5 s and contained a variable period of leading silence so that the spoken sentences ended at approximately the same time across items.

Following the auditory sentence offset, a blank screen was displayed for a jittered interval of 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 s prior to the response cue. The cue -a red or green frame indicating the response condition-was presented first, followed 500 ms later by the three response options. These options remained visible until the participant responded or until the end of the trial. Participants selected an option using an MRI-compatible three-button response pad operated with the index, middle, or ring finger. They were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible and not to attempt to correct errors. After a response, the screen remained blank for the remainder of the trial. Trial onsets were spaced 10 s apart, yielding a total task duration of 26 min 40 s (see Figure 1). The experiment was programmed in Presentation (Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc.; https://www.neurobs.com/). Participants listened to each incomplete sentence via MRIcompatible headphones, and the three completion options were displayed using the VisuaStim Digital System. Prior to scanning, all participants completed a training session in a mock MRI environment to ensure full comprehension of the task requirements and to minimize anxiety or discomfort during the actual experiment.

MRI data acquisition

Functional images were acquired on a 3 T GE SIGNA Premier MR scanner (GE Healthcare) using a 48-channel head coil and a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 21.6 ms, flip angle = 90°, matrix size = 64 × 64, 37 slices, 4 × 4 mm² in-plane resolution, slice thickness = 4 mm, no interslice gap). Slices were acquired in an axial orientation aligned with the anterior commissure-posterior commissure (AC-PC) line and provided whole-brain coverage. High-resolution anatomical images were acquired using a 3D T1-weighted MP-RAGE sequence (TR = 2413.3 ms, TE = 1.972 ms, TI = 1000 ms, flip angle = 8°, matrix size = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm).

Image preprocessing and analysis

Image preprocessing: Functional MRI data were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12; Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, London, UK) implemented in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Functional images were first corrected for head motion using rigid-body realignment with unwarping to reduce susceptibility-by-movement interactions. Slice-timing correction was then applied using an interleaved ascending acquisition order (odd slices first), with slice 19 as the reference slice, followed by coregistration of the anatomical T1-weighted image to the mean functional image. The deformation field obtained from segmentation of the coregistered T1-weighted image was then used to normalize the functional images to MNI space, resampled to 2 × 2 × 2 mm³ isotropic voxels. Finally, the normalized functional images were spatially smoothed using a 10 × 10 × 10 mm³ FWHM Gaussian kernel. Normalization quality was visually inspected for all participants before statistical analysis.

First-level statistical analysis: Individual subject analyses were performed within the framework of the General Linear Model (GLM). Four regressors of interest were defined by crossing Task (Initiation vs. Inhibition) and Sentence type (Sensory vs. Action). Each trial was modelled as an event (duration = 0 s) and convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF). Six head-motion parameters obtained during realignment were included as nuisance regressors. Low-frequency signal drifts were removed using a high-pass filter with a cutoff period of 128 s, and temporal autocorrelations were modelled using a firstorder autoregressive [AR(1)] model.

Because the primary aim of the present study was to investigate semantic inhibition, the main task contrast collapsed across sentence type and compared Inhibition with Initiation (Inhibition > Initiation).

Second-level statistical analysis: First-level contrast images were entered into a second-level random-effects 2 × 2 factorial model implemented in SPM12. The model included Condition (Initiation vs. Inhibition) as a within-subject factor and Group (Control vs. High-OCD-trait group) as a between-subject factor, allowing the assessment of the main effect of Condition, the main effect of Group, and the Group × Condition interaction. Whole-brain statistical maps were thresholded at p < .001 (uncorrected) with a minimum cluster extent of 10 contiguous voxels.

Region-of-interest (ROI) analysis: Because the exploratory whole-brain interaction peaked in the left preSMA region, anatomically defined ROI analyses were subsequently performed to further characterize the involvement of the left preSMA and adjacent SMA proper. Anatomical ROIs corresponding to the left preSMA and left SMA proper were obtained from the JuBrain Anatomy Toolbox, which provides probabilistic cytoarchitectonic maps allowing these adjacent regions to be anatomically distinguished (Ruan et al., 2018). ROI analyses were performed using MarsBaR (Brett et al., 2002), based on the estimated second-level SPM model and the same contrasts defined for the whole-brain analysis. Contrast estimates were extracted for each anatomical ROI. Statistical inference was based on directional (one-tailed) t contrasts, with p values corrected for multiple comparisons across the two ROIs using the Šidák procedure. Mean contrast estimates extracted from the ROIs were used for graphical representation of the results.

RESULTS

Behavioral results

Reaction times were analysed using a 2×2 mixed ANOVA with Condition (Initiation vs. inhibition) as the within-participant factor and Group (Control vs. High-OCD-trait group) as the between-subject factor. Responses were slower during inhibition than initiation (F(1,38) = 211.18, p < .001, η²p = .848). Neither the main effect of Group (F(1,38) = 1.95, p = .171, η²p = .049) nor the Group × Condition interaction (F(1,38) = 0.002, p = .965, η²p < .001) reached statistical significance.

A similar pattern was observed for error rates. Participants committed more errors during inhibition than initiation: F(1,38) = 80.49, p < .001, η²p = .679. The main effect of Group (F(1,38) = 0.11, p = .745, η²p = .003) and the Group × Condition interaction (F(1,38) = 0.13, p = .721, η²p = .003) were not significant.

Thus, inhibition increased both response times and errors, with no detected Group differences in any measure.

Whole Brain Analysis

Whole-brain analyses first examined the main effect of semantic inhibition (Inhibition > Initiation) across all participants. This contrast revealed extensive activation involving medial frontal, frontoparietal, occipital, cerebellar, and subcortical regions, which survived wholebrain FDR correction (FDR-corrected p < .05, Figure 2, red clusters). The exploratory whole-brain Group × Condition interaction did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. ROI Analysis

To further characterize the anatomical distribution of the whole-brain findings, region-ofinterest analyses were performed in the left preSMA and SMA proper (Figure 3A). Consistent with the exploratory whole-brain findings, the Group × Condition interaction was significant in the left preSMA (t(76) = 2.56, pFWE = .012), indicating differential task-related modulation between groups (Figure 3B1). In contrast, the interaction in the adjacent SMA proper did not survive FWE correction (t(76) = 1.74, pFWE = .084; Figure 3B2). As illustrated in Figure 3B1, the interaction in the left preSMA reflected a larger task-related increase in activation during semantic inhibition in the control group than in participants with elevated obsessivecompulsive traits. The corresponding effect sizes were Cohen's d = 0.76 for the preSMA and d = 0.54 for the SMA proper (Figure 3C).

DISCUSSION

The present study examined the neural correlates of semantic inhibition in individuals with elevated obsessive-compulsive traits using the Hayling Sentence Completion Task during fMRI. Although behavioural performance was comparable between groups, neuroimaging analyses revealed differences in task-related modulation of activity in the left presupplementary motor area (preSMA). In the whole-brain analysis across participants the inhibition > initiation contrast identified the strongest peak of activation in this region. In the same analysis a differential peak modulation between high-OCD-trait and control participants was also observed in a cluster in the posterior preSMA region. To explore this group effect in more detail, anatomically defined regions of interest (ROIs) were submitted to new analyses showing a significant Group × Condition interaction after familywise error correction (FWE) for multiple comparisons in the preSMA, although the interaction in the adjacent SMA proper did not survive FWE correction. Together, these findings suggest that elevated obsessivecompulsive traits are associated with altered preSMA recruitment during semantic inhibition, even in the absence of measurable behavioural differences.

The involvement of the preSMA is consistent with prior evidence that this region plays a role in higher-order inhibitory control, including action control, response selection and the suppression of prepotent responses, whereas the adjacent SMA proper is more closely associated with motor-related processes (Nachev et al., 2008). The present findings extend this framework to a task requiring participants to override strongly activated semantic representations. Importantly, the Group × Condition interaction remained significant after FWE correction in the anatomically defined preSMA ROI, whereas the corresponding effect in the adjacent SMA proper did not. This pattern is consistent with the established functional differentiation within the supplementary motor complex and supports a role for the preSMA in semantic inhibitory processing.

At first glance, the reduced inhibition-related modulation of the preSMA in participants with elevated obsessive-compulsive traits observed in this study contrasts with the increased activation during motor response inhibition reported in patients with OCD and their unaffected first-degree relatives (de Wit et al., 2012). One possible explanation may lie in the task demands. Previous neuroimaging studies of OCD have frequently employed Stop-Signal and Go/No-Go tasks, which require participants to suppress prepotent or ongoing motor responses. In contrast, the present task is primarily a cognitive inhibition task, requiring the suppression of a contextually dominant semantic response while maintaining the requirement to press a button. In principle, the preSMA activation in the inhibition > initiation contrast could be associated not only with semantic suppression, but also with differences in response selection, conflict and task difficulty. However, because both initiation and inhibition trials involved the same response device and response mappings, the contrast reduces the role of differences in motor demands. Thus, the apparently divergent patterns of preSMA recruitment may reflect differences in the inhibitory processes engaged by these paradigms.

These differences across inhibitory paradigms may also reflect the functional organization of the preSMA-SMA complex. Whereas preSMA territories show prominent connections with prefrontal regions, SMA territories have more prominent connections with motor and posterior parietal circuitry (Nachev et al., 2008; Lioumis et al., 2025). Such anatomical organization provides a plausible framework for understanding how different forms of inhibitory control may differentially recruit medial frontal territories. From this perspective, differences across OCD studies may reflect, at least in part, the specific inhibitory processes recruited by different paradigms rather than contradictory patterns of preSMA dysfunction. However, the present study did not directly compare semantic and motor inhibition or assess connectivity within these circuits. The proposed relationship between task demands and pre-SMA recruitment therefore remains a hypothesis for future investigation.

Beyond the preSMA, the exploratory whole-brain interaction also identified a cluster in the anterior cingulate cortex. This finding is relevant to a recent meta-analysis (Uhre et al., 2022), which identified convergent abnormalities in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex when analyses were restricted to cognitive inhibition tasks, but found no convergent regional abnormalities across different inhibitory paradigms considered together. Although the cognitive inhibition tasks in the meta-analysis differ from semantic inhibition, these findings suggest that the neural differences associated with obsessive-compulsive symptomatology may depend on the specific inhibitory processes examined. The present anterior cingulate finding, however, did not survive whole-brain correction and should be treated as preliminary.

Additional clusters were observed in the midbrain and cerebellum. According to the AAL3 atlas, the midbrain cluster partially overlapped the dorsal raphe region. This observation is noteworthy given evidence of serotonergic alterations in OCD, including reduced serotonin transporter availability in the midbrain and brainstem and altered functional connectivity of the raphe nucleus (Kim et al., 2019; Pastre et al., 2025). However, atlas overlap and the limited anatomical resolution of conventional fMRI are insufficient to identify the involvement of small brainstem nuclei reliably, and BOLD activity does not provide a direct measure of serotonergic function. Several cerebellar clusters were also observed (e.g., lobule VI and Crus I), consistent with previous evidence of altered cerebellar recruitment during inhibitory control in OCD (Norman et al., 2019).

The absence of behavioural differences requires careful consideration. Both groups showed the expected increase in reaction times and error rate during semantic inhibition, but neither measure showed a significant Group × Condition interaction. Thus, the neural differences in preSMA recruitment were not accompanied by a corresponding difference in behavioural performance. Similar dissociations between behavioural performance and neural measures have been reported in previous neuroimaging studies of inhibitory control in OCD (Hampshire et al., 2020; Lillevik Thorsen et al., 2020). In the present study, this dissociation suggests that similar observed performance can accompany different patterns of pre-SMA recruitment. It does not, however, establish behavioural equivalence or determine whether the neural difference reflects compensation, altered processing efficiency or a different task strategy.

Several limitations qualify the conclusions in this study. As the sample was selected on the basis of elevated obsessive-compulsive traits obtained in self-report questionnaires rather than clinical OCD diagnosis, the findings cannot be directly generalized to patients with OCD. However, studying individuals not taking psychiatric medication reduces a potential source of confounding commonly encountered in clinical OCD research. The sample size may also have limited sensitivity to behavioural differences and smaller neural effects. Additionally, the groups were not fully balanced by sex. Although the ROIs were anatomically defined, their choice after an exploratory whole-brain analysis warrants caution in treating the ROI result as independent confirmation. Replication in a larger sample, with prospectively specified ROI analyses and direct comparisons of semantic and motor inhibition within the same participants, would help establish the robustness of the effects, and whether differences in preSMA recruitment depend on the specific form of inhibitory control being examined.

CONCLUSION

The present study showed altered task-related modulation of the left preSMA during semantic inhibition in individuals with elevated obsessive-compulsive traits, whereas the corresponding Group × Condition interaction in the adjacent SMA proper did not reach statistical significance. Despite comparable behavioural performance between groups, these findings highlight the relevance of the preSMA to semantic inhibitory processing associated with obsessivecompulsive traits. More broadly, they support the view that inhibitory control should not be considered a unitary process when investigating obsessive-compulsive symptomatology. Different inhibitory paradigms may therefore provide complementary information about inhibitory control in relation to obsessive-compulsive symptomatology.
