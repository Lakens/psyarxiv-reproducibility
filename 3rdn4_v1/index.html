In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Full text

Abstract

Aging is one of the key drivers of cognitive decline and slowing neurogenesis. It is a major risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases. In the process of aging, neural stem cells stop proliferating at a high level, and the niche surrounding them begins to undergo extensive change.

This large shift consists of vascular changes, lower growth factor signaling, higher stiffness and tension, and reduced cell-to-cell interactions. In research to date, in vitro models have not recreated a full neural stem cell niche while taking into account all of the previously listed factors. In this proposal, a bioengineered, 3D niche specifically designed to recreate the environment of a young neurogenic niche is constructed. The niche will have the stiffness of its hydrogel extracellular matrix tuned, growth factors added and co-culturing of glial cells with neural stem cells. The neural stem cells, from aged mice and from young mice, will be sowed into the target niche and two controls, which will be developed into organoid systems.

Measurements of two neuronal proteins in these organoids will demonstrate how successful the bioengineered niche will be at restoring neurogenesis to aged neural stem cells. This work could show how much of age-related cognitive decline is driven by the surrounding niche rather than intrinsic issues with the cells. Additionally, a successful organoid would also be exceptionally useful for disease modeling and for modeling strategies to restore neurogenesis in the aging brain.

1. Introduction/Background

Aging is a large risk factor for cognitive and neurodegenerative diseases. Age-associated reductions in neural stem cell (NSC) efficacy often lead to severe cognitive decline and a reduction in brain function (Culig et. al, 2022). Neurogenesis, the process by which new neurons are formed in the brain, is an effective way of combatting cognitive decline. However, age is a factor that reduces neurogenesis to the point where it can no longer combat cognitive decline (Kempermann, 2015). Adult NSCs are the last hope for continued high levels of neural cell proliferation, but aging reduces that as well. NSCs are critical for neurogenesis, and their activity determines how much neurogenesis occurs (Ghosh, 19). One important factor for the reduction in proliferation is the increase in adult NSC quiescence. Most adult NSCs stay in this state and only re-enter the cell cycle after receiving certain growth factors increasing cell proliferation.

However, aging reduces the amount of these factors produced, lowering overall neurogenic output (Kempermann, 2015). Along with this decrease, the morphology of the NSC changes due to aging (Licht et. al, 2020). NSCs typically assume a radial glia shape, and extend to touch parts of their niche such as nearby blood vessels. With aging, these connections are severed and thus regenerative capacity is lowered.

While the NSCs themselves change, the niche around the NSCs also undergoes significant changes during aging that result in declining neurogenesis and NSC efficacy. Inflammatory signaling is promoted, certain growth factors are reduced, the vascular environment is altered, and interactions with neighboring glial cells are negatively affected (Kempermann, 2015). The subventricular zone (SVZ) and hippocampal niche, where NSCs primarily reside, contain many interacting cell types, such as NSCs and glial cells (Yeo et. al, 2023). Aging remodels the niche to disrupt these interactions. More specifically, vascular properties such as capillary density and blood flow volume are impacted. The expression of many growth factors is also reduced and the stiffness of the ECM of the niche increases. These changes alter mechanosensitive signals that help with neuronal differentiation (Mosher and Schaffer, 2019). However, a niche that promotes the right chemical signals while maintaining the mechanical properties of a young and healthy niche could be successful in determining what an adult NSC can do (Yeo et. al, 2023).

Measures that have been taken to combat the inevitable decline in neurogenesis that shows promise are exercise and diet maintenance (Culig et. al, 2022). While adult neurogenesis decreases with age, it can increase with consistent physical and cognitive activity (Kepperman, 2023). However, this in vivo approach does not target NSCs directly and thus there is no way of knowing how it impacts cell proliferation. An in vitro approach will provide researchers with a more quantifiable output.

Regulating the niche is critical for any effective approach to combatting neurodegeneration.

Previous in vitro approaches have reproduced selective features of the niche, but no individual model directly replicates all of the biochemical, cellular, and mechanical properties of the in vivo niche in one system (Matta and Gonzalez, 2019). The creation of such a system would be a breakthrough in the battle to restore aged NSCs.

When bioengineering a target niche containing all of these components, the extracellular matrix (ECM) is critical for success (Kazanis and Constant, 2015). The ECM provides critical biochemical signals and growth factors to the cells, while also maintaining certain mechanical properties that encourage cell proliferation. By creating an ECM that has all of the accurate features, and populating it with NSCs, the desired outcome should be an increase in neurogenesis. In order for this to occur, the ECM needs to be tuned to the correct stiffness. Adult NSCs show the greatest rate of differentiation on softer substrates, leading to higher amounts of proliferation (Leipzig and Shoichet, 2009). Thus, while bioengineering a niche, the softer the environment, the more neurogenesis.

Hydrogels function as viable biomaterial-based replacements for ECMs. They are tunable to soft levels of stiffness, similar to that of soft brain tissue. Softer gels show more long-term growth and differentiation. (Liang et. al, 2021). Then, the hydrogel could perform optimally if modified with certain growth factors and signaling molecules.

The system will eventually be developed into an organoid for further study. Brain organoids are viable models which are capable of showing selected aspects of human brain function (Koo et. al, 2019). In certain instances, cerebral organoids have been shown to form rosette-like structures useful in modeling epithelial tissue and ventricular structures (Kim and Chang, 2023).

The current largest benefit that organoids boast is that they are able to self-organize into 3D tissue. In the case of the bioengineered niche, it would include astrocytes, glial cells, neurons, and NSCs. Organoids have also been critical to the modeling of the brain and other diseases. On that note, they have also modeled the effects a drug would have on a patient (Kim and Chang, 2023). There have been several cases of brain organoid use that speak to their high potential for research. One common example is the modeling of genetic microcephaly, where researchers used patient-derived iPSCs to grow an organoid (Lancaster et. al, 2013). They found early neuronal differentiation that helped them explain reduced brain growth.

While organoids have been very effective in modeling disease and observing outcomes, it is important to note their limitations. The main limitation is the lack of overall circuitry. Organoids are able to start some neuronal network activity, but they currently lack the ability to recreate the actual behavioral environment of the brain and the nerve wiring that connects the brain to the rest of the body (Urrestizala-Arezana et. al, 2024).

There are many ways of quantifying neuronal differentiation, though there are not any ways of directly quantifying neurogenesis. One of the closest options is measurement of the protein NCAM (Bonfanti, 2006). NCAM is a marker for structural plasticity and neurogenesis across all mammals. Total NCAM increases with a cell culture that has greater neuronal development or neuronal survival. PSA-NCAM, which is NCAM modified with polysialic acid, is a better measure of neurogenesis because it measures immature neurons (Bonfanti, 2006). Measuring this protein with an ELISA will be a critical step for testing the success of the bioengineered niche. Doublecortin (DCX), a strong marker for neurogenesis expressed in neural progenitor cells and neuroblasts, will also be measured using immunocytochemistry (Kuhn et. al, 2016). The expression of DCX falls drastically when the population of mature neurons rises. Expression of DCX has also been shown to increase with exercise, demonstrating its validity as a proxy measure when direct measures of quantifying neuronal differentiation aren't available. It is commonly found in the hippocampus.

This study proposes to create a 3D, bioengineered niche that will replicate features of a healthy brain environment. The proposed model will combine a soft, hydrogel ECM, proneurogenic growth factors and the co-culture of glial cells with NSCs. The NSCs will be taken from aged mice and cultured in this system. They will be compared to aged and young NSCs grown in control conditions. The hypothesis is that this bioengineered niche will help partially restore neurogenesis in aged NSCs, and that aged NSCs will show positive results in this environment when compared to the aged niche. This will be evaluated through the measurement of the neuronal proteins PSA-NCAM and DCX.

2. Experimental Design

To determine whether a bioengineered NSC niche can restore or increase neurogenesis, aged and young NSCs cultured in two different settings with differently-aged ECMs will be compared with the outcomes of the NSCs sowed into a bioengineered niche.

Due to the nature of this experiment, obtaining neural stem cells directly from organisms rather than reprogramming them from iPSCs will be important. This is to prevent the loss of certain biomarkers associated with aging and to preserve the properties of NSCs found in aged dentate gyri (DG) (Navarro, Yeo and Brunet, 2021). NSCs will be collected from both aged mice (18-24 months) and young mice (2-3 months). These are collected to be compared in the young, aged, and engineered niche. The stem cells will be cultured with glial cells, as studies have proven that within days of co-culturing additional neurons have been formed (Song, Stevens and Gage 2002). There will be three batches of young and three batches of aged neural stem cells.

Only one batch of each is to be cultured with glial cells, the rest will be cultured normally to serve as controlled variables. Three types of ECMs will be tested, one that is bioengineered, one derived from aged mice and one from young mice. The bioengineered one will serve as the starting point for building a target niche meant to restore lost functions in the aged NSC. Cells sowed into the target niche will be the ones cultured with glial cells. The young and the aged ECMs will be collected by decellularizing tissue. Decellularized ECMs are effective in maintaining signals and biochemical components, making them a good choice for tissue regeneration (Liu et. al, 2025). It is important to collect data from both young and old ECMs as certain intrinsic factors affecting NSC quiescence occur after aging (Audesse and Webb, 2020).

After this, the construction process of the bioengineered niche can commence. When building the ECM, certain key components that promote neurogenesis will be required. These include laminin, because laminin containing matrices promote neurogenesis (Matta and Gonzalez, 2020). The ECM should also be rich in the glycosaminoglycan hyaluronan (HA) and tenascin-C, as those are proven to boost neurogenesis in the niche as well (Sherman and Preston, 2011). Tenascin-C is also found in abundance in strong neurogenic niches, and has been shown to increase neuronal differentiation in HA-based hydrogels (Shahi et. al, 2021). To promote neuronal differentiation, a soft stiffness similar to the brain will be required (Matta and Gonzalez, 2020). All of these factors lead toward developing a softer hydrogel, which directly drives more neuronal differentiation (Liang et. al, 2021). This hydrogel, as it gets constructed into an ECM, will need to be rich with growth factors such as EGF and FGF2 that promote neurogenesis and that can help expand NSCs in vitro (Matta and Gonzalez, 2020). BDNF, critical for neuronal survival, enhancement and development will be sowed in as well as VEGF (Jahanbin, 2026). VEGF is strongly expressed in healthy NSC niches, and promotes a process required to maintain the stem cell pool (Jin et. al, 2002). It is important that this scaffold is 3D, as a combination approach of both organoids and hydrogels remains the best way to model the NSC niche to date (Ionnakadis et. al, 2021).

After constructing the scaffold, it will be sowed with NSCs, specifically the ones cultured with the glial cells, to create a neural organoid. The primary comparison will be between the aged NSCs sowed into this bioengineered scaffold and the aged NSCs inputted into the previously decellularized tissue from aged mice. To fully tell whether the bioengineered niche improves neurogenesis, the concentration of the neuronal protein NCAM will be measured using an ELISA assay. The concentration of the protein DCX will be measured by immunocytochemistry. This will help answer the question on whether the bioengineered niche, which reflects the in vivo NSC niche, is more successful than current systems at rejuvenating neurogenesis in aged neural stem cells.

3. Methods

>Methods

3.1 Animals

A total of 12 C57BL/6J mice will be used for this experiment. 6 Young adult mice (2-3 months) will be acquired from the Jackson Laboratory and 6 aged mice (18-24 months) will be obtained from the National Institute on Aging Aged Rodent Colonies, which maintains mice specifically for research. All mice will be male for uniformity, and upon being acquired, all mice will stay in the same environment in groups of 2 per cage. Mice will be maintained in a specific pathogen-free vivarium at 22 ± 1°C and 55 ± 5% humidity under a 12-h light-dark cycle (Yanai and Endo, 2021). All following procedures will first be approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

3.2: Acquiring neural stem cells

Neural stem cells will be acquired from the mice aged 2-3 months and from mice between the ages of 18-24 months. A protocol to isolate NSCs from the DG and the subventricular zone (SVZ) of mice will be followed (Ahmed, Isaksen, and Yamashita, 2021).

Mice will be anesthetized using isoflurane. The brains will be dissected and transferred to an icecold dissection buffer. The DG and the SVZ of the hippocampus will be isolated and dissected. The tissue will be cubed with a scissor and added into a tube with 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA to separate the cells. This will then be incubated at 37° C for 15-20 minutes. When the trypsin is removed, 1 mL Trypsin inhibitor will be added and the tube incubated once again for 20 minutes at the same temperature. The tissue will then be triturated and washed in PBS. After, the cells will be put into 1mL of Neural Stem Cell Basal Growth Medium in a cell culture, and incubated in 5% CO2 at 37° C for an additional two days (Ahmed, Isaksen, and Yamashita, 2021). Clusters of NSCs called neurospheres should start to form. The spheres should be passaged every 2-3 days, but for a limited number of times to preserve age-related characteristics. The cells from each the aged and the young mice will be evenly divided into three different groups. One group from each age category will be cultured with glial cells, the other will not.

3.3A: Culturing stem cells with glial cells

Cells which will go into the bioengineered niche will be co-cultured with astrocytes to promote niche-like interactions (Song, Stevens and Gage 2002). Mouse astrocytes will be acquired from ThermoFisher Scientific (iXCells Biotechnologies., catalog #10MU003). Based on a modified protocol for the coculture of astrocytes and NSCs (Gottschling et. al, 2016), the mouse astrocytes will be expanded and purified to remove the extra microglia by shaking. They will then be put into wells 2-3 days before co-culturing. The purity of the culture will be verified by means of immunocytochemistry (see 3.2B). The astrocytes will be inputted into the NSC cultures meant for co-culturing (Gottschling et. al, 2016). The product will go back into the incubator and be harvested for the following 4 weeks.

3.3B: Verifying by immunocytochemistry

The verification of astrocyte purity is done by immunocytochemistry (ICC), using antibodies against the glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule Iba1, modified from Ippolito and Eroglu (2010). To start, a primary antibody mixture in antibody dilution buffer will be prepared containing 90% antibody buffer and 10% normal goat serum (NGS). Multiple samples will be incubated overnight; each including a GFAP and Iba1 antibody as well as 200 µL of the antibody dilution buffer (Ippolito and Eroglu, 2010).

Nuclei will be stained with DAPI. After incubation, the antibodies will be washed off using PBS and the mice-specific secondary antibodies conjugated to different fluorophores will be added. These fluorophores will allow for the labeling of GFAP and Iba1 to be visualized using a fluorescent microscope. Images will be collected using fluorescence microscopy to verify purity of the astrocyte culture. Cultures with less than a few percent of Iba1 and high GFAP labeling will be considered verified and pure (Ippolito and Eroglu, 2010). To calculate the percentage of fluorescent labeling, the amount of Iba1 positive cells and GFAP positive cells will each be divided by the total number of cells (stained with DAPI) and then multiplied by a hundred.

3.4: Decellularizing tissue for aged and young scaffolds

Decellularized tissue will serve as a comparison matrix for the bioengineered scaffold.

First, hippocampal tissue will be cut into 1.5 mm thick sections (De Waele et. al, 2015). The cuts will be restricted to the hippocampal tissue to prevent the loss of age-related biomarkers. Tissue will be incubated for 7 hours in demineralized water. Then it will be centrifuged in two detergents in repeated cycles. These are sodium deoxycholate (SDC) at a 4% concentration for 14 hours and Triton X-100 at a concentration of 3% for 2 hours. DNA will be removed by soaking the tissue in DNAse for 1 hour at the end of each cycle. This full cycle will be repeated 3 times, with PBS washes in between each one and a water rinse at the end (De Waele et. al, 2015). These decellularized tissues will be lyophilized in a process described at the end of section 3.5.

3.5: Bioengineering scaffold

A bioengineered NSC niche will be constructed as a 3D, soft, hydrogel scaffold which is purposefully designed to recreate key biochemical and mechanical properties of a potent neurogenic niche. This hydrogel will be made up of Hyaluronic Acid (HA), laminin, and tenascin-C. HA will be the primary backbone polymer, which physically resembles a brain-like matrix. Laminin will be mixed in with the HA, as matrices containing laminin have been shown to promote neurogenesis and also boost production of neurotrophic factors NGF and BDNF (Suri and Schmidt, 2010).

The mixture of these materials will follow a modified protocol creating a scaffold developed using fibrin, HA, and laminin (Arulmoli et. al, 2017). First, thiolated HA will be dissolved to a concentration of 1-2% w/v to help soften the hydrogel to promote differentiation, thus creating the target niche. Then both laminin and tenascin will be gently mixed in. Quantities will vary depending on desired outcome. The target stiffness is 800 Pa or 0.8 kPa, which is the best for elasticity (Roth et. al, 2023). Pa is the abbreviation for the unit Pascal, the SI (International System of Units) measure used to measure stress. To increase neuronal differentiation even more and to more accurately simulate a young niche, this study will aim for 200 Pa. To do this, HA can be dissolved to an even lower concentration.

3.6: Adding growth factors to scaffold

After the hydrogel is fully fabricated, growth factors will be embedded before the hydrogel is frozen into a scaffold. This will be using a direct loading approach, which is the most common method of loading proteins into hydrogels (Silva et. al, 2008). The factors to be loaded are EGF, FGF2, BDNF, and VEGF. EGF and FGF2 help increase NSC proliferation in vitro (Matta and Gonzalez 2020). BDNF and VEGF increase neuronal survival and signaling for the niche (Jahanbin, 2026). These factors will be reconstituted according to manufacturer instructions, and added to the gel solution (section 3.4). Target concentrations are around 20 ng/mL for each factor. This solution can be mixed using slow pipetting.

It is at this point that the scaffold can be finalized. The fully bioengineered scaffold now contains essential factors: the materials to boost neurogenesis, the mechanical properties to increase plasticity, and the correct growth factors and signaling pathways to operate as a young neural niche would. These factors will also aid in transforming the system into an organoid later.

In order to finalize the scaffold, it will first be frozen into a state where it can be rehydrated and can have NSCs inserted into it, as freezing the hydrogel helps with long term preservation and shelf life (Balster et. al, 2025). Freezing also provides a porous structure that will help give cells space to attach and grow once it is rehydrated (Grenier et. al, 2022). The hydrogel will be frozen using a protocol previously developed (Sornkamnerd, Okajima, and Kaneko, 2017). It will first be put in liquid nitrogen for ten minutes, at around -196℃. After that excess water must be removed through lyophilization, where the structure is put into a freeze-drying apparatus for 72 hours. Lyophilization is shown to improve mechanical strength of the scaffold (Xia et. al, 2018). This full freezing process will also apply to the decellularized tissues created in section 3.3.

3.7: 3D Modeling the niche

Prior to embedding the cells into the ECM, it must be rehydrated. A protocol that specifically outlines the rehydration of lyophilized tissues will be followed (Kornmuller et. al, 2017). It will start by suspending the tissues in a container of absolute ethanol, with about a 5-1 ethanol to tissue ratio. They will then be incubated at 4℃ for 4 hours. The process is repeated, while gradually lowering the ethanol amount each time and replacing it with PBS, and incubating after every wash, until the solution is down to 0% ethanol and pure PBS. After washing the scaffold an additional two times in PBS, it will be ready for use within 1-2 weeks.

Following rehydration, protocols for organoid generation will be as described by Bueno et. Al (2025). First, NSCs will be detached and separated using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA for 5 minutes at 37℃. The NSCs will then be suspended in StemPro Serum-Free Proliferation Medium to neutralize the trypsin (Bueno et. al, 2025). The NSCs that were co-cultured with astrocytes will be suspended in a 2:1 NSC to astrocyte ratio. Then, the young and aged decellularized ECM will be divided into 24 well plates, and suspended overnight in the same proliferation medium as to prep them for seeding. The same process can be repeated with the bioengineered ECM. The NSCs will then be seeded in; this process entails taking a droplet of NSC differentiation medium and placing it gently upon the ECMs. There will be 6 ECMs studied here: two bioengineered ECMs, one with aged NSC + astrocytes and one with young NSC + astrocytes, two aged tissues, one with young NSCs and one with aged NSCs, and finally two young tissues, one with aged NSCs and one with young NSCs. Each well will have warmed NSC differentiation medium added after 24 hours (Bueno et. al, 2025). After 5 days in this medium, with constant medium refreshments, the full structures will be moved to a NSC complete medium (Bai et. al, 2024). These cultures will be maintained in the complete medium, where there will be enough organoid in around 2-3 months. At this point, there would ideally be enough tissue to then study and analyze the results to determine the success of the bioengineered niche.

3.8a: Measure concentration of neuronal protein NCAM

The concentration of NCAM will be measured following the protocol outlined in Navarro-Lopez (2022). NCAM and PSA-NCAM will be quantified by ELISA (enzyme linked immunosorbent assay). 96-well plates will be prepped with a carbonate buffer for 2 hours. Each neural tissue will have small portions lysed in a protein extraction buffer. The resulting lysate will be collected. Each well will get 50 μL of lysate, each containing 10 μg/mL of protein. Wells are to be washed with PBS. Monoclonal mouse IgM anti-PSA antibodies (originally supplied by Prof. Genevieve Rougon, CNRS Marseille, France) are added to the tissue (Navarro-Lopez, 2022). Peroxidase-labeled second antibodies are then added. A chemical substrate (ophenylenediamine) plus hydrogen peroxide will be added and the peroxidase enzyme will convert it into a colored product. The intensity of the color will be measured with a spectrophotometer to determine NCAM concentration

3.8b: Quantification of DCX proportion with immunocytochemistry

Immunocytochemistry will be used to evaluate the amount of DCX as described in Vagaska et. al (2020). The 3D culture will be fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. Samples will then be rinsed with PBS, and blocked for 1 hour in a solution containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.2% Triton X-100. Then, the samples will be incubated with an anti-DCX antibody (Vagaska et. al, 2020). This is followed by a secondary antibody linked to a fluorescent marker. Nuclei will be stained with DAPI. After this, samples will be able to be imaged by means of confocal microscopy. DCX positive cell bodies will be counted, and the results will combine with the results of the previous ELISA assay to help determine whether the bioengineered niche has been successful.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1: Expected Results

It is expected that aged NSCs cultured in the bioengineered niche will show higher markers of neuronal plasticity than aged NSCs cultured in the aged decellularized ECM (dECM). This will be measured by an increase in concentration of the protein NCAM and an increase of prevalence of the protein DCX. The proposal outlines the mechanism by which this is accomplished, and highlights a bioengineered niche with softer mechanical properties, imbued growth factors, and co-culturing with astrocytes.

4.2: Interpretations

If the hypothesis is found to be correct, the bioengineered niche is more effective at restoring neurogenesis in aged NSCs than the aged dECM environment. It is also important to see the results of the controls, specifically the young NSCs in a young dECM. If the bioengineered niche approaches similar levels of NCAM concentration, it tells us that the process has been successful in restoring neurogenesis.

If the bioengineered niche approaches similar levels of NCAM concentration as the aged NSCs in a young dECM, this should still be interpreted as successful. If the bioengineered niche surpasses the aged NSCs in a young dECM, then the newly constructed niche is even more successful than previously reported.

However, if the aged NSCs specifically perform poorly in every model, it tells us that the factors of NSC aging are more significant than the niche around it. One of these factors would be the shape of the NSC, which is unrelated to the niche. This would be accentuated if the young NSCs perform very well in all 3 niches. If the young NSCs perform well in the aged dECM, it reaffirms that the NSC quality itself is much more important than the niche around it.

4.3: Applications

A successfully bioengineered niche has various possible applications that make it worth pursuing. The niche itself can be used for aging research, as certain factors can be turned on and off to see what contributes the most towards NCAM production. This would help researchers understand what to focus on when developing therapies using this technology.

The system itself could be adapted to study diseases such as Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, stroke, and other less common disorders by incorporating diseased NSCs into the system and seeing how they behave Positive outcomes could lead to strides in the field of neurological disease studying, and could guide us towards creating better disease models. The niche has potential in personalized regenerative medicine as well. By taking human iPSCs cells and reprogramming them into neural stem cells, and then putting them into this bioengineered niche, we can eventually implant the cells that proliferate in the human brain. While we have not figured out a way to remove NSCs from humans, being able to input this scaffold into the human brain to absorb aged NSCs and bring them back to a youthful state would fulfill the original mission of restoring neurogenesis.
