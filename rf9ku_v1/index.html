Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Prediction-by-association, in which a word pre-activates lexically associated words, is thought to be relatively automatic and therefore spared in healthy aging. In languages with grammatical gender, however, prenominal modifiers carry both semantic and gender information, so it remains unclear whether older adults also use morphosyntactic agreement to anticipate upcoming words. We tested whether younger (n = 32, 20-35 years) and older (n = 31, 55-75 years) native Hindi speakers use the meaning and the gender marking of a prenominal adjective to anticipate an upcoming noun. In a visual-world eye-tracking task, participants heard sentences such as Abhi aap ek nukili wali sui dekhenge ('Now you will see a pointed needle') while viewing a target (needle), an adjective competitor that was semantically compatible with the adjective but mismatched its gender (spear), and two unrelated distractors. In both age groups, the target and the adjective competitor attracted comparable anticipatory fixations shortly after adjective onset, and both were fixated more than the distractors. Later in the pre-noun interval, fixations to the target began to exceed those to the gender-mismatching competitor, although this advantage was modest. None of these effects interacted with age group, and filler sentences without a predictive adjective elicited no anticipatory fixations. These findings suggest that early anticipation is driven primarily by lexical-semantic association, with grammatical gender constraining predictions somewhat later, and that neither source of information shows age-related decline in contextually simple sentences.

Introduction

Language comprehension is not a purely reactive process: listeners routinely use the unfolding context to anticipate upcoming words before they are heard (Altmann & Kamide, 1999; Pickering & Gambi, 2018). Pickering and Gambi (2018) proposed that such prediction can proceed via two routes. Prediction-by-production is relatively effortful, drawing on the comprehender's own production system to covertly simulate what the speaker is likely to say, whereas prediction-by-association is relatively automatic and low-cost, relying on the spreading activation of lexical-semantic associations between the preceding input and upcoming words (Bar, 2007; Pickering & Gambi, 2018). This distinction is particularly relevant to cognitive aging. Healthy aging is accompanied by declines in processing speed, working memory, and executive control (Braver & West, 2008), whereas lexical-semantic knowledge remains stable across adulthood (Burke & Peters, 1986). Prediction that depends on effortful, resourcedemanding processes may therefore be vulnerable to age-related decline, whereas prediction that depends on well-established lexical associations should be relatively preserved.

We recently tested this proposal by asking whether prediction-by-association remains intact across adulthood (Anurag & Singh, 2026). Using the visual-world eye-tracking paradigm, younger and older Hindi speakers listened to sentences in which a gender-marked adjective (e.g., nukili 'pointed') was the only cue to an upcoming noun (e.g., sui 'needle') while viewing a display containing the target object and three unrelated, gender-matched distractors. Like younger adults, older adults directed anticipatory gaze to the target soon after hearing the associated adjective, and this effect was maintained under both long and shortened display preview durations. Filler sentences without a predictive adjective produced no anticipatory gaze in either age group, confirming that the effect was contingent on the adjective. These findings are consistent with the view that prediction-by-association is preserved in healthy aging, because this route is thought to operate through well-established, largely automatic lexical connections that are relatively spared by age-related cognitive change (Braver & West, 2008; Salthouse et al., 1995). This conclusion converges with previous visual-world studies of older adults. Baltaretu and Chambers (2018) found that older adults launched anticipatory fixations to an upcoming referent as quickly as younger adults when the prediction was supported by combined semantic and lexical associations. Huettig and Janse (2016), testing Dutch listeners aged 32 to 77 years, found that anticipatory eye movements cued by gender-marked articles were, if anything, positively related to age once working memory and processing speed were taken into account. Milburn et al. (2023) found that older adults showed more pronounced anticipatory eye movements than younger adults when an agent and a constraining verb jointly cued an upcoming argument. Fernandez et al. (2020) presented younger and older native listeners, as well as non-native listeners, with spoken sentences containing filler-gap dependencies while they viewed a related visual display, and systematically varied speech rate from 3.5 to 6.0 syllables per second. All groups made anticipatory eye movements toward the upcoming referent. However, older adults did so only at the slower rates (3.5 and 4.5 syllables per second), whereas younger adults did so up to 5.5 syllables per second, and anticipatory looks decreased markedly in all groups at the fastest rate.

More recently, using eye-tracking during natural reading rather than the visual-world paradigm, Wong and Veldre (2025) found that older adults showed processing costs when a semantically unrelated word replaced a highly predictable one. These costs were similar in magnitude and time course to those previously reported for younger adults, although comparable ERP studies have not consistently observed the same pattern in older adults. Together, these findings provide converging evidence that older adults continue to use predictive information during language comprehension, although the extent to which prediction is preserved may depend on the task, the type of predictive information available, and the processing demands involved. Overall, the eyetracking literature paints a considerably more consistent picture of age-preserved prediction than the mixed ERP literature (e.g., DeLong et al., 2012; Federmeier & Kutas, 2005; Wlotko et al., 2010), which makes anticipatory eye movements a well-suited measure for isolating the mechanism behind that preservation.

One feature of our previous design, however, leaves open which cues listeners used. That study adopted the paradigm of Mishra et al. (2012), in which Hindi-speaking high and low literates listened to simple spoken sentences (e.g., Abhi aap ek … dekhenge 'Now you will see a …') while viewing a display of four line drawings, the target and three distractors. The adjective and the following particle (wala/wali) provided semantic, associative, and grammatical information about the upcoming target. High literates shifted their gaze to the target well before the noun was heard, whereas low literates did so only once the noun itself had been heard. As in Mishra et al. (2012), the adjectives and the particle in our previous study carried grammatical gender marking in addition to their semantic content. Because all four objects in the display were gendermatched, however, gender marking was uninformative about which object would be mentioned, and anticipation must have been driven by the meaning of the adjective. The design therefore could not reveal whether listeners, and older listeners in particular, also use the gender marking carried by the adjective and particle to anticipate an upcoming noun. This question is not merely theoretical: a substantial cross-linguistic literature shows that grammatical gender marking is an effective cue to prediction in its own right, independent of the lexical-semantic content of the words that carry it.

Many of the world's languages, including Hindi, mark nouns for grammatical gender. In these languages, articles, adjectives, and other modifiers agree with the gender of the noun and therefore often provide gender information before the noun itself is heard. Gender marking thus offers listeners a cue to the identity of an upcoming noun that is independent of its meaning. Dahan et al. (2000) demonstrated this in French: when a gender-marked article preceding a target noun was inconsistent with the gender of a phonological competitor, listeners fixated that competitor less often, suggesting that the article had already narrowed the set of candidates under consideration before the noun was heard. Using the same visual-world logic in Spanish, Lew-Williams and Fernald (2007) found that both children and adults oriented faster to a target object when the preceding gender-marked article was informative (i.e., when the two pictured objects differed in grammatical gender) than when it was not, demonstrating that gender information on determiners is used as soon as it becomes available.

Electrophysiological work points to the same conclusion. Van Berkum et al. (2005) found that Dutch listeners who had formed a specific lexical prediction from prior discourse showed a differential ERP response to a gender-marked adjectival suffix that mismatched the gender of the predicted noun, before the noun itself had been presented. This finding indicates that comprehenders can pre-activate the grammatical gender of a predicted word, not only its meaning. Similarly, Wicha et al. (2004) found that Spanish readers showed a larger ERP response to a gender-marked article that disagreed with the gender of an expected upcoming noun than to one that agreed with it, again before the noun appeared. These findings suggest that the use of morphosyntactic information as a cue to upcoming words goes beyond prediction based on lexical associations alone (Pickering & Gambi, 2018).

Our previous study therefore did not test whether older adults can use gender marking to anticipate an upcoming noun, and the present study addresses this question. This is not a trivial extension, because it is unclear whether the morphosyntactic processing needed to exploit gender marking is preserved in older age to the same extent as lexical-semantic knowledge. This question has received less attention than the aging of lexical-semantic knowledge, and the available evidence is mixed. Several studies report that basic syntactic comprehension remains largely intact across the lifespan, with older and younger adults showing similar sensitivity to syntactic information (e.g., Tyler et al., 2010), and syntactic planning in production appears to be comparably robust in older and younger speakers even when lexical access is slowed (Hardy et al., 2020). Other studies, however, indicate that older adults are slower and less accurate than younger adults at detecting subject-verb agreement violations, for both real verbs and novel pseudo-verbs (Poulisse et al., 2019), and are more likely to accept sentences containing agreement attraction errors (Reifegerste et al., 2017), suggesting that morphosyntactic processing is not entirely spared by aging. Direct evidence on gender-based prediction in older adults is scarce. Huettig and Janse (2016) found no age-related decline in anticipation cued by Dutch gender-marked articles, but in their design gender was the only cue to the target, and age was examined across a continuous adult sample rather than by comparing younger and older groups. Given this mixed evidence, it cannot be assumed that gender marking supports prediction in the same age-invariant way as semantic association. Gender-based prediction might decline with age while association-based prediction does not, both might remain stable, or both might decline. Dissociating the two cues is therefore necessary to establish whether the apparent age invariance of adjective-driven prediction generalizes to a cue that depends on morphosyntactic rather than purely lexical-semantic processing.

The Present Study

The present study was designed to dissociate these two potential sources of prediction. Whereas our previous display involved only a target-versus-distractor contrast, the present display included an adjective competitor alongside the target and two unrelated distractors. The adjective competitor was semantically associated with the spoken adjective but did not agree with the gender marking carried by the adjective and the particle, so that only the target was compatible with both the meaning and the grammatical gender of the adjective. The two distractors were semantically unrelated to the adjective. All other aspects of the design, including the sentences, the adjectives, the visual-world eye-tracking procedure, and the filler sentences without a predictive adjective, were retained from our previous study to allow direct comparison.

The main aim was to examine whether older and younger adults use morphosyntactic information, specifically grammatical gender marking, to anticipate an upcoming noun during spoken sentence comprehension. In the sentence Abhi aap ek nukili wali sui dekhenge ('Now you will see a pointed needle'), the adjective and particle provide both semantic information about the upcoming noun and information about its grammatical gender. Accordingly, the visual display consisted of a target object (sui 'needle'), which matched both the semantic and the gender information provided by the adjective; an adjective competitor (bhala 'spear'), which was semantically compatible with the adjective but mismatched in grammatical gender; and two distractors that were semantically unrelated to the adjective, one matching and the other mismatching the target's gender.

We hypothesized that if comprehenders use both semantic and morphosyntactic information during prediction, the target should attract more anticipatory fixations than the adjective competitor, because only the target is compatible with both the semantic and the gender information. This difference should emerge in the interval between adjective onset and noun onset. Further, if older adults are able to use morphosyntactic information in the same way as younger adults, they should show a comparable anticipatory advantage for the target over the adjective competitor. If, however, older adults rely primarily on lexical-semantic association, the target and the adjective competitor should receive comparable anticipatory fixations in this group, because both are semantically consistent with the adjective. The difference between anticipatory fixations to the target and to the adjective competitor therefore provides a critical test of whether older adults incorporate gender information into prediction during spoken sentence comprehension.

Finally, as in our previous study, filler sentences without a predictive adjective were included as a control condition. We hypothesized that no anticipatory eye movements toward the target would be observed in the filler condition in either age group, because participants would have no predictive cue from which to generate expectations about the upcoming noun.

Methods

Participants

Sixty-three adults took part: 32 younger adults (20-35 years, M = 26.62, SD = 3.77) and 31 older adults (55-75 years, M = 61.94, SD = 4.84). All participants were native Hindi speakers with at least 15 years of formal education, normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and no reported history of speech-language, hearing, or neuropsychological disorders. All participants completed the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; Folstein et al., 1975) to screen for unreported memory or other cognitive disorders. All scored 28 or higher (younger adults: M = 29.19, SD = 0.78; older adults: M = 28.71, SD = 0.74), above the lower-quartile cutoff scores for healthy older adults (Bleecker et al., 1988). The study was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Allahabad (IERB ID: 2021-11CBCS; approved 14 February 2022), and all participants gave informed consent.

Materials and Stimulus Presentation

The experiment consisted of 58 trials (28 experimental and 30 filler), each with a four-picture display. The spoken sentences, adjectives, particle, and target nouns were identical to those used in our previous study and to the stimuli of Mishra et al. (2012). Each sentence consisted of a lead-in phrase (Abhi aap ek 'Now you will … a') followed by an adjective (e.g., nukili 'pointed'), a particle (wala/wali), a noun (e.g., sui 'needle'), and a verb (dekhenge 'see'). All sentences were recorded by a female native Hindi speaker, as in our previous study.

The present study departed from our previous study only in the composition of the visual display. Whereas the previous display contrasted a single target object with three unrelated distractors, each experimental display in the present study contained four image types: (1) the target object denoted by the upcoming noun, which matched the adjective in both meaning and grammatical gender (e.g., sui 'needle', feminine, like nukili wali); (2) an adjective competitor, an object that was semantically associated with the adjective but did not match its grammatical gender (e.g., bhala 'spear', which is masculine and therefore incompatible with wali, but pointed, like nukili); and (3, 4) two distractors (Distractor 1 and Distractor 2) that were semantically unrelated to the adjective, one matching and one mismatching the target in grammatical gender (e.g., topi 'cap', feminine; jag 'jug', masculine). As in our previous study, the filler trials did not include a predictive adjective (e.g., Abhi aap ek kitab dekhenge 'Now you will see a book'). The filler display contained the eventual target and three unrelated distractors (Distractor 1, Distractor 2, and Distractor 3), none of which bore any systematic semantic, phonological, or gender relationship to one another or to the target, so that the filler condition served as a control for any general bias in image position, salience, or scene layout.

The visual display (1920 × 1080 pixels) was divided into four quadrants, with each picture (350 × 350 pixels) centered in a quadrant; the assignment of image type to quadrant was randomized across trials, as in our previous study. All pictures were line drawings of common objects familiar to both younger and older participants.

Procedure

Participants were seated 60 cm from a 24-inch monitor, with head movements minimized by a chin and forehead rest. Eye movements were recorded with an EyeLink 1000 eye tracker (SR Research Ltd., Ottawa, Ontario, Canada) at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz, and the experiment was programmed and run in Experiment Builder (SR Research Ltd.). The session began with a 9point calibration. On each trial, a central fixation point appeared for 750 ms, followed by a blank screen for 500 ms, after which the four pictures appeared. The spoken sentence began 1500 ms after display onset, giving participants a preview of the display before sentence onset. Participants performed a look-and-listen task (Altmann & Kamide, 1999).

Data Coding

Data from the right eye were parsed into fixations, saccades, and blinks. As in our previous study, fixations were time-locked to adjective onset on experimental trials and to noun onset on filler trials. Fixations were assigned to one of four areas of interest (AOIs) corresponding to the four pictures: in the experimental condition, the target, the adjective competitor, and the two distractors; in the filler condition, the target and Distractors 1-3. Fixations falling outside these AOIs were excluded from the analysis.

Data Analysis

For the experimental condition, four time windows (TWs) were defined relative to adjective onset. TW1 (0-200 ms) served as the baseline, because fixations in this interval could not yet reflect processing of the adjective. In our previous study, a single window spanned the entire interval from 200 ms after adjective onset to noun onset. In the present study, this interval was divided into two equal halves so that early and late phases of anticipatory processing could be examined separately: TW2 (early adjective window) and TW3 (late adjective window) covered approximately 200-720 ms and 720-1240 ms after adjective onset, respectively, given the average noun onset of 1240 ms. This division made it possible to test whether any contribution of gender information emerged later than the contribution of semantic association, once the adjective and the gender-marked particle had been processed. TW4 covered the duration of the noun.

Fixation proportions were calculated for each trial and time window and logit-transformed prior to analysis. To avoid undefined values, proportions of 0 and 1 were replaced with values approaching these limits (ε = 10⁻⁸). Logit-transformed fixation proportions were modeled as a function of Time Window (TW1-TW4), Group (older, younger), Image Type (target, adjective competitor, Distractor 1, Distractor 2), and their interactions, using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for participants and items, fitted with the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) in R (R Core Team, 2023).

For the filler condition, three time windows were defined relative to noun onset: TW1 (-200 ms to noun onset) served as the baseline, TW2 extended from noun onset to 200 ms after noun onset, and TW3 extended from 200 ms to noun offset (on average 560 ms after noun onset). Filler data were analyzed with the same model structure, with Image Type comprising the target and three distractors.

Discussion

The present study examined whether older and younger adults use morphosyntactic information during anticipatory processing in spoken sentence comprehension. It extended our previous study (Anurag & Singh, 2026), in which older adults, like younger adults, launched anticipatory gaze toward a target soon after hearing an associated adjective, even when display preview duration was shortened to impose time pressure. Specifically, the present study examined whether older adults can use gender marking on the adjective to distinguish the target from another object that is semantically associated with the adjective but does not match its grammatical gender.

The visual display was designed to separate semantic from morphosyntactic information. Along with the target (matching the adjective in both meaning and grammatical gender), it contained an adjective competitor that was semantically associated with the adjective but mismatched it in grammatical gender, and two distractors that were semantically unrelated to the adjective. This design allowed us to ask a question that could not be addressed with the gender-matched displays of our previous study: whether the gender information carried by the adjective contributes to anticipation over and above the lexical-semantic association between the adjective and the upcoming noun.

The critical comparison was between the target and the adjective competitor during the pre-noun interval following adjective onset. Both objects were semantically compatible with the adjective, but only the target was consistent with its gender marking. If anticipation were guided only by lexical-semantic association, the target and the adjective competitor would receive comparable anticipatory fixations; if comprehenders also incorporated gender information, the target would receive more anticipatory fixations than the adjective competitor.

The results showed that in the early adjective window (TW2), the target and the adjective competitor attracted a comparable and substantial share of anticipatory fixations, and both were fixated more than the unrelated distractors. In the late adjective window (TW3), fixations to the target began to pull ahead of fixations to the adjective competitor. This divergence was modest: it was reliable in the model's Time Window × Image Type interaction but did not survive the conservative correction applied to the full set of pairwise comparisons. Neither pattern differed between older and younger adults, as indicated by the absence of any reliable Group × Image Type or three-way interaction involving the adjective competitor. Thus, early anticipation was driven by the meaning of the adjective in both groups, with no initial advantage for the target over the semantically compatible competitor. The subsequent divergence suggests that gender marking on the adjective and particle may have begun to constrain anticipation shortly before the noun was heard, and older adults showed this divergence to the same extent as younger adults. The present results therefore provide no evidence that older adults are less able than younger adults to use gender information for anticipation, although the absence of an interaction does not by itself establish equivalence, and the modest size of the late effect means that it should be confirmed in future work.

These findings indicate that the early anticipatory gaze observed in our previous study, and replicated here, was driven primarily by the lexical-semantic content of the adjective: semantic association alone was sufficient to draw anticipatory fixations to an object even when that object conflicted with the grammatical gender of the adjective. This is consistent with the view that prediction-by-association arises from spreading activation among semantically, associatively, or phonologically related representations (Bar, 2007; Kuperberg, 2007; Pickering & Gambi, 2018). On this account, early prediction draws primarily on lexical and semantic knowledge rather than directly on morphosyntactic agreement cues.

This pattern also converges with visual-world evidence that lexical association can drive fixations toward objects that are not appropriate referents of the unfolding sentence. Kukona et al. (2011) found that listeners hearing Toby arrests the … fixated a policeman, a good agent but an implausible patient of the verb, and Kukona et al. (2014) found that listeners hearing The boy will eat the white … fixated a white car even though the verb ruled it out, because the car matched the adjective. The latter finding is particularly relevant here: in both that study and the present one, an adjective activated a semantically matching object that was incompatible with other information in the sentence. The comparable fixations to the target and the adjective competitor observed here extend this logic to grammatical gender, showing that the meaning of an adjective can attract fixations to an object that its gender marking rules out, and that older adults are as susceptible to (and therefore as capable of generating) this form of associative prediction as younger adults. That fixations to the adjective competitor began to fall behind those to the target only late in the pre-noun interval (TW3), and fell reliably below them once the noun had been heard (TW4), further shows that both age groups used subsequent information to refine their initial, coarser semantic prediction.

These findings add to the evidence that prediction-by-association remains relatively stable with age and are consistent with visual-world studies showing that healthy older adults maintain, and under some conditions enhance, anticipatory processing (Baltaretu & Chambers, 2018; Huettig & Janse, 2016; Milburn et al., 2023). Within Pickering and Gambi's (2018) framework, prediction-by-production is cognitively demanding, drawing on resources for covertly simulating the speaker's intended message, and is accordingly more vulnerable to the declines in processing speed and working memory capacity that accompany healthy aging (Braver & West, 2008; Raz, 2000). Prediction-by-association, by contrast, is thought to rely on well-established, largely automatic lexical connections that are preserved, and in some respects even enriched, across the lifespan (Bowles et al., 1983; Burke & Peters, 1986; Salthouse et al., 1995). In the present contextually neutral sentences, in which the adjective was the only predictive cue, older adults' anticipatory fixations were neither delayed nor reduced relative to those of younger adults, for either the target or the adjective competitor. ERP work similarly suggests that word-level semantic processing is relatively preserved with age, whereas the use of sentence-level context is reduced or delayed (Federmeier et al., 2003). The present findings, however, are most directly comparable to the visual-world literature, which likewise uses anticipatory eye movements as an online measure of prediction.

Importantly, the present findings do not indicate that older adults are impaired in using gender marking for prediction. If older adults had difficulty exploiting agreement information, they should have continued to fixate the gender-mismatching adjective competitor for longer than younger adults, or shown no divergence between the target and that competitor before the noun was heard. Neither was the case. The target began to pull ahead of the adjective competitor in the late pre-noun window to the same extent in both age groups, none of the interactions involving age group and the adjective competitor approached significance, and older and younger adults did not differ in fixations to any image in any time window. That the gender-related effect was modest and emerged relatively late in both groups is better explained by the design of the present study than by a limited ability to use gender information. In earlier visual-world studies of gender-based prediction, gender marking was the only predictive cue: in Dahan et al. (2000), Lew-Williams and Fernald (2007), and Huettig and Janse (2016), the gender-marked article was the only information that distinguished the target from the other objects before the noun was heard, so listeners could rely on it fully. The ERP studies of Van Berkum et al. (2005) and Wicha et al. (2004) likewise isolated gender agreement as the critical cue. In the present study, by contrast, the adjective conveyed both semantic and grammatical information, and its meaning alone was sufficient to narrow the display down to two candidates, the target and the adjective competitor. Gender marking could therefore contribute only by discriminating between two objects that were already equally compatible with the meaning of the adjective, with the gender information on the adjective reinforced by the particle wala/wali that followed it. Under these conditions, a strong semantic cue is likely to dominate early anticipation for listeners of all ages, leaving a later and narrower window in which gender can add to prediction, which is the pattern observed in both groups. Evidence from other languages also suggests that older adults can use morphosyntactic cues predictively. Huettig and Janse (2016) found that gender-cued anticipation in Dutch was, if anything, positively related to age once working memory and processing speed were taken into account, and Oh et al. (2022) found that older Korean speakers relied more strongly than younger speakers on case-marker-based prediction, which in turn made it harder for them to revise those predictions when a passive structure was encountered. Older adults may nonetheless have greater difficulty when morphosyntactic cues must be integrated in more complex structures; for example, Hwang et al. (2024) found that older Korean speakers were less accurate and slower than younger speakers in comprehending comparative sentences and showed different word-order preferences in their eye movements. The present results suggest that older adults can use gender marking when it refines a semantically based prediction. They do not show whether older Hindi speakers use gender as effectively as younger speakers when it is the sole predictive cue; a design in which the adjective is semantically neutral, so that only its gendermarked ending and the particle distinguish the target from the other objects, would be needed to test this directly.

The filler-sentence analysis provides an important control for this interpretation. When the adjective was absent and therefore provided no cue to the upcoming noun, neither age group showed an anticipatory shift in gaze toward the eventual target before it was heard, and fixations to the three unrelated distractors did not differ from fixations to the target during the pre-noun period. This confirms, as in our previous study, that the early, selective fixations to the target and the adjective competitor in the experimental condition cannot be attributed to a general bias in image position, salience, or scene layout, but were contingent on the presence of a predictive adjective, and that this held equally for older and younger participants.

Taken together, the present findings do not merely replicate the age invariance of adjectivedriven prediction reported in our previous study; they sharpen its interpretation. By including a semantic competitor that conflicted grammatically with the target, the present study shows that older adults' early anticipatory gaze reflects the same lexical-semantic associative mechanism proposed to support prediction-by-association in younger adults (Pickering & Gambi, 2018). In Mishra et al. (2012) and in our previous study, there was no adjective competitor, so the contributions of the two cues could not be separated. The present results indicate that semantic association alone is sufficient to draw early anticipatory fixations, even when it conflicts with grammatical gender, which is a stronger demonstration than the mere co-occurrence of the two cues could provide, whereas gender information contributes later in the pre-noun interval.

As in our previous study, these results indicate that when a task places minimal demands on working memory and message-level integration, as in the present contextually neutral, adjectivecued sentences, older adults engage in prediction as readily as younger adults, and do so primarily via the lexical-semantic associative route. Eye-tracking studies that report reduced or delayed prediction in older adults typically involve conditions that increase the processing demands on an otherwise available predictive cue rather than removing the cue itself. For example, Fernandez et al. (2020) found that older adults made anticipatory eye movements at slower speech rates but, unlike younger adults, not at 5.5 syllables per second, whereas in our previous study shortening display preview duration left the age-invariant pattern intact, suggesting a fairly high threshold before associative prediction is disrupted by time pressure in older adults. ERP studies have likewise reported age-related reductions in higher-order, messagelevel prediction (DeLong et al., 2012; Federmeier & Kutas, 2005; Wlotko et al., 2010). In contrast, the present study used a purely associative manipulation that minimized such higherlevel processing demands. The absence of an age difference is therefore consistent with the view that different forms of prediction are differentially affected by age-related changes in cognitive processing (Federmeier et al., 2003).

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present findings replicate and extend those of our previous study, providing further evidence that prediction-by-association is preserved across adulthood. Older adults, like younger adults, spontaneously generated anticipatory fixations toward both the correct referent and a semantically related competitor that mismatched the target in grammatical gender as soon as the adjective provided sufficient lexical-semantic information to do so. Later in the pre-noun interval, fixations to the target began to pull ahead of fixations to the gender-mismatching competitor, and both groups resolved their initial prediction onto the correct target once the noun was heard. None of these effects differed between age groups. Early anticipation therefore appears to be carried primarily by meaning rather than by morphosyntactic agreement, with gender information contributing somewhat later, and the present results provide no evidence that either source of information is used less effectively in healthy aging.
