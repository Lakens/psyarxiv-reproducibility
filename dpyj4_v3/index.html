Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Affective polarization is rising in many societies, prompting a growing body of research on interventions to reduce partisan animosity. Although these interventions demonstrate promise, their effectiveness often diminishes outside of controlled experimental settings, leaving open questions regarding their durability, scalability, and real-world impact. In this review, we introduce the SCOPE Barriers Framework, highlighting five key challenges facing affective polarization intervention research: Statistical, Cross-disciplinary, Overgeneralization from WEIRD samples, Practical, and Ethical barriers. We argue these barriers systematically constrain the translation of promising interventions into meaningful societal change. By synthesizing insights across disciplines and evaluating intervention practices through the SCOPE lens, we identify limitations in measurement, generalizability, implementation, and ethical consideration. We conclude by offering recommendations for researchers, practitioners, and institutions to design, evaluate, and deploy robust, context-sensitive, and socially responsible interventions.

Barriers to Bridging Divides in the Real World: A Framework for Addressing

Challenges in Affective Polarization Intervention Research

Many societies are facing growing affective polarization (Boxell et al., 2022). This division has raised legitimate concerns about the health of democracy (McCoy et al., 2018) and the rise of political violence (Kalmoe & Mason, 2022). The urgency of addressing affective polarization is exemplified by the growth in intervention development. However, while many of these interventions are effective in highly controlled (experimental) settings, effectiveness decays quickly (Holliday et al., 2025), raising questions about their applicability in real-world contexts.

Here, we critically examine the state of the affective polarization intervention literatureidentifying key barriers impeding the efficacy and scalability of interventions. Specifically, we introduce the SCOPE (Statistical, Cross-disciplinary, Overgeneralizing from WEIRD samples, Practical, and Ethical) Barriers Framework which highlights the limits of many affective polarization interventions in real-world contexts. After discussing some key areas in affective polarization intervention research, we then use the framework to highlight the challenges these interventions face in addressing real-world divisions before pointing to avenues for future research.

Why Affective Polarization?

The term "polarization" can refer to many forms of division-including more policy-based divisions (see Dalton, 1987) and perceived polarization (Enders & Armaly, 2019). Affective polarization is traditionally defined as one's growing warmth towards political in-groups and coldness towards political outgroupsfoot_0 (Iyengar et al., 2012). Others have defined it more broadly as conceptually related to othering, aversion, and moralization (Campos & Federico, 2025), and liking vs disliking (Wagner, 2024), connecting the construct to many other outcomes such as disrespect (Kubin et al., 2021) and dehumanization (Landry et al., 2023). We should note that affective polarization can be directed vertically toward politicians and political elites or horizontally toward family, friends, colleagues, and community members. We focus on affective polarization here for 3 key reasons: 1) Growing Prevalence.

Affective polarization is increasing in many societies (Boxell et al., 2022) and scholars are warning of the deleterious effects affective polarization has on some democracies (McCoy et al., 2018), though others do question the extent to which affective polarization hurts democracy (Broockman et al., 2023) 2.

2) Growing Threat. Theories of polarization suggest affective polarization is a uniquely serious threat to society (see Connective Democracy; Overgaard et al., 2022) and is a key reason for growing political divisiveness in society (Iyengar et al., 2019).

3) Growing Response by Researchers.

Greater scholarly attention is being paid to understanding how affective polarization and its consequences emerge with many developing interventions to combat it (e.g., Voelkel et al., 2024). For example, one systematic review identified 332 studies assessing affective polarization, with research activity increasing markedly since the mid-1990s (Abad Borger et al., 2026).

By considering affective polarization interventions and their limitations, we provide insights for how the field can better develop effective, long-lasting real-world interventions.

Throughout this review, we use the term "real-world" interventions to refer to interventions implemented beyond the standard (controlled) research settings and at meaningful scale within the social contexts where political divisions occur-for example, in schools, communities, digital environments, or broader society. Our central concern is therefore whether promising research can ultimately be translated into interventions that reach and benefit broader populations.

Finally, our approach is conceptual rather than systematic: we draw on established and recent research across affective polarization, intergroup relations, and related disciplines to identify recurring challenges in intervention development and implementation. We prioritize work that illustrates or provides empirical and theoretical grounding for these challenges, while also drawing on broader interdisciplinary scholarship where it helps contextualize these challenges.

Accordingly, the studies discussed are intended to substantiate and illustrate the barriers we identify rather than provide an exhaustive representation of the literature. At the individual level, many interventions focus on changing the mindset or belief system of partisans. For example, fostering intellectual humility (i.e., the recognition of gaps in one's knowledge) is beneficial for reducing affective polarization (Knöchelmann & Cohrs, 2024). Other interventions draw on clinical psychology to reduce partisan animosity via therapeutic approaches, such as mindfulness programs (Simonsson et al., 2022).

Affective Polarization Interventions

At the relational level, interventions improve understanding and interactions between political opponents. Some interventions focus on building acknowledgment of commonalities between groups, finding that recognizing shared moral values (Puryear et al., 2024), threat concerns (Kubin et al., 2022), interests (Puryear et al., 2024), and identities (Levendusky, 2018) can reduce affective polarization. Other interventions focus on directly improving interactions between political opponents, such as through cross-partisan conversations, which can reduce affective polarization (Combs et al., 2023). For example, discussing personal topics, such as how one would spend a perfect day, can improve attitudes toward political opponents (Santoro & Broockman, 2022).

At the societal level, interventions reduce affective polarization by changing the broader social contexts. These approaches focus on institutions, social norms, and information environments that can attenuate partisan hostility (Čehajić-Clancy & Halperin, 2024). For instance, scholars have identified elite incivility and polarizing political rhetoric as contributors to affective polarization (Skytte, 2021), suggesting interventions promoting more constructive political discourse may reduce partisan animosity. Similarly, research highlights the role of media environments in shaping affective polarization, pointing to the potential value of reforms that reduce exposure to polarizing media environments (Kubin & von Sikorski, 2023). Other societallevel approaches focus on structural drivers of polarization, for example finding that redistributive policies may reduce affective polarization over time (Stewart et al., 2021).

While affective polarization interventions show promise, many face obstacles that limit their effectiveness in real-world contexts. Highly controlled experimental settings are essential for identifying causal mechanisms, but they represent only the first step in intervention development.

To meaningfully reduce affective polarization, interventions should be implemented and evaluated outside the laboratory, where complex social environments, long-term relationships, and the cumulative effects of everyday interactions can shape outcomes in ways that are rarely considered in controlled experiments. Below, we introduce the SCOPE Barriers Framework as a way to examine some of these obstacles to translating interventions to the real-world.

Barrier 1: Statistical and Methodological Barriers

Current interventions face numerous statistical (e.g., effect meaningfulness) and methodological (e.g., measurement choices and research settings) barriers that limit their effectiveness and usability. These barriers include how affective polarization is measured, difficulties in testing interventions in naturalistic contexts, and concerns about the magnitude and durability of intervention effects.

Measurement.

First, the very way we measure affective polarization is a methodological barrier to affective polarization research. Affective polarization is measured using varying metrics, making it challenging to synthesize and compare intervention efficacy, particularly in metaanalytic reviews (Druckman & Levendusky, 2019).

Many scholars use the classic 101-point warmth thermometer to measure warmth/coldness towards political allies/opponents (Iyengar et al., 2012). In measuring affective polarization, it also matters who participants are asked about.

Traditionally, affective polarization measures ask about their political allies/opponents (e.g., Iyengar et al., 2012). Measuring affective polarization within multiparty systems is even more challenging.

While traditional measures of affective polarization focus on "in-groups" and "out-groups", in multiparty systems the idea of an "out-group" can be complex (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?M2sJJB Wagner, 2024). Here, people often like some political out-groups more than others (Röllicke, 2023) and may even affiliate with more than one political group (Mayer & Schultze, 2019). This has led to the development of a variety of different measurements (e.g., Boxell et al., 2022; Gidron et al., 2019; Wagner, 2021).

Some have suggested measuring affective polarization in multiparty systems by assessing the spread of like-dislike scores of individuals (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?jZWQ1y Wagner, 2021). However, others have pointed to challenges with this measure as it treats affect towards all parties as equal-regardless of whether those parties are extremist or not (Gidron et al., 2023). Therefore, some have promoted focusing on both multiple-and single-in party approaches (Kekkonen et al., 2022) or block approaches (e.g., attitudes to certain coalitions/political camps) (Rothers, 2025). We propose scholars consider which form of affective polarization is most problematic within the specific context they are studying (e.g., general affective polarization across the electorate vs towards extreme groups) to determine which measure of affective polarization is most appropriate.

Another challenge of measurement is that many emerging affective polarization interventions assess a range of adjacent outcomes beyond affective polarization. In the strictest sense, affective polarization is defined as the difference score between in-group warmth and outgroup coldness (Iyengar et al., 2012)https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1iTd32 however, affective polarization is often defined as a much broader set of affective constructs centered on improving feelings towards opponents (e.g., reduced dehumanization; Puryear et al., 2024). While it is not necessarily problematic for researchers to study a broad set of outcomes, it can become challenging to decipher exactly what researchers mean when they say they are studying affective polarization (Kubin & von Sikorski, 2023).

We encourage scholars to develop affective polarization measures that can be utilized effectively both within two-party and multiparty political systems. In terms of the standardization of measurement, we advise scholars to use standard "classic" measures of affective polarization (e.g., the 101-point warmth feeling thermometer; Iyengar et al., 2012), while also including newer measures that assess other elements of affective division (e.g., Finkel et al., 2020; McMurtrie et al., 2025). In this way, they can continually re-test the efficacy of classic measures while also validating new measures. Using such strategies could make meta-analytic reviews more feasible, thereby facilitating a more comprehensive synthesis of the literature.

External and ecological validity.

Many interventions are tested in controlled contexts that differ substantially from everyday political life-for example, interactions with individuals that participants would not normally encounter (Levendusky & Stecula, 2021), imagined political opponents (Warner & Villamil, 2017), or fictitious social media content (Kubin et al., 2023).

Brief online experiments also frequently rely on convenience samples recruited through platforms such as MTurk and Prolific (Krupnikov et al., 2021). Although these designs are valuable for establishing internal validity, they leave uncertainty about whether observed effects generalize to the social environments in which political identities are expressed and reinforced in the real world. We therefore encourage researchers to complement internally rigorous experiments with subsequent tests using authentic political content and more naturalistic settings.

Statistical meaningfulness and longevity.

Although many interventions reduce affective polarization, effects are typically modest. A recent meta-analysis of 77 treatments across 25 published studies found an average reduction of only 5.4 points on a 101-point scale (Holliday et al., 2025). Effects also tend to fade quickly, often within two weeks (Holliday et al., 2025), raising questions about their practical impact. Some interventions demonstrate greater durability; for example, cross-party discussions emphasizing shared commonalities reduced animus for approximately one week (Levendusky & Stecula, 2021). However, other interventions show no lasting effects (e.g., Voelkel et al., 2024) and many do not assess longevity at all (e.g., Puryear et al., 2024).

Barrier 2: Cross-Disciplinary Barriers

Understanding and combating political polarization has become a central theme in a wide range of social science disciplines, making it a highly interdisciplinary topic. In many ways, this array of disciplinary approaches is a major strength-providing a broader set of perspectives, methodologies, and theories to tackle affective polarization. Political scientists explore how political systems exacerbate polarization (Daltonhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?B6ItuY 2021)https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?R9i1qW psychologists examine how perceived threats and emotion drive affective polarization (Renström et al., 2023) , sociologists study how inequality can create polarization (Koch et al., 2021) , economists examine how polarization shapes individuals' household financial decisions (e.g., home sales; McCartney et al., 2024) and media scholars examine the relationship between social media and affective polarization (Kubin & von Sikorski, 2023).

However, disciplinary diversity also creates tensions, with scholars adopting different https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KaxqLp Voelkel et al., 2024) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KaxqLp their research on theoretical foundations established within their own discipline-leading to divergent theoretical bases for each field's line of research. For examplehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kC6LGV media scholars frequently rely on the Spiral of Silence Theory (Noelle-Neumann, 1974) , which suggests individuals' perceptions of public opinion influence their willingness to express their own beliefs and opinions. Many psychologistshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?AcH7KA for example, base their polarization research on theories of morality (Graham et al., 2013; Schein & Gray, 2018) , personality (Franco & Pound, 2022) and interpersonal perceptions (Lees & Cikara, 2021). While it can be a strength to tackle affective polarization from divergent theoretical approaches, with little interdisciplinary overlap, the benefits of these diverse theoretical perspectives are weakened as disciplines do not learn from the theoretical foundations of other fields to inform their work.

Why do disciplines rarely cross-pollinate? One major barrier to cross-disciplinary research is the lack of a shared "language." Disciplines can differ substantially in how they conceptualize key terms-for example, "liberal" and "conservative" may represent simple scale endpoints in psychological surveys but are more nuanced and contested ideological positions in political science (e.g., Bell, 2014). Disciplines also differ in what constitutes reliable data and appropriate methods, from data collection and experimental design to interpretation. Every discipline will have slightly different approaches to data collection, experimental design, and data interpretation and will have different perspectives on the strengths and weaknesses of their own approaches compared with those of other disciplines.

Even successfully conducted interdisciplinary research can face challenges at the back end.

When it comes time to publish findings, deviation from the established methods of one's own field may be seen as a weakness by reviewers, harming the chances of publication. Academics across disciplines are likely conducting this kind of cost-benefit analysis on cross-disciplinary work and concluding it is too risky for the resources it requires. We suggest this disciplinary barrier is especially problematic in affective polarization work given the large overlaps in research interests with very little cross-pollination of ideas, theories, and methods-likely reducing advances in the field as a whole.

Interdisciplinary perspectives are essential for addressing affective polarization. While disciplinary research generates valuable insights, greater collaboration requires shared language and measurement approaches that enable integration across fields. We encourage scholars to leverage disciplinary strengths while developing interdisciplinary initiatives that synthesize insights to advance intervention development.

Barrier 3: Overgeneralization from WEIRD Samples

As in many areas of social science (Arnetthttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Ud06rP 2008) , research in this area is heavily concentrated in WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic) societiescontexts that are not representative of the full range of human cognition, motivation, or political experience (Henrich et al., 2010). Although this body of work has yielded promising interventions in countries such as Germany (Kubin et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?fY38Jj 2023) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Bxfkxs the United Kingdom (Tausch et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?w3v2pz 2024) , and the United States (Voelkel et al., 2024) , it remains unclear whether these approaches generalize to societies with different political systems, levels of industrialization, or educational contexts. At the same time, growing evidence indicates that affective polarization is increasing well beyond WEIRD contexts (Baqir et al., 2023). Rising polarization has been documented in countries such as Chile (Lunahttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zDNb8x 2024) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?LYINDU South Korea (Cheong & Haggardhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?pcIYOU 2023) , and South Africa (Southall, 2019) , underscoring the need to ensure that intervention strategies are effective in diverse international settings.

The dominance of U.S.-based research further amplifies this concern. Owing to the United States' exceptionally high levels of polarization (Kleinfeldhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FWSOiN 2023) and the relative ease of recruiting U.S. participants via online platforms such as Prolific (Anderson et al., 2019) , much of the existing intervention literature relies on U.S. samples (e.g., Santoro & Broockman, 2022). For example, a recent systematic review on the relationship between media and polarization found approximately 66% of samples came from the U.S. (Kubin & von Sikorski, 2021). While understanding and developing tools that reduce polarization within a single highly divided society is valuable, greater effort is needed to conduct cross-cultural comparisons.

Barrier 4: Practical Barriers

Even when interventions demonstrate effects that extend beyond controlled settings, scaling up these interventions to reach the masses introduces additional challenges. Effective interventions need to be accessible, feasible to deliver at scale, and supported by the institutions and resources necessary for sustained implementation. While some scholars have begun collaborating with practitioners to translate research into practice (e.g.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?2cxR5r Kubin et al., 2025) , the majority do not engage directly with practitioners (Taylor et al., 2024). This gap likely reflects the substantial practical challenges involved in moving interventions from the laboratory to real-world settings. Below, we outline several of these challenges.

Barriers to Participation.

A central challenge for polarization interventions is reaching the people who may need them most. Barriers to participation can be both geographic and motivational. Geographic polarization means that partisans are often concentrated in different communities (Motyl et al., 2014), limiting opportunities for interaction and potentially fueling polarization (Thomsen & Thomsen, 2023). This separation may be particularly consequential because, in the absence of direct contact, people have fewer opportunities to correct inaccurate impressions or stereotypes of political opponents (Parker et al., 2025). At the same time, partisans are often reluctant to engage with political opponents in the first place (Frimer et al., 2017). This is particularly relevant for interventions based on intergroup contact, such as crosspartisan conversations (Santoro & Broockman, 2022), where participation itself requires a willingness and opportunity to interact with political opponents. These challenges highlight the value of delivery channels that do not require individuals to seek out or travel to dedicated intervention spaces. Digital and social media environments may provide one such avenue by embedding interventions within contexts where people already encounter political information and interaction, potentially expanding their reach beyond those who would be motivationally willing and geographically capable of participating in traditional interventions.

Structural inequalities can further constrain equitable intervention reach. Interventions delivered through universities, community organizations, or public events, for example, may more readily reach urban or socioeconomically advantaged populations while excluding geographically isolated or economically disadvantaged communities. Intervention development should therefore consider not only whether an approach is effective, but who is realistically able to access it.

Tailoring delivery to communication channels widely accessible to underserved populations, including digital platforms where appropriate, may provide one avenue for expanding reach (Kubin & McKeown, 2026). Other approaches may seek to reduce reliance on voluntary crossparty contact altogether-for example, by working within partisan groups (Blair et al., 2025) or shifting social norms surrounding engagement with political opponents (Pryor et al., 2019).

Scalability and Resource Demands.

Reaching broader populations also introduces substantial financial and temporal constraints. Evaluating intervention efficacy across cultures and contexts, assessing long-term effects through longitudinal designs, and translating effective approaches into large-scale programs can require considerable resources. Although some scholars have undertaken such work (e.g., Tausch et al., 2024), its feasibility often depends on sustained funding.

These constraints become particularly pronounced for interventions requiring intensive participation. Many practitioner-led approaches, for example, bring individuals together for extended, moderated discussions (Fishkin, 2018). Such interventions require substantial commitments from both participants and facilitators, making widespread-and potentially repeated-implementation costly and time-intensive. This creates a fundamental scalability challenge: interventions that are feasible and effective with small groups may be difficult to deliver with sufficient reach and intensity to generate societal-level effects.

However, digital and social media interventions may help overcome some of these constraints (Kubin & von Sikorski, 2023). While such interventions still require resources and platform access, once developed, videos, interactive tools, or social media interventions can potentially reach thousands of users without requiring additional facilitators or physical meeting spaces for each participant, reducing the costs of large-scale and repeated delivery.

Time is also a relevant resource constraint. Determining whether effects persist requires replications and longitudinal research, meaning assessing intervention durability requires both additional funding and time from both researchers and participants. These demands create a tension between the desire to evaluate the robustness of promising interventions and the resources required to establish whether their effects are sustained and generalizable.

Institutional and Systemic Constraints. Practical implementation is also shaped by academic and political institutions. Within academia, incentives often prioritize novelty and publication over implementation and sustained evaluation. Once an intervention has demonstrated efficacy, testing its application at scale may offer fewer opportunities for theoretical novelty despite its potential societal value. Journals, funders, hiring committees, and other academic institutions can help address these constraints by placing greater value on applied research, replication, and longitudinal evaluation.

Constraints also extend beyond academia. Political leaders may be unwilling to support large-scale polarization-reduction initiatives when societal division provides electoral or strategic advantages (Phillips, 2024; Wilson et al., 2020). In authoritarian contexts, researchers may additionally face restrictions regarding permissible research questions and methods, alongside potential risks to participants and researchers alike (Janenova, 2019). Even where governments support polarization-reduction efforts, implementation may compete with other demands on limited public resources, particularly during periods of economic uncertainty-conditions that may themselves contribute to political division (McCarty et al., 2003).

These challenges illustrate that translating polarization interventions into societal impact depends on more than demonstrating efficacy. Successful implementation also requires addressing motivational and geographic challenges, investing monetary and temporal resources, and operating within academic and political institutions that support rather than constrain translation to practice.

Barrier 5: Ethical Barriers

Although interventions aimed at reducing affective polarization are often motivated by prosocial goals, they raise an important normative question: when is reducing affective polarization actually desirable? Some intergroup animosity stems from misperceptions, such as exaggerated beliefs about opponents' extremity or moral character (e.g., Parker et al., 2025).

However, negative affect can also reflect legitimate concerns about political opponents who support policies or leaders perceived to violate democratic norms, perpetuate injustice, or threaten core values. A key ethical challenge is therefore determining when reducing affective polarization represents an appropriate goal.

Political disagreement-and the negative affect accompanying it-is not inherently problematic. Agonistic theories of democracy view conflict as an unavoidable feature of pluralistic societies; the goal is therefore not to eliminate disagreement, but to enable opponents to disagree while still pursuing democratic processes and discourse (Mouffe, 2000). From this perspective, the aim of polarization interventions should not necessarily be to make people feel more positively toward political opponents regardless of the positions those opponents hold. Negative reactions may sometimes represent meaningful responses to political disagreement or perceived wrongdoing that are a cornerstone of a functioning pluralistic society.

This concern becomes particularly important in contexts characterized by power asymmetries or inequality. Negative emotions such as anger and moral outrage can motivate collective action in response to perceived injustice (Spring et al., 2018), meaning that reducing such emotions may sometimes undermine efforts to challenge existing inequalities. Consistent with this possibility, the "irony of harmony" literature suggests that positive intergroup contact can lead disadvantaged-group members to perceive advantaged groups as fairer, potentially reducing support for collective efforts to address inequality (Saguy et al., 2009). Interventions that reduce animosity may therefore have unintended consequences when substantial inequalities or injustices remain, particularly if greater harmony dampens the motivation to challenge them.

Interventions should distinguish between destructive partisan hostility and legitimate political opposition rather than treating affective polarization as inherently undesirable. As Balinhas (2023) cautions, social harmony should not be pursued uncritically, because intergroup conflict can catalyze positive social change. The challenge is thus to reduce forms of hostility that undermine constructive political engagement without suppressing disagreement, moral concern, or opposition that may serve important democratic and social functions.

Moving Forward in Polarization Intervention Research

The SCOPE barriers should not discourage efforts to develop affective polarization We encourage researchers to engage beyond their own disciplines and draw on the methods, theories, and literatures offered by scholars in other fields. Affective polarization is a complex, multi-layered phenomenon that spans individual cognition, group dynamics, media and political ecosystems, and structural inequalities; no single discipline can fully capture this complexity. Interdisciplinary collaboration not only enables a more comprehensive understanding of affective polarization but also fosters the development of intervention strategies that are both theoretically grounded and empirically robust. By bridging disciplinary silos, researchers can design more informed and impactful interventions.

We also argue that addressing the measurement challenges discussed previously is essential for advancing the study of affective polarization. This will require renewed efforts in scale development, with the goal of reaching greater consensus around appropriate and validated measures. Establishing common measurement standards would facilitate more accurate crossstudy comparisons and strengthen the quality of meta-analyses. To this end, scholars might consider pairing more established measures of affective polarization with newer instruments, allowing for direct comparisons of their validity and sensitivity. Progress toward shared measurement practices may also be supported through cross-disciplinary conferences and working groups aimed at developing a common language across fields, while involving practitioners and policymakers to ensure measurement choices remain relevant to key stakeholders.

In addition, we encourage researchers to more systematically examine the longitudinal efficacy of polarization interventions in more realistic settings. Doing so will strengthen the robustness of findings-not only by improving comparability of effect sizes across studies through shared measurement practices, but also by clarifying whether interventions produce meaningful and durable effects outside laboratories and online survey environments. This distinction is critical, as interventions that succeed in tightly controlled settings may become less effective when implemented in real-world contexts (IJzerman et al., 2020). These efforts would ultimately benefit practitioners seeking to translate research into practice, while also enhancing the scientific and societal impact of affective polarization research.

We also need far more cross-cultural research (especially in non-WEIRD societies) to assess the generalizability of interventions in societies with differing levels of polarization, differing cultural values, and diverse political and media ecosystems. Such intervention testing can reveal context-dependent mechanisms, boundary conditions, and new moderating factors. This work is not only scientifically valuable but also ethically imperative. Overgeneralizing from WEIRD samples risks developing interventions that are ineffective, inappropriate, or even harmful when applied elsewhere. We encourage scholars to recruit more diverse samples from non-WEIRD societies while also considering the cultural and historical context within those cultures by collaborating with researchers from these societies.

An increasingly common and promising approach to addressing many of these concerns is the use of mega-studies. Mega-studies bring together interdisciplinary teams of researchers-often from around the world-to tackle a shared research question across multiple countries and contexts (e.g., Van Bavel et al., 2022). These high-powered studies, frequently involving tens of thousands of participants, are well suited to testing intervention effects cross-culturally and in non-WEIRD contexts, offering valuable insight into both global patterns and cross-national similarities. They also foster interdisciplinary collaboration, strengthening methodological rigor and theoretical integration. However, mega-studies are not a panacea and can introduce their own ethical challenges.

When led primarily by researchers based in WEIRD contexts, they risk reproducing Western-centric assumptions in research design, measurement, and theory. They may also reproduce inequitable research hierarchies if scholars from the Global South are primarily engaged as local implementers or data collectors while researchers from wealthier institutions retain intellectual leadership, decision-making authority, and recognition. Meaningful international collaboration should therefore involve local scholars throughout the research process-from conceptualization and intervention design to analysis, interpretation, authorship, and dissemination.

Despite these limitations, mega-studies offer substantial benefits when implemented thoughtfully. By pooling resources, researchers can assemble larger and more diverse international samples than any single team could feasibly collect, making these efforts both cost-effective and time efficient. They also create durable global research networks-benefiting the field broadly and providing early-career researchers with opportunities for high-impact collaboration, international visibility, and professional development. We suggest that mega-studies represent one promising avenue for evaluating the efficacy of affective polarization interventions.

Recommendation 2: Research should engage practitioners to co-create interventions that connect research to practice

Collaborating with practitioners enables researchers to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions in naturalistic settings. Such partnerships create mutual benefits: researchers gain opportunities to test interventions beyond the laboratory, while practitioners access evidencebased methodologies to inform their ongoing work with diverse communities. Yet, despite these advantages, academic-practitioner collaborations remain relatively rare. The following examples illustrate how such partnerships can take different forms-ranging from co-developing practical resources to studying practitioners' existing approaches to jointly implementing and evaluating interventions in the field.

1. Co-creating practical tools for use in the field

Beckerman and colleagues (2025) exemplify collaborative efforts centered on knowledge translation. In partnership with community leaders, they developed a freely accessible toolkit designed to help practitioners address polarization and conflict during community crises. This resource integrates recent scholarly advances in depolarization strategies with practitioners' experiential knowledge of what is feasible and effective in local contexts. Their work illustrates how combining theoretical insights with on-the-ground expertise can produce practical, actionable tools that enable communities to apply research-based approaches in real time.

2. Studying how practitioners already engage with theory

Williams and colleagues (2019) took a different approach by examining how religious education practitioners implicitly draw on psychological theory in their everyday work. Their study investigates the extent to which teachers apply principles from Intergroup Contact Theory (Allport, 1954) to promote positive community relations. This example demonstrates how academic research can uncover both points of alignment and gaps between theory and practice, generating insights that can inform future practitioner training and intervention design.

3. Co-implementing and evaluating interventions in real-world settings

A third model of collaboration involves academics and practitioners jointly conducting applied research. In collaboration with the organization Narrative 4, Kubin and colleagues (2025) evaluated a storytelling-based empathy intervention implemented in schools. Using controlled measures within authentic educational environments, the research team found that narrative exchange strengthened empathy and perspective-taking while reducing affective polarization. This case illustrates the value of embedded research partnerships, in which scientific rigor is combined with practitioners' contextual knowledge to address affective polarization.

Taken together, these examples highlight the range and promise of academic-practitioner collaboration. Whether through co-producing resources, systematically analyzing existing practice, or embedding interventions within applied settings, each model advances our understanding of how to translate research into meaningful societal impact. Strengthening these partnerships will require greater institutional (and financial) support for co-design and iterative evaluation. We strongly encourage future initiatives that build sustained networks between scholars and practitioners-allowing theory to inform practice, and practice to refine theory-in the shared pursuit of reducing affective polarization.

Recommendation 3: Explore digital and social media interventions to address barriers of participation, resources, and scalability

The practical barriers identified above highlight a central challenge for polarization interventions: even effective approaches may have limited societal impact if they require substantial time, resources, or voluntary participation to reach broad populations. Digital and social media environments offer one potential way to address these constraints (Kubin & von Sikorski, 2023). Rather than requiring individuals to attend dedicated in-person interventions or be motivated to actively seek cross-partisan interactions, interventions can potentially be embedded within environments where people already encounter political information and political opponents.

Their broad reach and comparatively low delivery costs may make digital interventions more scalable than many intensive face-to-face interventions. This possibility is particularly noteworthy because social media is more commonly examined as a contributor to polarization through misinformation (Vosoughi et al., 2018), moral outrage (Carpenter et al., 2020), and ideologically homogeneous information environments (Levendusky, 2013; Tucker et al., 2018). Yet the same environments in which polarization develops may also provide opportunities for intervention.

Similar to other digital interventions focused on healing intergroup divisions (e.g., Bruneau et al., 2021; Porat et al., 2020), social media interventions show promise for reducing affective polarization by targeting the psychological and behavioral processes that sustain partisan animosity (Littman et al., 2023). Social media interventions that encourage perspective-taking and narrative sharing, for example, have been shown to increase empathy and mutual understanding across partisan lines (Kubin et al., 2023). Other approaches focus on modifying online behaviors and information environments: behavioral nudges that encourage users to disengage from hyperpartisan influencers can substantially improve attitudes toward political outgroups (Rathje et al., 2024), while "polarization warnings" that flag divisive content appear to indirectly reduce affective polarization by increasing users' awareness of manipulative or inflammatory messaging (Kubin & von Sikorski, 2024). Together, these findings suggest social media platforms-often viewed primarily as drivers of polarization-can also serve as powerful venues for scalable, low-cost interventions when designs are grounded in theory and empirical evidence.

Digital and social media interventions offer particular promise for addressing several SCOPE barriers, especially those concerning reach, cost, scalability, and ecological validity. At the same time, their growing use raises important questions for future research that map onto the broader SCOPE framework. Researchers should examine whether effects persist over time (Holliday et al., 2025), how interventions can be adapted to differences in platform use and digital access across populations (Kubin & McKeown, 2026), and how partnerships with technology companies can facilitate implementation at scale (Roozenbeek et al., 2022). Further work should also consider how researchers can responsibly address issues of consent, privacy, and collaboration with social media platforms (e.g., Heiss & Freiling, 2026). Addressing these questions may help realize the considerable potential of digital interventions for translating polarization research into practice.

One particularly important question concerns the institutional conditions needed to implement these interventions at scale. Access to platforms is often restricted, and the commercial priorities of social media companies-such as maximizing engagement-may conflict with academic or public-interest goals. Nevertheless, partnerships between researchers and social media platforms demonstrate that collaboration is possible (Rajkumar et al., 2022) foot_3 . Policy frameworks could help further incentivize companies to collaborate with researchers to reduce affective polarization via their digital platforms. Just as regulation has driven social media companies to address the spread of misinformation on their platforms (e.g., the EU's Digital Services Act; Car, 2025), similar initiatives could encourage or require platforms to support research on polarization, and to implement evidence-based depolarization tools.

In short, digital and social media interventions offer a promising avenue for addressing key SCOPE barriers by increasing the reach, scalability, and real-world relevance of polarization interventions. Collaboration among researchers, practitioners, technology companies, and policymakers may help transform digital spaces often blamed for deepening divides into part of the solution.

Recommendation 4: Scholars should further grapple with the ethics of polarization interventions

Ethical reflection should extend beyond whether an intervention successfully reduces affective polarization to consider both the outcomes researchers seek and the processes through which interventions are developed and implemented. As discussed in Barrier 5, reducing affective polarization is not always inherently desirable. Efforts to encourage partisans to "meet in the middle" may overlook asymmetries in radicalization (Hacker & Pierson, 2015), power inequalities, and the potentially constructive role of disagreement and negative emotions in motivating political action.

Clarifying intervention goals also requires specifying whose animosity toward whom should be reduced. Affective polarization may operate horizontally (toward fellow citizens) or vertically (toward political elites), with potentially different democratic consequences. Reducing hostility toward ordinary citizens may promote social cohesion (Orazani et al., 2023), whereas reducing negative attitudes toward powerful actors could sometimes weaken accountability (Kubin & von Sikorski, 2026). The appropriateness of an intervention may also depend on the target: reducing hostility between supporters of mainstream parties raises different ethical considerations than encouraging warmer attitudes toward extremist or anti-democratic actors. Likewise, interventions may carry different implications across political systems depending on factors such as democratic stability, political violence, and power asymmetries. Researchers should therefore consider not simply whether an intervention reduces animosity, but whose animosity is being reduced, toward whom, and whether doing so is desirable in that particular political context. Finally, reducing affective polarization should not itself be treated as the ultimate marker of success. As highlighted by the ethical barriers above, less animosity does not necessarily translate into greater trust, cooperation, or improved democratic attitudes. Future research should therefore evaluate interventions across a broader range of outcomes and consider which outcomes are most desirable in different political contexts.

Recognizing the Limits of Polarization Interventions and Their Development

Conclusion

Affective polarization requires interventions that are not only effective but also scalable.

Although the literature has made progress, many approaches remain limited by recurring statistical, cross-disciplinary, generalizability, practical, and ethical barriers. The SCOPE Barriers Framework provides a structured way to identify and address these constraints, shifting attention from whether interventions can work in ideal settings to when, where, and for whom they are viable in practice. Advancing this agenda will require not only methodological innovation, but also institutional support, practitioner engagement, and sustained ethical reflection.
