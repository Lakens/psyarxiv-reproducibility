Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Patients within the neurodivergent spectrum, high-ability profiles, and other high processing cost phenotypes (HPCPh) frequently present a clinical paradox: marked cognitive domains alongside debilitating functional collapse, burnout, dysautonomia, and migrating multisystem symptoms. Standard cross-sectional laboratory investigations against population references routinely prove inconclusive or trigger fragmented, iatrogenic interventions. Here, we define the Third Allostasis: an evolutionarily conserved biophysical regime wherein the organism displaces peripheral homeostasis and lowers systemic throughput to defend central nervous system membrane viability when environmental and informational demands approach the kinetic limits of transmembrane ion clearance. Through within-person longitudinal follow-up of five adult HPCPh trajectories-spanning stepped recovery from decade-long containment, live entry into acute biological helplessness, endothelial-natriuretic decompression, cognitive restoration, and exit from chronic matrix dysautonomia-we demonstrate how this regime becomes clinically visible using four routine laboratory instruments. We introduce accessible arithmetic tools for any consulting room: the red-cell hydration index (Ht -3 × Hb) and two "Rules of Three" (OsmP@290 and Na@290) that unmask hidden plasma osmolal gaps driven by uncharged organic osmolytes, even when raw osmolality and sodium remain within normal limits. Crucially, while an open osmolal gap signals fast-axis intercompartmental shift, clinical direction requires "whole-column reading" across slower coupled buffers, including circadian cortisol, tissue thyroid gating (FT3/rT3), iron-ferritin dynamics, prolactin, and monocytes. Recognizing the Third Allostasis shifts clinical practice from treating isolated organ abnormalities to reading the organism's adaptive effort as a whole, guiding contextual adjustments that relieve central overload and restore functional vitality.

Introduction

The model presented here emerged progressively from naturalistic clinical follow-up: systematic observation and long-term tracking of clinical and laboratory profiles in patients who did not respond to standard protocols, nor fitted easily into any single diagnostic label. In prior work we have described them as high processing cost phenotypes (HPCPh), a transdiagnostic perspective that reconnects neurodevelopmental trajectories and atypical presentations with developmental biology, systems neuroscience and allostatic physiology 1, 2 . These patients commonly show a clinical paradox: marked high-performance domains alongside impaired day-to-day functioning, with clinical patterns that span depression-like states, burnout, dysautonomia and migrating multisystem symptoms (skin, gut, muscle, autonomic, vestibular) that leave investigations inconclusive 3, 4 . Many are currently classified within the neurodivergent spectrum: autism, ADHD, twice-exceptionality, high ability and giftedness, with or without learning disabilities.

The challenge of understanding the multiple systems involved and the diversity of clinical presentations has always been accompanied by the perception of common features pointing towards a connection that explains clinical and laboratory presentations across the cases. Two findings organize the common ground in these cases: an intense, rapid and atypical biological response to changes in the nature or intensity of informational load, and the regression of symptoms and laboratory parameters when the external context was adjusted to each patient's architecture. Organizing those parameters longitudinally, and reading them within the person, revealed an allostatic multisystem dynamic that is completely missed when laboratory results are read one by one, against population references 5.

Classical allostatic overload comprises energetic deficit (type 1) and social-conflict stress (type 2) 6 . Here we define a third: a biophysical overload and the conserved regime that counters it, in which HPCPh lower neural and systemic throughput, despite intact energy supply. From an evolutionary standpoint, synaptic excitability did not emerge de novo for neural computation, but rather through the exaptation of ancestral osmoregulatory mechanisms originally evolved to counteract the Gibbs-Donnan equilibrium in primordial oceans: to prevent lethal osmotic swelling driven by impermeant intracellular polyanions, early cells established steep transmembrane ionic gradients; a critical failure there kills the cell in minutes [7] [8] [9].

Every action potential and every excitatory postsynaptic current admits Na⁺ and releases K⁺ at no immediate cost; the bill is paid afterwards by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, which consumes most of the signalling ATP of grey matter 10, 11 . Hyperconnected microcircuits and sustained parallel firing in HPCPh can push the rate of Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ influx and of interstitial K⁺ accumulation beyond the maximal turnover of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and the buffering capacity of astrocytes, approaching the threshold of spreading depolarization, first described in 1944 by the Brazilian neurophysiologist Aristides Leão [12] [13] [14] [15] . What the organism then faces is a configuration that signals a concrete and immediate threat to the function of the CNS: a biophysical problem met first at the cellular level, by feed-forward programmes far older than vertebrates.

The membrane budget has been a non-negotiable condition of life since the first cells 16 . The compatibleosmolyte strategy that defends it is shared by bacteria, archaea and marine invertebrates 7, 17 . An architecture with a narrow margin of trade-offs therefore lives with a permanent threat: one set not by primary mitochondrial failure, tissue hypoxia or substrate deprivation, but by the instantaneous kinetics of transmembrane transport and by the maximum rate at which extracellular K⁺ can be cleared per unit of time. It is a matter of rate, not of fuel 18, 19.

The threat is always close by and depends directly on the processing load imposed by the environment. Hence the central allostatic task, whose regulatory purpose is normally guided by stable patterns of systemic homeostasis (T4 and TSH; iron, ferritin and inflammation; glycaemia and insulin; osmolality and volume against vasopressin and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system), is displaced to guarantee the integrity of the central nervous system 20, 21 . External performance is sacrificed to the defence of membrane viability (executive collapse, fatigue, fog, impaired memory, avoidance of novelty), peripheral feedback is subordinated to that defence, and throughput is lowered as a limit on information processing.

The water and ion axis is the primary line of that defence, but it is not the only one, because the source of the load is external, unpredictable and may not relent. When the environment stays adverse, containment extends from the membrane to the set points that govern the whole organism: cortisol and the circadian rhythm, thyroid conversion, protein synthesis, iron handling, immune tone and the cellular memory of previous states are re-tuned, in a coordinated cascade, so that the central nervous system remains viable at the expense of everything else 22, 23 . Read one value at a time, each of these shifts looks like a mild and separate abnormality, or passes as normal, and in practice they are often treated as independent failures of the gland or organ concerned, with strategies that often add to the very load the regime is trying to contain.

The serum osmolal gap is the difference between the osmolality measured by the laboratory, which is the real number, and the osmolality calculated from sodium, glucose and urea; it opens when particles that the calculation does not count are present in plasma 24 . The osmolal gap is the most direct trace of the primary mechanism, but it certainly does not stand alone. As the cases presented illustrate, it appears in the company of "all the rest": a multisystemic line of defence that is always on guard. Five longitudinal adult series, offering different perspectives on the phenomenon, show its dynamics empirically. The gap emerged from within-person longitudinal laboratory follow-up, but it was neither the first pattern we noticed nor the only one that can be made visible with the instruments of any clinical laboratory.

A finding typically seen only in severe ICU patients with multiple organ failure, in alcohol and other intoxications, in diabetic ketoacidosis, in renal failure and after osmotic infusions such as mannitol was chosen on purpose as the centre of this paper, written so that the findings could be translated immediately to any consulting room: not as a certainty, but as one more question, opening a window where none is easy to find 25, 26 . The gap represents an effort the organism is making to survive, and illustrates its magnitude: an effort that is more often than not taken for granted or simply ignored. It also illustrates one of the most primitive manoeuvres of the cell, one that allowed life to evolve and, in humans, to consciously experience it.

Patients and methods

This is a retrospective, observational, within-person longitudinal study of patients followed in a psychiatric practice who match the HPCPh profile 1, 2 . The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital de Clínicas de Porto Alegre (CAAE 99556626.7.0000.5327). All laboratory tests were ordered, and all clinical decisions made, strictly on clinical grounds; the decision to report came afterwards. Data are pseudonymized, and no name, date or personal identifier appears in the text, figures or tables. This paper reports five cases selected from a series of twelve previously described 27 , to show the dynamics of the Third Allostasis through the opening of the plasma osmolal gap and the multisystem patterns that accompanied it across transitional states, in response to adjustments of external and contextual load (adequate reduction and modulation of the load, or a stressor or change with no escape). All five patients gave written informed consent.

Scope and anonymization of the data

To avoid identification, the data are restricted to what is strictly necessary to show the biological mechanisms: calendar dates, age and sex are not reported, and no reference interval that depends on sex or age is shown (such tests are given as values only). Clinical history, psychiatric or somatic diagnoses and pharmacological treatments are not reported; the cases were selected so that the absence of this information introduces no bias, and the context of each patient is stated solely in technical terms of allostatic load, throughput demand and environmental controllability. Time is given as relative intervals from a zero stated per patient.

Reading the biology within the person

The unit of reading is the collection: all laboratory results from the same day and moment. Every value, and the physiological systems the values compose, are read together as a single vertical column, against the person's own longitudinal baseline and against the simultaneous values of the same draw 5, 28 . As a routine in the clinic, patients are advised to have laboratory tests collected within two hours of waking 29 . Longitudinal reviews of the laboratory records were made with a diligent effort to register the time, the laboratory, the method of measurement of each analyte and the laboratory's reference interval at that point. Serum and urine osmolality were measured by freezing-point depression (cryoscopy) and sodium by direct potentiometry, as printed in each individual report; where a report stated an osmolality calculated by the laboratory, the value is used and marked † 30, 31.

Questions typically asked to evaluate a rapid shift between collections: (1) does the collection itself explain it (acute inflammation via CRP, a discordant dual result, a pre-analytical note or a change of laboratory); (2) does textbook physiology explain a shift of that magnitude in that interval; (3) does a known stable condition of the person explain it; if none does, the shift is recorded as a state transition or change of context 32 . A collection with C-reactive protein ≥ 5 mg/L (high-sensitivity CRP where reported) is shown, flagged and not read as cold containment 33, 34.

Osmolal gap: to exclude known causal factors, the first question includes fasting glucose and blood urea, which enter the calculated osmolality equation, and the classical exogenous or pathological causes of an osmolal gap (ethanol or toxic alcohols, ketonaemia, lactataemia, renal failure, or pseudohyponatraemia from severe hyperlipidaemia or paraproteinaemia), verified in the same collection and clinical record 24, 25.

Four routine instruments, four physical questions

Concerning the osmolal gap, the reading depends on four routine instruments that answer four different physical questions 35, 36 (Fig. 1.1c): (1) a freezing-point osmometer measures the depression of the freezing point of plasma water, and so counts every dissolved particle, charged or not; (2) a direct ion- selective electrode measures the activity of free Na⁺ only; it is blind to any uncharged particle and, being direct, is also immune to the plasma water-fraction artefacts that affect indirect (diluted) electrodes 37 ; (3) a reagent strip for urine specific gravity reads a colour change driven by ionic strength; it sees ions and nothing else 38, 39 , which is why a dilute-looking strip can coexist with a cryoscopic urine osmolality that is not dilute at all; (4) a haematology analyser sizes red cells after re-equilibration in an isotonic diluent, so the mean cell volume reports the cell's intrinsic water content rather than the osmolality of the plasma it came from 40, 41, 42.

Simple indices for a complex phenomenon: Ht -3×Hb and the two Rules of Three

This series, and the model that emerged from describing what was happening and the physiology that plausibly explains the pattern, came from the very real effort of treating patients who had been resistant to standard protocols. Establishing methods that any clinician could use to assist their patients was therefore always a goal. The haematocrit-haemoglobin index, with zero as the line that matters, and the two Rules of Three were born from that effort.

The Ht -3×Hb index is an example of a strategy that can be used in any clinical setting with one of the most common laboratory tests, the blood count, as a proxy for the state of intercompartmental water distribution. Haemoglobin is measured as mass per volume of blood; haematocrit is the volume occupied by the red cells after the analyser has re-equilibrated them in an isotonic diluent; their ratio is the mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC). At an MCHC of 33.3 g/dL, haematocrit equals three times haemoglobin, so haematocrit minus three times haemoglobin is zero 43 . A positive value means more cell volume per gram of haemoglobin: red cells carrying more water. A negative value means denser, thinner cells. Because the diluent is isotonic, the water the analyser reports is the cell's own, set by the solutes the cell holds and by its own volume regulation, not by the osmolality of the plasma at the moment of the draw.

The two Rules of Three. The laboratory measures the osmolality of serum directly, by freezing it, and can also calculate what it should be from sodium, glucose and urea. The difference is the gap: particles the calculation does not know about. When those particles are present, even when the measured osmolality stays within or close to the population reference, the sodium value is no longer what would be needed to account for it, because other particles are represented in the measured osmolality. Two Rules of Three show this immediately: (1) the first asks what the measured osmolality would be if the calculated value sat at 290 mOsm/kg; (2) the second asks where sodium would be if the measured osmolality were 290 mOsm/kg within that column.

Formulas applied in the analyses

• Calculated serum osmolality (OsmCalc) = 2 × Na + + glucose/18 + urea/6 (with Na + in mEq/L, and fasting glucose and blood urea in mg/dL), computed only when sodium, glucose and urea come from the same collection 44, 45.

• Serum osmolal gap = measured serum osmolality (OsmP) -OsmCalc. Where glucose or urea was not ordered in that draw, an estimated gap = OsmP -2 × Na + -k is given and marked e, where k is the person's own longitudinal median (glucose/18 + urea/6) across complete draws (Patient 1, k = 9.6; Patient 2, k = 9.1; Patient 3, k = 13.1; Patient 4, k = 11.7; Patient 5, k = 8.9 mOsm/kg).

• First Rule of Three on measured serum osmolality (OsmP@290) = 290 × OsmP ÷ OsmCalc (or 290 × OsmP ÷ [2 × Na + + k] when estimated, marked e), which answers what the osmometer would read if calculated ionic osmolality sat at 290 mOsm/kg.

• Second Rule of Three, on sodium (Na@290) = 290 × Na⁺ ÷ OsmP along the measured route, and 290 × Na⁺ ÷ OsmCalc along the calculated route. The measured route projects sodium onto the number the osmometer read: it keeps the proportion between sodium and all the particles counted, so it falls when uncounted particles are present. The calculated route projects sodium onto an osmolality built from sodium itself, so it stays near 140 mEq/L whatever the gap: it is the control. The difference between the two routes is the share of the measured osmolality that sodium does not account for.

• Yielded sodium = 2 × (140 -Na@290, measured route), which expresses in mOsm/kg, counting Na + with its accompanying anion, how far Na@290 by the measured route falls below 140; it equals the serum osmolal gap exactly when OsmP is 290 mOsm/kg and glucose/18 + urea/6 is 10 mOsm/kg.

• Urine-to-serum phase ratio = measured urine osmolality ÷ OsmP of the same collection (<1.0, free water released; ~1.5, equilibrium; ≥2.0, water held under vasopressin command) 46.

• Δgap = current gap minus the immediately preceding available gap.

• Red-cell hydration index = haematocrit -3 × haemoglobin (Ht -3×Hb), which equals zero at a mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) of 33.3 g/dL and is read against zero with mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH) and red-cell distribution width (RDW) as controls 43.

• Anion gap = Na + -(Cl -+ HCO3 -) 47.

Each derived quantity is computed from the printed values and rounded half away from zero. The numbers 290 mOsm/kg and 140 mEq/L serve as fixed physical reference marks, not population means.

The whole column as the unit of direction

The configurations described across the cases are possibilities, not rules: the direction of a column is always read from the system as a whole, within the person, and in relation to the columns that precede it. The background matters: the same movement reads differently depending on how pervasive and how long the containment has been, and entries and exits happen in steps, some changes being characteristic of one step and not of another. The iron trio (ferritin, serum iron and transferrin saturation) is one example: it has several possible combinations, not a single pattern. Absorption may be braked at entry; ferritin may be retained as a vault without inflammation; iron may be allowed back into the system and used as the brakes allow; and, once absorption returns, ferritin may take on a finer regulatory role.

Reference values for the gap

With the same equation in consecutive hospital patients, the serum osmolal gap is centred at -2 ± 6 mOsm/kg 48 , and its within-person biological variation in healthy fasting adults is small 49 ; a gap ≥ 10 mOsm/kg lies at the upper boundary of the 95% reference interval and serves as our transition mark. The gap measures the fluidity of the fast axis, that is, that uncharged solutes are crossing compartments. It does not say in which direction the organism is travelling.

Direction is read from the slower buffers that the fast axis serves. Entry is the column settling: water held (ratio ≥ 2), axes suppressed, iron braked, insulin quiet. Reconstruction is the column releasing: water let go or natriuresis, axes returning, iron allowed back into the system and used as the brakes allow, IGF-1 rising when the person is recovering from longer anti-anabolic states, monocytes waking if they had been suppressed under prolonged overload, working insulin, a warm but non-inflammatory hs-CRP. The gap says that the column is moving; the column, and its relation to the columns that precede it, says where. This paper focuses on the osmolal gap to show the dynamics of the Third Allostasis; the multisystem patterns that change with it are mentioned where they are needed to show the movement of a transition, and were described previously in the report of twelve cases 27.

Patient 4: the gap cannot tell the story without the context, the whole-column valence

• By day 143, Na + shifted to 136 as urine diluted (OsmU 652, ratio 2.26), opening a +5.6 gap unmasked by OsmP@290 jumping to 295.8 (raw OsmP 288; Ht-3×Hb -1.1) as IGF-1 surged to 287 ng/mL and dysautonomia symptoms diminished significantly, alongside cognitive improvement, leading to a consistent functional gain.

Across all 5 cases, as we have been observing systematically in HPCPh, the line that always converges crosses the common ground that led us to organize the data in the first place: an intense, rapid and atypical biological response to changes in the nature or intensity of informational load, and the regression of symptoms and laboratory parameters when the external context was adjusted to each patient's architecture. Organizing those parameters longitudinally, and reading them within the person, made the Third Allostatic Load evident to us. The strategies to unveil it, such as the Rules of Three and the Ht -3×Hb index, were developed to make it visible in an easy way that can reach people who could benefit from the perspective (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Five patients, opposite trajectories, one invariant biophysics. Whether leaving prolonged containment in steps (Patient 1), entering a biological configuration named here "acute helplessness" under inescapable load (Patient 2), decompressing via the endothelial-natriuretic arm (Patient 3), resolving executive arrest in 4.6 months (Patient 4) or unlocking chronic peripheral-matrix dysautonomia (Patient 5), the systematically observed pattern draws one trajectory: the osmolal gap opened whenever uncharged solutes crossed the extracellular space and closed in held states, while the Rule of Three (OsmP@290 and Na@290) and the vertical column resolve magnitude and direction 16.

All five cases showed major biological turns in response to changes of context that consistently altered the load pressing on the individual architecture, without any pharmacological intervention or structural disease that could offer an alternative explanation. That is what we propose as the Third Allostasis: the pervasive and costly effort, paid at the cell membrane, that HPCPh make to sustain the function of the central nervous system against adverse environmental patterns 11, 75 . By showing these mechanisms we hope not to provide definitive answers but to raise many questions. We have treated some of these patients for decades and never saw the pattern until a convergence of inexplicable findings and many inconclusive consultations made it progressively visible. It means years of providing the best care we could, and yet not what the patient needed and deserved. But then we did not know. And that is a fact. Reading the whole column also unifies the iron, thyroid, prolactin and monocyte signals that otherwise trigger iatrogenic treatment 77, 78 . Iron braked at entry (Patients 2 and 5), ferritin retained without inflammation as a protective vault (Patients 1 and 4) and iron, ferritin and saturation consumed together during IGF-1-driven reconstruction (Patients 1 and 3) 79, 80 ; thyroid hormone gated at the tissue level, with low FT3/rT3 while TSH stays quiet 81, 82 ; prolactin rising as a brake; and monocytes waking in reconstruction: every marker acts as a coupled governor. Recognizing the Third Allostasis through four routine instruments, the Rule of Three (OsmP@290 and Na@290) and whole-column reading may replace fragmented organ diagnoses and iatrogenic counter-measures, focusing the lens where it belongs: on the whole organism, on the relation between its systems and on their adaptive dynamics when the problem is the third allostatic load. It then becomes possible to work on stabilizing what is really pushing the patient's systems towards shutdown, which may free the potential for a healthier and well-deserved life.
