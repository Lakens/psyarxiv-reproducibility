Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

Abstract

Obtaining accurate measures of changing abilities is essential for online adaptive learning systems (ALS) to provide learners with instructional material and practice items at the appropriate level. Despite the large data volume in ALS, focusing on a single individual at a specific moment yields limited data, reducing measurement precision. To improve precision, it has been proposed to incorporate response time (RT) data into measurement (Klinkenberg et al., 2011). Using data from an ALS for primary school mathematics Math Garden (Straatemeier, 2014), we compare different models that incorporate RTs and can be used for ability tracking with each other and with the benchmark Rasch model, in which ability is measured based only on the accuracy of the responses, in terms of next-response prediction. We also contrast these empirical findings with simulation results that match the empirical setup but where the generating model matches the way in which RTs are incorporated into measurement, to study what can be gained under ideal circumstances. Our results show that while theoretical gains are large, in the studied empirical setting RTs at best provide a modest improvement that was not fully consistent across domains and depended on the reward system implemented in the ALS.

Implications of the results for the choice of the measurement model in ALS are discussed.

Enhancing measurement in adaptive learning systems: How much can we gain from response times?

Introduction

One of the most impactful and promising ambitions in educational innovation is the move toward large-scale personalized learning, facilitated by the emergence of online adaptive learning systems (ALS), also known as adaptive learning environments or computer adaptive practice systems (Ennouamani & Mahani, 2017). These systems are designed to dynamically adjust the level of practice and instructional content according to each learner's abilities, as well as other potential characteristics such as learning preferences, interests, and motivation, fostering an engaging environment (Bernacki et al., 2021; Pelánek, 2025; Van Schoors et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2019). Examples of such systems include Franel, an item-based intelligent tutoring system for French and Dutch languages (Desmet et al., 2006), Outline Maps, an adaptive practice system for geography facts (Papoušek et al., 2016), and Math Garden, an online learning system for primary school mathematics (Straatemeier, 2014). Depending on the system, it can also provide diagnostic feedback, such as which skills and abilities the learner is deficient in, navigational support regarding which skills to work on, and learning resources for improving these skills.

To effectively optimize feedback, instruction, and educational materials, it is essential to have continuously updated, accurate, and reliable assessments of students' evolving abilities. Consequently, measurement becomes a crucial aspect of ALS. However, measuring change is inherently complex and the adaptive nature of ALS, combined with the necessity of serving thousands of learners who require ongoing updates to their ability estimates, adds further complexity. These characteristics present significant challenges to traditional psychometric models and algorithms (see e.g., Deonovic et al., 2020; Wauters et al., 2012).

To address these challenges, rating systems such as the Elo rating system (ERS; Elo, 1978) have been used to monitor abilities in ALS (Klinkenberg et al., 2011; Pelánek et al., 2017). In the ERS the students' abilities are updated based on every student-item interaction, based on the difference between the observed and expected response: where θjt and θj(t-1) are the estimates of ability of student j at timepoints t and t -1, respectively, and K is the step-size parameter.

Smaller K-values can be selected to reduce noise, while larger values allow to track changes better. The expected response at timepoint t is calculated using the estimated ability at timepoint t -1 under the chosen measurement model. A simple measurement model often used in this context is the Rasch model (RM; Rasch, 1960), in which the probability of a correct response only depends on the difference between ability of the student and the difficulty of the item.

The difficulty of the items can either be updated alongside the abilities of the students, or fixed at pre-calibrated values.

The ERS provides a computationally efficient, easy-to-implement, and transparent way of tracking student abilities. Nonetheless, a significant challenge persists: Despite the large volume of data generated by ALS, focusing on a specific individual at a given moment yields limited data, making it difficult to achieve high measurement precision using information from response accuracy alone. Additionally, items with relatively high correct response probabilities (0.7-0.8) are typically selected to boost motivation and engagement (Jansen et al., 2013). Consequently, the response accuracy data would provide less information about ability compared to settings where items are presented in the statistically optimal way with a probability of a correct response close to 0.5 (as is done in computerized adaptive testing aimed at optimizing test information in a personalized way; see e.g., van der Linden, Glas, et al., 2000). Hence, selecting items that do not provide a lot of information makes the speed of convergence slower. For example, in the cold-start problem, estimates of abilities move away from their initial values slower (Bolsinova et al., 2025), requiring a lot of responses before the estimates get close to their true values. Mismatches between the true ability level of the student and their estimated level of ability can therefore result in long periods for which the difficulty level of the presented items is not well-aligned with the ability level of student, causing issues for the ALS such as for example reducing student motivation, risk of dropout, and a slower learning rate due to presenting misaligned learning material.

To improve measurement precision in ALS and to speed up the convergence of the ability estimates, it has been proposed to utilize response times (RTs), which are easy to collect and have many potential uses in ALS (Pelánek, 2024). Klinkenberg et al. (2011) proposed a modification of the standard ERS, which in addition to the response accuracy The SRTM makes strict assumptions about the exact way in which ability and RTs are related to each other, and when these assumptions are violated this may lead to bias. This suggests that the exact specification of the SRTM might not always be the most optimal way to model the link between ability and RTs, and that it is possible for other similar models to result in a better measurement (quantified through next-response prediction) than the base SRTM. For example, some of the assumptions of the SRTM can be relaxed while still keeping the general idea that fast correct responses are indicative of higher ability than slow correct responses (Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2021), or RTs can be incorporated into the scoring rule in an alternative way (Vermeiren et al., 2025).

Regardless of which specific scoring-rule-based model is adopted, ability can be monitored using a version of the ERS where instead of the binary item score X, a different score and its expected value are used: where S jit is the model-specific score of student j to item i at timepoint t.

In this way, RTs can be incorporated into ability tracking in different ways, based on different assumptions (e.g., more or less strict) about the relationship between ability and RT.

While there is a wide range of ways of modeling RTs (e.g., Bolsinova & Tijmstra, 2018; Molenaar et al., 2015; Van der Linden, 2007), in this project we focus on the scoring-rule-based approach, because it makes ability estimation transparent and easy to explain to stakeholders (students, parents, teachers) as opposed to using black box approaches that may lack interpretability (e.g., Zhang et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2026) and data-driven statistical approaches which do not provide motivation of why RTs should influence estimation of ability is a certain way (e.g., in the hierarchical modeling framework of van der Linden, 2007, how RTs influence ability measurement depends on the estimated correlation between ability and speed). Furthermore, the scoring-rule-based approach allows to keep the model relatively simple as opposed to most alternatives, which is important to make on-the-fly estimation of ability within an ALS feasible.

In this study, we compare different models that incorporate RTs and can be used for monitoring ability with ERS (i.e., models based on scoring rules) with each other and with the benchmark RM in which ability is updated based only on accuracy data. The logic for the setup is that if including RTs into the model improves measurement of ability, then it should increase prediction accuracy of next responses based on the ability estimates. Since model misfit may introduce construct-irrelevant variance to the estimates, it is not a given that using the models with scoring rules that include RT as well as response accuracy will outperform the RM in terms of prediction accuracy, due to possibly introducing bias in the estimates that outweigh potential gains in precision.

Thus, we suggest to use prediction accuracy (rather than model-based assessments of precision of measurement) as the tool to assess whether including RTs into the scoring rule improves measurement in ALS, and to study which scoring rule seems to perform best in practice.

We use data from an online learning system called Math Garden for primary school mathematics, described in more detail in the Methods section. The system has two interesting properties that make it an interesting case study. First, gamification elements in the system focused on explicitly communicating the scoring rule to the students by showing the number of coins that they receive or lose based on the correctness of their response and the RT. The measurement model corresponding to that communicated scoring rule, which we will refer to as the normative RT model, was used for item selection when the data were collected. Second, a change in the scoring rule was made in 2019, moving from the SRTM to only differentiating the scores for correct responses based on RTs (Vermeiren et al., 2025), rather than for both correct and incorrect responses. This intervention essentially presents a natural experiment, and hence we obtained two datasets: one from when the old scoring rule was used and another from when the new rule was adopted as a normative scoring rule. Since our objective is to evaluate the performance of a wider range of relevant scoring rules, in this study we also retroactively fit models to the data that differ from these normative models.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, we describe the online learning system and the datasets that we used in this study. Second, we describe and motivate the scoring-rule-based models which we used. Third, we describe the data analysis strategy. Fourth, we present the results, describing the differences between the scoring rules on the different parts of the data. Fifth, we present a simulation study, in which we mimic the conditions of the empirical example but generate data using the two normative models used in Math Garden, in order to study the improvements in measurement under ideal conditions. Finally, we discuss the implications of our results for the choice of a measurement model in ALS.

The idea behind working with dichotomized RT instead of continuous RT is that the linearity assumptions for the relationship between RTs and ability made by the SRTM may be too restrictive in some settings. The DSRTM is less restrictive, while still retaining the idea of giving higher credit/penalty to fast responses than to slow responses.

If the SRTM holds, it would provide a better ability measure than the DSRTM since the latter uses only part of the information contained in the RTs. But if the SRTM does not adequately represent the data generating process, the simplicity of the DSRTM might allow it to be more robust, for example in the presence of fast guessing or timed-out responses, which are especially impactful under the SRTM due to its linear scoring rule.

Under the SRTM and the DSRTM, the probability of a correct response is the same as in the RM. This, however, does not necessarily mean that the estimates of the ability and the difficulty parameters under the RM, the SRTM and the DSRTM will be very close to each other. The more misfit the RT-based models have, the more discrepancy there would be between the estimates of the model parameters under different models.

The DSRTM can be seen as a restricted version of the partial credit model (PCM; Masters, 2016) which is a commonly used IRT model for ordinal responses in which the sufficient statistic for ability is also i Z ji . On the item side, the PCM has three item intercept parameters (δ i1 , δ i2 , δ i3 ) instead of a single difficulty parameter, which allows for more flexibility in modeling the joint distribution of response accuracy and dichotomized RT than under the DSRTM. The same as we have considered a discretized version of the SRTM, for the CRTM one can also decide to only consider dichotomized RTs (i.e., fast, t ji ≤ .5, vs. slow, t ji > .5). However, unlike the transition from the SRTM to the DSRTM, deriving a discretized version of the CRTM does not result in a model that belongs to the exponential family, and the resulting model would be problematic to estimate. For these reasons, we instead take the main elements of the CRTM -fast correct responses being indicative of a higher ability level than slow correct responses, but no distinction between fast and slow incorrect responses, -and propose to use a restricted version of the nominal response model (NRM; Bock, 1997) that imposes exactly this ordering of these three possible response outcomes. That is, we distinguish fast correct (Z * ji = 2), slow correct (Z * ji = 1), and incorrect responses (Z * ji = 0; regardless of RT) from each other, using the NRM to model these three possible outcomes. Note that to make the model comparable to the other models in this study we do not include item-specific discrimination parameters, which are typically used in the NRM (see Table 2). We use the same scores as in the PCM for the slow correct (a1 = 1) and the fast correct (a2 = 3 2 ) responses, but treat the score for the incorrect response a 0 as a tuning parameter similar to the penality P in the CRTM. Only distinguishing between dichotomously scored fast and slow responses can be beneficial when the linearity assumption about the relationship between ability and RT of correct responses is violated or in the presence of many outliers (for example, extremely fast responses or responses just before the timelimit which are likely to indicate guessing).

Analysis

Our analysis consisted of three steps.

In Step 1, the item parameters are calibrated (the discussion of Step 1 below will explicate why pre-calibration of item parameters was needed). Step 2 constitutes the training phase, where the tuning parameters K and -for those models where applicable -P and a 0 are optimized. Finally, Step 3 constitutes the testing phase, where we compute the prediction error for the accuracy of the next response for all models. Since more data is needed for estimating the item parameters than for optimizing the tuning parameters and for computing the final output measures with sufficient precision, we divided the data folds across the steps with two data folds assigned to Step 1, one data fold to Step 2, and one to Step 3. The four data folds were assigned to the calibration, training, and testing datasets in twelve different possible ways (see Figure 4), resulting in twelve data splits. The analyses were repeated for each data split, and the final measures were averaged across the twelve data splits. All analyses were performed in R (R Core Team, 2025).

Step 1: Calibration.

When working with ERSs, one has to decide whether the item difficulties are updated alongside the abilities of the students, or whether precalibrated values will be used for the item difficulties that are not updated alongside ability. While updating the item difficulties has the benefit of tracking changes over time in the item parameters, an important downside in adaptive settings is that it also introduces variance inflation (Bolsinova et al., 2025; Hofman et al., 2020). That is, the variance of the estimates of ability across students artificially increases over time if item difficulties are updated.

While it has been shown that this issue can be avoided by using parallel chains of estimates (Bolsinova et al., 2025), this is something that needs to be implemented during data collection and cannot be applied retroactively. Thus, when using existing data that were collected adaptively without such an implementation, updating item difficulties together with student abilities would lead to variance inflation. Moreover, such variance inflation may be affecting different models to a different extent and hence would jeopardize our objective of comparing the performance of the different models. Therefore, to ensure comparability of the models, we use the ERS with item parameters fixed at calibrated values, and a calibration step was needed. In this step, the item parameters for each of the models were estimated using the calibration dataset and then fixed to these estimates when running the Elo algorithm to dynamically measure the ability of the students in the training and testing datasets in Steps 2 and 3.

In this step, the objective is solely to obtain estimates for the item parameters based on a calibration data set, to be used in the later two steps. While the focus is on estimating the item parameters, this cannot be done without also modeling ability of the students. On the one hand, fitting a measurement model to a calibration dataset that would fully ignore the change in abilities over time can be expected to lead to some degree of bias in the item parameters. On the other hand, implementing a full dynamic model to account for change within and between sessions would not be computationally feasible, and exceed the purpose of this calibration step. With these considerations in mind, we opted to allow student ability to change across sessions, without explicitly modeling growth. To achieve this, we divided the data of each person into separate sessions (items answered within one day) and treated each of these sessions as if they came from separate respondents. For simplicity, the data of different 'pseudo-respondents' originating from the same student were treated as though they were independent (i.e., dependence between the different sessions of a student was ignored), and the full set of pseudo-respondents were taken to come from a single population distribution, allowing marginal maximum likelihood estimation (Bock & Aitkin, 1981) to be applied. While this assumption of independence between sessions is of course a simplification, it should not impact point estimates of the item parameters (similar to the stepwise approach in dynamic factor models; Rein et al., 2025), which are the objective of this step. Similar to typical testing scenarios, we assumed that within a session ability is stable.

Some data filtering needed to be applied to make sure that for each item included in the analysis there would be enough responses to achieve sufficient precision for estimating its parameters, and that each pseudo-respondents included in the analysis had at least three responses. 5 To start, we first removed pseudo-respondents with fewer than five responses (across datasets between 14.2% and 18.7% of the pseudo-respondents and between 0.7% and 2.7% of the students were removed), after which we removed items with fewer than 300 responses (13.4-36.4% of the items were removed across different datasets), and finally we removed pseudo-respondents with fewer than three responses (i.e., those pseudo-respondents for whom too few responses remained after dropping the items with too few observations; additional 0.8-1.7% of pseudo-respondents and 0.1-0.4% of students were removed). Overall, RT-based models performed best in the Addition and Subtraction domains when the old scoring rule was used. Here, the SRTM, the PCM, and the NRM performed better than the RM, consistently across the twelve data splits. When the new scoring rule was introduced, the NRM was the only RT-based model that had lower MSE than the RM in three of the domains. For the Division domain when the new scoring rule was used, the RM and the NRM performed similarly, with one model performing better in some data splits and another in others (i.e., the range of MSE differences covers both negative and positive values).

In all datasets the best performing RT-based model was the NRM. It is the model that makes the least assumptions about the relationship between RT and ability: It only uses RTs of correct responses in its scoring rule, does not assume linearity in the relationship between RT and ability, and does not assume that only one item parameter is sufficient to describe both the accuracy and RT aspects of item performance. The score for incorrect responses in the NRM ranged from -4 to -1 in the different datasets (the scores were less negative in datasets with a larger difference between the NRM and the benchmark RM; for the optimized values across datasets see Appendix C). The score for the incorrect responses was always lower in the NRM than in the PCM, meaning that the NRM gives relatively less weight to the RTs (i.e., the distance between the scores of correct and incorrect responses were much larger than the differences between the scores for fast and slow correct responses).

Interestingly, the CRTM with the small penalty for incorrect responses was one of the worst models in all datasets, even though it was the normative model when the new scoring rule was used. This is not surprising if we look at the correlations between the ability estimates under the different models. We computed the correlations between the expected a posteriori estimates of ability in Step 1 of the analysis for each of the RT-based models on the one hand and the RM on the other (i.e., ability measure reflecting only accuracy). The correlations for the CRTM P = 0.1 ranged between .868 and .924 in the different datasets, while for the best performing NRM they were .977-.998. That is, for these datasets the ability measure obtained under the CRTM with a small penalty deviated notably from those obtained under the RM, and this difference was much larger than for any of the other RT-based models. This can be explained by considering that the CRTM with P = 0.1 gives a relatively high weight to being fast vs. slow in its scoring rule, compared to the difference between giving a correct and an incorrect response. That is, the difference in score between a fast and a slow correct response can be as large as 1, while the difference between a (slow) correct response and an incorrect response can be as small as 0.1. Thus, the ability measure of the CRTM much more than that of the other models tends to reflect individual differences in speededness than in ability to produce correct responses, and in settings where speed is not only determined by ability but also by other factors (e.g., risk-taking of the student or student self-confidence) one can expect this ability measure to deviate the most from that of the RM.

The DSRTM which was introduced as a way of relaxing the assumption of the linear relationship between ability and RT present in the SRTM performed worse than the RM across datasets, and in some datasets worse than all other models. That is, we did not find added value of moving from continuous to discrete scores within the signed-residual time scoring rule. However, further relaxing the model by including extra item parameters (PCM) leads to better prediction compared to both the RM and the SRTM in some of the datasets.

Importantly, the NRM is the only model that did not deteriorate next-response prediction across all datasets. All other models are showing results worse than the benchmark in at least some datasets, with the increase of MSE compared to the RM often being much larger (e.g., +.00202 for the SRTM in the Division domain when the old scoring rule was used) than the decrease (-.00008 for the SRTM in the Addition domain when the old scoring rule was used), which underscores the importance of evaluating the effects of adding RTs on prediction accuracy.

To investigate how the results in the empirical data compare to what one can expect in terms of improvement of measurement in the best case scenario, that is when the normative RT model is the data generating model, we performed a simulation study.

For each empirical dataset we generated simulated datasets that would mimic its structure. We kept the same number of responses and the same items that each student received in the empirical data. The response accuracy and RT data were generated under the SRTM for the datasets with the old scoring rule and under the CRTM with a small penalty (P = 0.1) for the datasets with the new scoring rule. Additionally, we considered a scenario in which the RTs do not follow the normative models and provide no information about ability. In this scenario we re-sampled RT values with replacement from the empirical distributions specific to each item and conditional on response accuracy. This allowed to create simulated data where the RT distributions and the relationships between RT and accuracy are the same as in the empirical data, but RTs are not expected to improve measurement of ability.

Discussion

Models that make use of RT-based scoring rules have the potential to improve quality of measurement in ALS over what can be achieved using response-accuracy-only models such as the RM. In this research, we focused on prediction accuracy as one of the aspects of measurement quality and studied both the empirical and theoretical gains that can be obtained using different RT-based models, with both the empirical and simulation-based analysis considered in the context of a case study ALS in mathematics for elementary school students. Both the empirical and simulation-based results suggest potential for improving the measurement quality, as evaluated by the accuracy with which next responses can be predicted based on the current ability estimate under each of the different models. However, the simulation-based results show a much more positive picture than was observed in the empirical setting, where the gains in measurement were somewhat small and not fully consistent, and where the normative models did not notably outperform the benchmark model that made use of response accuracy only.

While there was some improvement in the MSE when using some of the RT-based models instead of the RM in some of the datasets, the improvement over the benchmark in those cases was rather small. When we compared it to the improvement in simulated data, we observed that the largest improvement in the empirical data roughly matched what can be expected if there were 10% more responses available. The achieved improvement, although non-negligible, is not large and does not hold universally across datasets. The notable difference between the observed gains when studying the empirical data versus when studying simulated data obtained under the normative models comes from notable differences between the relationship between RT and ability as specified by the models versus what happens in reality, which can be considered problematic for the interpretation of scores produced under these models. When the ability and RTs are not as strongly related to each other compared to what the normative model assumes, the empirical gains of including RTs are notably smaller. And when the assumed relationship is largely absent in the data or when it is in the different direction from what is observed in the empirical data, using a normative model leads to the decrease of prediction accuracy compared to the benchmark. These results demonstrate the importance of evaluating the assumptions of RT-based models in empirical data.

Both the SRTM and the CRTM have been designed with the idea that explicitly communicating the RT-based scoring rule to the respondents should lead to them behaving according to that model. Analyzing the data from an online learning environment where these scoring rules have been implemented, we found that this is not necessarily the case. The normative models were not the best performing models in our study, nor did they provide notable improvements in measurement over using the RM, while in some datasets notably increasing prediction error. These results emphasize that when implementing a measurement model which takes RTs into account it is important to check whether its theoretical expectations match the empirical relationships in the data.

More specifically, studying the relationship between ability and RTs of correct and incorrect responses is a crucial step in choosing a measurement model for the system.

Our results also show that changing the instructions for respondents (here, changing the rewards/penalties within the system communicated to the students) may change the relationship between ability and RTs, and may also affect whether RT-based models are beneficial for measurement. In this case, making the penalty for incorrect responses independent of RT made RTs of correct responses less informative about ability, as evidenced by the correlation between the RT of correct responses and ability shifting further away from where the normative model predicts it to be. Findings such as these strongly suggest that any changes in the instructions or the reward system in the game should be followed by a new evaluation of the measurement models.

Interestingly enough, the RT-based model that showed the best performance in the empirical setting was the NRM. This model has to our knowledge not yet been considered in the context of utilizing RTs in ALS, but it outperformed both the normative models and the other considered models. It is likely that its better performance can to some extent be attributed to it also being the most flexible model of the set. It should be noted that this may suggest that the degree of misspecification present in the other models is likely their main obstacle to showing good performance in the empirical setting, as was also evidenced by the large contrast between the theoretical gains (studied through simulation) versus the empirical gains obtained for the two normative models. It is quite plausible that a model that is more specific than that NRM but that also more closely matches the actual response process than the models considered in this research could also outperform the NRM. However, what this model would need to look like needs to be a topic of further research, and would likely require a more in-depth study of the response behavior of students in the ALS. It is however clear that because it outperformed all other models, future studies in this direction should definitely consider including the NRM as one of the benchmark models.

A notable limitation of the current study is the assymetry between the compared models: First, the item sequences presented to students were determined based on the normative model. Second, the instructions to the students were aligned with this model (note, that while in Math Garden the scoring rule of the normative model is explicitely communicated to the students, in general the model used for abiltiy estimation and item selection does not have to be aligned with the scoring rule communicated to the student).

The counterfactual data (e.g., items selected based on the RM and/or students not instructed to pay attention their response times) are not available. Since we have observed that when instructions changed from the old to the new scoring rule, student behavior and relationships between ability and response times have changed, we can also expect that if the scoring rule from the RM were communicated to the students, their behavior would be different as well, and therefore the consequences of incorporating RTs for prediction accuracy could be different. The assymetry in terms of the which model was used for item selection is less crucial. Previous simulation studies (Niznan et al., 2015) showed that while the magnitude of differences between the models depends on the model used for item selection (the better the model used for item selection is, the closer the probabilities-correct of the selected items are to the target probabilities, the smaller the difference between the different models become), the model closest to the data generating model still provides better estimates of ability and higher prediction accuracy.

The starting point of this study has been that RTs are incorporated into the measurement model to increase precision of measurement, without altering the measurement itself. That is, we assume that ability measured by accuracy only is a valid measure, but that in standard ALS settings the precision of the accuracy-based measure is low due to data sparsity and item selection aimed at optimised student engagement rather than statistical information in the responses. From this perspective it makes sense to use prediction of the accuracy of the next response as the criterion for model comparison and for contrasting the RM with each of the RT-based models. However, a different perspective that could be taken is that an accuracy-based ability measure is not necessarily the best measure of ability (e.g., one may argue that to be good at addition one needs to not only be correct but also demonstrate fluency), and that incorporating RTs is not just a way to increase precision, but a way of changing the operationalization of the construct and potentially improving validity of score interpretations. From this perspective, misalignment of accuracy and RT information within RT-based models would not necessarily be taken as a reason to not use the model, but rather as a sign that the resulting measure of ability is a composite score of the accuracy and RT components, which are not perfectly aligned with each other. However, to make such an argument, one would need to provide evidence of increased validity of these alternative interpretations, for example by comparing the ability estimates form different models with student grades or with scores from standardized assessments, as a way of evaluating external validity.

Since differences in RTs may reflect not only construct-relevant variance (i.e., reflect higher mastery of skills that require fluency and automatization) but also carry construct-irrelevant variance (e.g., reading speed, motor speed, familiarity with computerised practice, rapid guessing), the alternative operationalization should not be taken as a better measure of ability without such evidence.

Regardless of the answer to the question about the best operationalization of ability, increasing prediction error for the accuracy of the next response after incorporation of RTs in the model is an important problem within an ALS. When prediction is less accurate, selected items would be less closely matching the desired level of accuracy, which is often used as a criterion for item selection (e.g., in Math Garden students choose a difficulty level with either 60%, 75% or 90% expected correct responses), which can lead to either an over-or an under-challenging environment for some students.

An undesired consequence of that could be that students from specific groups would be disadvantaged. One possibility concerns gender differences in response behavior. For example, research often shows that girls are more cautious than boys (Jansen et al., 2016). If such differences occur, girls' ability would be underestimated and boys' abilities overestimated if RTs of correct responses are taken into account, and as a result item selection would unintentionally result in presenting more difficult items to boys than to girls, given the same ability level of the student. It was not possible to examine this gender-related implication as part of the current study because information on gender was not available. It therefore represents a conjecture that warrants further investigation.

In this study we compared data from a natural experiment consisting of two periods, during which different scoring rules were implemented in Math Garden. It is important to note however that while the change in the scoring rule was the main change introduced in the system, it is not the only thing that was different between the academic years of 2018-2019 and 2020-2021, which is a limitation of the current study. For example, new domains have been added to the system and new ways of recommending different domains within the system to students were introduced (Brinkhuis et al., 2020). The consequences of this can be noticed in there being fewer students and fewer responses per student in the 2020-2021 dataset, since some students who would have otherwise been practicing in the four domains included in this study chose other domains to practice.

Potentially the composition of the population of the students practicing in our four domains in 2020-2021 could be different compared to that from 2018-2019, which could also be contributing to the differences in response behavior that we found. Furthermore, there may be other differences between the two time periods included in the study, for example in terms of increased digitalisation at schools (also due to the COVID-19 pandemic). RT patterns may have changed if the students became more used to working with digital platforms or if the dominant type of devices used for practice changed.

Detailed study of potential mode effects goes beyond the scope of the current paper but is an important direction for further research.

All RT-based models included in this study assume that the same relationship between ability and RTs holds for all students. As such, these models do not take into account any individual differences in response behavior that go beyond the differences in ability. However, some students might be generally faster than others with the same ability level. Failing to account for these differences may be one of the reasons that the models do not provide a lot of improvement over the benchmark RM. Additionally, the environment allowed students to choose their desired difficulty level (operationalized through the expected proportion correct), and there may be relevant differences between students who choose a relatively easy versus hard difficulty level, which could also potentially have had an impact on the results. Developing ways of taking all of these individual differences into account could lead to better models, which would improve measurement precision without introducing confounding. Future research should also focus on studying the differences between people from different subgroups (e.g., gender, language spoken at home).

While for the standard ERS the underlying measurement model is the RM, where ability is measured based on binary item scores (1 -correct, 0 -incorrect), in this modified ERS the signed-residual-time model (SRTM; Maris & van der Maas, 2012) is used which incorporates RTs in the item scores. The idea of this RT-based score is that RTs provide information about ability beyond what is captured by response accuracy alone: Correct responses still indicate a higher ability level than incorrect responses, but the speed with which that response was provided additionally influences the indicated ability level, and the way in which RTs relate to ability depends on response accuracy. Concretely, it is assumed that responses that are both correct and fast indicate higher ability compared to responses that are correct yet slow. The converse is assumed for incorrect responses, where fast incorrect responses are taken to indicate a lower ability level than slow incorrect responses. Note that these assumptions are not guaranteed to hold universally as RTs are influenced by many different factors. The SRTM has been developed for applications where items are presented to students with an explicit timelimit, but have also been applied in setting without item-level timelimits (van Rijn & Ali, 2017).
