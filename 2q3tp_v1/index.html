How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Mounting evidence suggests that sleep disturbances and depression are linked through disruptions in reward processing. This pathway may be especially relevant to adolescent depression, as adolescence is characterized by more frequent sleep disturbances, reduced sleep, and refinement of reward circuitry.

Aim: This preregistered study uses high-density ecological momentary assessment (EMA) data from the Characterization and Treatment of Adolescent Depression Studies (CTADS) cohort (collected from 2023 to 2026) to test sleep disturbances and duration as predictors of anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia and to determine whether effects exhibit diurnal variation.

Methods: Sixty-five adolescents (12-18 years; 43 female, 22 male; 41 with major depressive disorder, 24 healthy volunteers) completed 120 EMA prompts over 30 days. Participants reported prior-night sleep disturbances and duration once per day, and anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia four times per day. We ran two Bayesian multilevel linear models, estimating anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia, respectively, as functions of prior-night sleep disturbances, time of day, diagnostic group, and their interactions. We then ran two exploratory models assessing sleep duration in the same manner.

Results: In adolescents with MDD, whereas self-reported sleep disturbances did not predict anhedonia, longer sleep duration predicted both lower anticipatory and lower consummatory anhedonia. In healthy adolescents, greater sleep disturbances predicted lower anticipatory and lower consummatory anhedonia. Effects of sleep disturbances on anhedonia tended to increase across the day in adolescents with MDD and healthy adolescents.

Conclusions: Sleep disturbances and duration differentially predict next-day anhedonia in adolescents with MDD. For adolescent MDD patients, treatments that target sleep duration, as opposed to sleep disturbances, could provide more immediate effects on anhedonia. Time-of- day moderation effects suggest the mechanisms through which sleep disturbances (but not sleep duration) affect anhedonia may involve diurnal biological processes.

Sleep and the Daytime Course of Anhedonia in Adolescent Depression: An Ecological

Momentary Assessment Study

In the United States, approximately 19% of adolescents aged 12-19 meet criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD; Brody & Hughes, 2025). Sleep disturbances-an overarching term to describe a variety of problems, including insomnia, frequent nighttime waking, low sleep efficiency, and light sleep-impact at least two-thirds of adolescents with MDD (Zhao et al., 2024; see Lovato & Gradisar, 2014). Sleep disturbances are a common symptom of depression, but they also contribute to syndrome onset (Scott et al., 2021), interfere with treatment (Troxel et al., 2012), and persist after other depressive symptoms improve (Clarke & Harvey, 2012), which may increase risk of depression recurrence. These relations between sleep disturbances and depression may be partly explained by an indirect pathway involving aberrant reward processing (see the Integrated Sleep and Reward Model; Boland et al., 2020). This pathway may be especially consequential during adolescence, as adolescence is characterized by more frequent sleep disturbances and reduced sleep duration (Kansagra et al., 2020; Morrison et al., 1992), refinement of reward circuitry (Walker et al., 2017), and heightened depression risk (Thapar et al., 2012). In the current study, we investigate this putative pathway using highdensity ecological momentary assessment (EMA) data to examine the effects of sleep disturbances and duration on anhedonia: a subjective indicator of aberrant reward processing and a core symptom of adolescent MDD.

Research examining between-person effects of sleep on the subjective experience of reward reliably finds that individuals with greater sleep disturbances report higher anhedonia contemporaneously. In a study of 166 Finnish adolescent psychiatric outpatients with unipolar depression, those with multiple sleep problems reported higher anhedonia and more suicidal thoughts than those with no or minor sleep problems; the groups did not differ in reports of any other depressive symptom (Urrila et al., 2012). Likewise, in a study by Wang and colleagues (2025a), 200 adolescent psychiatric outpatients with MDD completed questionnaires assessing depression, anhedonia, and sleep quality at a single time point. The authors used network analysis to construct a sleep quality network and a co-occurrence network with anhedonia; in the co-occurrence network, sleep disturbances had a prominent bridging connection with anhedonia. In another study of 200 adolescents with MDD, sleep disorder was contemporaneously associated with higher anhedonia, even when controlling for the mediating role of life stress (Zhao et al., 2024).

To our knowledge, only two studies have examined within-person effects of sleep on anhedonia in young people. Both found that on nights that individuals reported more sleep disturbances or shorter sleep duration, they tended to report higher next-day anhedonia; although, effects differed for anticipatory versus consummatory anhedonia. In a study by Wieman and colleagues (2022), over one week, 260 unselected undergraduates completed daily diaries assessing sleep, reward responsiveness, depression, and anhedonia. At the day level, poorer sleep quality, shorter sleep duration, and longer awakening after sleep onset predicted blunted anticipatory and consummatory reward responsiveness, while increased sleep onset latency and lower sleep efficiency predicted blunted consummatory reward responsiveness only. In another study (Patel et al., 2025), 48 adolescents with mixed diagnoses and high suicide risk completed 28 daily diaries assessing perceived sleep onset latency and sleep duration as well as EMA prompts assessing anhedonia 3-6 times per day. The authors found that within individuals, longer sleep onset latency was associated with higher consummatory anhedonia (although this finding was inconsistent across models) and was not associated with anticipatory anhedonia. Consistently, within individuals, longer sleep duration predicted lower consummatory anhedonia but not anticipatory anhedonia. In addition to demonstrating that sleep impacts next-day anhedonia, these results suggest a functional distinction between anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia, supporting the separate examination of these temporal domains of reward processing.

There are two patent gaps in the literature. First, as noted, to our knowledge, only two studies (Wieman et al., 2022; Patel et al., 2025) have examined within-person effects of sleep on anhedonia, and only one (Patel et al., 2025) did so in a clinical sample of adolescents.

Second, no studies have examined whether or how effects of sleep on anhedonia might vary by time of day. Identifying when sleep most strongly influences anhedonia could suggest whether the effects of sleep are driven by circadian rhythms, accumulated sleep pressure, or other timevarying cognitive or affective processes. Additionally, if associations exhibit diurnal variation, interventions targeting sleep or anhedonia may be more effective when delivered at particular times.

The Current Study

We leveraged data from a high-density EMA study to test sleep disturbances and duration as predictors of anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia in adolescents with and without MDD, and to determine whether effects exhibit diurnal variation. We hypothesized that within individuals, sleep disturbances would positively predict anhedonia and that this effect would be stronger in adolescents with MDD compared to healthy adolescents. We also hypothesized that within individuals, time would moderate the effect of sleep disturbances on anhedonia, such that the effect would weaken across the day. We expected the moderating effect of time would weaken less across the day in adolescents with MDD compared to healthy adolescents. In addition to our preregistered analyses pertaining to sleep disturbances, we conducted exploratory analyses pertaining to sleep duration, for which we did not pose a priori hypotheses.

Methods

This preregistered study utilized data from the Characterization and Treatment of Adolescent Depression Studies (CTADS) cohort (National Institute of Mental Health Institutional

Review Board protocol 18-M-0037; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03388606; collected from 2023 to 2026). Only methods relevant to the current study are described below. Data collection for CTADS is ongoing; the current study includes data collected as of May 21, 2026: the date of preregistration.

Participants and Recruitment Procedures

Adolescents aged 11-17 with and without MDD were recruited from Washington, D.C. and surrounding areas through social media advertisements, paper advertisements posted in the community, mailings to local zip codes, and community referrals. Participants were ineligible for the study if they had an estimated IQ<70; regularly used substances within the previous three months; had ever experienced cardinal bipolar disorder symptoms; met diagnostic criteria for posttraumatic stress disorder, a psychotic disorder, or moderate-to-severe autism spectrum disorder; or had a neurological disorder or unstable medical condition. Before data collection, adolescents provided informed, written assent and parents/guardians provided informed, written consent. Following data collection, two participants were excluded from analyses because they completed <25% of EMA prompts (a preregistered exclusion criterion ensuring each participant contributed enough observations to reliably estimate within-person effects).

Our final sample consisted of 65 adolescents (43 female, 22 male; M=15.96 years, SD=1.39, range=12.75-18.67): 41 who met DSM-5 criteria for MDD within the six months prior to enrollment (as determined using the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia [K-SADS] interview; Kaufman et al., 1997), and 24 healthy adolescents. The sample was 49.23% White, 13.85% Black/African American, 6.15% Asian, 1.53% American Indian/Alaskan Native, and 24.62% biracial/multiracial; 4.62% of participants did not report race.

The sample was 13.85% Hispanic/Latino; 81.54% of participants were neither Hispanic nor Latino, and 4.62% did not report ethnicity. Most adolescents (44.62%) were from families with an annual household income over $150,000, followed by those with incomes of $125,000-149,999 (10.77%), 100,000-124,999 (7.69%), 75,000-99,999 (6.15%), 50,000-74,999 (4.62%), and 25,000-49,000 (3.08%); 26.15% did not report income. On average, adolescents completed 75.45% of EMA prompts (M=90.54, SD=22.67) over 28.51 days.

Procedures

Following informed consent, adolescents were trained on the high-density EMA protocol.

Training involved meeting with a research assistant to review EMA items and practice responding to prompts using the m-Path software (Mestdagh et al., 2023). Participants who did not own a smartphone received a study-provided smartphone configured solely for EMA data collection.

Adolescents were asked to complete EMA prompts four times a day (before school, during school, in the afternoon, and before bed) for 30 days. At each prompt, adolescents were asked to rate their experiences since the previous prompt. Prompts were scheduled pseudorandomly, within 1-hour timeslots tailored to each adolescent's schedule to increase feasibility and compliance (e.g., during lunchtime at school). When requested, prompt timeslots were adjusted to accommodate changes in adolescents' schedules. All participants received a check-in during the third week of the EMA protocol to encourage continued participation. where0=not at all and100=extremely. Participants reported anhedonia since the previous prompt at every prompt (four times per day). Prior to analyses, anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia were reverse-scored so that higher values reflected higher anhedonia.

Statistical Analyses

We conducted all analyses in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025), primarily using the brms package (Bürkner, 2017). For each model described below, four Markov chains were run, each with 4,000 iterations. Package-default, weakly informative priors were used for all fixed effects and variance components. Sleep disturbances, sleep duration, anticipatory anhedonia, and consummatory anhedonia were person-mean centered prior to analyses.

Preregistered Analyses

We ran two Bayesian multilevel linear models (one modeling anticipatory anhedonia and one modeling consummatory anhedonia), with EMA reports (level 1) nested within days (level 2) nested within individuals (level 3). In each model, anhedonia was estimated as a function of prior-night sleep disturbances (level 2), time of day (level 1), diagnostic group (level 3), and all interactions. We then re-ran the models including sex (level 3) and age (level 3) as covariates.

All models included random intercepts for participants and for EMA day nested within participants, and random slopes for time, sleep disturbances, and their interaction across participants, with correlations among all participant-level random effects estimated by default.

Exploratory Analyses

We ran exploratory analyses investigating the effects of sleep duration on anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia, using the same model specifications as in our preregistered analyses, only substituting sleep disturbances with sleep duration.

Inference

Inference was based primarily on posterior distributions and 95% credible intervals for model parameters. We considered posterior probabilities ranging from 0.90 to 0.95 as moderate evidence of effects, and posterior probabilities exceeding 0.95 as strong evidence of effects.

Discussion

The current study utilized 30 days of EMA data from adolescents with and without MDD to test sleep disturbances and duration as predictors of anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia, and to determine whether effects exhibited diurnal variation. Self-reported sleep disturbances did not meaningfully impact next-day anhedonia: Sleep disturbances did not predict anticipatory or consummatory anhedonia in adolescents with MDD, and sleep disturbances predicted lower anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia in healthy adolescents.

With respect to sleep duration, however, more sleep predicted lower anticipatory and lower consummatory anhedonia specifically in adolescents with MDD. Time-of-day moderation effects suggest that the mechanisms through which sleep disturbances (but not sleep duration) affect anhedonia may involve diurnal biological processes.

Self-Reported Sleep Disturbances May Not Meaningfully Impact Next-Day Anhedonia

Contrary to our hypotheses, sleep disturbances did not predict anticipatory or consummatory anhedonia in adolescents with MDD. This may reflect a cumulative effect of sleep disturbances on anhedonia that is not observable at the day level. For example, for an adolescent who usually experiences sleep disturbances, a single night of high-quality sleep may not be sufficient to improve next-day symptoms; instead, symptoms may only be impacted after multiple consecutive nights of sleep. The notion that symptoms may only improve after an enduring reduction in sleep disturbances is consistent with evidence that adolescents with MDD exhibit greater emotional inertia (their emotional states tend to persist and are difficult to change; e.g., Liu et al., 2026).

More Sleep Predicts Lower Anhedonia Only in Adolescents with Depression

Building on existing literature in healthy adults (Wieman et al., 2022), this is the first study to demonstrate in adolescents with MDD that getting more sleep predicts next-day decreases in both anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia. In the current sample, most adolescents with MDD reported getting too little sleep on average (see Table S1). Reduced sleep is associated with diminished effort expenditure in pursuit of rewards, which can result in individuals experiencing less pleasure in the moment and anticipating less future pleasure (Boland et al., 2020; Treadway et al., 2012). Sleep deficits are also known to impair emotion regulation (e.g., Lollies et al., 2022), meaning when adolescents get less sleep, they may have more difficulty upregulating positive affect the next day. These alterations may occur because sleep duration constrains the time available for nocturnal processes implicated in depression.

For example, reduced sleep limits the opportunity for synaptic homeostasis within reward and affective regions, which may contribute to the pathophysiology of depression (Wang et al., 2025b). Similarly, not getting enough sleep can disrupt the consolidation of emotional memory (Niu et al., 2025; Bennion et al., 2015; Hutchison & Rathore, 2015), impairing individuals' ability to accurately valuate (and, in turn, seek out) potential future rewards based on past pleasurable experiences.

Contrarily, in healthy adolescents, self-reported sleep duration was not associated with next-day anhedonia. This may be because healthy adolescents lack the underlying vulnerabilities associated with MDD. MDD is characterized by altered reward processing and dysregulation in frontolimbic circuitry involved in emotion regulation (e.g., Miller et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2026). For adolescents with MDD, reduced sleep may further compromise these systems (by limiting the time available for nocturnal process, as described above), leading to increases in anhedonia; however, for healthy adolescents without these pre-existing aberrations, the effects of reduced sleep on these systems may not be pronounced enough to impact anhedonia the following day.

The Effects of Sleep Disturbances (But Not Sleep Duration) May Involve Diurnal

Biological Processes

Findings concerning the moderating effects of time of day suggest that the mechanisms through which sleep disturbances (but not sleep duration) affect anhedonia may involve diurnal biological processes. The associations between sleep disturbances and consummatory anhedonia increased across the day in adolescents with MDD and healthy adolescents, and the same was found for anticipatory anhedonia in healthy adolescents. The fact that sleep disturbances were more positively associated with anhedonia later in the day suggests that accumulated sleep pressure, fatigue, or related circadian processes may be implicated. Conversely, time did not moderate the effects of sleep duration in either group, supporting the notion that the effects of sleep duration may be related to nocturnal processes, such as synaptic homeostasis or consolidation of emotional memory, as opposed to diurnal biological processes.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several considerations should be made when interpreting the present findings and identifying directions for future research. First, the current study only employs subjective measures of sleep; future work should integrate subjective and objective measures to facilitate a broader investigation of sleep effects. Second, while the current study examines whether sleep variables predict anhedonia, these effects are likely bidirectional. Putative bidirectional processes should be examined in future work using methods like dynamic structural equation modeling, which are well-suited to bidirectional and autoregressive frameworks. Additionally, in the current sample, healthy adolescents reported a relatively low incidence of sleep disturbances (see Table S1), which may have introduced floor effects. To better understand whether findings are unique to adolescents with MDD, future researchers may wish to include a sample of adolescents with significant sleep disturbances who are otherwise healthy.

Constraints on Generality

More female than male adolescents enrolled in the study, consistent with the higher prevalence rate of MDD in adolescent females relative to males (Brody & Hughes, 2025). The racial composition of the current sample was broadly representative of the U.S. population.

While this enhances the generalizability of findings to the broad population, findings may not generalize to racial groups that are underrepresented nationally, such as American Indians and Alaskan Natives.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, the present study is one of only a few to examine person-specific, within-day effects of sleep on anhedonia, and is the first to examine these effects specifically in adolescents with MDD. We found that more sleep consistently predicted lower anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia in adolescents with MDD. In contrast, sleep disturbances were not meaningfully associated with next-day anhedonia. Thus, for adolescent MDD patients, treatments that target sleep duration, as opposed to sleep disturbances, could provide more immediate effects on anhedonia. Notably, associations between sleep disturbances (but not sleep duration) and anhedonia varied across the day, suggesting that the mechanisms through which sleep disturbances affect anhedonia may involve diurnal biological processes. More broadly, these findings provide a potentially fruitful direction for future research on adolescent depression and anhedonia, particularly research incorporating complementary, objective sleep measures.
