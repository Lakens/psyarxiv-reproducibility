Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Recent advances in the acquisition of intensive longitudinal data (ILD) have enabled affective science to investigate emotional experience in increasingly ecologically valid contexts. These developments highlight the complexity of psychological systems and the challenges involved in understanding emotions. In this perspective paper, we argue for a conceptual adjustment in how measurement is practiced in emotion-dynamics research. Drawing on affective science, computational modeling, and the philosophy of science, we propose that measurement should be treated as an interaction that may partially structure and modify the phenomenon being measured. This perspective has implications for the design, acquisition, and analysis of ILD. We identify three complementary directions for future research. First, measurement frameworks should be theory-informed and transparent, with methodological choices and relevant hyperparameters explicitly documented to support cumulative science. Second, subjective self-report should be complemented, where appropriate, by behavioral, physiological, and environmental measures to provide partially independent perspectives on complex psychological processes. Third, greater attention should be given to temporal scale in ILD measurement and analysis, including multiscale dynamical network approaches that connect proximal, observable, and potentially perturbable processes with higher-level psychological phenomena. Together, these directions support a shift from describing statistical associations toward a more process-oriented understanding of psychological phenomena.

Introduction

Over the past couple of decades, technological advances in intensive longitudinal methods have given psychological science the means to gain more insight into the dynamic nature of mental health. Through assessment methods such as ecological momentary assessment (EMA), experience sampling methods (ESM), and electronic diaries, combined with the analysis of the resulting intensive longitudinal data (ILD), opportunities have emerged to capture the cascade of events, interactions, and behaviors as a sequential continuous process, shaping our living experience and thus our mental health. By repeatedly sampling individuals in their everyday environments, ILD approaches can provide striking access to fluctuations in experiential, cognitive, and behavioral states while substantially increasing ecological validity compared to traditional designs, as data are mainly gathered out of the lab, in natural environment conditions, and over extended periods of time (Ariens et al., 2020; Scherer, 2005; Shiffman et al., 2008; Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2009).

The empirical shift driven by the plethora of ILD research has developed hand in hand with rapid methodological and theoretical advances. The availability of extended time series with high-density data (multiple measurements per day for several days/weeks) has motivated the development of increasingly formal statistical, computational, and dynamical models, with the intent to capture features like within-person change, temporal dependencies through cross-lagged relations, and interrelations among psychological variables (Hamaker, 2025).

In this paper, we argue that there is a need to advance our conceptual framework for understanding psychological measurement in order to fully capitalize on recent technological and methodological advances. After painting a picture of recent developments, state-of-the-art approaches in emotion-dynamics research, and some of the problems that the field has yet to overcome, we conceptualize a perspective of measurement as an interactive process informed by the natural sciences, including physics and biology. Building on this perspective, we translate the conceptual framework into practical recommendations for how ILD research can be designed, conducted, and interpreted more effectively, outlining a path toward more rigorous, adaptive designs and multicomponent approaches that make use of complementary measurement scales.

Summary metrics and their limitations

In affective science, analytical tools have been developed and applied to ILD to enable the study of observed emotional phenomena. These tools capture patterns in emotional experience such as emotional inertia (reflecting the carryover of an emotion), emotional variability (defined as the overall swings in experienced emotions), and emotional instability (high temporal variability and reactivity of experienced emotions) in a systematic manner (Bornas et al., 2014; Kuppens, Oravecz, et al., 2010; Kuppens et al., 2012). Here, single-value metrics are often used to describe key aspects of these emotional phenomena. The standard Deviation (SD) expresses the extent of the overall change, and when applied to ILD measurements of emotions, is regarded as a measure of emotional variability. The Mean Square of Successive Differences (MSSD) is the change between consecutive measurement time points and is often presented as a measure of emotional instability. Lastly, inertia is reflected in autoregressive (e.g., lag-1 AR) autocorrelation coefficients, which quantify the extent to which an emotional state depends on its preceding state in a time series (Jahng et al., 2008; Koval et al., 2013; Thompson et al., 2012; Trull et al., 2008). By quantifying distinct aspects of emotional experiences, these measures have enabled researchers to identify distinct characteristic patterns of emotional dynamics across a range of mood and anxiety disorders (Bosley et al., 2019; Bowen et al., 2006; Heller et al., 2019; Schoevers et al., 2021).

It should be noted that these affect-dynamics metrics also have limitations that should prevent us from overinterpreting them and overestimating their relevance (Houben et al., 2015). Their reduction of complex temporal dynamics to scalar summaries, sensitivity to distributional characteristics of affect (i.e., variations of intensity and frequency of emotional experiences across time), and mathematical co-dependence challenge the assumption that they uniquely capture distinct constructs such as inertia, instability, or variability (Koslowski & Holtmann, 2025). These limitations matter because prior literature has sometimes interpreted these indices as direct operationalizations of emotional mechanisms (Brose et al., 2015; Kuppens, Allen, et al., 2010; Trull et al., 2015).

A common instantiation of that matter is the inertia-instability paradox, which refers to the empirical finding that depression is associated with both higher affective instability (MSSD) and higher affective inertia (lag-1 AR) (Bos et al., 2019; Jahng et al., 2008; Koval et al., 2013), despite the conceptually opposing nature of these constructs. Consequently, these statistical descriptors can only indicate the general direction rather than delineate specific pathways through which our understanding of emotional processes can advance (Kuppens & Verduyn, 2017).

In response to the concerns outlined above, recent years have witnessed the emergence of a broader analytical and methodological movement aimed at modeling affective dynamics more formally and comprehensively. This methodological shift reflects an increasing recognition that understanding emotional functioning requires analytical tools capable of leveraging the complexity inherent in ILD data, beyond what can be inferred from traditional variability, instability, or inertia measures alone.

The complexity theoretic mindset

The ambition for greater mechanistic understanding of underlying emotional processes has increasingly directed affective research toward more advanced analytical approaches, including models inspired by complexity science, dynamical systems theory, and network-based perspectives (Borsboom & Cramer, 2013). The shift from relatively static or linear approaches toward more dynamic and non-linear conceptualizations in psychology is visible in the network theory of psychopathology (Borsboom et al., 2011). This framework conceptualizes symptoms not as passive indicators of a latent construct, but as mutually interacting components embedded within a dynamic system through which psychopathology may emerge.

However, an important distinction must be maintained between the general dynamical and complexity-oriented framework of network theory and the more specific practice of estimating cross-sectional symptom association networks and interpreting their parameters causally. One of the central debates surrounding the early development of psychopathology networks concerned precisely this issue. Forbes and colleagues (Forbes et al., 2017a) criticized the tendency to overinterpret edge weights, centrality indices, and cross-sectional network structures as evidence of causal psychological mechanisms, despite limited replicability and insufficient justification. Such criticism proved highly productive for the field. Rather than undermining the broader complexsystems perspective, these debates (Borsboom et al., 2017; Forbes et al., 2017b) contributed to methodological refinement and a growing recognition that understanding psychopathology as a dynamic system requires temporally resolved data, formal dynamical models, and intensive longitudinal methodologies. Consequently, recent developments increasingly emphasize time-series approaches, vector autoregressive and dynamical systems models, person-specific analyses, and hybrid latent-network frameworks that can model evolving affective and symptomatic processes directly (Briganti et al., 2024; Bringmann, 2021; McNally, 2021).

The Formal Modeling Trend

There is a growing awareness that psychological phenomena may be better understood as dynamic systems that evolve over time through reciprocal interactions among cognitive, emotional, and behavioral processes (Haslbeck et al., 2021; Ryan et al., 2025). Several researchers have begun integrating dynamical and computational approaches to model different mental disorder processes such as panic disorder (Robinaugh et al., 2024) and suicidal thinking (Wang et al., 2023), explicitly incorporating feedback loops, nonlinear interactions, and state-dependent transitions. These efforts reflect an emerging interdisciplinary movement, situated between psychology, computational science, and complexity theory, aimed at developing formalized models that closely match the theoretical structure of emotional systems while creating an environment for model refinement through subsequent tests of formal predictions.

Formal and computational modeling approaches are often presented as a response to several longstanding challenges in psychology, including weakly specified theories, vague or inconsistent constructs, limited reproducibility, and difficulties establishing cumulative scientific progress (Eronen & Bringmann, 2021; Gernigon et al., 2024; Navarro, 2021; Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019; Perski et al., 2025). Are all these problems going to be solved simply by incorporating such an approach? We would really like to say "yes", but unfortunately the answer is more likely to be negative. As the subfield has grown, so too has a debate about the limits of current data collection and modeling practices. While formal and computational models are often presented as a solution to longstanding problems of imprecision and theoretical vagueness in psychology (Guest & Martin, 2021; Haslbeck et al., 2021), it has also been noted that increased mathematical sophistication does not automatically translate into deeper understanding (Borsboom et al., 2004; Bringmann et al., 2022; Eronen, 2020; Oude Maatman, 2021).

Therefore, the aim of this perspective paper is not to argue for more theory or more elaborate models to explain empirical observations by any means necessary, but for a conceptual adjustment in how empirical and analytical research is practiced and interpreted in psychological science, and in emotion-dynamics research in particular.

Here, we take a unifying stance, integrating insights from affective science, computational modeling, natural sciences and the philosophy of science to highlight an optimal framework to face the extreme complexity of psychological systems. The investigation of psychological phenomena from a broader philosophical perspective on measurement could appear detached from active research practice. One might ask how such philosophical considerations can become practically useful. Our position is that their immediate value lies precisely in how they shape the design, data acquisition, and analysis practices of ILD research.

The core argument here is that measurement in affective science is not a passive extraction of internal states, but an interaction that partially structures and modifies the experiential process itself. Recognizing this has direct consequences for how ILD studies should ideally be constructed, documented, and interpreted. In what follows, we identify three interconnected directions and recommendations to account for the interactive nature of measurement in emotion-dynamics research.

1. Rethinking measurement practices in Affective Science

There is growing recognition of the need to strengthen the scientific rigor of ILD to address fundamental challenges, including limited falsifiability, threats to validity and reproducibility, and, consequently, constraints on cumulative scientific progress (Degroote et al., 2020; Perski et al., 2022). These concerns are increasingly reflected in literature, particularly in the documented heterogeneity of study protocols, limited justification for methodological choices, insufficient reporting of study procedures and analytic decisions, and inconsistent attention to the psychometric properties of ILD measures (McNeish et al., 2021; Stinson et al., 2022). We wholeheartedly agree with these concerns and elaborate on them by highlighting that measurement should be understood as an interaction between the phenomenon being studied, the measurement procedure, and the context in which measurement occurs. This perspective suggests that many practical challenges facing EMA stem from the absence of a sufficiently integrated measurement paradigm (Hall et al., 2021; Hopwood et al., 2022; Janssens et al., 2018). A concerning image is drawn by surveys among researchers regarding their experimental design choices. Many researchers responded that they did not know it was relevant to have clear documentation of their measurement design or that they did not know how to do it (Vogelsmeier et al., 2024). Such findings suggest both a need and an opportunity to develop a deeper understanding of the measurement paradigm underlying EMA and to integrate it more coherently with contemporary knowledge of psychological phenomena and the methodological resources available to study them. If EMA measurement is not merely a passive observation but an act that interacts with and partially structures the affective process being measured, researchers currently lack a principled basis for anticipating, modeling, and justifying how methodological choices influence the resulting data. The practical inconsistencies identified across the literature referenced above could therefore be understood as downstream manifestations of this deeper conceptual gap.

The question is how to bridge this gap. Building on philosophical and practical considerations, as well as the plethora of meta-analytic works urging stricter design guidelines (e.g., Stone et al., 2023), we suggest that one condition for this is to aim for greater standardization of research practices. However, rather than arguing for a top-down dictated set of strict, gold-standard guidelines, we advocate for a relative standardization for EMA studies in affective science. In the absence of a clear and uncontested unified theoretical framework, imposing rigid universal protocols to guide all aspects of design, measurement and analysis of emotion dynamics research is likely to be unproductive.

Instead, we advocate for the development of a looser set of structured baseline frameworks, where methodological choices such as sampling density, prompt timing, regularity, study duration, item construction, and quantification methods can be chosen by researchers as they see fit but must always be theoretically and empirically justified.

For instance, the choice to assess discrete emotions (e.g., anger, fear or sadness) rather than measures of general negative affect should be based on theories of emotional structure and existing evidence regarding the specific research interest. This provides much-needed flexibility for the field to progress while ensuring the conditions are met for truly iterative scientific practice. Existing recommendations have already moved in this direction by emphasizing transparency and theory-based design choices (Hamaker et al., 2018; Hopwood et al., 2022; Kaurin et al., 2023; Stone et al., 2023; Vogelsmeier et al., 2024). However, these recommendations have often remained at the level of methodological advice or guidelines. We suspect that because such guidelines are quite general in scope and not tied to any strong theoretical claims, their uptake has been limited and they have not yet become a part of a cumulative scientific paradigm.

A clear illustration of this problem is the way sampling schedules are often selected. Despite growing knowledge about the temporal characteristics of affective processes, decisions regarding sampling frequency remain highly variable across studies and are frequently tailored in an ad hoc fashion, with limited truly theoretical or empirical justification. For example, literature on affective dynamics suggests that certain temporal densities (roughly around 10 assessments/day) are often necessary to meaningfully capture mood processes. Depending on the study's focus, a lower bound of assessments needs to be applied when targeting specific constructs and/or dynamics. For instance, studies into critical non-linear transitions of negative affect (NA), need enough measurements to make sure these transitions are not missed. Otherwise, important aspects of the underlying processes will be obscured (Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2009).

However, a counterargument for denser prompts over prolonged measurement periods is that such protocols increase burden (Eisele et al., 2022). These considerations often lead researchers to base their choice of prompt density on a compromise between the required optimal density on the one hand and burden for participants on the other. However, rather than accepting this trade-off as a given, we argue that it should be an explicit part of our theoretical considerations, as well as our empirical investigation. For instance, if we assume that repeated measurement is inherently burdensome for participants, what does that imply about the meaning of what we measure and how do we establish when the burden crosses the tolerability threshold for participants?

Of course, EMA measurements involve numerous interacting variables, and no framework can perfectly regulate all of them. Methodological approximations are therefore inevitable, and some design decisions regarding variables, thresholds, sampling intervals, or analytical choices will later prove suboptimal. However, this uncertainty should not encourage methodological fragmentation nor force researchers to resort to a set of strict but mostly rule-of-thumb design rules. Rather, it strengthens the case for a common framework built on transparent documentation and explicit justification of methodological choices. Scientific progress is cumulative. Even imperfect measurement protocols can contribute meaningfully when their assumptions and limitations are thoroughly documented, allowing future studies to compare, refine, and improve upon them. Such an approach could improve our understanding of mood disorders while advancing and refining the practice of EMA and other ILD approaches.

Taken together, the first opportunity for the field to progress that we identify lies in a transparent, theory-informed measurement framework. This could serve as a stepping stone towards adaptive measurement systems which optimize the trade-off between data quantity and quality. More concretely, we propose that a useful framework for making the best possible use of EMA is a dynamic protocol. Ultimately, advancing EMA methodology requires moving beyond treating measurement as a fixed data collection procedure, toward understanding measurement as a dynamic process that actively shapes the data it produces. Developing a richer theoretical and empirical understanding of measurement processes will improve our ability to design assessments that are both scientifically informative and sensitive to participant burden. Rather than relying on fixed schedules and static questionnaires, future EMA applications could incorporate measurable "measurement hyperparameters" like burden, response latency, compliance trajectories, missingness, contextual factors (e.g., working versus non-working days), and other behavioral indicators to dynamically adjust prompt frequency, questionnaire length, item complexity, or assessment timing. The objective would not simply be to maximize compliance, but to optimize the trade-off between data quantity and data quality for each participant and context. Designing such adaptive measurement systems requires us to systematically investigate these interactive parameters in real EMA datasets so that adaptation strategies are grounded in empirical evidence rather than intuition. This can only start with thorough documentation of the relevant influences on measurement outcomes. Prompt frequency, specific timing on the day, context of the participant's environment, response scales, and burden are only some of the features that should ideally be thoroughly documented across measurements and participants. Formulating such a scheme of documentation can have consequences far beyond the measurement stage itself: it will influence the quality and validity of the collected data, the robustness of downstream statistical analyses, and ultimately the conclusions that can be drawn about the psychological phenomena under investigation. Better understanding and control of the measurement process itself is therefore not merely a methodological refinement but a prerequisite for improving the entire chain of inference in intensive longitudinal research using EMA and related methodologies.

2. Measurement as Interaction: From Physical Systems to Psychological Processes

To realize the full potential of modeling in psychology, we posit that a conceptual shift is required in how psychological quantities, measurements, and models are understood. Central to this shift is a reconsideration of measurement, not as a passive readout of existing properties, but as an interaction that partially constitutes the phenomenon itself. This perspective, well established in the physical sciences, has profound implications for psychological research. It is an issue that has been raised occasionally, and recently the size of its impact is becoming more apparent (Bringmann et al., 2022; Eronen, 2020). For instance, receiving the question "How happy are you right now?" immediately before or after an argument with a loved one may not only coincide with a change in the person's emotional state, but may also change how they interpret and evaluate the question. They may consider their happiness in relation to the immediate event or instead view it as a broader reflection of how they have been feeling overall.

In all scientific domains, measurement is not a neutral act. Observation influences the phenomenon being studied. The most famous example from physics is the double-slit experiment, in which the interference pattern collapses when particle trajectories are being measured. Fundamental particles, including photons and electrons, have been confirmed to exhibit both wave and particle characteristics, a behavior that runs contrary to our intuition. In addition, the quantum Zeno effect demonstrates that repeated measurement can inhibit or "freeze" the natural evolution of a quantum system (Itano et al., 1990). None of these effects are exotic anomalies but fundamental features of how measurement interacts with physical systems. These counterintuitive concepts for human perception are essential to understand the behavior of matter on atomic and subatomic levels. However, the same logic extends beyond the quantum domain. In cellular biology, patch-clamp recordings, one of the most important tools for studying ion channels, alter membrane properties and can perturb the very states they aim to measure (Neher & Sakmann, 1976). Fluorescent tagging, although widely used, can change protein conformation, kinetics, and localization (Day & Davidson, 2009). In each case, the measurement apparatus becomes part of the system's structure. These scientific fields have responded to this by explicitly modeling, quantifying, or correcting for these influences.

However, beyond these concerns, we raise a deeper issue, closer to the ontological implications of measurement in quantum mechanics. When individuals are asked to report their current emotional state, they must interrupt the ongoing flow of experience, direct attention inward, and engage in processes of recognition, categorization, and evaluation. This act of introspection is not neutral. It transforms the experiential state by stabilizing, simplifying, or even reinterpreting it. In this sense, the measurement process introduces a form of state reduction, which often depends strongly on the measurement's features. In affective dynamics research, a probabilistic, dynamically evolving affective process is condensed into a discrete reportable category. Just as measurement in quantum systems selects a specific outcome from a range of possibilities (Rovelli, 1996), introspection may result in selection and stabilization of one interpretation of an emotional state among multiple potential experiential configuration. We do not wish to imply that emotions follow quantum laws but rather highlight the inherently observer-dependent nature of experiential data: what is reported reflects not only the underlying process but also the conditions and constraints of the measurement act. In fact, such views, i.e., the relational and dynamic nature of affect, are familiar in psychological theory through Interactionism (Shott, 1979). To some extent, these views have already been embedded in contemporary fields like affective science, cognitive science and neuroscience (Kim & Sinatra, 2018; Pfeiffer et al., 2013; Reynolds et al., 2010; Seth & Friston, 2016; Tsakiris & Critchley, 2016). However, the interactive nature of measurement is not yet widely accepted within the broader experiential-data research community and, consequently, has not been incorporated into the paradigmatic strategies typically employed by ILD researchers. Still, the interaction between the observable and the observer is evident in well-documented issues in psychological measurement. Linguistic framing, contextual influences, response styles, and individual differences in emotional differentiation all affect how individuals interpret and respond to questions (Barrett, 2006; Erbas et al., 2019). These factors introduce variability in measurement outcomes that are not caused by the underlying emotional process but by the measurement process itself. Moreover, the very act of categorizing an experience into predefined labels formulates a type of structure, which can have a defining effect on the ongoing continuous emotional process (Nook et al., 2018). Everyday practices also provide intuitive support for the influence of introspection on the dynamic experiential process. In psychotherapy and contemplative practices such as mindfulness or meditation, attending to and labeling emotions is known to alter emotional experience (Sparacio et al., 2024). Recognizing a feeling can modulate its intensity, duration, or meaning. This suggests that the process of measurement, in this case understood as directed attention and categorization, is itself a mechanism of change, not merely a tool for observation (Barcaccia et al., 2024; Sparacio et al., 2024; Zainal & Newman, 2024). While such effects are often discussed in applied contexts, their implications for research methodology are rarely fully integrated into the design and interpretation of empirical studies (Borsboom et al., 2004; Schwarz, 1999). We propose that the inherently interactive nature of ESM/EMA, together with the need for complementary and at least partially independent measures that can inform and crosscheck one another, underscores the importance of incorporating multiple means of characterizing emotional processes within a common modeling framework.

Based on the above, the second opportunity we recognize for the field is to move toward multicomponent models that include additional, at least partially independent, sources of measurement. We argue that experiential self-reports are essentially very rough approximations that on their own are unlikely to adequately capture all meaningful aspects of our rich emotional world (Weidman et al., 2017). A concrete way to address this problem could be the inclusion of additional, at least partially independent, sources of measurement to identify, control, and ultimately reduce biases introduced by the measurement processes themselves. Examples could be physiological measures like heart rate and heart rate variability, behavioral measures like movement, phone-use patterns, social interactions, contextual measures like location, and environmental characteristics. At the same time, we recognize that experiential self-reports provide a unique type of information that cannot simply be replaced by other measurement modalities. They offer direct access to the subjective, first-person quality-or the feel-of an experience. In this sense, there is considerable truth in the frequently cited principle that "if you want to know how someone feels, the best way is to ask them" (Barrett, 2004).

Although this distinctive perspective makes experiential self-report an indispensable component of emotion research, it does not imply that self-reports offer a perfect window into the emotional experience. Instead, they contribute one important source of evidence that should be interpreted alongside complementary forms of measurement.

To illustrate the limitations of relying on a single measurement modality, we turn to an analogy from the physical sciences, which highlights a generally applicable principle: every measurement involves a trade-off between the information it provides and the extent to which it alters the phenomenon it seeks to measure. Consider the task of understanding the motion of a moving ball. A single measurement of its speed provides useful information, but it does not capture the overall character of the motion. One approach is to stop the ball with our hand and infer its speed from the force we experience. This method provides information that cannot be obtained through distant observation alone-the direct sensory experience of the ball's momentum. However, because kinetic energy is transferred to our hand, the act of measurement inevitably alters the ball's speed. If our hand were a weightless sensor with perfect sensitivity, this perturbation would be negligible. An analogous situation in psychology would be an ideal, but completely fictional EMA assessment capable of obtaining an instantaneous and complete report of an individual's emotional experience without influencing that experience. In an alternative approach, we could estimate the ball's speed by observing it move between two points and calculating the time required to travel the distance. Although this method also involves interaction, light reflected from the ball reaches our eyes. Its influence on the ball's motion is negligible, allowing the measurement to be treated as practically independent of the phenomenon. This analogy highlights that two measurement strategies aimed at measuring the same thing are not by definition interchangeable because they capture different aspects of the phenomenon. Measuring the ball through direct contact provides information about the interaction itself that cannot be recovered from distant observation, while distant observation minimizes interference with the ball's motion. Similarly, EMA and ESM provide unique access to the subjective, moment-to-moment qualities of emotional experience that cannot be fully reconstructed from more independent measures such as behavioral observations, physiological recordings, or passive sensing (Csikszentmihalyi & Larson, 1987; Lindquist et al., 2012). Yet precisely because these methods require individuals to attend to and report their ongoing experience, they can also modify the emotional processes they are aimed to measure.

Thus, the implication here is not that EMA should be replaced by less reactive methods, but that EMA as a single measurement approach is insufficient to characterize the totality of emotional processes. Just as understanding the motion of the ball benefits from combining direct and indirect measurements, understanding emotion requires integrating methods that vary in how much they perturb the phenomenon and in the aspects of experience they reveal. Building comprehensive models of emotion therefore depends on combining the unique strengths of self-report, behavioral, physiological, and other less reactive measures, while explicitly recognizing the limitations and biases each introduces (De Boer et al., 2021). Scientific progress often depends on combining multiple partially independent forms of measurement (Bertz et al., 2018; Colombo et al., 2019) and this paradigm also applies in the case of ILD and emotions. More concretely, in affective science this could entail integrating EMA data with less intrusive and faster physiological or environmental measures, such as circadian rhythms, heart rate variability, cortisol, respiration, or passive sensing streams, such as routine use of smartphones (Beierle et al., 2020), tracking of voice features during speech (Du et al., 2024) and speech recognition itself (Wang et al., 2025). These should not be treated as reductions of emotion to biology or other factors, but as complementary layers of a complex multicomponent process (Lohani et al., 2019). With current technological advances, innovative methods for analyzing fast-paced data are rapidly emerging (Maselli et al., 2023). Combined with the pattern-recognition capabilities of artificial neural networks (Kanjo et al., 2019), such multimodal approaches may identify psychologically meaningful temporal patterns without requiring overly rigid assumptions beforehand (Kim et al., 2019; King et al., 2024).

3. Approaching the Temporal Scale of Measurement and Multiscale

Dynamics of Psychological Phenomena.

When studying dynamic processes and systems, a key consideration is the temporal scale used to measure and analyze the process under study. However, despite its obvious importance to ILD-based affective science (Barrett, 2006; Kuppens & Verduyn, 2017; Scherer, 2005; Smith et al., 2018), we argue that this aspect has received less attention than it deserves. Broadly used measures such as the MSSD and AR(1) are strongly scale-dependent and therefore directly affected by the choice of intervals between measurements. To illustrate this, we take a closer look at the MSSD and the AR(1) coefficient, respectively. Although the dependence of these metrics on the interval between successive observations has already been recognized, most notably by Jahng et al. (2008), who also proposed a time-adjusted approach for situations involving unequal intervals, the implications of temporal scale extend beyond the technical correction of irregular sampling. The question is not only whether a measure is reliable or statistically stable within a given measurement design, but also what temporal scale it represents and whether that scale is theoretically appropriate for the process under investigation.

To make this distinction explicit, we simulated a single latent affective process and sampled it at different temporal resolutions while leaving the underlying process unchanged (See simulation code in the Supplementary Material). To illustrate the effect of temporal sampling on the characterization of dynamic affective processes, we used a firstorder autoregressive process AR(1) describing the current state as a function of its immediately preceding state and a stochastic noise term for data generation: where 𝑋 𝑡 denotes the state at time 𝑡, 𝜙 is the autoregressive parameter, and 𝜖 𝑡 is white noise.

The parameter 𝜙 controls the temporal persistence of the process, with higher values indicating stronger dependence between successive observations. The process was generated on a 30-minute temporal grid, providing a common underlying trajectory, from which different observation schedules could be constructed. This choice should be understood as the simulation time step rather than as an assumption that affective processes intrinsically operate on a 30-minute timescale. The resulting trajectory was subsequently sampled at increasingly wider intervals, while leaving the underlying process unchanged. Thus, the different sampling conditions represent alternative observation schemes applied to the same latent trajectory, rather than different psychological processes. The simulation results (Figure 1) show that as the interval between observations increases, the magnitude of successive differences and therefore the observed MSSD changes, despite the absence of any change in the underlying process. This illustrates a simple but consequential point: MSSD is not solely a property of the distribution of affective states, but it is also a property of the distribution of states as they are ordered and separated in time. Changing the temporal distance between successive observations changes the temporal comparison on which MSSD is based. This does not imply that MSSD is inherently unreliable or that one sampling frequency is universally preferable above all others. Rather, it highlights that interpreting MSSD depends on the temporal resolution of the measurement design. Figure 1 shows that the same phenomenon is evident when we look at the estimated AR(1). A value obtained from observations separated by 30 minutes, and a value obtained from observations separated by 3 hours may both be perfectly well estimated, yet they quantify successive change over different temporal windows. Consequently, treating them as interchangeable measures of the same underlying form of instability or inertia can obscure an important component of what such statistics represent.

Of course, the illustration is an oversimplified example. The AR(1) process provides a deliberately simple and controlled representation of affective dynamics and does not capture nonlinearities, regime changes, time-varying parameters, or other complexities that may characterize real affective processes. Likewise, the 30-minute simulation step is an arbitrary modeling choice. Still, the general principle holds and although we focus on MSSD and AR(1), the same issue applies to other statistics that depend on temporal separation, including higher-order autocorrelations, lagged associations, and changebased measures. Irregular sampling, missing observations, and non-compliance introduce additional complications that are not considered here. Taken together, the illustration highlights that the temporal scale of measurement is not merely a methodological detail but part of what determines the statistical representation of a dynamic psychological process.

Based the abovementioned points and illustration, we formulate our third and most general recommendation. We propose to focus on the development of measurementinformed dynamic models that integrate proximal, observable processes with higher-level psychological phenomena across different timescales. Such models should distinguish descriptive temporal dependence from causal inference and should be evaluated by whether they generate testable predictions that can subsequently be examined using appropriate causal or experimental designs.

From this perspective, we propose complementing analyses of psychological symptoms or constructs with analyses of more proximal elements of a person's lived psychological and environmental system. The relevant criterion is not simply that these elements occur at a "lower" level of organization, but that they are relatively directly observed, more densely measurable and potentially perturbed. Such elements may include concrete behaviors, affective experiences, situational conditions, interpersonal interactions, physiological states, environmental exposures, or other temporally proximal features of everyday life.

A complexity-theoretic perspective provides a useful theoretical basis for this approach. Rather than assuming that higher-level psychological phenomena can be adequately represented as collections of relatively independent variables, complex systems perspectives emphasize how system-level patterns can emerge from interactions among multiple interdependent components over time. In this view, several general properties become relevant: emergence, whereby higher-level patterns arise from interactions among lower-level processes (Rupe & Crutchfield, 2024); multiscale dynamics, whereby processes operating at different temporal and organizational scales interact (Cohen & Harel, 2007; Gao & Xu, 2021); and state-space dynamics, whereby psychological systems can move through changing configurations and potentially occupy relatively stable or recurrent states (McClintock et al., 2020). Such principles have increasingly informed complex-systems approaches to psychopathology and provide a theoretical rationale for studying psychological phenomena as evolving patterns within person-environment systems (Bringmann et al., 2023; Hayes & Andrews, 2020; Olthof et al., 2023). These principles suggest that analytical models should be capable of representing time-dependent interactions, feedback, cross-timescale dependencies, and transitions between system states, rather than treating associations among variables as necessarily stationary or context-independent. For example, smartphone use, social interaction, sleep, affective experiences, and depressive symptoms could be represented as components of an interconnected system, in which changes in one component may be followed by changes in others over time. Network-based and complexity-theoretic approaches, combined with dynamic ILD, offer a promising framework for investigating how these measurements may be integrated across time and levels of abstraction in a multi-layer approach (De Boer et al., 2021; Jover Martínez et al., 2026). This perspective leads to a practical question that differs somewhat from the conventional question of whether psychological variables are associated with each other. Rather, more specific questions arise: if a measurable and potentially perturbable aspect of a person's life or ongoing experience is changed, how does that perturbation propagate through the system, what psychological states might subsequently change, and under what conditions?

Developing analytical approaches capable of addressing this question may allow network and complexity-based methods to retain their exploratory strengths while bringing them closer to an intervention-oriented understanding of psychological dynamics (Borsboom et al., 2021; Bringmann et al., 2013; Eronen, 2020).

Conclusion

In conclusion, the field of affective dynamics is at a promising yet challenging stage of development. Further progress requires more than increasingly sophisticated models or denser data collection. It requires closer alignment between theory, measurement, and analysis, while recognizing that the process of measurement itself can shape both the data obtained and the emotional processes under investigation.
