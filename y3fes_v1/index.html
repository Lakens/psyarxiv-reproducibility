Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Self-enactable behavior change techniques (SE-BCTs) are actions individuals can apply independently to support their behavior change. Adults may use some techniques intuitively without formal instruction; however, little is known about how they understand, enact, and evaluate SE-BCTs in daily life. We examined middle-aged adults' SE-BCT use, preferences, and perceived usefulness across physical activity initiation, action, and maintenance stages. Nineteen middle-aged adults with varying levels of self-reported physical activity participated in focus groups. Deductive framework analysis identified SE-BCTs in participants' accounts. Inductive thematic analysis explored participants' experiences of understanding, selecting, applying, adapting, and sustaining SE-BCTs across physical activity stages. We identified 216 technique-coded occurrences, representing 56 of the 123 techniques in the SE-BCT compendium. Of these occurrences, 66.2% were congruent with the compendium definitions. Goal setting and revision, self-monitoring, and problem solving were enacted only partially. Participants gave limited attention to some techniques (e.g., source credibility) and viewed others (e.g., environmental restructuring) as insufficient when used alone. SE-BCTs were perceived as interconnected strategies whose usefulness depended on personal fit, context, and phase of behavior change. SE-BCT education may therefore be most useful when it emphasizes practical implementation, combinations of complementary techniques, and flexible adaptation to individual needs and changing behavioral demands.

Introduction

In 2022, approximately one-third of adults worldwide did not meet the World Health Organization's (WHO) physical activity recommendations. More specifically, age-stratified estimates indicate that around three in ten adults aged 40-59 years were insufficiently active (Strain et al., 2024; World Health Organization, 2024). Fortunately, it has been shown that becoming physically active later in adulthood (40-61 years of age) may provide comparable health benefits to long-term participation in physical activity (Saint-Maurice et al., 2019).

Midlife is characterized by circumstances that may make regular physical activity difficult to initiate or maintain. Work, family, and caregiving responsibilities often compete for time and may disrupt previously established routines. Job strain and competing demands on discretionary time can constrain opportunities for leisure-time physical activity (Fransson et al., 2012; Lachman et al., 2018), while parenthood, particularly caring for young children, has been associated with lower physical activity participation among both women and men (Gropper et al., 2020; Palomäki et al., 2023). Consequently, strategies that can be flexibly incorporated into everyday life may be particularly relevant for this population.

Despite these challenges, middle-aged adults have received limited attention as a distinct target group for physical activity promotion, in comparison to other age groups. A recent rapid review highlighted the limited availability of physical activity and healthy-eating health-promotion programs specifically targeting middle-aged adults through local government and health-service partnerships (Wallbank et al., 2023). This gap is important because midlife may represent a strategic window for preventing long-term declines in physical activity and reducing future chronic disease risk. Addressing this gap requires approaches that can be delivered at scale and enable individuals to manage their behavior with limited ongoing support (Ntoumanis & Mazéas, 2026).

One potential approach is to equip individuals with self-enactable behavior change techniques (SE-BCTs; Knittle et al., 2020), defined as strategies that individuals can apply on their own to self-regulate motivation and behavior. In 2020, Knittle and colleagues published a compendium listing 123 SE-BCTs, including goal setting, self-monitoring, action planning, problem solving, and restructuring everyday routines. SE-BCTs shift the narrative from BCTs being delivered by an intervention to the behavioral and cognitive actions that individuals themselves undertake in the process of behavior change (Hankonen, 2021; Knittle et al., 2020). Supporting individuals in acquiring and using these techniques may offer a promising avenue for fostering autonomy and sustaining behavior change (Ntoumanis & Moller, 2025; Smit et al., 2019).

Recent advances in digital technologies and the conceptualization of SE-BCTs offer new opportunities to support the acquisition and use of SE-BCTs. Digital technologies enable the delivery of SE-BCT training at scale while tailoring content to individuals' needs, preferences, and experiences (Ntoumanis & Mazéas, 2026). In terms of conceptual advancements, Mazéas and Ntoumanis (2026) proposed a theory-informed sequencing framework that organizes 108 SE-BCTs within 27 groups, which are in turn organized into nine overarching clusters. While clusters reflect key steps in the behavior change process (e.g., setting goals, developing habits, or managing emotions), groups address more specific, practical questions within each cluster (e.g., how to set, review, and integrate goals into daily life). The framework also identifies potential prerequisite relations between techniques and suggests how their teaching and application might be progressively structured across different phases of behavior change. Together, these advances offer a promising foundation for developing structured, yet personalized, approaches to SE-BCT training.

Crucially, personalizing such training also requires researchers to understand that individuals may already have experience using behavior change strategies. Recent research on SE-BCTs suggested the potential value of considering individuals' existing strategies, goals, and circumstances when selecting techniques to support behavior change (Schüler & Bieleke, 2024). Hence, individuals should not necessarily be considered naïve recipients of SE-BCT training: they may already use some techniques spontaneously, vary in their understanding of these techniques, and differ in the extent to which they perceive particular strategies as useful, acceptable, or relevant to their circumstances (Fu et al., 2022; Schüler & Bieleke, 2024).

Emerging evidence suggests considerable heterogeneity in how individuals spontaneously use SE-BCTs to manage physical activity and healthy eating, both in terms of the techniques used and their frequency of use (Fu et al., 2022; Heino et al., 2019; Schüler & Bieleke, 2024). Thus, participants may already use strategies corresponding to formal BCTs without knowing their technical labels or having received explicit instruction in them. This is supported by studies examining physical activity-related BCT use, which have documented the use of self-regulatory techniques among participants in control groups and prior to intervention exposure (Hankonen et al., 2017; Palsola et al., 2025).

In addition, individuals may also differ in how they perceive and understand BCTs.

Research on intervention receipt has shown considerable variation, both between participants and between BCTs, in how self-regulatory techniques such as self-monitoring, goal setting, problem solving, and action planning are understood and subsequently used (Miles et al., 2022; Palsola et al., 2020). Importantly, understanding a technique does not necessarily lead to its use.

For example, Renko and colleagues (2022) found that some adolescents perceived planning as restrictive, burdensome, or incompatible with their preference for spontaneity, which contributed to their reluctance to use it. Individual preferences may, therefore, represent another important consideration, as previous research has pointed out that individuals differ in which BCTs they perceive as useful, appealing, or appropriate for a particular behavior; assessing such preferences has been proposed as one component of user-centered intervention development (DeSmet et al., 2019). Therefore, the relevance and uptake of a particular SE-BCT may depend not only on its theoretical potential to influence the target behavior but also on whether the individual understands it, already uses it, perceives it as useful, and is willing to enact it.

Taken together, these studies highlight the importance of learning more about how SE-BCTs are subjectively experienced and used when developing self-management interventions based on SE-BCT learning. However, although previous research has examined individuals' reported BCT use, understanding, and preferences, less attention has been given to systematically assessing whether spontaneously reported strategies align with formal (SE-)BCT definitions and reflect congruent, partial, or divergent enactment. Assessing current use and the extent to which reported strategies align with formal definitions can help identify techniques that participants already employ, those that are relatively unfamiliar, and those that may be only partially enacted. In addition, exploring participants' understanding, experiences, challenges associated with enactment, and preferences can help identify techniques that are difficult to interpret or apply, or perceived as useful, acceptable, or relevant to their circumstances. Such information could inform the subsequent design of more personalized SE-BCT support by allowing training to build on existing skills while directing greater attention towards techniques that are unfamiliar, poorly understood, difficult to enact, or perceived as potentially useful.

The Present Study

We aimed to explore middle-aged adults' existing use and enactment of SE-BCTs for physical activity (RQ1), their experiences of enacting these techniques in everyday life with associated challenges (RQ2), and their preferences and perceptions of their usefulness and relevance (RQ3). By characterizing participants' existing behavioral repertoires and perspectives on SE-BCTs, the study seeks to inform the development and sequencing of personalized SE-BCT training adapted to participants' understanding, preferences, and perceived needs.

Methods

Study Design

This exploratory qualitative research employed focus group discussions, conducted as part of a co-creation workshop of the ENACTING project held in Odense, Denmark, in 2025.

The project aims to co-create a digital platform for training adults to use SE-BCTs to support their physical activity. Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee at the University of Southern Denmark (approval ID REC523). Participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Data were pseudonymized for analysis and reporting.

Participants and Recruitment

Eligibility criteria were being 35-55 years old, able to communicate in Danish, and able to attend the workshops in person. Participants were recruited via purposive sampling to ensure diversity in physical activity levels, gender, socioeconomic status, age, occupation, and digital literacy. We aimed to recruit participants with varying levels of physical activity, with a greater proportion of less active adults, while also including more active individuals to capture experiences of both initiating and maintaining physical activity. Screening questions assessed self-reported physical activity, intentions to be physically active, and participants' perceived stage of behavior change. Recruitment was conducted primarily through social media, including local Facebook groups. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time. Participants received a gift card of 300 DKK (about $45 USD) for attending this workshop.

Procedure and Materials

At the beginning of the workshop, participants received a brief introduction to the ENACTING project and to the general concept of behavior change. The facilitators also introduced three broad phases of physical activity behavior change (i.e., initiation, action, and maintenance) to provide a common frame of reference for subsequent activities. Workshop activities progressed from relatively open-ended exploration of participants' existing experiences to more structured discussion of explicit SE-BCTs. This procedure was intended to distinguish strategies generated spontaneously by participants from their responses after being exposed to formal descriptions of SE-BCTs. Participants were divided into four focus groups (i.e., participant panels) of 4-6 participants. Each panel focused on a different BCT theme (i.e., Skills and Self-Motivation, Emotions, Behavioral Regulation, and Goals), to broaden the range of SE-BCTs discussed in the workshop and reduce repetition across panels.

The themes were informed by the SE-BCT clustering and grouping of the sequencing framework developed by Mazéas and Ntoumanis (2026). Although the thematic focus differed across panels, they all followed the same overall sequence of activities.

1. Scenario-based discussion

The first activity consisted of a scenario-based focus-group discussion designed to elicit participants' spontaneous perspectives on managing physical activity. Each panel received a short written scenario (see Table S1 in the supplementary materials) aligned with its assigned BCT theme. The scenarios incorporated challenges relevant to different phases of behavior change and invited participants to consider how a person might initiate, regulate, or sustain their physical activity. Participants first read the scenario individually and then discussed it collectively using a semi-structured format, facilitated by a member of the research team. Each panel was guided by one facilitator with extensive experience in co-designed activities with end users. Facilitators encouraged participants to draw on their own experiences, perceived barriers, and strategies. No predefined SE-BCTs were presented at this stage. The purpose was to identify behavior-change strategies that participants generated spontaneously, before their responses could be influenced by exposure to the SE-BCT terminology or descriptions.

2. Prompted reflection

Following the open scenario discussion, participants completed a more structured reflection exercise focused on their panel's theme. Theme-level prompt cards were used to encourage discussion of relevant self-regulatory processes without yet introducing the formal labels or definitions of individual SE-BCTs. For example, participants in the Goals group were asked to reflect on how they chose physical activities, established goals for being active, adapted goals when they became too difficult or too easy, and planned actions to remain on track. Facilitators used prompts to explore how these processes related to participants' previous experiences and existing approaches to physical activity. The list of the prompts and task instructions is provided in Table S2 of the supplementary materials.

3. Technique familiarization and evaluation

Participants were subsequently introduced to ten SE-BCTs selected for their panel's theme. Each technique was presented using a standardized description derived from the SE-BCT compendium (Knittle et al., 2020). Participants were invited to describe how they interpreted each technique and whether it was familiar to them. Discussion also explored participants' perceptions of each technique's usefulness, relevance, and acceptability.

Facilitators used neutral language and avoided providing normative guidance about how participants should interpret or enact the techniques. A complete list of the SE-BCTs presented within each panel is provided in Table S3 of the supplementary materials.

4. Ranking, prioritization, and perceived sequencing

In the final activity, participants individually evaluated the 10 SE-BCTs presented within their panel. Using paper-based materials and adhesive dots to count votes, participants indicated which techniques they considered most useful or interesting for supporting their own physical activity. Participants were also asked to classify each technique according to the phase of behavior change in which they perceived it to be most useful (i.e., initiation, action, or maintenance). Participants then discussed their choices collectively. Facilitators encouraged participants to explain the reasoning underlying their evaluations, compare perspectives, and discuss differences in how techniques were interpreted or considered useful across the phases of behavior change.

Data Collection and Processing

Data collected during the workshop activities included audio recordings, physical copies of completed activity materials, and researcher and facilitator notes. Audio recordings were transcribed using OpenAI's Whisper automatic speech recognition model (Radford et al., 2023), run locally on a researcher's computer. The Danish-language transcripts were subsequently translated into English using OpenAI's gpt-oss open-weight language model (OpenAI et al., 2025), run locally via Ollama (Ollama, 2025). All translated transcripts were reviewed against the original Danish transcripts by a research assistant (MKM), who corrected and supplemented the translations where necessary before qualitative analysis.

Data Analysis

A hybrid inductive-deductive approach was taken to the analysis. The first stage used a deductive framework analysis (Gale et al., 2013), with the SE-BCT compendium (Knittle et al., 2020) providing the coding framework for identifying and categorizing spontaneously reported SE-BCTs. In the next sections, the names and numbering of SE-BCTs are, therefore, based on the aforementioned compendium. The analysis distinguished between participants' spontaneous accounts of behavior-change strategies, generated before formal SE-BCTs were introduced, and their subsequent responses to explicitly presented SE-BCTs. Strategies spontaneously described during the scenario-based discussions and the prompted reflections were compared with the definitions in the SE-BCT compendium and mapped to corresponding techniques. Congruence was classified into three categories: congruent, when a participant's description corresponded closely to the defining features of the SE-BCT; partially congruent, when the overall concept was consistent with the SE-BCT but one or more defining features were absent, unclear, or reflected recognition rather than full enactment (i.e., by "enactment" we refer to whether participants have applied a given SE-BCT in the past); and divergent, when the participant referred to a concept related to the SE-BCT but described or operationalized it in a way that differed substantially from the compendium definition. Two authors (AM, MKM) independently extracted candidate SE-BCTs from the transcripts. Two authors with expertise in SE-BCTs (AM, EH) then mapped these strategies onto corresponding SE-BCTs and assessed the degree of congruence between participants' descriptions and the relevant compendium definition. Disagreements regarding SE-BCT coding or congruence ratings were resolved through discussion and, where consensus could not be reached, through consultation with a third author (NN).

The second stage of this research used a theoretically informed, primarily inductive thematic analysis (V. Braun & Clarke, 2006) to explore participants' understanding, experiences, and perceptions of the techniques, allowing patterns and themes to be developed from the data. This analysis focused on exploring divergences in the congruence of the techniques, participants' understanding and interpretation of the presented SE-BCTs, their experiences of using them, and their perceptions of their acceptability, usefulness, and relevance across different phases of physical activity behavior change. The coding was carried out by two researchers (AM, MKM). Data from the ranking and prioritization activities were used alongside the qualitative discussions to contextualize participants' evaluations of individual techniques. To enhance the trustworthiness of the analysis, analytical decisions and the development of codes and themes were documented throughout the process, and regular discussions within the research team were used to review emerging interpretations and ensure that the findings remained grounded in participants' accounts.

Figures were generated in R (version 4.6.1, 2026) using ggplot2, patchwork, and scales.

Participant Characteristics and Demographics

Participants (n = 19) had a mean age of 45.2 years (SD = 7.6; range = 35-55 years) and included 14 women (73.7%). Participants had varied educational backgrounds, although 16 had completed some form of higher education (84.2%), two reported vocational education (10.5%), and one reported upper-secondary education (5.3%). Most participants reported relatively low levels of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity: seven reported less than 30 minutes per week (36.8%), nine reported 30-89 minutes (47.4%), one reported 90-149 minutes (5.3%), and two reported 150-299 minutes per week (10.5%). Participants' reporting of muscle-strengthening activity was also limited, with 10 participants reporting no weekly muscle-strengthening activity (52.6%), six reporting one session per week (31.6%), two reporting two sessions (10.5%), and one reporting three or more sessions per week (5.3%). Most participants described their main occupation as predominantly sedentary (n = 16, 84.2%), while two (10.5%) reported work performed mainly while standing or walking, and one (5.3%) reported standing or walking work involving some lifting or carrying. Finally, 17 participants indicated an intention to increase their physical activity level (89.5%), while two (10.5%) intended to maintain their current level.

Limited Consideration of Goal Specificity, Attainability, and Adjustment

Goal-setting strategies did not always align with recommendations that goals should be appropriately challenging, tailored to individuals' capabilities and circumstances, and regularly monitored and adjusted in light of progress (Swann et al., 2026). Some participants associated motivation with setting increasingly ambitious goals, rather than with selecting an appropriately challenging target. Mathias, for example, argued that "the goal always motivates... the bigger the goal, the better the motivation" (Skills & Self-motivation panel). In other accounts, goals were loosely defined or absent altogether. Casper explained: "I don't really set goals. One day I go a lot, and the next day I don't go as much. So there's nothing I have to reach 8,000 steps or something. It's just a feeling that I've been active" (Behavioral Regulation panel). Participants also rarely described systematically reviewing and adapting their goals. When a goal became too demanding, Andrea reported abandoning it rather than revising it: "I think I'm so bad at that stuff about adjusting. Because if it's too high, I just drop the whole damn thing" (Goals panel).

Similarly, Silje described goals losing their salience over time: "Basically, I forget that I'm working toward a goal. Or I forget that I have a goal. Or I set a new goal. And that usually happens. It's after New Year or after summer break" (Goals panel). Thus, although participants recognized the motivational potential of goals, their accounts were only partially congruent or divergent with the defining components of behavioral goal setting [#5], and particularly with reviewing goals in light of achievement [#9 and #10].

Awareness Without Systematic Tracking or Problem Resolution

Participants frequently mentioned actions that can be regarded as equivalent to selfmonitoring and problem solving, but their enactment did not fully align with the compendium descriptions. In relation to self-monitoring, participants demonstrated awareness of their activity or used devices capable of collecting activity data, but this did not necessarily involve deliberate and systematic tracking. August explained: "I'm very aware of frequency. It's exactly about not breaking out of the routine, and about whether you've gone a week, where maybe you haven't [exercised]" (Skills & Self-motivation panel), without describing how this frequency was recorded. Similarly, Anne reported that her watch collected step and GPS data but added: "I don't check the data that much" (Behavioral Regulation panel), suggesting passive data collection rather than active self-monitoring. This is in line with previous research that has emphasized that monitoring practices can differ in how information is recorded, reviewed, and used by different individuals to support behavioral regulation (Webb & de Bruin, 2020).

Problem solving was likewise sometimes limited to identifying a barrier, without generating or selecting a strategy to address it. Andrea recognized that fixed exercise arrangements were incompatible with her family responsibilities: "I cannot go at five every Tuesday, because it just does not fit: the kids may have something on, be ill, there may be a birthday, or whatever" (Goals panel); but did not describe a concrete resolution strategy. Trine similarly recognized that she "didn't quite reach the goal" because she had not understood how to set a realistic one (Behavioral Regulation panel), without explaining how she would resolve this difficulty. These accounts therefore reflected partial congruence of self-monitoring and problem-solving techniques with the compendium definitions; awareness was present, but systematic recording was missing from self-monitoring [#17 and #18], while the selection of a coping strategy was missing from problem-solving [#7].

Restructuring the Environment Was Not Always Perceived as a Sufficiently Effective Strategy on Its Own

Participants mentioned some environmental restructuring strategies but did not consider them effective or consistently regard them as sufficient to produce behavior change on their own. Accounts primarily concerned the Adding objects to the environment technique [#24].

Some accounts illustrated a gap between acquiring an object and using it. Reflecting on previous attempts to start running, Olivia explained: "I would buy the most expensive [brand] shoes, I had all the gear. But it did not get used" (Goals panel). She later described this tendency more critically: "My brain tends to think I can buy my way to it" (Goals panel). Changes to the social environment [e.g., #26] also appeared capable of supporting participation without necessarily producing further progression. Patricia reported that walking with a friend helped her maintain her activity, but that she "had not been able to increase it, to get better, so to speak" (Skills & self-motivation panel). Together, these accounts suggest that objects and supportive environments can reduce practical barriers or reinforce existing activity, but may need to be combined with motivational, planning, or self-regulatory strategies to translate opportunity into sustained action.

Limited Consideration of Source Credibility

When participants described obtaining information or instructions, the accessibility, visual presentation, and motivational value of the material appeared more salient than the credibility of its source. August described YouTube as "easy and quick to find inspiration," as well as visual and enjoyable, but acknowledged: "I had not really looked into whether it was actually correct. But she had been to the Olympics, so come on" (Skills & Self-motivation panel). Credibility was therefore inferred from the demonstrator's status rather than evaluated systematically. Mathias was even more explicit that reliability was secondary to inspiration: "At this point it is not so important whether the information is reliable; it is mainly about finding inspiration" (Skills & Self-motivation panel). Although August subsequently questioned whether brief online videos provided appropriate guidance: "Who knows whether the running technique I am using right now is actually good for me?" ; he did not identify a credible alternative source or a strategy for evaluating the available information. These findings suggest that obtaining instruction on how to perform the behavior [#56] could be enacted without the corresponding use of a credible source [#39].

Challenges in Understanding, Selecting, Applying, Adapting, and Sustaining SE-BCTs

Four themes were identified regarding participants' perceived difficulties with SE-BCTs. These related to a) difficulties understanding and interpreting technique descriptions, b) perceived interdependence of techniques, c) interindividual variation in the personal fit of individual techniques, and d) the need to adapt techniques over time.

Interpreting SE-BCT Descriptions

Participants experienced difficulties understanding some of the SE-BCTs from the descriptions provided, which were based on the SE-BCTs compendium (Knittle et al., 2020).

In some instances, participants explicitly stated that a technique or the task was difficult to understand, whereas in other cases their uncertainty became apparent through questions to the moderator about how a technique should be interpreted. For example, when discussing the Body Changes [#22] technique, Anne asked whether this could include losing weight before beginning to walk, or whether the technique was intended to refer more specifically to physical changes such as muscle mass. Participants could relate the general idea of a technique to their experiences without being certain what counted as an instance of it.

Similar uncertainty arose around Obtain information about others' approval [#38]. Clara asked whether the technique should be understood in "positive directions, or negative directions, or both?" and subsequently asked, "How should it be understood?" After receiving further explanation, she suggested that "it might be rephrased a bit." These quotes are good examples that BCT labels and definitions provided in the SE-BCT compendium were not always sufficiently clear for self-enactment, as some participants required examples, reformulation, or discussion before they could translate a technique into concrete action.

Interdependence of SE-BCTs

Participants experienced the SE-BCTs as more interconnected than their presentation as separate techniques suggested. Theis explained: "What I find a bit hard to understand is that these things are connected. Feelings and goals. It's just hard to pull one thing out. Everything is probably dependent on each other." This interdependence made it difficult to consider individual techniques in isolation. Participants also identified processes that they considered essential, but that had been allocated to other panels' themes. In the Behavioral Regulation panel, Casper noted the apparent absence of techniques involving friends or social networks, despite social commitment helping people move from intention to action. In the goals panel, Natalie questioned whether "some more feelings" should be included because "there are always more things at play." Participants therefore appeared to understand behavior change as a configuration of interacting processes, making the selection of a single technique seem somewhat artificial.

Personal Fit of SE-BCTs

A third theme concerned substantial individual variation in whether participants considered particular SE-BCTs appropriate or useful for themselves. They evaluated SE-BCTs in relation to their preferences, identities, circumstances, and previous experiences, rather than solely according to their potential effectiveness. August, for example, acknowledged that punishment-based techniques "could be motivating for some" but stated that he was "not definitely into" self-punishment or self-dissuasion. His rejection concerned personal suitability rather than an assumption that the techniques could never work for anyone. The same technique was also perceived differently across participants. Natalie considered "what works for me," "who I am as a person," and "what's realistic for me," explaining that crowded settings and advance booking did not suit her. Silje described herself as "completely opposite," finding scheduling classes with others helpful because "if it's just a promise to myself, it's very easy to cancel." These contrasting experiences illustrate how participants assessed SE-BCTs in relation to their individual preferences and circumstances. This emphasis on personal fit was also reflected in participants' selective adoption of techniques. Lise summarized this selectivity directly: "The three I've chosen are the three things I thought I could do. I can't do the others; that's not for me." Thus, beyond considering whether a SE-BCT was generally effective, participants frequently made a more idiosyncratic assessment regarding whether this technique was likely to work for them.

Adaptation of SE-BCT Use Over Time

Finally, participants' accounts suggested that choosing an initially appropriate technique was not sufficient for sustained enactment. They described modifying or abandoning strategies in response to experience, changing circumstances, physical capacity, or unintended effects. Some participants successfully recalibrated their techniques. Theis stopped monitoring average cycling speed because "it's no longer fun to look at" and instead focused on distance.

Casper made a similar adjustment to his running watch: "I have also stopped measuring speed on my running watch, so it only counts kilometers," because monitoring speed distracted him from "getting out, running, enjoying it, and being present in nature." Self-monitoring was therefore retained, but its implementation was modified to avoid undermining enjoyment.

Adaptation also occurred as participants' circumstances changed. After approximately 20 years of attending fitness centers, Clara concluded that her previous goals and choice of activities "simply doesn't work for me anymore" and shifted toward activities at home and outdoors.

Overall, sustained self-enactment appeared to require ongoing review and recalibration, rather than the continued use of a technique in its original form.

Participants' Preferences for and Perceived Usefulness of SE-BCTs

During the workshops, participants were asked to identify the techniques they preferred and considered most useful for them within initiation, action, and maintenance. The resulting voting patterns suggested a broad sequencing of techniques, with different strategies becoming more salient as participants moved from considering change, to performing the behavior, and finally to sustaining it, while some other techniques did not receive attention (Figure 3). Overall, participants' preferences indicated that the usefulness of SE-BCTs was dynamic across the behavior-change process. Some techniques appeared particularly relevant to a specific phase, such as Hypothetical thinking [#4] during initiation or reviewing goals during maintenance. However, Imaginary punishment [#96] received three points, suggesting that punitive techniques were not prioritized rather than being uniformly rejected. Nevertheless, it is important to note that participants experienced difficulties determining how techniques should be classified across different phases of behavior change. The subsequent discussion suggested that part of this difficulty resulted from differences between how the techniques had been described in the study materials and how participants understood them from their own experience.

Discussion

We found that middle-aged adults with varying levels of physical activity reported using a broad range of SE-BCTs in their everyday lives. Overall, 216 technique-coded occurrences referred to 56 distinct techniques. However, this broader total included techniques that were only partially enacted or described in a manner that diverged from the compendium definitions.

The 143 technique-congruent occurrences represented 49 distinct, clearly enacted techniques spanning all nine clusters of the organizational framework proposed by Mazéas and Ntoumanis (2026). Although most coded occurrences were congruent with the compendium definitions and recommendations, incomplete or divergent enactment was also evident. The thematic analysis provided further insight into these patterns. Goal setting, and more especially goal revision, were not consistently enacted, while techniques such as self-monitoring and action planning or problem solving were often only partially enacted. Participants also placed limited importance on the credibility of information sources and did not generally view physical or social environmental restructuring strategies as sufficient to produce behavior change on their own. More broadly, participants sometimes found the technique descriptions difficult to understand and viewed SE-BCTs as interconnected strategies that need to be used in combination, matched to individual needs and circumstances, and adjusted over time.

Consistent with this perspective, the perceived usefulness of particular SE-BCTs varied across the initiation, action, and maintenance phases, although several techniques were considered useful across more than one phase.

Our findings converge with previous quantitative and qualitative research highlighting the complexity and variability of SE-BCT use. The broad but uneven pattern of spontaneous SE-BCT use observed in the present study is consistent with previous research documenting variation in the use of different techniques and identifying clusters of BCTs that tend to be used together 11/13/26 2:38:00 PM. Similarly, Fu and colleagues (2022) found that participants commonly combined multiple BCTs. Extending these observations, our findings illustrate the broad repertoire of BCTs spontaneously used by participants when trying to change their physical activity and the fact that participants themselves perceived functional interdependencies between techniques and considered a single technique used in isolation to be potentially insufficient. Importantly, although SE-BCTs are defined as techniques that individuals can enact themselves, this does not necessarily imply that they must be enacted alone. Participants' accounts also highlighted the potential role of social interactions and support in facilitating the enactment of certain strategies, consistent with the dyadic BCT compendium, which recognizes the role of interpersonal collaboration in behavior change (Berli et al., 2025).

Regarding the understanding of techniques, French and colleagues (2021) found that participants of different ages perceived supportive planning as complex, had difficulty distinguishing it from other techniques, and struggled to generate appropriate strategies. Similar challenges have been documented in face-to-face interventions, where misunderstandings and difficulties with the uptake of planning persisted despite guidance (Renko et al., 2022). Furthermore, Palsola and colleagues (Palsola et al., 2020) found that although participants generally understood the key intervention messages and self-regulatory techniques, this understanding did not necessarily translate into enactment. Our findings extend these observations by highlighting that difficulties in understanding certain techniques persisted despite the SE-BCT compendium (Knittle et al., 2020) providing simplified language, clear explanations, and practical examples. This suggests that written descriptions alone may not always be sufficient to convey how techniques are intended to be applied and that additional guidance or practical illustrations may be needed.

Previous studies have also emphasized the importance of personal preferences and contextual factors in selecting and applying behavior change strategies (M. Braun et al., 2023; Hintsa et al., 2025), as well as the need to adapt them over time (French et al., 2021; Hintsa et al., 2025). For instance, French and colleagues (2021) found that diaries and plans could be useful initially but were sometimes subsequently abandoned, while Hintsa and colleagues (2025) reported that successful planning depended on flexibility in response to barriers and changing circumstances. Our findings add nuance to these observations by illustrating how participants assessed the personal suitability of SE-BCTs, adapted their use according to changing needs and circumstances, or discontinued techniques they perceived as unnecessary or counterproductive. Taken together, these findings suggest that sustained behavior change may depend less on rigid adherence to particular techniques and more on the ability to modify, replace, or discontinue them as needs and circumstances evolve (Kwasnicka et al., 2016).

At a broader level, the present findings support several premises underlying the sequencing framework proposed by Mazéas and Ntoumanis (2026). First, participants' emphasis on feasibility, acceptability, autonomy, and personal fit is consistent with the exclusion by this framework of potentially unsuitable techniques, such as Biofeedback [#21], Self-penalty [#93], and Imaginary punishment [#96], which behavioral and motivational science experts have identified as potentially suboptimal for supporting behavior change (Knittle et al., 2023). These findings also highlight the importance of allowing individuals to select SE-BCTs that align with their personal preferences, needs, and sense of identity. This is consistent with Renko and colleagues (2022), who found that some individuals perceived planning as incompatible with their self-identity or preferred ways of approaching physical activity. Second, participants' perception that techniques need to work together supports both their organization into conceptually coherent groups and the specification of prerequisite relations. Finally, the finding that the perceived usefulness of SE-BCTs varied across initiation, action, and maintenance is consistent with sequencing techniques according to the changing demands of behavior change. Although participants' accounts cannot directly validate the Mazéas and Ntoumanis' framework, they nevertheless provide qualitative evidence consistent with its emphasis on interdependence, personalization, and phase-specific application of SE-BCTs.

Implications for Practice

Given the number and diversity of SE-BCTs that participants already reported using spontaneously, educational interventions targeting the use of these techniques may be more valuable when they improve the understanding, selection, and enactment of techniques rather than simply introducing a larger repertoire. Instructions should explain the purpose of each technique, describe its essential components, provide concrete examples, and address common implementation difficulties. It could also incorporate recent conceptual advances in behavior change research such as open goal setting (Swann et al., 2025) or flexible goal striving (Riddell et al., 2026), where appropriate. In these instructions, breaking techniques down into explicit steps may help prevent incomplete enactment. For example, problem solving involves not only identifying a barrier but also generating, selecting, implementing, and evaluating a possible solution.

To reduce complexity and make connections between techniques more visible, SE-BCTs could be taught through interconnected learning modules rather than as a long list of isolated techniques. The group-level organization proposed in the sequencing framework (Mazéas & Ntoumanis, 2026) offers one potential structure for such modules, although its effectiveness as an educational approach remains to be evaluated. Modules could be organized around practical questions, such as "How can I set, review, and integrate goals into my routine?", "How can I reinforce or reward my behavior?", or "How can I restructure my environment and use repetition to develop habits?". One promising approach would be to deliver these modules through a digital training platform incorporating educational resources and interactive learning activities, such as podcasts, quizzes, practical exercises, and periodic refreshers (Ntoumanis & Mazéas, 2026). Such an approach could help individuals better understand the purpose and application of SE-BCTs, practice using them in different situations, and develop coherent sets of complementary strategies adapted to their needs and circumstances. Importantly, SE-BCT education could also provide guidance on when and how frequently different techniques should be enacted to support behavior change. As highlighted by Hankonen (2021), some techniques may require only one-off application, whereas others may need to be revisited periodically or enacted repeatedly over time. The distinction between prospective and reactive functions of SE-BCTs proposed in the sequencing framework (Mazéas & Ntoumanis, 2026) could, therefore, also be interesting to guide such structure and offer participants some guidance on the enactment of SE-BCTs.

Finally, the prerequisite relations specified in the sequencing framework (Mazéas & Ntoumanis, 2026) could also help intervention developers determine how techniques should be introduced to participants and explain why certain combinations may be more effective than individual techniques used in isolation. For example, users could be encouraged to evaluate the credibility of sources when seeking information and to combine environmental restructuring with motivational, planning, or self-regulatory strategies. Similarly, the proposed sequencing of techniques could provide a flexible guide for matching strategies to users' needs, circumstances, perceived feasibility, and current phase of behavior change. Artificial intelligence could further support this personalization by dynamically adapting the selection and sequencing of learning modules based on individual preferences, self-reported experiences, and sensor-derived physical activity data (Gensitz et al., 2026; Ntoumanis & Mazéas, 2026).

Strengths and Limitations

This study extends existing research on SE-BCTs by providing an in-depth examination of how middle-aged adults with varying levels of physical activity understand, use, and perceive the usefulness of SE-BCTs across different phases of behavior change. Beyond identifying which SE-BCTs participants reported using, this study examined the congruence of their reported enactment with the compendium definitions and explored how individuals selected, combined, and adapted these techniques in everyday life. Moreover, combining deductive framework analysis with inductive thematic analysis enabled both a systematic assessment of reported SE-BCT enactment against the compendium and a more nuanced exploration of participants' subjective experiences and preferences.

Several limitations should nevertheless be considered. First, the frequency counts derived from the qualitative coding are merely descriptive and should therefore not be interpreted as estimates of the prevalence or effectiveness of particular SE-BCTs. Similarly, congruence with the compendium indicates that a technique was described in a manner consistent with its definition; it does not demonstrate that the technique was effective in changing physical activity. Conversely, partial or divergent enactment does not necessarily imply that the strategy was ineffective for the participant. Determining the effects of individual techniques or combinations of techniques would require prospective experimental evidence.

Second, as with other focus-group research, participants' responses may also have been shaped by group interactions, including agreement with other participants, social desirability, or the greater influence of more vocal group members (Smithson, 2000). Finally, the limited representation of participants reporting higher levels of physical activity, together with the small sample size, precludes meaningful comparisons of SE-BCT use according to participants' physical activity levels.

Third, participants' accounts were retrospective and may therefore have been affected by recall errors or memory biases (Manzoni et al., 2010), as well as by post-hoc interpretations of the causes and consequences of their behavior (Kwasnicka et al., 2017). The identification and evaluation of SE-BCTs also depended on how clearly participants described their experiences. Although coding was conducted by multiple researchers, the interpretation of participants' language and its correspondence with the compendium definitions inevitably involved researcher judgement. Some techniques may therefore have been overlooked, misclassified, or assigned a level of congruence that did not fully reflect their actual enactment.

Suggestion for Future Research

Future studies could address these limitations through prospective mixed-method designs combining ecological momentary assessment (Dunton, 2017), device-measured physical activity, longitudinal qualitative interviews (Tuthill et al., 2020), and N-of-1 experiments (Shaffer et al., 2018). Ecological momentary assessment could capture technique use, barriers, and contextual factors close to the time at which they occur, thereby reducing reliance on retrospective accounts. Device-based measures could determine whether the reported use of SE-BCTs corresponds with changes in physical activity, while longitudinal interviews could clarify how the selection, combination, and perceived usefulness of techniques evolve over time. N-of-1 experiments would be particularly valuable for estimating withinperson effects and determining which techniques or combinations of techniques are useful for a particular person under particular circumstances. A recent personalized trial involving middleaged and older adults illustrates the feasibility of using N-of-1 designs to examine individual responses to goal setting, self-monitoring, feedback, and action planning (Friel et al., 2025). Conclusions

Our findings highlight the need for SE-BCT training and suggest that such education should focus less on expanding the number of techniques available to users and more on supporting their comprehension, complete enactment, appropriate combination, and flexible adaptation. Prospective and experimental research is now needed to determine whether educational and self-management programs can improve technique understanding, combination, enactment and, ultimately, individual behavior change.
