Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Academic meetings are central to sharing and discussing research, and key events for networking and community building. Their design shapes interaction patterns and knowledge flow. Drawing on insights from cultural evolution, we outline how meetings can be structured to reduce disciplinary echo chambers and to foster inclusive, interdisciplinary exchange and cumulative knowledge building. Specifically, we aim to help scholars align intended outcomes with design choices through theory-informed guidance. Cultural evolution offers tools for understanding how meeting formats shape information transmission, learning, and the persistence of ideas, and how design choices at all stages of the cultural evolutionary process act as selection pressures. For example, meetings aimed at generating new ideas benefit from broad exposure to diverse perspectives and flexible interaction patterns, whereas meetings focused on consolidating knowledge rely on repeated interaction for refinement and alignment. While few scholars organise large-scale conferences, many take on the role of organising symposia, workshops, or lab retreats, for which the framework we outline offers practical, goal-directed design principles.

Introduction

Whether small lab group meetings, skill-training workshops, or multi-day conferences with several thousand attendees, academic meetings offer opportunities for exchange around a shared agenda. Scientific progress and responses to global societal challenges rely on inclusive, interdisciplinary collaboration across diverse research communities and beyond academia (Norström et al. 2020 , Specht & Crowston 2022). Academic meetings can support this collaboration and knowledge exchange through networking, with benefits that extend years beyond the event itself (Edwards et al. 2017). Because meetings are costly in time and travel, researchers expect them to be meaningful and efficient relative to the resources invested. However, large conferences are discipline-specific and compartmentalise sub-fields and topics in parallel sessions, limiting exchange across research programmes. Smaller meetings and workshops are often densely packed, leaving little room for informal socialising or reflective thinking. Such constraints can compromise creativity and innovation, which require both necessity and opportunities for exploration (Greenbaum et al. 2019).

This combination of scientific demands and the constraints faced by time-pressed, resource-limited researchers presents a timely opportunity to rethink how academic meetings are organised. This need is compounded by the fact that many such meetings are organised by academics themselves, who rarely have any formal training in event management. Existing guides provide valuable advice on the logistics and management of events, including academic meetings. For example, Corpas et al. (2008) outline ten rules focused on planning covering budgeting, time management, coordinating committee members, and collecting post-conference feedback. A Best Practices guide produced by a collaborative cross-disciplinary community of evolution researchers (NESCent 2012) emphasises leadership, group dynamics, clear agendas, effective breakout groups, and thoughtful participant recruitment. These guidelines remain important for ensuring well-run events.

Here, we turn our attention to how the design of academic meetings affects social dynamics and learning outcomes. Drawing on research in cultural evolution, social learning, and network science, we outline theory-informed design principles that foster innovation, effective learning, and the spread of rigorous scientific practices.

Cultural evolution is a theory of culture change focused on how behaviours, knowledge, norms, and practices are generated, transmitted, and accumulated across populations (Boyd & Richerson 1985; Mesoudi 2016). New information or behavioural variants can arise through various mechanisms, such as trial-and-error discovery or copying error (Perry et al. 2021).

We use innovation to refer to the process of introducing novel cultural variants, regardless of whether they arise through asocial or social processes, deliberate or accidental, by de novo creation, recombination, or improvement of existing forms, and irrespective of whether they subsequently spread or persist (cf. Fogarty et. al 2015). Cultural variants can then spread through social learning , defined as the acquisition of information or skills by observing or interacting with others or their products (Heyes 1994). Transmission is not random; because cognitive resources such as attention and memory are limited, people rely on selective social learning strategies to decide what, when, and from whom to learn (Kendal et al. 2018; Laland 2004). Cultural variants that are instrumentally or socially adaptive are most likely to be selectively transmitted, eventually stabilising to become part of a community's cultural repertoire.

Cultural evolutionary theory is consistent with, and complementary to, observations from events management, a broad field covering topics such as festivals and tourism, as well as academic meetings (Rogers & Wynn-Moylan 2023; Mair & Whitford 2013). Research shows how event design and social interactions can impact both individual experiences (Neuhofer et al. 2024; Crowther et al. 2018) and collective innovation (Maskell et al. 2006; Zhong & Luo 2018). Events are increasingly designed for transformative personal experiences and learning which extends beyond the immediate event, for example, by considering the choice of facilitators and venue (e.g., contact with nature; Neuhofer et al. 2024). Organisers may use play to help break down power structures and encourage participants to share their perspectives and co-create knowledge (Crowther et al. 2018). At the collective level, business events such as trade fairs bring firms and competitors together to see and compare new products and interact with customers and suppliers. These temporary clusters facilitate network building and knowledge exchange (Maskell et al. 2006), and can promote innovation and imitation (Zhong & Luo 2018). From a cultural evolutionary perspective, these processes can be understood as the generation, transmission, and selection of cultural variants.

Building on this work, here we treat academic meetings as goal-directed cultural systems, where knowledge and norms are diffused along social pathways. We argue that thoughtful design choices can actively shape cultural evolutionary dynamics. To do so, we synthesise theoretical and empirical insights from cultural evolution to guide the planning of scientific meetings that support effective information transmission, and create learning environments where new ideas can emerge and persist. We structure our argument tracing a simple cultural evolutionary pipeline from variation generation to transmission and stabilisation.

Accordingly, we call attention to (1) interaction patterns that promote innovativeness, (2) the social learning mechanisms that govern information transmission, and (3) how organisers can intentionally stabilise collective outcomes. Throughout, we translate theory into concrete design recommendations for meeting organisers. While these are often already intuitively implemented to varying degrees, and are discussed in the event management literature, here we link them explicitly to a cultural evolutionary framework in order to clarify the mechanisms by which design choices contribute to knowledge building. We end by arguing that our framework is not only useful for organisers of academic meetings, but also for cultural evolutionary researchers who may consider meetings both as model systems and as sites for field experimentation.

Academic meetings have always been goal-directed cultural systems

Large-scale international scientific conferences emerged in the nineteenth century alongside improved travel infrastructure. Historical accounts highlight their role as venues for both formal and informal communication, and the importance of face-to-face interactions in shaping how knowledge is created, shared, and refined (Bigg et al. 2023). By bringing researchers together, meetings facilitate rapid, high-fidelity transmission of information through social interactions (Lewis & Laland 2012). Beyond knowledge exchange, academic meetings served multiple purposes from the outset, including community building and establishing shared standards. For instance, in the late eighteenth century, the French government convened an international body of scientists to review the reliability of measurements used to support the metric system (Alder 2012). Today, conferences and workshops continue to establish consensus and field-unifying conceptual or methodological frameworks (Osterhammel 2009).

Academic meetings also curate exposure and endorsement. Whereas early disciplinary conferences often aimed to represent entire fields, smaller and selective meetings emerged to foster focused discussions and convergence. For example, the 836 attendees of the 1900 physics congress in Paris reportedly brought together over half of all professionals in the field at that time (Nye 1984). The first invitation-only Solvay Conference on Physics , held in 1911 in Brussels, brought together 23 scientists to debate foundational disagreements over early quantum theory (Marage & Wallenborn 1995). This approach extended to interdisciplinary meetings, such as the Macy Conferences (Fremont-Smith 1961) or policy-oriented meetings addressing urgent societal challenges (Bigg et al. 2023). Choices about invited speakers affect which ideas and practices gain visibility at academic meetings today.

Together, these functions position academic meetings as intentional settings where field-wide knowledge and norms are stabilised, while new ideas and frameworks are generated. In the following sections, we unpack the goal-process-outcomes links and consider trade-offs in design decisions and mitigation strategies to help organisers choose interventions suited to their desired outcomes.

Structuring networks for innovation

Cultural evolution depends on the introduction of novel variants. Scientific meetings can facilitate this process by creating opportunities for diverse ideas to emerge and converge.

This section explores how diversity and inclusion of participants, and shaping their interactions can maximise innovation.

Diversity of participants and perspectives

Across domains, diversity has been shown to benefit innovativeness and adaptability. For example, heterogeneous groups outperform homogenous ones in problem-solving (Hong & Page 2004); diverse cities are more economically productive (Bettencourt et al. 2014); and policies that restrict diversity, such as immigration quotas, reduce rates of innovation (Moser & San 2020; Posch et al. 2025). In scientific contexts, diversity improves innovation and collective problem-solving (Wu et al. 2023). Two forms are particularly relevant: diversity in disciplinary background, which introduces variety in theoretical and methodological approaches, and diversity in lived experiences, which can highlight blind spots and challenge assumptions. Meeting organisers can broaden participation by reducing barriers of attendance for underrepresented researchers. Measures include providing accessibility and funding support, extending reach through remote participation and online engagement (Raby et al. 2022), and signalling a welcoming environment for scholars of diverse backgrounds (Walters & Higgins-Desbiolles 2025).

Importantly, diversity among participants does not automatically translate into innovation.

Productive engagement in diverse groups requires sufficient social connectivity to ensure repeated interaction and recombination across differences (Schimmelpfenning et al. 2021; Baumann et al. 2024). Likewise, increasing diversity introduces coordination problems; the underlying norms, assumptions, and terminology used in one discipline may not be shared by others, leading to misunderstandings. Finally, diversity among participants does not guarantee that all perspectives are voiced or carry equal influence (Chen et al. 2025). For instance, gender dynamics can result in women's opinions being underrepresented while men ask a disproportionate number of questions (Carter et al. 2018).

Practical interventions can support equitable active engagement. For example, organisers can diversify conveners (Casadevall & Handelsman 2014) and actively invite contributions during discussions (Carter et al. 2018). To help participants navigate disciplinary differences, organisers can explicitly communicate norms and expectations through a code of conduct, and establish shared language by circulating foundational readings or providing working definitions for key terms.

Transient diversity and network connectivity

Diversity can be structured dynamically over time, for example by varying how long different ideas are allowed to coexist. Zollman (2010) introduced the concept of transient diversity to describe a dynamic in which high diversity in exploratory phases is followed by consolidation, when ideas are developed into concrete implementations. Smaldino and colleagues (2024) identified transient diversity as a general principle for collective problem solving. They argue that it can be fostered by increasing initial diversity of perspectives and problem-solving strategies, while strategically limiting early information flow to allow subgroups to explore a range of different options before comparing them.

Network connectivity also plays an important role for collective problem-solving, by affecting how ideas spread between individuals and subgroups. Fully connected groups tend to easily and rapidly converge on solutions, but risk premature consensus on suboptimal ones.

In contrast, partially connected or modular networks allow parallel exploration and are more likely to produce diverse and high-quality solutions to complex problems (Lazer & Friedman 2007; Mason et al. 2008; Derex & Boyd 2016). Occasional mixing between subgroups allows ideas to be combined and refined, potentially leading to globally optimal solutions. For example, Derex and Boyd (2016) showed that partially connected groups were faster than fully connected groups in discovering the remedy to a virus in an experimental task.

Similarly, Kline and Boyd (2010) found that Oceanic societies with more inter-island contact had more tool types than expected for their population size, suggesting that inter-group contact supports the emergence of more solutions.

Weak ties connect otherwise distant clusters of well-connected individuals; this helps information spread efficiently, and introduces non-redundant information (Granovetter 1973), with consequences for economic outcomes (Pfeffer & Parra 2009) and norm change (Miu & Colleran 2025). However, some information requires reinforcement from multiple independent sources rather than a single exposure, a process called complex contagion (Firth 2020). In these cases, diffusion depends on whether groups are linked by overlapping connections that can provide social affirmation, i.e. 'wide' bridges across a network (Centola & Macy 2007). Modularity addresses this trade-off between reach and reinforcement by enabling parallel connections in largely separated groups, which improves transient diversity and collective problem solving (Schilling & Steensma 2001; Gomez & Lazer 2019; Smaldino et al. 2024).

Academic meetings can complement transient diversity by providing occasional mixing between otherwise separate subcommunities. Once groups have explored different approaches in parallel, meetings allow new approaches to be compared, combined, and refined. Meetings also create weak ties between scholars who would otherwise be unlikely to meet. For example, Chai and Freeman (2019) found that participating in small scientific conferences can lead to collaborations and citations among scientists who were not previously associated. Formats that foster repeated contact are especially valuable, such as thematic gatherings, early-career mixers, extended coffee breaks, and buddy systems that pair volunteers with newcomers. To keep these opportunities accessible, organisers should avoid limiting social events to evenings or centering them on alcohol, since both can exclude attendees with caregiving duties or those who do not drink. Daytime alternatives such as excursions or social lunches can broaden participation.

It is important to note, however, that groups characterised by lower connectivity take longer to converge onto shared knowledge (Segovia-Martin et al. 2020) and can generate clustered networks of specialists if groups are not sufficiently interconnected (Smolla & Akçay 2019).

Further, scheduling too much unstructured social time can lead to participant disengagement, tangential discussions, and can reinforce established networks when participants mix with known collaborators rather than new colleagues. Mitigating these risks therefore requires a combination of tight clusters with repeated connections and periodic recombination across groups, followed by a final consolidation phase. This can be achieved by combining independently working breakout groups with deliberate recombination of participants or synthesis sessions. Themed working breaks, such as encouraging participants to discuss a central topic or shared problem over coffee, can maintain focus while promoting exploratory exchange. Allocating time at the start of subsequent structured sessions for sharing ideas discussed over informal breaks can further promote cross-disciplinary conversations.

Transmission dynamics

Creating opportunities for interaction among diverse participants is the first step in exposure to new ideas, and subsequent processes of transmission, refinement, and accumulation. In this section we focus on transmission as a structured, non-random process.

Cultural knowledge can be transmitted vertically (from parents), oblique (non-parental adults), and horizontally (from peers) (Cavalli-Sforza & Feldman 1981; Hewlett et al. 2011).

In academic training contexts, vertical transmission can be conceived of as occurring from advisors or principal investigators, oblique transmission as occurring from senior mentors or collaborators, and horizontal transmission as occurring within research groups, cohorts, or peer communities. Vertical and oblique transmission are particularly effective for establishing and maintaining complex skills and norms, leading to uniform and persistent knowledge, while horizontal transmission facilitates exposure to new ideas and the rapid spread of these novel variants (Miu & Colleran 2025).

Transmission outcomes further depend on interaction frequency, task complexity, and learning strategies. While simple information can spread through single encounters, complex skills or normative knowledge typically require repeated interactions (Centola & Macy 2007), often supported by imitation or teaching (Castro & Toro 2014). Social learning strategies that are particularly relevant in academic contexts are prestige bias, whereby individuals preferentially attend to and copy those who are deferred to by others (Henrich & Gil-White 2001; Henrich & Broesch 2011; Jiménez & Mesoudi 2019), and conformist bias, which leads to individuals disproportionately copying the majority of the population as their behaviours are perceived as common or normative (Boyd & Richerson 1985; Morgan & van Leeuwen 2024). These biases shape attention, learning, and retention, and influence which ideas and practices become dominant. In sum, learning outcomes depend jointly on interaction patterns (who interacts with whom, and how often) and on the rules governing transmission (e.g., vertical or horizontal, simple or complex contagion, one-to-many or one-to-one, prestige or conformity bias).

Academic meeting formats should align transmission pathways with intended outcomes. For example, one-to-many formats (e.g., talks, plenary sessions) are suited for broad knowledge dissemination and can increase the visibility of research, as reflected in paper citations and downloads (Leite Lopez de Leon & McQuillin 2020). Sharing asynchronous materials such as recordings can further extend reach. Keynote speakers, plenary panels, and session chairs are positions of prestige. By inviting scholars who embody the scientific practices organisers aim to promote (e.g., Open Science practices, interdisciplinarity, or new methods), one-to-many formats paired with prestige biased social learning can ensure the proliferation of these practices. In contrast, when the goal is to develop specific skills, workshops should combine high-fidelity transmission and supervised individual practice, a strategy that has been suggested to be critical for reliable skill acquisition and cumulative culture (Aoki et al. 2012).

When working in isolation, each of these goals and design choices come at a cost.

One-to-many formats are unlikely to ensure the depth and durability of knowledge transfers.

Workshops, on the other hand, require small group formats, and thus, have limited reach.

Mixed designs can overcome these costs by pairing small-group sessions that support collaborative sense-making with plenary talks or cross-disciplinary panels that expand exposure across disciplinary boundaries. Participants may bring different expectations to the same event and engage differently with these formats. For example, an early-career researcher may prioritise broad overviews and learning about the norms of their field, while others may seek exposure to new ideas. Accounting for such differences by clearly signalling meeting or session aims and offering formats that target different outcomes in parallel helps reduce mismatches between outcomes and attendee expectations.

Stabilising practices

Beyond exchanging research ideas, academic meetings are key sites where normative standards of interaction, visibility, and evaluation are enacted and reinforced. This section focuses on dynamics that shape which practices persist over time.

Cultural accumulation depends on norms and institutions which shape shared expectations and behaviour (Currie et al. 2021; Chudek & Henrich 2011). Many aspects of meetings (e.g., when to ask questions, turn-taking in discussions, how decisions are reached) are best understood as coordination problems, where the challenge is aligning behaviour rather than overcoming competing individual interests. Disagreement, norm violations, or interpersonal conflict due to heterogeneity in preferences or individuals' influence can hinder coordination (Gavrilets et al. 2016). This distinction matters because coordination and cooperation fail for different reasons and require different solutions. Cooperation requires individuals to pay a personal cost for collective benefit, and thus depends on incentives and enforcement; In academia, many core practices of knowledge-building rely on interaction norms and are not governed by formal rules; for example, how disagreement is expressed, whose contributions are taken seriously, or how credit is allocated. When aiming to consolidate knowledge and produce a shared output, norms should be actively shaped with incentives or enforcement mechanisms. Because consensus is not always achievable or efficient, meetings benefit from structured procedures for decision-making (e.g., majority voting) and conflict management. Recent scholarship suggests that codes of conduct are not the norm across academic societies (Foxx et al. 2019; Lu & Webb Williams 2024). Yet, providing a code of conduct that specifies roles and responsibilities alongside mechanisms for intervention and enforcement, helps maintain coordination and effective transmission. Organisers and session chairs can also promote norms by signaling which standards, values, or practices are endorsed by the community, by managing discussions and evaluating interactions to ensure inclusive and respectful participation (Carter et al. 20219), or by deciding whose contributions receive recognition (e.g., awards). Norms can be refined over time through feedback, for example through shared notes, anonymous suggestion boxes, or follow-up discussions. Such iterative design mirrors processes of cumulative cultural evolution and contributes to community building by allowing norms to persist, adapt, and accumulate.

When consistently upheld and enforced by organisers, chairs, and participants, these norms become stabilising institutions.

Importantly, setting strong norms can produce clusters of specialists with limited exposure to new ideas and reduced uptake of new skills (Smolla & Akçay 2019). In scientific communities, such ' echo chambers ' have been associated with lower robustness and replicability (Danchev et al. 2019). For academic events, organisers should aim to carefully balance consolidation and exploration: interaction structures must support coordination and transmission while preserving sufficient diversity and modularity to enable parallel exploration and innovation. Deliberately including participants from closely related fields may introduce sufficient diversity to maintain exploration without jeopardising consensus building. Similarly, structuring events as adversarial collaborations (Rakow 2022), where researchers with different views on a scientific question collaborate, can also drive progress towards rigorous consensus building.

Meetings as model systems for cultural evolution

While in the present paper we separated cultural evolutionary processes analytically to clarify mechanisms and derive theory-informed, actionable recommendations, we recognise that meeting design choices affect multiple goals, processes, and outcomes simultaneously.

Cultural evolutionary research, however, tends to investigate one mode or strategy of cultural transmission in isolation or, very rarely, compare social learning strategies (Atkisson et al., 2012; Deffner et al., 2020; Miu & Morgan 2020). Rarely do studies investigate when and how social learning strategies might be traded off against each other. Our exercise in translating cultural evolutionary theory into practical guidance emphasises the importance of considering cultural transmission not in isolation, but in realistic contexts. Individuals and groups rationally and flexibly trade-off learning strategies (Kendal et al. 2018; Miu et al. 2020). Understanding the conditions under which they do so will contribute to fundamental cultural evolution work.

Because academic meetings shape cultural transmission by structuring networks and learning opportunities, they lend themselves to testing theoretical predictions about social learning and innovation. For example, academic events could be used to investigate how short-term network rewiring alters information diffusion and long-term collaboration in scientific communities; the role of prestige cues; or how unstructured networking and formal content sharing (e.g., talks) differ in outcomes. Conclusion

Academic meetings are cultural systems in which network structure shapes diversity and transmission, while coordination and selection processes determine how ideas move, who is copied, and which behaviours and practices persist. Throughout the paper, we have argued that organisers can deliberately shape meetings through three cultural evolutionary processes. First, designers can cultivate a diverse pool of ideas, since variation underpins innovation and collective problem-solving. Second, they can influence how knowledge is transmitted, and so which ideas gain traction. Third, they can stabilise ideas and norms over time, supporting the accumulation and persistence of knowledge. Thinking explicitly about these dynamics helps organisers recognise trade-offs between generating new ideas and consolidating existing knowledge, and between breadth and depth of the knowledge produced (Derex 2022).

Therefore, effective meeting design begins with identifying a focal goal beyond mere content delivery, and aligning formats accordingly. To help organisers, in Table 1 we summarised how the cultural evolutionary principles translate into concrete design choices for academic meetings, linking goals, mechanisms, and expected outcomes. We foreground goals because many academic meetings are organised by researchers who have specific outputs in mind, such as a joint paper, an edited volume, or the uptake of a specific methodological innovation. Meetings aimed at maximising innovativeness benefit from broad exposure, transient diversity, and modular networks with weak ties, whereas meetings focused on consolidation require repeated interaction, high-fidelity transmission, and coordination norms. Large conferences usually combine several goals in parallel sessions, and different stakeholders, such as funders, host institutions, and attendees, may hold competing objectives. In such cases, our framework can be applied at the level of individual sessions or formats, and can be used to identify tensions between them (e.g., prestige-driven plenaries may dampen the diversity that subsequent parallel breakouts aim to foster). When organisers identify and communicate intended goals, participants and other stakeholders (e.g., session chairs) can decide whether it fits their aims and attend accordingly.

Although relatively few researchers organise large, recurring conferences, the theory-informed recommendations outlined above can also support the design of symposia, workshops, summer schools, or lab retreats. Across scales, meetings face similar coordination challenges and rely on interaction norms and selective stabilisation to support cumulative knowledge building. Online meetings can reproduce some features of in-person interactions, for example through virtual poster presentations, social mixers, or virtual spaces where participants' avatars can spontaneously interact, much like in casual hallway encounters.
