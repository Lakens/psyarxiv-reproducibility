Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract

Interpretation of cognitive profiles commonly involves the identification of low scores, discrepancies and relative strengths and weaknesses. However, a finding that is unusual when considered individually may be considerably less unusual within a person's broader cognitive profile. To account for this, Crawford et al. (2012) developed a multivariate approach that placed individual WAIS-IV findings within their wider normative context. The present study extends this approach to the five primary indices of the WAIS-5.

Monte Carlo simulation of 1,000,000 normative profiles was used to estimate base rates for four complementary aspects of profile interpretation: the rarity of the overall index-score configuration using the Mahalanobis Distance Index (MDI), multiple low index scores, multiple unusual pairwise discrepancies, and multiple relative strengths and weaknesses. Individually unusual findings occurred substantially more frequently when considered across the complete profile, particularly for pairwise discrepancies, where ten overlapping comparisons are possible. The MDI provided complementary information about the rarity of the complete five-index configuration and was more strongly associated with within-profile variability than with overall score level. Together with the accompanying clinical calculator, these analyses provide an accessible framework for placing individual findings and broader patterns of WAIS-5 performance within their normative context.

Introduction

Distinguishing potentially unusual cognitive test scores from the variation commonly seen in healthy individuals represents a significant challenge in neuropsychological assessment. Research in healthy populations has shown substantial within-person variability in neuropsychological performance, including the occurrence of low scores and marked differences between cognitive abilities (Schretlen et al., 2003; Binder et al., 2009). Consequently, a finding that appears unusual when considered individually may not be unusual when viewed within the person's broader profile, particularly as the number of scores examined increases (Binder et al., 2009; Kiselica et al., 2024). It is therefore important to consider not only how unusual an individual finding is but how unusual it is to observe that finding, or several such findings, within the person's profile as a whole.

One way of addressing this problem is through the use of multivariate base-rate methods. These methods estimate how frequently unusual findings occur when multiple cognitive measures are considered together, allowing an individual finding to be interpreted within the context of the wider assessment (Crawford et al., 2007; Kiselica et al., 2024). Their use has become increasingly established in neuropsychological interpretation, with Kiselica et al. (2024) identifying 67 papers involving multivariate base-rate methods in a recent review. By taking account of the relationships among cognitive measures, these methods can also estimate how unusual a particular pattern of performance is within the normative population (Crawford et al., 2007; Huizenga et al., 2016).

A particularly comprehensive application of multivariate methods to cognitive profile interpretation has been developed by Crawford and colleagues. Crawford et al. (2012) applied these methods to the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-Fourth Edition (WAIS-IV; Wechsler, 2008), asking how unusual different features of an individual's profile were within the normative population. Building on earlier work (Crawford et al., 2007; Crawford & Allan, 1994), they examined the overall configuration of index scores, the occurrence of multiple low scores and unusual pairwise discrepancies, and strengths and weaknesses relative to an individual's own mean level of performance. Together, these methods provided a broad framework for evaluating the normative rarity of different features of WAIS-IV performance.

The release of the WAIS-5 creates an opportunity to revisit these multivariate methods. The WAIS-5 provides five primary index scores and normative procedures for evaluating individual pairwise discrepancies and relative strengths and weaknesses (Wechsler et al., 2025a (Wechsler et al., , 2025b)). However, it does not provide corresponding base rates for the occurrence of multiple low index scores, multiple unusual pairwise discrepancies, or multiple relative strengths and weaknesses within the same profile, nor an estimate of the rarity of the complete five-index configuration. This is particularly relevant because five primary indices generate ten possible pairwise comparisons, compared with six among the four primary WAIS-IV indices examined by Crawford et al. (2012). These comparisons are not independent: each index contributes to four pairwise differences, meaning that several unusual discrepancies can arise from the same feature of a profile. The frequency of such patterns therefore needs to be considered within the relationships among the complete set of index scores (Crawford et al., 2007 (Crawford et al., , 2012)).

The present study therefore sought to replicate and extend the framework developed by Crawford et al. (2012) for the five primary index scores of the WAIS-5. Four complementary aspects of profile interpretation were examined: the rarity of the overall five-index profile, the occurrence of multiple low index scores, multiple unusual pairwise discrepancies, and multiple relative strengths and weaknesses around an individual's Mean Index Score. Monte Carlo simulation was used to estimate the normative frequency of these profile characteristics while accounting for the relationships among the five indices, consistent with established multivariate base-rate methods (Crawford et al., 2007 (Crawford et al., , 2012)). In keeping with Crawford et al. (2012), a clinical calculator was also developed to make these analyses readily accessible for use alongside routine WAIS-5 interpretation.

Method Ethical Approval

The present study involved secondary analysis of published aggregate psychometric data and statistical modelling only. No participant-level or identifiable data were collected or accessed. The study therefore did not require formal institutional ethics approval.

Analytical approach

To examine the normative rarity of WAIS-5 cognitive profiles, four complementary analyses were undertaken following the approach developed by Crawford and colleagues (Crawford et al., 2007 (Crawford et al., , 2012)). The configuration of the five primary index scores was first evaluated using the Mahalanobis Distance Index (MDI; Mahalanobis, 1936), extending Crawford et al.'s four-index WAIS-IV approach to the five-index WAIS-5. Three further analyses examined specific characteristics of the profile: the frequency of multiple low primary index scores, multiple unusual pairwise index discrepancies, and multiple relative strengths and weaknesses compared with the individual's Mean Index Score (MIS).

Normative data

Analyses were based on the published intercorrelations among the five primary WAIS-5 indices: Verbal Comprehension (VCI), Visual Spatial (VSI), Fluid Reasoning (FRI), Working Memory (WMI), and Processing Speed (PSI), obtained from the Australian & New Zealand WAIS-5 Technical and Interpretive Manual (Wechsler et al., 2025a). Following the method of Crawford et al. (2012), the published intercorrelations were converted to a covariance matrix and used to define the multivariate normative model for subsequent analyses.

Monte Carlo simulation

Following Crawford et al. (2007 Crawford et al. ( , 2012)), Monte Carlo simulation was used to estimate normative base rates for characteristics of WAIS-5 cognitive profiles. Monte Carlo methods involve generating large numbers of synthetic observations from specified statistical parameters to model the expected behaviour of a distribution (Robert & Casella, 2004). Crawford and colleagues have previously used this approach to estimate the frequency of multiple low scores and other profile abnormalities across neuropsychological measures (Crawford et al., 2007 (Crawford et al., , 2012)).

Monte Carlo simulations and statistical analyses were conducted in Python using NumPy, pandas, and SciPy packages. Using the published WAIS-5 parameters, 1,000,000 cognitive profiles were generated from a five-dimensional multivariate normal distribution. Each simulated profile comprised five correlated primary index scores and represented a hypothetical observation from the normative population. This simulated population was then used to estimate normative base rates for the profile characteristics described below.

As checks on the computational implementation of the multivariate model, the means, standard deviations, and intercorrelations of the simulated index scores were compared with the specified WAIS-5 parameters (Wechsler et al., 2025a). Analytical SD Dev and SD Diff values were also compared with their Monte Carlo equivalents. Finally, because squared Mahalanobis distances are evaluated against a χ² distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of variables (Crawford et al., 2012), the simulated MDI distribution and tail probabilities were compared with their theoretical values under the χ² distribution with five degrees of freedom.

Mahalanobis Distance Index

The Mahalanobis Distance Index (MDI), proposed for the analysis of cognitive profiles by Huba (1985) and subsequently applied to the Wechsler scales by Burgess (1991) and Crawford and Allan (1994), provides a measure of how unusual a configuration of scores is within a multivariate normative distribution. Mahalanobis distance quantifies how far an observation lies from the centre of a multivariate distribution while accounting for the covariance among variables (Mahalanobis, 1936). This is particularly relevant to cognitive profiles because index scores are correlated, meaning that the rarity of a particular combination of scores cannot be determined by considering each score independently. Crawford et al. (2012) subsequently extended this approach to the four WAIS-IV index scores, providing a measure of the statistical rarity of the overall configuration of index scores.

Following Crawford et al. (2012), the MDI was calculated for each WAIS-5 profile using the five index scores, normative mean vector, and covariance matrix. Under multivariate normality, squared Mahalanobis distances follow a chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of variables. The rarity associated with each MDI value was therefore estimated from the χ² distribution with five degrees of freedom (Crawford & Allan, 1994). As a validation check, the simulated MDI distribution was compared with the theoretical χ²(5) distribution, including selected percentile values and tail probabilities.

To assist interpretation of the MDI, its association with two readily interpretable characteristics of the cognitive profile was also examined across the simulated normative sample. Overall level was represented by the absolute difference between the mean of the five index scores and the normative mean of 100, while within-profile variability was represented by the standard deviation of the five index scores. Spearman correlations were calculated between the MDI and each of these profile characteristics.

Frequency of Multiple Low Scores

Low scores occur with appreciable frequency in healthy individuals and become increasingly likely as more cognitive scores are examined (Binder et al., 2009; Crawford et al., 2007). Interpretation of low scores therefore requires consideration of both their number within a cognitive profile and the frequency with which such patterns occur in the normative population (Binder et al., 2009; Crawford et al., 2007).

Following Crawford et al. (2012), each simulated WAIS-5 profile was classified according to the number of primary index scores falling below the 25th, 15th, 10th, 5th, 2nd and 1st percentiles. The percentage of simulated profiles containing at least one, two, three, four, or five low index scores was then calculated for each criterion.

Pairwise Discrepancies

The WAIS-5 manual provides empirical base rates for individual pairwise index discrepancies, reported separately according to which index is higher. The present analysis addressed the complementary profile-level question examined by Crawford et al. (2012): how frequently multiple unusual pairwise discrepancies occur within a single cognitive profile. This distinction is important because the probability of observing one or more unusual discrepancies increases when multiple comparisons are examined, meaning that the rarity of an individual discrepancy does not indicate the rarity of observing multiple unusual discrepancies within the profile as a whole (Crawford et al., 2012).

Differences were calculated for all 10 possible pairs of the five primary index scores. The expected standard deviation of each pairwise difference (SD Diff ) was derived from the WAIS-5 covariance matrix and used to express each discrepancy relative to its normative distribution. Crawford et al. (2012) used two-tailed probabilities when evaluating pairwise discrepancies. The present analyses instead used directional probabilities to correspond with the WAIS-5 manual, which reports empirical base rates separately, according to which index is higher. Each discrepancy was therefore evaluated according to how often a difference of that size or larger occurs in the same direction. Discrepancies meeting the same rarity criterion were then counted together, regardless of direction. Monte Carlo simulation was used to estimate the percentage of normative profiles containing increasing numbers of unusual pairwise discrepancies at rarity criteria ranging from 25% to 1%. As a validation check, the resulting directional discrepancy thresholds were compared with the corresponding empirical thresholds reported in the WAIS-5 manual.

Relative Strengths and Weaknesses

The WAIS-5 normative supplement (Wechsler et al., 2025b) provides procedures for identifying relative strengths and weaknesses by comparing each primary index score with the individual's own average (MIS). In addition to determining whether a difference is statistically significant, the manual provides empirical base rates indicating how frequently strengths and weaknesses of a given magnitude occur in the normative sample. These base rates are reported separately for strengths and weaknesses.

The present analysis extends this approach from individual strengths and weaknesses to the profile as a whole. Specifically, we estimated how frequently normative profiles contain multiple strengths and weaknesses meeting a given rarity criterion. For each simulated profile, the MIS was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the five primary index scores, and each index was expressed as a deviation from the MIS. Following Crawford et al. (2012), the expected standard deviation of these deviations (SD Dev ) was calculated separately for each index from the WAIS-5 covariance structure and used to standardise the deviations.

As with pairwise differences, Crawford et al. (2012) used two-tailed probabilities when estimating the frequency of multiple unusual deviations. We again modified this procedure to correspond more closely with the WAIS-5 strength/weakness analysis, in which base rates are reported separately for strengths and weaknesses. Each simulated strength or weakness was therefore evaluated using a one-tailed probability. Strengths and weaknesses meeting the same rarity criterion were then counted together within each profile. Monte Carlo simulation was used to estimate the percentage of normative profiles containing at least one, two, three, four, or five strengths or weaknesses at the 25%, 15%, 10%, 5%, 2%, and 1% rarity criteria.

Clinical Calculator

With the exception of the MDI, the multivariate base rates described above can be tabulated in advance and applied clinically using lookup tables. The MDI, in contrast, requires calculation for the individual's specific combination of index scores and involves matrix operations, making routine manual calculation impractical. Consistent with Crawford et al. (2012), who provided accompanying software to implement their WAIS-IV procedures, a calculator was developed to facilitate clinical application of the present analyses. The calculator accepts the five primary index scores and provides results for the MDI, multiple low scores, pairwise discrepancies, and relative strengths and weaknesses. An optional FSIQ can also be entered to provide contextual information about the individual's overall level of performance; FSIQ does not contribute to any of the multivariate calculations. Results

Validation of the Analytical and Monte Carlo Models

The Monte Carlo simulation closely reproduced the published WAIS-5 psychometric parameters. The simulated means and standard deviations closely reproduced their specified normative values (Supplementary Table S1), and the maximum absolute difference between simulated and published intercorrelations was .0013 (Supplementary Table S2). Analytical SD Dev and SD Diff values were also closely reproduced by their Monte Carlo equivalents (Supplementary Table S3). Finally, the simulated MDI distribution closely approximated the theoretical χ² distribution with five degrees of freedom: the simulated mean and variance were 5.001 and 9.982, respectively, compared with theoretical values of 5.000 and 10.000, and simulated tail probabilities differed from their theoretical expectations by no more than 0.032 percentage points (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Collectively, these checks indicated that the analytical procedures and Monte Carlo simulation accurately reproduced the specified WAIS-5 multivariate normative model.

Mahalanobis Distance Index

The MDI evaluates how unusual the configuration of an individual's five primary index scores is, taking account of the correlations among the indices. Larger MDI values indicate configurations that are increasingly unusual within the normative multivariate distribution.

The distribution of MDI values closely approximated the theoretical χ² distribution with five degrees of freedom. The simulated mean was 5.001 compared with the theoretical value of 5.000, and the simulated variance was 9.982 compared with the theoretical value of 10.000. Similarly, the simulated 95th and 99th percentiles were 11.070 and 15.072, respectively, compared with theoretical values of 11.071 and 15.086 (Supplementary Table S4).

Monte Carlo estimates of overall profile rarity also closely matched the corresponding theoretical tail probabilities. For example, 4.9995% of simulated profiles exceeded the theoretical 5% threshold and 0.9949% exceeded the 1% threshold. Differences between simulated and theoretical base rates were no greater than 0.032 percentage points across the criteria examined (Supplementary Table S5). These findings support the use of the theoretical χ²(5) distribution to estimate the normative rarity of WAIS-5 primary index profiles.

To further clarify the characteristics of cognitive profiles associated with MDI, its relationship with overall level and within-profile variability was examined across the simulated normative sample. MDI was positively associated with both the absolute distance of the Mean Index Score from the normative mean of 100 (r s = .373) and the standard deviation of the five index scores (r s = .860). Thus, although both characteristics were associated with MDI, the relationship was substantially stronger for within-profile variability than for overall level.

Discussion

In clinical practice, the meaning of an individual cognitive score depends partly on how it relates to the person's broader cognitive profile. A low index score, a marked discrepancy, or a relative strength or weakness may appear noteworthy when considered alone, but its significance within a person's cognitive profile depends partly on how often such findings arise when the profile is considered as a whole. This distinction matters because clinicians rarely encounter a single score in isolation. A WAIS-5 assessment provides multiple, related opportunities to identify apparently unusual findings, and each additional comparison changes the context in which any one of those findings should be understood. The multivariate approach developed by Crawford et al. (2012), and extended here to the WAIS-5, provides a way of placing those findings back into that broader context.

The MDI addresses the person's profile at its broadest level by asking how unusual the overall configuration of their five index scores is. Importantly, this is not the same as asking how unusual the person's overall level of cognitive performance is. Although MDI was associated with both overall level and within-profile scatter, the association with scatter was substantially stronger (rₛ = .860 vs. rₛ = .373). For example, a person with similarly low scores across all five indices may have a relatively common MDI despite a statistically rare (low or high) FSIQ. Clinically, a common MDI should therefore not be taken to mean that the person's cognitive performance is unremarkable. It means that the configuration of their index scores is not unusual.

When a person obtains a low index score, its percentile rank tells us how unusual that particular score is, but not how unusual it is to encounter a low score within their broader cognitive profile. Low scores occur among healthy individuals, particularly when several measures are considered together (Binder et al., 2009; Kiselica et al., 2024). Even across only five WAIS-5 indices, 17.60% of normative profiles contained at least one score at or below the 5th percentile, while 5.20% contained two or more. While an index score at the 5th percentile is itself unusual, encountering at least one such score within a five-index profile is considerably less so. The multivariate base rate provides this broader context by indicating how often the observed number of low scores would be expected within normative profiles.

Pairwise discrepancies present a particular challenge because five WAIS-5 indices generate ten possible comparisons. At the directional 5% criterion, 46.47% of normative profiles contained at least one unusual discrepancy and 28.92% contained two or more. A clinician may therefore commonly encounter several apparently unusual differences within a person's profile. These findings are not independent: each index contributes to four pairwise comparisons, meaning that one relatively high or low score can produce several unusual discrepancies within the same person's profile. Finding several uncommon discrepancies should not be interpreted as several independent pieces of evidence about that person's cognitive functioning (Crawford et al., 2007 (Crawford et al., , 2012)). The profile-level base rate provides the necessary context by showing how unusual that collection of discrepancies is when the complete set of comparisons is considered.

Finally, relative strengths and weaknesses consider whether an index is unusual for the person given their own overall level of performance. A score may be unremarkable when compared with population norms but stand out when considered alongside the person's other index scores, or vice versa (Crawford et al., 2012). Identifying such a difference, however, does not necessarily mean that the pattern itself is unusual. At the directional 5% criterion, at least one relative strength or weakness occurred in 37.43% of normative profiles, while two or more occurred in 11.24%. For the clinician, the multivariate base rate helps distinguish between something that stands out within the person's profile and a pattern of strengths and weaknesses that is itself unusual in the normative population.

The practical value of these analyses lies in using them together without collapsing them into a single judgement. Each provides a different perspective on the person's cognitive profile: whether the overall configuration of scores is unusual, whether the number of low scores is unusual, whether the observed discrepancies remain unusual when all possible comparisons are considered, and whether strengths and weaknesses within the person's own profile are themselves unusual. Taken together, they provide a way of moving between the individual score, the relationships among scores, and the profile as a whole, while keeping clear what each analysis can and cannot tell us about the person's cognition. The accompanying calculator is intended to make this process more feasible in routine clinical practice, providing the relevant base rates without requiring clinicians to undertake the underlying multivariate calculations.

The large number of simulated profiles that we were able to generate represents a particular strength of the present study. Whereas Crawford et al. (2012) were able to generate 100,000 simulated WAIS-IV profiles, modern computational power allowed us to increase this to 1,000,000 simulated WAIS-5 profiles. Perhaps because of that, our validation analyses showed that the simulated profiles closely reproduced the published WAIS-5 characteristics on which they were based, while the MDI distribution closely matched its theoretical expectations. Importantly, these analyses do not represent new statistical methods but rather extend Crawford et al.'s established multivariate approach to accommodate the fiveindex structure of the WAIS-5.

These strengths should also be considered alongside several limitations. The multivariate base rates were modelled from published WAIS-5 means, standard deviations, and intercorrelations rather than individual-level standardisation data. Previous comparisons with empirical data have generally supported this approach, although small differences between simulated and observed base rates have been reported (Decker et al., 2012). Notably, previous studies suggest that simulated base rates tend to underestimate the occurrence of low scores among people with lower ability and overestimate their occurrence among those with higher ability (Brooks & Iverson, 2010). The present analyses were also conducted using the WAIS-5 Australian and New Zealand standardisation sample. Since base rate estimates depend on the relationships among the five primary indices, differences in these relationships across standardisation samples may produce different multivariate base rates. Replication using other WAIS-5 standardisations would help establish how well the present findings generalise and, where necessary, provide base-rate estimates appropriate to those normative populations.

A particularly important distinction for clinicians to bear in mind is the difference between statistical rarity and clinical significance. The present analyses can tell us how unusual a person's WAIS-5 scores are, or wider cognitive profile is, within the normative population, but they cannot tell us why they are unusual. A statistically rare profile may arise for many reasons and does not, by itself, establish a diagnosis, the presence of cognitive impairment or acquired change. Equally, a statistically common profile does not exclude clinically important cognitive difficulties or meaningful change from the person's previous level of functioning. Multivariate base rates should therefore inform, rather than determine, clinical interpretation (Crawford et al., 2012), alongside the person's history, circumstances, and broader assessment findings.

Conclusion

The present study extends Crawford et al.'s (2012) multivariate approach to the five primary indices of the WAIS-5 ANZ, providing normative base rates for the overall configuration of index scores, multiple low scores, pairwise discrepancies, and relative strengths and weaknesses. These analyses provide clinicians with a broader context for interpreting apparently unusual findings within an individual's cognitive profile. Together with the accompanying calculator, they provide a practical framework for incorporating multivariate base rates into routine WAIS-5 interpretation.
