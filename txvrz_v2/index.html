Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Cross-national research has consistently documented sociodemographic gradients in depression across European countries. Most comprehensive analyses, however, predate the COVID-19 pandemic and its far-reaching consequences for population mental health and social hardship. Using data from the European Social Survey Round 11 (ESS 11, collected in 2023(ESS 11, collected in -2024)), the most recent wave to include a measure of depression (CES-D 8), we examined the social patterning of depressive symptoms across 22 EU countries and the UK (N = 39,645) using multilevel modelling with sample design weighting. We confirm findings from prior ESS rounds of consistent gender, age, and income gradients in depression, confirming their persistence in the post-pandemic period. Women reported higher depressive symptoms than men across all countries, and income emerged as the strongest individual-level predictor, with those in the lowest income tertile scoring on average 2.09 points higher on the CES-D 8 than those in the highest (the scale ranging from 0-24). Critically, these associations are not uniform across national contexts. The gender gap in depressive symptoms was larger in Southern European countries-particularly Portugal and Spain than in Northern and Eastern Europe, while the protective effect of higher income was strongest in Bulgaria, Hungary, Belgium, and Greece. Country context alone accounted for only a small proportion of total variance in depressive symptoms (ICC = 0.033), indicating that differences in depression at the population level are driven primarily by sociodemographic factors, but that their magnitude is meaningfully shaped by where people live.

Introduction

Depression is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide, affecting an estimated 332 million people globally (WHO, 2025), and 32.18 million people living in the EU -7.2% of the population in 2019 (Eurostat, 2021). Mild-to-moderate depressive symptoms affect one in five adults across OECD and EU27 countries, and often go unrecognised and untreated, increasing the risk of progression to more severe conditions while increasing overall societal costs (OECD, 2025). Consequences extend also beyond individual suffering: the economic burden of mental ill-health across the EU has been estimated at over €600 billion annually-equivalent to around 4% of EU GDP-driven primarily by reduced productivity, healthcare costs, and social welfare expenditure (European Parliament, 2023).

Policy responses that support the prevention and treatment of depression require reliable data. Identifying who carries the greatest burden of depression, and how that varies across countries, is a prerequisite for designing effective and equitable interventions. Evidence has accumulated that structural and economic factors-such as poverty, low income, unemployment, housing insecurity-are among the strongest drivers of depression and mental ill-health at the population level (Lund et al., 2018 , Ridley et al., 2020 , Talmatzky et al., 2026). National income support programmes, for instance, have been shown to reduce depressive symptoms among low-income households (Lund et al., 2018), and the expansion of access to therapeutic interventions, such as the NHS' Talking Therapies, has demonstrated improvements in depression outcomes at scale (see NHS, n.d. for an overview of evaluations). School-based mental health programmes targeting younger populations have been shown to be effective in reducing depressive symptoms and improving resilience across multiple European countries (OECD, 2025). Yet the design and targeting of such interventions depends critically on knowing which groups and which countries are most affected -and where the social determinants of depression operate most strongly.

The European Social Survey (ESS), a biennial cross-national probability survey of the resident population across Europe, has been one of the largest sources of comparable population-level data on depression on the continent. Since 2006, several ESS rounds have included an eight-item short version of the Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D 8), enabling consistent measurement of depressive symptom burden across a large and diverse set of countries using a single validated instrument and rigorous probability sampling. The psychometric properties of the CES-D 8 in the ESS context have been established by Van de Velde et al. (2010a), who confirmed its validity, internal consistency, and cross-national measurement invariance across 25 European countries. These analyses further showed that the magnitude of the gender gap in depression varies between countries (Van de Velde et al., 2013). Analyses based on earlier ESS rounds have documented consistent sociodemographic gradients in depression, with women reporting higher depression scores (Van de Velde et al., 2010b). Data from the European Health Interview Survey (EHIS) also showed higher rates of clinical depression for those in lower socioeconomic positions, and certain age groups reporting higher depression scores (Arias-de la Torre et al., 2021). These previous ESS analyses were conducted on data from 2006, well before a series of developments that have substantially impacted the state of population mental health in Europe. Two trends in particular are relevant. First, there is growing evidence of a deterioration in overall mental health among younger people. For many years, wellbeing data followed a U-shaped pattern by age -signaling higher well-being among the young and the old, with lower reported wellbeing in midlife. This pattern could not be replicated in multiple studies since 2021, most recently in a 92-country study on well-being (Ruggeri et al., 2026). While this does not necessarily indicate we should expect a mirrored pattern for mental illness or depression specifically, these trends raise questions about whether the age gradient in depression documented in earlier ESS analyses remains accurate, and whether the population groups most in need of mental health support and policy consideration have shifted.

Second, the COVID-19 pandemic triggered one of the most significant disruptions to population mental health (and health in general) in recent history. Global analyses estimate that the pandemic caused an almost 28% increase in the prevalence of major depression and a 26% increase in anxiety disorders worldwide in 2020 (COVID-19 Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2021). While some recovery has been documented as acute pandemic conditions subsided, the social and economic consequences of the pandemic-including elevated unemployment, rising costs of living, and growing income inequality-have continued to shape population mental health, and their effects have been unevenly distributed across sociodemographic groups. Whether the sociodemographic gradients in depression established in earlier ESS rounds have persisted, widened, or shifted in the post-pandemic period is therefore an important and, as of now, unanswered question. This paper uses ESS Round 11 data from 23 European countries (n = 39,645) to address three related issues to inform the European Commission's (2023) Comprehensive Approach to Mental Health through five specific research questions. First, we assess how depressive symptoms are distributed across sociodemographic groups and countries in post-pandemic Europe. Second, we explore to what extent country-level factors account for variation in depression, and what the average individual-level effects of gender, age, education, and income are across countries. Third, we analyse how the effects of gender and income on depression vary across national contexts, and identify where these associations are strongest and weakest. To address these questions, we use multilevel modelling to account for the cross-national nature of the data. In doing so, we provide an update to the pre-pandemic evidence base on depression in Europe, and contribute to the growing evidence on which population groups and national contexts should be prioritised for mental health investment.

Methods

Data and sample

Data were drawn from Round 11 of the ESS collected between 2023 and 2024. The ESS is a biennial cross-national survey that uses strict probability sampling of the resident population aged 15 and above in each participating country. Full methodological documentation is available from the ESS website (www.europeansocialsurvey.org).

The analytical sample comprised all 22 EU countries that participated in round 11 (+ the UK) and included the health inequalities module containing the CES-D 8, namely: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, UK, Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, and Sweden. The UK is included in addition to EU countries in line with the overall approach of the Resilient and Mentally Healthy Societies (REMESOS) project, which this paper is part of. After excluding respondents with fewer than six valid CES-D 8-item responses-requiring completion of at least 75% of scale items-the total analytical sample comprised 39,645 individuals (n = 169 excluded). The analysis plan was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework prior to analysis [ Annex 1; timestamped PR to be made public upon acceptance]; the analysis was conducted in R; the code is available for review and reproduction.

Outcome measure

Depressive symptoms were measured using the CES-D 8, an eight-item short form of the Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (Radloff, 1977). The items assess the frequency of depressive symptoms in the past week, including depressed mood, lack of energy, restless sleep, loneliness, sadness, difficulty getting going, and-worded positively-happiness, and enjoyment of life. Responses follow a four-point scale from 1 ("all or most of the time") to 4 ("none of the time"), which are rescored 0-3 for analysis (Beller et al., 2021). The two positively worded items were reverse-scored prior to summing, so that higher scores consistently reflect greater symptom frequency. Total scores ranged from 0 to 24, with higher values indicating more frequent depressive symptoms. While some have attempted to use the CES-D 8-item version with a threshold to indicate the onset of clinical depression (Beller et al., 2021), the tool generally lacks an established clinical cut-off point (Van de Velde et al., 2010b) -unlike, for example, various versions of the PHQ, or the full 20-item version of the CES-D itself (Radloff, 1977).

The validity, internal consistency, and cross-national measurement invariance of the CES-D 8 in the ESS have been established by Van de Velde et al. (2010a), who confirmed adequate psychometric properties across 25 European countries and found a one-dimensional factor structure to be the best-fitting model. The use of a sum score as the outcome in multilevel models is consistent with this established factor structure and with prior ESS-based analyses, and is appropriate for an epidemiological research context where a simple, interpretable measure of symptom frequency is the primary interest.

Predictor variables

Four sociodemographic predictors were included: gender, age, education, and income. These were selected on the basis of prior theoretical and empirical work on the determinants of depression (Lorant et al., 2003 , Van de Velde et al., 2010b) and pre-specified in the analysis pre-registration. All predictors are treated as confounders of each other's associations with depression; no mediating variables are included. All four predictors are included as covariates without imposing a causal ordering. Given that plausible pathways exist in multiple directions among gender, age, education, income, and depression (e.g. depression affecting occupational attainment and income; income shaping access to resources that buffer depression), and given the cross-sectional study design, we treat the resulting coefficients as descriptive conditional associations rather than causal estimates. Gender was coded as male (reference category) or female. Age was grouped into seven categories consistent with ESS standards: 15-24 (reference category), 25-34, 35-44, 45-54, 55-64, 65-74, and 75 and older. Education was operationalised as three levels following the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED): low (ISCED 0-2: up to lower secondary education; reference category), medium (ISCED 3-4: upper secondary and post-secondary, but non-tertiary education), and high (ISCED 5-8: Tertiary education, any university education up to doctoral degrees or equivalent). Household income was measured using the ESS ten-decile scale per country (hinctnta) and grouped into three categories: low (deciles 1-3, reference), middle (deciles 4-7), and high (deciles 8-10). Missing value codes were recoded to missing for all variables prior to analysis and handled via listwise deletion in regression models. Missing data on predictor variables resulted in the exclusion of 8,677 individuals (21.9%) from regression models via listwise deletion, yielding a multilevel model sample of n = 30,968. Missingness was driven almost entirely by non-response on the household income measure (n = 8,399 missing), with minimal missing data on education (n = 242) and age (n = 263), and no missing data on gender. Given the potential non-random nature of income non-response-those with missing income may systematically differ from those who reported it-results from the multilevel models should be interpreted with this in mind (see limitations section).

Weighting and sample design

All descriptive analyses used the ESS analytical weight (anweight), which corrects for differential selection probabilities within countries, non-response, non-coverage, and differences in population size across countries, enabling comparable weighted estimates across national contexts (Kaminska, 2020). The full sample design-including clustering (primary sampling units) and stratification-was accounted for in all descriptive analyses. This approach follows the ESS weighting guidelines (Kaminska, 2020) for producing nationally and cross-nationally representative estimates. For the multilevel model, anweight was scaled within each country to a mean of 1. This preserves within-country variation in selection probabilities while preventing countries with larger populations from disproportionately influencing the model estimates -a standard approach in weighted multilevel analysis of cross-national survey data (Rabe-Hesketh & Skrondal, 2006). Applying the country-scaled analytic weights did not alter the sample size, confirming that weighting adjusted respondent composition, not sample count.

Analytical strategy

The analysis proceeded in two stages. First, we computed weighted means and 95% confidence intervals for CES-D 8 scores by country and by each sociodemographic subgroup. These descriptive estimates used the survey-weighted design-based procedures appropriate for complex probability samples.

Second, we estimated three multilevel models of increasing complexity, with country as the second level. We deemed multilevel modelling the most appropriate analytical framework here because individuals are nested within countries -meaning that people in the same country are more likely to be similar to each other than to those in other countries, due to shared social, economic, and cultural conditions. Standard regression ignores this clustering and risks underestimating standard errors, potentially producing misleading statistical inference. Multilevel models explicitly partition the variation in depression into individual-level and country-level components, and allow both average effects and cross-national variation in those effects to be estimated simultaneously.

M0 -Null Model: How much of the variation in depressive symptoms can be explained by country alone?

The null model (M0) included no predictors and a random intercept for country, and was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Its purpose was to compute the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which expresses the proportion of total variance in CES-D 8 scores attributable to country-level factors rather than individual differences. A low ICC indicates that most variation in depression lies between individuals within countries rather than between countries, but does not preclude meaningful cross-national variation in sociodemographic gradients.

where γ₀₀ is the grand mean, u₀ⱼ ~ N(0, τ₀₀) is the random intercept for country j, and εᵢⱼ ~ N(0, σ²) is the individual-level residual.

M1 -Fixed effects model: Average estimates of how strongly gender, age group, education level and income are associated with depression across all 23 countries

The fixed effects model (M1) added all four sociodemographic predictors as fixed effects alongside the random intercept, and was estimated using maximum likelihood (ML) to allow model comparison. This model estimates the average association of each predictor with depressive symptoms across all 23 countries simultaneously, while adjusting for all other predictors and accounting for the clustering of individuals within countries, which induces non-independence of observations sharing the same national context. The resulting coefficients represent the expected difference in CES-D 8 score associated with each predictor category, relative to the reference group, averaged across the full sample.

M2 -Random slopes model: Do gender and income effects differ depending on the country a person lives in?

The random slopes model (M2) allows the effects of gender and income to vary randomly across countries, alongside the random intercept. This model tests whether the relationship between gender and depression, as well as between income and depression, differs across national contexts -that is, whether the gender gap and the income gradient are larger in some countries than others. Country-specific slope estimates (best linear unbiased predictors, or BLUPs) were extracted from M2 and visualised to illustrate cross-national variation in each gradient. M1 and M2 were compared using a likelihood ratio test. The large sample size gives the test high statistical power to detect even small improvements in fit. We also report differences in AIC and BIC as complementary fit indicators. As income is a three-level ordinal variable, M2 estimates two random slopes-for middle versus low income and for high versus low income-along with their covariances with the random intercept and gender slope.

Country-specific slope estimates (BLUPs) are extracted from M2 and visualised as forest plots showing country variation in the gender gap and income gradient.

All analyses were conducted in R using the survey package for weighted descriptives and the lme4 and lmerTest packages for multilevel modelling. The reference category throughout all analyses is males, aged 15-24, with low education (ISCED 0-2), and low income (deciles 1-3).

Sociodemographic variation in depressive symptoms

Women reported higher depressive symptoms than men on average across all 23 countries, with a weighted mean of 6.11 compared to 5.1 for men. This gender gap, while modest in absolute terms, was consistent and statistically significant.

Depressive symptoms were relatively low among the youngest group (15-24 year olds, mean 5.12), rose slightly and remained broadly flat through mid-life, and then increased sharply among those aged 75 and over, who reported by far the highest average scores of any age group (mean 6.66). There was no clear mid-life peak in depression with the highest burden concentrated at the oldest age group (see Figure 2). We discuss the age groups used in the limitations section. Education and income showed clear and consistent gradients each. Those with low education reported a mean score of 6.47 compared to 5.51 for medium and 4.96 for high education (Figure 3). The income gradient was even more pronounced -those in the lowest income group reported a mean of 7.06, compared to 5.49 in the middle-income group and 4.64 in the highest-income group. In both cases, each successive step up in education or income was associated with lower depressive symptoms (Figure 4). Tables with exact values shown in the visuals are available in Annex 2.

Multilevel model

The null model (M0) indicated that only 3.3% of the total variation in CES-D 8 scores was attributable to the country a person lives in (ICC = 0.033), with the remaining 96.7% reflecting differences between individuals within countries.

The fixed effects model (M1) confirmed that all four sociodemographic predictors were independently and significantly associated with depressive symptoms when examined simultaneously. Women scored on average 0.75 points higher than men (p < .001). Compared to the youngest age group (15-24), all other age groups showed somewhat higher scores, with the effect increasing markedly for those aged 75 and over (+1.08 points, p < .001). Education was inversely associated with depression, with medium education associated with scores 0.73 points lower and high education 0.95 points lower than low education (both p < .001). Income showed the largest coefficients of any predictor -middle income was associated with scores 1.35 points lower than low income, and high income with scores 2.09 points lower (both p < .001). As shown in Figure 5, the European average gap was +0.77 points, with individual country estimates ranging from +0.20 in Latvia to +1.43 in Portugal. Bulgaria (-2.68), Hungary (-2.06), Belgium (-1.97), and Greece (-1.96) showed the steepest gradients, meaning that the depression burden associated with low income was most severe in these countries. Across all 23 countries, those in the highest income tertile scored on average 2.09 points lower on the CES-D 8 than those in the lowest tertile after adjusting for gender, age, and education, with unadjusted descriptive means of 7.06 for the lowest income group and 4.64 for the highest -a raw difference of 2.42 points. Country comparisons were broadly consistent at the steeper end: Hungary (-4.19) and Bulgaria (-4.06) again showed the steepest gradients, yet the protective effect of high income in Hungary was considerably larger then between low and middle income. In Spain, the association between high versus low income and depressive symptoms was comparatively small (-1.14), as was the case in Sweden (-1.19) and Poland (-1.25). The pattern of cross-national variation was broadly consistent with that observed for the middle vs low income contrast, though some notable differences emerged. In Slovakia, the difference between low and middle income groups was relatively small, whereas the difference between low and high income groups was considerably larger (-2.35), suggesting that income-related differences in depressive symptoms are more pronounced at the extremes of the income distribution. Model Comparison

To assess whether allowing the effects of gender and income to vary across countries improved model fit, we compared M1 against M2 using a likelihood ratio test. M2 fitted the data significantly better than M1, χ²(9) = 258.3, p < .001, though the differences in AIC (M1 = 175,638 vs M2 = 175,398) and BIC (M1 = 175,755 vs M2 = 175,590) were very small relative to the overall model scale, and the fixed effect estimates were virtually unchanged between the two models -as visible in Figure 8, where M1 and M2 point estimates overlap almost entirely, with M2 producing somewhat wider confidence intervals for gender and income reflecting the additional uncertainty introduced by allowing these slopes to vary. The significant likelihood ratio test should therefore be interpreted cautiously, given the large sample size, which gives the test high power to detect even negligible improvements in fit. Nonetheless, the country-specific slope estimates from M2 confirm meaningful variation in the strength of the gender and income gradients across national contexts, which the fixed effects model cannot capture. Limitations

Firstly, and most critically, this study does not make causal claims. While causal inference approaches can in principle be applied to cross-sectional survey data, they require strong theoretical assumptions about the causal structure of the predictors and the availability of suitable instruments. The four predictors included in this analysis-gender, age, education, and incomeare plausibly related to one another through multiple causal pathways. For instance, education shapes income -or vice versa. Depression may, in turn, reduce income-earning capacity. For this investigation, we were interested in identifying how depression varies across these sociodemographic variables to inform policy on the strength of their influence and differences between countries.

Missing data on predictor variables, driven almost entirely by non-response on the household income measure (n = 8,399 missing, 21.2% of the analytical sample), resulted in the exclusion of 8,677 individuals from regression models via listwise deletion, yielding a multilevel model sample of n = 30,968. Given the likely non-random nature of income non-response, we conducted a sensitivity check by re-estimating M1 excluding income as a predictor on both the full analytical sample (n = 39,645) and the listwise-deleted sample (n = 30,968), so that any differences in the remaining coefficients would reflect sample composition rather than the effect of income itself. Gender and education coefficients were stable across both samples, suggesting listwise deletion did not materially bias estimates for these predictors. However, age coefficients were somewhat larger in the full sample, most notably for those aged 75 and over, whose scores were on average 2.20 points higher than the 15-24 reference group in the full sample, compared to 1.63 points in the restricted sample, suggesting that older respondents are disproportionately represented among those with missing income data. Estimates for age in the main models should therefore be interpreted with some caution.

While we followed standards set by the ESS for age grouping, which are frequently employed in investigations using its data, age grouping by default comes with some level of arbitrariness. We identified one particular concern, which is that the youngest age group mixes young people often still in mandatory educational settings (15-17) with people who are predominantly free to choose between continued education, vocational training, or work. However, integrating a separate group of 15-to 17-year-olds into analysis was unfeasible due to the small number of participants in that age group (ranging from a maximum of 69 in Germany to a minimum of 5 in Cyprus). In a descriptive analysis splitting the group at that age across the entire sample, however, we find that the group of 18-24 still scores below average on the CES-D and can therefore conclude they are not the sole driver of lower depression scores in the young age group as applied in our analysis.

A further limitation concerns the absence of a validated clinical cut-off for the CES-D 8 as scored in the ESS context, i.e. a score that indicates the presence or absence of clinical depression. While the scale has well-established psychometric properties and is widely used as a continuous measure of depressive symptom burden, no consensus cut-off exists for the 0-24 scoring used here. The majority of ESS-based studies using the CES-D 8 rely exclusively on continuous scores (Van de Velde et al., 2010a Velde et al., , 2010b)), and the one ESS study that applied a binary threshold used a cut-off of >9 to indicate 'at-risk' depression, explicitly does not make a clinical interpretation (Beller et al., 2021). As a result, we report the distribution of depressive symptoms as a continuous outcome and do not estimate clinical prevalence. This limits how much numerical differences can be translated into tangible interpretations of burden of disease. Future work should consider developing and validating a cut-off appropriate for this instrument and scoring convention in general population samples across Europe.

Finally, the dataset in question contains 22 of 27 EU countries, meaning there is no comparable data for Czechia, Denmark, Luxembourg, Malta, and Romania, for which no data were available at the time the data were downloaded (February 2026).

Discussion

This study provides an updated picture of the sociodemographic patterning of depressive symptoms across Europe, using ESS Round 11 data collected in 2023-24 -nearly two decades after the analyses conducted by Van de Velde and colleagues on ESS Round 3 (2006-07). The findings confirm the persistence of several well-established gradients while also revealing shifts in their magnitude and distribution.

Income as the dominant individual-level predictor

Income emerged as the strongest individual-level predictor of depressive symptoms, with those in the lowest income tertile scoring on average 2.09 points higher on the CES-D 8 than those in the highest tertile after adjusting for gender, age, and education. This aligns with an extensive body of evidence linking economic hardship to common mental disorders (Lund et al., 2018; Lorant et al., 2003) and to depression specifically (Kersjes et al. 2025). The strength of the income gradient relative to education and gender suggests that policies targeting income adequacy and poverty reduction may have the greatest potential for reducing the population burden of depressive symptoms. Critically, the income gradient is not uniform across Europe. The association between low income and depression was most pronounced in Hungary and Bulgaria, and comparatively modest in Ireland, Slovakia, and the UK. Notably, a population-level investigation from Germany (N = 92,267) found a strong increase in the gap between clinically relevant depression cases between high and low-income groups (Kersjes et al. 2025), indicating the need for this to be further monitored across countries.

Most variation is between individuals, not countries -but context still matters

The null model indicated that only 3.3% of the total variance in CES-D 8 scores was attributable to country. This figure is not directly comparable to the 8.8% reported by Van de Velde et al. (2013) in their unadjusted model, as their earlier estimate drew on ESS Round 3 and a sample composed of different countries. The comparatively low ICC in the current study nonetheless reinforces that individual sociodemographic characteristics-particularly income-account for the strongest differences in depressive symptom burden across Europe. This finding has a clear implication for the use of population strategies (Rose, 1985) to reduce depression: interventions targeting individual-level risk factors are likely to have greater population-level impact than country-level structural interventions alone. Low income is a particularly difficult factor to influence for most policymakers, as they a) can rarely simply provide additional funds to people and b) this likely would not solve the complex issues through which low income can increase depression. However, identifying who is most impacted within this group and pathways through which low income increases depression can inform strategies, such as increasing the availability of therapy or creating opportunities for communities to connect if a major driver is loneliness.

While low income and gender affect depression across almost all countries, cultural context should not be ignored in addressing them -either in policy (Ruggeri et al., 2021), mental health promotion more generally (Naeim & Basharpoor, 2026), or treatment (Kirmayer, 2012). The random slopes model demonstrated that the associations between gender, income, and depression vary meaningfully across countries. The same individual-level risk factor-being female, or being in the lowest income group-thus carries different implications for depressive symptoms depending on where a person lives. As such, policy responses that treat all countries equally risk overlooking crucial differences. For example, while Latvia and Lithuania score higher than almost all countries on average, there appears to be no difference in depression levels between men and women, whereas Portugal also scores higher than average generally, but shows the largest difference between the two groups, making gender focused policy likely impactful in the latter country, but not in the other two and definitely warrants country-level investigation.

What has changed -and what has not

The gender gap in depressive symptoms remains. Women scored on average 0.77 points higher than men on the CES-D 8 in the current study, compared to a regression coefficient of 0.81 points reported for 2006-2007. The gap remains statistically robust and near-universal across countries. The broad regional pattern also persists: Portugal, Spain, Cyprus, and Italy show the largest gender gaps. This is consistent with Van de Velde et al.'s (2013) finding that macro-level gender equality-particularly equality in power over economic resources and political participation-is associated with smaller gender differences in depression, and that Southern European countries, which score comparatively lower on these dimensions, tend to show larger gaps.

The age gradient in depressive symptoms shows signs of change relative to earlier ESS data. Beller et al. (2021), analysing ESS rounds from 2006 to 2014, found that depressive symptoms declined across all age groups over that period, but the improvement was weakest among younger adults and strongest among older adults -a difference they partly attributed to improvements in income, education, and health among the latter. In the current data, the 25-34 age group reports elevated symptoms relative to the youngest reference category, and the oldest group (75+) records the highest symptom burden of any age group. The improvement in older adults documented through 2014 appears not to have been sustained into the post-pandemic period, in which older populations faced disproportionate social isolation and healthcare disruption.

Implications for Policy

These findings carry several implications for policy, particularly the European Commission's Comprehensive Approach to Mental Health (European Commission, 2023). The magnitude and cross-national variation of the income gradient point to economic policy as a primary lever for reducing depression. Countries where the association between low income and depression is most pronounced-particularly Hungary and Bulgaria-may require more intensive income support mechanisms alongside investment in mental healthcare and treatment.

The persistent gender gap, and its variation across countries, reinforces Van de Velde et al.'s (2013) argument that gender equality is a structural determinant of mental health. Progress on formal equality indicators may not be sufficient if economic and resource-based inequalities between men and women persist, particularly in Southern European countries, where the gap remains largest.

The elevated depressive symptoms among the 25-34 age group, alongside the well-documented deterioration in youth mental health across Europe, suggest that age-targeted prevention strategies for young adults navigating labour market entry in conditions of economic uncertainty warrant prioritisation. The Commission's approach addresses youth mental health, but the current findings suggest that early adulthood-not only adolescence-is a critical window for intervention, when it comes to depression specifically. In the long-term, such strategies will only remain truly successful within an economy that creates meaningful work opportunities that pay livable wages.

Finally, as Van de Velde et al. (2013) concluded, focusing solely on top-level average European benchmarks will not be sufficient. The differences in both the income gradient and the gender gap between countries indicate that effective mental health policy should simultaneously consider the structural determinants of depression at both the individual and national level, and not disregard national contexts when building EU wide responses.
