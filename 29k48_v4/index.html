Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Introduction 1

Cognitive science has made substantial progress in understanding how minds extend into and are shaped by their material and social environments. The predictive processing framework (PP), 4E cognition, material engagement theory (MET), and cultural learning research have described cognition as not skin-bounded, context-neutral, or culturally invariant. Yet there is still much to be said about the developing mind: about how cognition emerges, transforms, and is shaped by its material and cultural embeddings in the early stages of childhood and adolescence. This paper argues that some resources for a richer account have been available for over a century, in the work of a scientist who has been consistently present in educational discourse and consistently absent from the history of science.

That scientist is Maria Montessori. Conceived at the beginning of the twentieth century, her method is organised around the idea that development and learning are embodied and enacted processes, in which the sensorimotor system and cognition are intertwined, and in which knowledge, concepts, and higher cognitive abilities emerge dynamically from the interaction between children's autonomous exploration and their material embeddings (Di Paolo et al., 2024). Teaching happens indirectly, through the design and organisation of what Montessori called the "prepared environment" (l'ambiente preparato) an immersive ecology of purposely designed educational objects, organised in hierarchical complexity and presented by teachers at developmentally appropriate moments. Not a pedagogical convenience, the prepared environment is an engineered site where the mechanisms linking individual development, designed material culture, and cultural context are made explicit and tractable.

1. The scientist of the developing mind: formation and framework Montessori is remembered as the founder of a global educational movement. She was trained as something considerably more specific: a scientist of the developing mind, formed at the intersection of biological anthropology, experimental psychology, comparative anatomy, psychiatry, and the nascent behavioural sciences of late nineteenth-century Rome. Understanding what she built requires understanding where she came from, not biographically but intellectually. Her formation was not incidental to her method. It was its foundation.

Montessori began her university studies at the Faculty of Natural Sciences at La Sapienza in Rome in 1890, attending classes in zoology and comparative anatomy before transferring to the Faculty of Medicine, where she earned her degree in psychiatry (Foschi, 2012; Kramer, 1988; Matellicani, 2007). She subsequently returned to La Sapienza for a second degree in philosophy, completed between 1904 and 1907 at the faculty that today houses philosophy and education. This trajectory, from natural sciences, to medicine, psychiatry, and eventually philosophy, is not the curriculum of an educator. Rather, of someone attempting to understand the human mind from a multidisciplinary perspective. Her formation unfolded within the specific intellectual environment of late nineteenth-century Rome, shaped above all by two figures with whom Montessori had sustained and formative relationships: the anthropologist Giuseppe Sergi and the experimental psychologist Sante De Sanctis.

1.1 The biological framework

While Montessori started working as a psychiatrist and as a doctorhttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/RiQJ+5bAL+XPDX+OKKk and was engaged with social battles for the welfare of children and women (Babini, 2023; Foschi, 2012; Montessori, 1898a Montessori, , 1898b)) , she came into contact with the anthropologist Giuseppe Sergi (1841 Sergi ( -1936)). Sergi was a physical and biological anthropologist, a strenuous defender of evolutionary theory in an Italian academic environment increasingly drawn toward idealism, and a public intellectual engaged with the social implications of science (Cerro, 2017; Foschi, 2012; Pesci, 2022a) 5 . His influence on Montessori was enormous: she consistently referred to him as "il maestro", and provided the fundamental framework of her analysis.

Central to understanding that influence is a little pamphlet published by Sergi in 1884 (Sergihttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/N2Cs 1884) , titled "Antropologia Biologica" [Biological Anthropology], largely unknown to international scholarship and unmentioned even in most Italian accounts of the Montessori-Sergi relationship (Cerro, 2017; Matellicani, brain is "the organ of the mind" and that anthropology cannot exclude the study of psychical functions reflects a specific philosophical position: a naturalistic monism that refused the separation of the physical and the mental while rejecting both crude materialism and spiritualism. This position placed him within a tradition running from Helmholtz's physiological psychology through to contemporary cognitive science, and it is the philosophical foundation that Montessori inherited along with the methodological programme. Sergi was not, in other words, simply a source of empirical methods and evolutionary concepts. He gave Montessori a philosophy of mind, one in which the brain, behaviour, and environment are irreducibly connected, that underlies everything she subsequently built.

In this text, Sergi drafted the guidelines for what he called a "living science"a science in which both the structures and the functions of human organisms must be taken into account. Most importantly for our purposes, Sergi insisted that the brain is "the organ of the mind" and that it would be "strange" if anthropology excluded the study of its psychical functions (Ibid.: 3-4). The comparison he drew with ethological studies is striking in its contemporary resonance: just as zoology had moved beyond mere classification toward the observation of animal behaviour in relationship to the physical environment, anthropology needed to enter "a new phase of research and observations" in which human beings are studied in all their manifestations within "the composite environment in which the man lives and multiplies" (Ibid.: 5; 8). This is recognisably a proto-cognitive science programme. Sergi was proposing an integrated study of structure and function, brain and behaviour, individual and environment, that anticipates the agenda of contemporary cognitive science with remarkable precision [see, e.g. (Clark, 2004 (Clark, , 2016;; Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Newen et al., 2018; Varela et al., 2018) https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/IKP8+g7s2+9o8J+AbA0+248Z. He went further, identifying three environments within which human existence unfolds -physical, organic, and social -and arguing that anthropological phenomena of both biological and psychical nature are produced within all three simultaneously (Ibid.: 7-8). The organism, he wrote, "modifies and adapts, or tries to adapt to the alive and continuously acting forces of external nature" (Ibid.: 6). This ecosystemic conception of the human organism is precisely the framework that Montessori would elaborate into a theory of cognitive development in the prepared environment and its materials.

Sergi also transmitted to Montessori a specifically evolutionary and biological mindset that shaped her understanding of development in ways that have been consistently underestimated. His "frank Darwinian evolutionism," as Pesci [(https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/8XCy 24)] characterises it, gave Montessori the conceptual tools to think about childhood as a period of biological and cognitive formation rather than simply a stage of preparation for adult life. Within this biological framework, the concept of 'variation' (i.e., any difference among a population, genetic or phenotypic) gave Montessori the conceptual tools for understanding and accounting for inter-individual differences in children's development. Each child, in Montessori's view, is considered a variation, a specific phenotype whose cognitive development unfolds through interaction with an individual environment. This is the reason why, even today, in Montessori schools children with diverse learning styles and neurodivergent profiles are more easily accommodated and welcomed (Foster, 2025; Koifman, 2025 https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/gSbX+HoZN.

Sergi's influence extended to the methodology of observation, which became essential to her programme (Foschi, 2012). He insisted that the study of human beings required not only experimental methods but sustained anthropological observation: the patient, systematic recording of behaviour in naturalistic conditions. This methodological commitment, which Montessori absorbed directly, became one of the most distinctive features of her approach and one of the most misunderstood. American psychologists like Dewey and his followers read her insistence on observation as a rejection of experimental science (Kilpatrick, 1914; Thayer-Bacon, 2012). It was in fact a different and in some respects more sophisticated conception of what experimental science required, one that anticipated the later turn in cognitive science toward naturalistic and ecological methods, including the 4E account of cognition (Chemero, 2013; Fabry, 2025; Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014).

Sergi was also the figure who introduced Montessori to the overlap between anthropological observation and education. His own writings on pedagogy, including Educazione ed istruzione (1892) [Education and Instruction] and L'educazione del carattere (1893) [The Education of the Character], anticipate many of the themes Montessori would develop: a school without punishments and 'military' discipline; the importance of the physical environment for learning; the introduction of experimental sciences into educational settings; and the role of attention, imitation, and social interaction in cognitive development (Pesci, 2002). It is difficult not to read this as a description of exactly what Montessori would become.

1.2 Experimental psychology

The second formative relationship was with Sante De Sanctis, who supervised Montessori's thesis in psychiatry, collaborated with her at the Orthophrenic Hospital, and later taught her psychology at the Faculty of Philosophy (Matellicani, 2007; Pesci, 2002). For the academic year 1903-1904, Montessori attended De Sanctis's lessons three times a week. The intellectual debt is traceable and specific. De Sanctis's primary scientific interests during the period of his closest contact with Montessori covered attention and its disorders, voluntary attention investigated experimentally, the psychophysiology of attention in typical and psychopathological subjects, the scientific method, dreams and emotions, and the anatomical, physiological, and biological foundations of psychopathology (Matellicani, 2007). Attention, in particular, is central to Montessori's entire pedagogical and theoretical framework: the concept of 'concentration', the design of materials to capture and sustain attention, the observation of attentional patterns as the primary data of her experimental method (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/xC0h+8ngT+rpd0. Lillard, 2011; Rathunde, 2001; Sobe, 2004). De Sanctis gave her the theoretical framework for understanding attention as a cognitive function with developmental stages, and the experimental tools for studying it.

His theory of attention is worth examining briefly because of its direct relevance to Montessori's method. De Sanctis distinguished between the concentrative function of attention (i.e., its capacity to fix on a single object) and its distributive function (i.e., its capacity to coordinate multiple objects simultaneously) (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/1HP1) see also: (Cimino & Lombardo, 2004). He observed that in children, distributive attention is not yet developed, while strong concentrative attention (fixation on a task or object) is characteristically present. Development consists in the gradual elaboration of distributive from concentrative attention, through both growth and exercise. This developmental account maps directly onto Montessori's observation that children in the prepared environment characteristically enter states of deep concentration. She called them "polarisation of attention", and considered them the primary mechanism of cognitive development (Montessori, 2014). The prepared environment, with its carefully designed materials and its freedom from interruption, is structured precisely to enable and extend this concentrative state.

De Sanctis also transmitted to Montessori a vision of pedagogy as a science that requires both experimental psychology and biological anthropology, a vision that maps exactly onto the same interdisciplinary programme that Sergi had outlined. In fact, together with Sergi, De Sanctis was the founder of the Rivista quindicinale di Psicologia, Psichiatria, Neuropatologia [Fortnightly Journal of Author's Manuscript Submitted to History of Psychology Psychology, Psychiatry, and Neuropathology], which pursued and diffused a multidisciplinary approach to cognition and the mind. Accordingly, De Sanctis's lectures on pedagogical psychology, which Montessori attended, described education as the attempt to follow the development of human capacities through "observations and deductions obtained through facts" that are always both physiological and psychological, individual and social (De Sanctis, 1913; in Pesci, 2002). His insistence that pedagogy does not deal with the abstract student but with a "real individual" -"not schemata are needed, but the knowledge of living things; not theories, but experiences" (De Sanctis, 1922; in Pesci, 2002: 190-191)anticipates almost exactly Montessori's own methodological commitments.

The relationship with De Sanctis introduced Montessori to the work of W. James, whose minor works De Sanctis had translated and used in his lectures as "an alternative to dry positivism, without yielding to the temptations of spiritualistic and anti-positivistic currents" (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/8XCy 25-26). James's pragmatist and functionalist psychology, and insistence on the continuity of mind and action, on habit as the flywheel of behaviour, on the stream of consciousness as an active and selective rather than passive and receptive process, reinforced the framework Montessori was absorbing from Sergi and De Sanctis and added a specifically functionalist dimension that would later connect her work with what today counts as 4E cognition: the family of accounts holding that cognition is embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended beyond the boundaries of the brain and into the body, the environment, and the artefacts with which organisms interact (Chemero, 2013; Newen et al., 2018; Varela et al., 2018).

1.3 The framework that emerged

Even from this brief analysis of only some elements of Montessori's formation -the bio-anthropological training under Sergi, and the immersion in De Sanctis's experimental psychology of attention -an interest in the mind and its cognitive functions is already visible. This interest would be developed, both theoretically and practically in her method, along three dimensions: the individual, the material, and the socio-cultural.

Following De Sanctis's insistence on the "real individual" as the proper subject of pedagogy, Montessori directed her attention not to the child but to each child: each one different from the others. This difference, given her biological formation, is to be read in an evolutionary sense: each child is a variation shaped by its particular genotype and its particular developmental history. Inter-individual differences in learning are not problems to be corrected but data to be observed and taken into account. The educational environment must accommodate and reveal these differences, not erase them (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/2efl+kXay. Frierson, 2018; Politi, 2023).

The second is the material dimension. Cognitive development does not happen in an abstract space but through concrete engagement with a material world of objects, spaces, tools, and artefacts, exactly what MET (Malafouris, 2013) identifies as the constitutive role of physical things in cognitive processes. The mind, in Montessori's account, is formed in and through its engagement with the material world. The educational materials she designed are not aids to a process that happens independently of them: they are essential to the process (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/R3Q6+GgLc. Frierson & Di Paolo, 2025; A. Lillard, 2008).

The third is the social and cultural dimension. The child develops within a cultural environment that is not merely a context but a constitutive condition of cognitive development -exactly what cultural learning Author's Manuscript Submitted to History of Psychology research identifies as the irreducibly social character of human cognitive formation (Heyes, 2018; Tomasello, 2009). Language, social norms, cultural practices, and artefacts are absorbed by the developing mind not as external additions to a pre-formed cognitive system but as the medium through which the cognitive system is formed (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/YlY8. Montessori's concept of the mente assorbente, i.e. the capacity proper of the young child to take in and internalise the cultural environment, is a theoretical claim about the relationship between mind and culture, not merely a metaphor. These three dimensions -individual, material, cultural -are irreducible and simultaneous. No adequate account of cognitive development can proceed by holding any one of them constant while varying the others. This is the framework that the misreading (see Section 3) has consistently failed to see, reading the individual dimension as a philosophy of child-centred education, the material dimension as a collection of didactic toys, and the cultural dimension as a vague commitment to holistic development. It is also, as we shall argue in the following section, the framework that the prepared environment was designed to make explicit and tractable, which is why it functions as a model system for cognitive science rather than merely as a successful educational method.

2. The prepared environment as cognitive science

The prepared environment [ambiente preparato] is perhaps the central contribution of Montessori's method, at once theoretical and practical. It is the solution to a practical problem that also constitutes a theoretical claim, because Montessori found herself doing two things at once. On the theoretical side, her formation in biological anthropology, comparative anatomy, and experimental psychology had given her an understanding of the environment not as the backdrop of development but as the material medium necessary to it -and so she conceived the classroom as the place where "optimal development" could naturally unfold (Catherine et al., 2020; Foschi, 2012; Marshall, 2017; Montessori, 2025; Rathunde, 2023). On the practical side, she was effectively in the business of opening schools: real institutions with real obligations to real families and to society as a whole, which meant ensuring that children learned to read, write, and calculate (Caracchini, 2019; Fresco, 2018; Giovetti, 2014; Regni, 2007). The prepared environment, then, had to satisfy both demands at once: it had to respect and leverage the actual mechanisms of cognitive development, while reliably producing specific cognitive outcomes. The solution was to recognise that these demands were not in tension. The most effective way to ensure children learn to read, write, and calculate is, in fact, to create an environment that reflects how developing minds actually work. Children in traditional schools, Montessori argued, are "suffocated" in their natural expressions (Montessori, 1909) https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/ci5D15) 6 . To create a proper scientific pedagogy it is fundamental not only to have teachers trained as scientists but a proper environment that allows the free manifestation of the child: "This is the necessary reform", she claims (Montessori, 2016).

Thus, even if commonly conceived as a child-friendly classroom or a carefully organised learning space, the prepared environment is something considerably more than its surface description suggests: it goes to the heart of Montessori's understanding of the relationship between individuals and environments (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/GZBb+HwH5+CFaR+O2kp+lkSI 6 It is worth noting that the Italian original uses the word "soffocati" ["suffocated"], which appears repeatedly across different texts. The available English translation renders this as "restrained", a word that exists in Italian as "ristretti" and that Montessori could have chosen but did not. The difference is significant: "soffocati" carries the sense of suppression, of something that cannot express itself at all, rather than merely something held back. The English version flattens a vivid and theoretically loaded term into a much milder one, losing precisely the force of Montessori's claim about what traditional schooling does to the child's natural cognitive development.

This is because the prepared environment was the instrument of a larger project: the establishment of a pedagogia scientifica [scientific pedagogy], a way of conceiving pedagogy not merely as education but as a science embedded in multidisciplinary experimental practices: anthropology, psychology, and medicine, including what Montessori and the science of her time referred to as 'hygiene' (Pesci, 2002) (which involves health and self-care routines, such as cooking, tidying, dressing (Karlovitz, 2019; Plekhanov, 1992; Yildirim Doğru & Güler, 2025)). Within this project, the classroom became a laboratory: the place where the new science of pedagogy could be developed, and where teachers trained in the experimental sciences could observe the unfolding of optimal development (Montessori, 1909 (Montessori, , 1911 (Montessori, , 2025)) https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/ZAWp+KHba+ci5D.

To appreciate the full theoretical depth of the classroom as a 'prepared environment', it helps to consider it alongside one of the most influential frameworks in contemporary cognitive science: PP (Clark, 2016; Hohwy, 2013; Parr et al., 2022). On this account, the brain is not a passive receiver of sensory information but an active prediction machine: it continuously generates expectations about the causes of incoming sensory signals and updates those expectations when they are not met, driving both perception and action. Learning, in this view, is not the accumulation of facts but the progressive refinement of a model of the world through cycles of prediction and error correction (Friston et al., 2016 (Friston et al., , 2021)). The developing mind is always actively engaged with its environment, not waiting to receive knowledge but reaching out to sample, predict, and revise.

Read through this lens, the prepared environment becomes not a neutral container for learning activities but a deliberately structured cognitive environment, an extension of the predictive mind (Constant et al., 2022). The environment is organised to make the child's active engagement with the world as productive as possible, to ensure that errors are informative rather than discouraging, to support the child's natural drive to predict, explore, understand, and self-regulate, but also to make concrete the teaching of the teachers, who literally not only use the environment, but let the organised environment teach (Ahmadpour & Mujembari, 2015; Denervaud, 2023; Kersna et al., 2025; Kocabaş & Bavlı, 2022). It is in this light that the prepared environment can then absolve both functions: upholding optimal development while teaching basic literacy. It should be taken as an interface situated between the mind and the activity of the children and of the teachers, by design also incorporating a social and community dimension.

2.2 The educational materials: cognition externalised

The educational materials [materiali didattici] are the most original and most theoretically loaded element of the prepared environment (Montessori, 2014; Müller & Schneider, 2002). Curiously, they are also the most consistently underestimated by commentators (Błaszczak et al., 2021; İslamoğlu, 2017; Mendoza et al., 2021; Pesci, 2018). Too often treated as clever didactic toys or as props for learning activities that could in principle happen without them, they are in fact the theoretical core of Montessori's cognitive science. Adopting today's terminology, they are cognitive operations externalised in physical form and extended cognitive tools (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/h9uX+2mEu+R3Q6. Frierson & Di Paolo, 2025; Heersmink, 2013 Heersmink, , 2016)).

The materials were designed according to several interconnected principles, each of which reflects a specific scientific claim (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/CFaR+0CWv. Lillard, 2016; A. S. Lillard & Heise, 2016). First, they are multisensorial: they engage touch, sight, weight, sound, and movement simultaneously, reflecting Montessori's understanding, grounded in her formation in experimental psychology, that perception is not a passive reception of sensory data but an active, embodied engagement with the world (Montessori, 1909). Second, they are self-correcting, designed so that errors are immediately perceptible to the child without Author's Manuscript Submitted to History of Psychology external intervention (Denervaud, Fornari, et al., 2020; Denervaud, Knebel, et al., 2020). The Spindle Box, for instance -a wooden box divided into ten compartments from zero to nine, used with forty-five spindles -makes mathematical error tangible: if the child miscounts, she runs out of spindles before the compartments are filled. The material is an 'error-minimisation device' in predictive processing terms, a structure that makes prediction errors salient and informative rather than discouraging (Di Paolo et al., 2024).

Third, the materials are designed with incremental difficulty, moving from concrete to abstract, from simple to complex, from large to small, and thus reflecting Montessori's understanding that abstract cognitive capacities emerge from concrete material interaction rather than being innately present or spontaneously developing (Montessori, 2023; Pesci, 2018) 8 . The development of what she calls mente matematica [the mathematical mind] is her most explicit statement of this claim. Mathematical objects, she argued, are not scattered within the ordinary environment of the child [gli oggetti matematici non sono sparsi nell'ambiente] (Montessori, 2023: 240-241) and without encountering specifically designed mathematical objects, the child misses the occasions for developing its more sophisticated and abstract forms. A basic mathematical disposition might develop in any case (see Section 4.2), but the materials open the path toward forms of mathematical reasoning that the ordinary environment does not afford. In this sense they do not merely aid mathematical learning: they help constitute its more complex expressions (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/R3Q6. Frierson & Di Paolo, 2025). They are astrazioni materializzate [materialised abstractions] from which abstract thought can progressively be built (Montessori, 2023): 241). This is a 4E claim stated with full theoretical clarity a century before the framework was even formalised.

Fourth, the materials are designed to capture and sustain attention, and that Montessori herself described to an audience of doctors at the Royal Society of Medicine in 1919 as "fixation of attention" (Kramer, 1988: 264). The colours, geometrical forms, textures, and weights of the materials are not chosen for aesthetic reasons (Pironi, 2017; Rose, S. E., Jolley, R. P., & Charman, A., 2011). Or better, they work aesthetically because of their attentional properties, that is, their capacity to engage the child's concentrative attention at the right developmental moment (Ahlquist, 2023; A. The materials are designed to assign high attentional salience to specific perceptual features at specific developmental moments, guiding the child toward increasingly refined engagement with the structure of the world and toward the formation of stable patterns of attention (Ramstead et al., 2016).

2.3 The teacher as experimental observer

The role of the teacher in the prepared environment is perhaps the most counterintuitive feature of Montessori's method and the one most revealing of its cognitive science foundations. The teacher in a Montessori classroom does not teach in the conventional sense. She does not transmit information, correct errors, or direct activity (Bhat, 2021). She prepares the environment, observes the children, and intervenes only to introduce a new material or to redirect a child who is disturbing others (Foschi, 2012 https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/OKKk. Montessori's term for this role -"la direttrice" [the directress] rather than "la maestra" [the teacher]signals the distinction: the teacher directs and organises the environment that the child is free to use (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/ci5D+Zyzd+LnaW. Lillard & McHugh, 2019a; Montessori, 1909; O'Donnell, 2013). This is as pedagogical as much as a methodological choice, grounded in Montessori's conception of the classroom as a laboratory. Refraining from intervention, the teacher is fulfilling their (scientific) function by systematically observing the children's spontaneous behaviour in a controlled and prepared environment (Trabalzini, 2018). As Montessori writes: "How can we conceive the content of Pedagogical Anthropology if not as drawn with experimental methods from the observation of pupils in schools? And where could we conceive a laboratory possible for such a science, if not in the school?" (Montessori, 1911 https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/KHba 22). The teacher is then a "naturalist psychologist": trained in anthropology and experimental psychology, able to observe behaviour without distorting it, and to record what they see without imposing theoretical preconceptions.

The training Montessori required of her teachers reflects this conception directly (Montessori, 2025). Teachers were trained not only in pedagogical techniques, but in observation: in the patient, systematic recording of children's behaviour, interests, attentional patterns, and developmental stages (Montessori, 1911). They were taught to see the child's spontaneous activity as data, from which pedagogical intervention might take form. With this aim in mind, Montessori cared intensely on teachers' training which she wanted to supervise herself constantly (Kramer, 1988; Pesci, 2019). 10 They were required to apply the method without variation not out of rigidity but out of necessity: only consistency and replicable protocols could yield results that were genuinely (experimentally) informative about children's cognitive development, and thus being useful to the aim of scientific pedagogy. One of the reasons why Montessori's relationship with the fascist regime ended so abruptly was the regime's insistence on interfering with teacher training programs and selection -beyond the more obvious incompatibility between fascist ideology and the culture of freedom and self-direction characteristic of Montessori schools. Montessori consistently declared herself apolitical, but she never permitted any government to interfere with the training of her teachers, who were to be educated, not indoctrinated. As Pesci (2019: 149) puts it: "To any government that interfered, wanting to impose how things should go in her schools -whether the radicals of Catalonia or the fascists in Italy -Montessori would have opposed herself, not necessarily for political reasons, but for pedagogical and educational ones; and not necessarily because Maria could not live in the way they intended to guide their nations, but because only she should determine the way of running her schools" [our translation]. The concept of "periodi sensitivi" [sensitive periods] is Montessori's most explicit bridge between her biological formation and her conception of the mind. Sensitive periods are restricted windows of time in the development of the child during which specific environmental stimuli must occur for the development of particular cognitive capacities. Outside these windows, the same stimuli have a much reduced effect. The child who does not encounter written language during its sensitive period for language acquisition will acquire literacy later, with greater difficulty and with a qualitatively different result (Schaefer Zener, 2003).

The term was suggested to Montessori, it seems, by the Dutch botanist and geneticist Hugo de Vries, whom she met in Holland in 1917 and who proposed using his own concept of developmental mutation periods to describe what Montessori had observed in children (Kramer, 1988 https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/IHow 252). But the concept itself had multiple and deeper roots in her formation. Her studies in zoology and comparative anatomy had introduced her to the concept of developmental phases before and after birth. Her work with De Sanctis at the Orthophrenic Hospital had given her direct experience of the consequences of missing critical developmental windows in children, particularly those cognitively atypical. Her reading of Mémoire et Rapport sur Victor de l'Aveyron by Jean Itard had shown her the limits of cognitive development outside the normal cultural environment. And her sustained cross-cultural observation across several decades had given her empirical evidence that these windows were universal rather than culturally specific (Foschi, 2012; Fresco, 2018; Kramer, 1988; Matellicani, 2007).

The parallel with Konrad Lorenz's critical periods -which Lorenz formalised in work that earned him the Nobel Prize in 1973, two decades after Montessori's death -has not been fully developed in the literature. It is worth being precise about the nature of the parallel. Montessori was not anticipating Lorenz, but was working from the same intellectual tradition: ethology and animal behaviour, and comparative biology. This passion and knowledge were transmitted to her mainly by Sergi, who wrote in his Anthropologia Biologica (1884) that the study of animal behaviour is very useful and he wishes that anthropology arrives to the very same phase in which ethological studies are: "human morphology is not enough, it is necessary to study the functions and all the series of various and diverse manifestations in the composite environment where the human lives and multiplies" (Ibid.: 5). Trained with this aim, and in a cultural context in which the idea was spread across nations, she arrived at Lorenz's same theoretical conclusion through independent but convergent routes (Laeng, 2000). That Irenäus Eibl-Eibesfeldt, founder of human ethology and student of Lorenz, cites Montessori in his discussion of innate dispositions to learn and sensitive periods (https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/pmsY 396) suggests that the convergence was not lost on those closest to the ethological tradition, even if it has been invisible to cognitive science.

3. Misreading Montessori

That Montessori has been read only as an educator rather than also as a scientist is not a neutral observation about disciplinary boundaries. It is the outcome of a specific historical process with identifiable causes. We identify primarily three, which operated simultaneously and reinforced each other: (a) the transformation of her communicative style under biographical and political pressure; (b) the losses incurred in translation and international reception; and (c) the structural disadvantages she faced as a female scientist in a male-dominated academic environment. Together they account for a misreading that has persisted for over a century and that has had concrete consequences for the history of cognitive science.

3.1 Style

Montessori's earliest publications are unambiguously scientific in character. Her work in psychiatry, neuroanatomy, and anthropology from the late 1890s was published in technical journals including the Rivista quindicinale di Psicologia, Psichiatria, Neuropatologia [Fortnightly Journal of Psychology, Psychiatry, and Neuropathology] and belongs squarely within the experimental and positivist tradition of late nineteenth-century Italian science (Foschi, 2012; Matellicani, 2007; Trabalzini & Michener-Rutledge, 2023). Her pedagogical anthropology lectureshttpshttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/d95g//paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/KHba L'Antropologia pedagogica (Montessori, 1911) , described as four hundred large-format pages filled with statistical tables on anatomical measurements of children (Pesci, 2022b) : 28), constitute the starting point of her subsequent pedagogical reflection and have been now forgotten 11 and not reprinted since their first edition. The technical and scientific character of this early work is not in dispute among Italian historians of science who have examined it closely (Bertuletti, 2024; Pesci, 2002 Pesci, , 2012)). 3.2 Translation and international reception

The scientific character of Montessori's work was further obscured by the process of translation and international reception. The most obvious instance is the fate of her first and most famous book. 3.3 Gender and institutional exclusion

The third cause is structural: an academic environment that was deeply hostile to female scholars, particularly in scientific and technical fields. She was among the first women to study medicine in Italy, attending at the cost of numerous special arrangements (e.g., dissecting cadavers alone rather than alongside male classmates) and only after having attended the faculty of Natural Sciences (Kramer, 1988: 35-38). Her academic appointments -i.e., two short periods of "libera docenza" in anthropology, in 1904 at the Faculty of Natural Sciences and from 1906 to 1910 at the Pedagogical School of Rome, both obtained only through the intervention of Sergi -were precarious, unaffiliated, and paid by students directly. No stable academic position followed.

The contradictions of Sergi's role are worth noting. The style shift was driven by financial necessity and experimental ambition, not by the abandonment of scientific rigour. The translation losses were the product of dissemination urgency and commercial reframing, not of any absence of theoretical content in the original. The gender and institutional exclusion reflected the structural conditions of early twentieth-century academia, not any deficiency in Montessori's scientific standing. What got lost is the incorporation of a genuinely productive theoretical framework into the history of cognitive science and developmental psychology at the moment when that history was being made. We examine this loss in the following section.

4. The cost of the misreading: the Piaget case

The three causes of misreading identified in Section 3 -the style shift, the translation losses, the gender and institutional exclusion -operated primarily at the level of reception: they determined how Montessori's work was read, classified, and positioned within the emerging scientific landscape (Whitescarver & Cossentino, 2008). But the misreading had a further consequence: the theoretical framework Montessori had developed, an account of cognitive development as individual, material, and cultural simultaneously, was partially absorbed, stripped out its most important features, and then canonised in a form that made Montessori's contribution invisible. Jean Piaget gives us an example of this.

A full comparison of Montessori's and Piaget's theories lies beyond the scope of this paper (but see: (Ültanır, 2012)). Our aim is not to adjudicate between the two frameworks. However, we note, in passing, that Montessori's account appears to us more coherent with the directions contemporary cognitive science has taken today, for reasons that will become apparent. Our aim in this work is to show that the connections between Montessori's framework and contemporary cognitive science might have gone unnoticed in large part because of the misreading we have traced in the preceding sections. The Piaget Author's Manuscript Submitted to History of Psychology case is the sharpest illustration of how that misreading operated at the level of the history of science itself. Had Montessori been read also as a scientist rather than only as an educator, that is, had she been a man and held a stable academic position, or had her early Italian publications reached international audiences, the history of the sciences of the mind might have looked different today. To be clear, the Piaget case is not a story of intellectual dishonesty but of historical contingency: how theoretical frameworks travel, get absorbed through proximity and exposure, and in the process both preserved and distorted.

4.1 The historical connection

The evidence for Piaget's sustained exposure to Montessori's work is substantial and has been underestimated in the literature. Piaget made his first observations of children in the 1920s at La Maison des Petits in Geneva, a modified Montessori nursery funded by the J.J. Rousseau Institute, of which Piaget subsequently became director (Kramer, 1988). Montessori teachers at the time were required, in order to hold that title, to have been trained by Montessori herself or by her closest associates. This training was grounded strictly in the theoretical framework set out in Il metodo (Montessori, 2000) and L'Antropologia pedagogica (Montessori, 1911). Both texts were available in English by 1913 and had been translated into more than twenty languages in the years that followed (A. S. Lillard, 2016; O'Donnell, 2013 O'Donnell, , 2014)). The Metodo had already circulated in Spain from 1911 (Sanchidriánhttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/JWZj 2015) https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/jkAf and appeared in French translation in 1912 (De Giorgi et al., 2021) , the year before the first French-speaking Montessori school opened in Paris. Montessori's ideas were thus in active international circulation, and specifically in French-language literature that Piaget was well placed to encounter. Piaget went on to become president of the Swiss Montessori Society, a position he held for nearly twenty years and acknowledged in his own correspondence (Hofstetter & Schneuwly, 2022; Quarfood, 2023). He had in all likelihood assumed this role following his personal attendance at the last Montessori Congress in Rome in 1934, held shortly before the fascist regime closed all Montessori schools and institutions in Italy (Kramer, 1988: 326). Piaget and Montessori met in person at least once, at the inaugural meeting of the UNESCO Institute of Education in Hamburg in 1951, where Montessori presented her developmental theory and her findings on early education directly (Elfert, 2013).

The structural similarities between the two theories are unsurprising in this light: the constructivist emphasis on the child as active agent, the developmental trajectory from sensorimotor engagement to abstract thought, the constitutive role of the environment in cognitive formation. Both thinkers also shared a biological formation that predisposed them toward a naturalistic, developmental account of mind. There is reason to think, moreover, that Piaget was genuinely drawn to Montessori's concept of 'sensitive periods', which may have contributed more than has been acknowledged to what he would later formalise as genetic epistemology.

Montessori's theory of development could be considered an historical "elephant in the room" of Piaget's theory: present everywhere in its structure but not completely acknowledged, in a way reflecting the same gendered dynamics of intellectual erasure that Thayer-Bacon (2012) identified. It also reflects the institutional asymmetry: Piaget was a professor at Geneva with a fully equipped research programme and international standing; Montessori was the founder of an educational movement, classified as an educator, and therefore not a scientific interlocutor. In fact, Piaget did cite Montessori [e.g., (Piaget, 1970) https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/SS2k, but the trajectory of his engagement is telling. In his work on the development of mathematical thinking in Author's Manuscript Submitted to History of Psychology children (Piagethttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/cWfn 1952) , a text that dedicates substantial space to Montessori-like sensory materials and experiments, he does not cite Montessori at all. When he cites her in later work, the recognition is consistently accompanied by qualifications that diminish her theoretical standing (Piaget, 1970) https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/SS2k 9;98;145), attributing her achievements to intuition and clinical observation rather than to systematic theoretical thinking. That he could recognise what she had accomplished while simultaneously refusing to engage with her as a thinker is perhaps the sharpest illustration of the gendered intellectual erasure that shaped the reception of Montessori work 15.

4.2 Divergences in developmental theories

Piaget's genetic epistemology aligned with the constructivist core of Montessori's account and formalised it into a stage theory of remarkable precision and influence. But the formalisation diverged from Montessori's framework in three respects, each concerning a feature central to her account: the continuity of development across individually variable sensitive periods, the constitutive role of the specific material environment, and the intelligence of perception and action from the earliest stages of development. We address each in turn.

The first divergence concerns the relationship between continuity and individuality in development. Montessori's piani di sviluppo [planes of development] are not stages in Piaget's sense. They are defined by the sensitive periods during which each child constructs its own individual developmental trajectory, guided by its embodied capabilities and the activities it engages in within its material and cultural world. The boundaries between planes are gradual and individual -"each level descends from the previous one and in turn prepares the next" (Tornar & Ahlquist, 2023 https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/BLmY 67) -and the pace and character of development within each plane varies significantly across individuals and cultural contexts, making it the teacher's task to discern each child's developmental position. Piaget's stages, by contrast, are discrete, universal, and largely context-independent: every child ought to pass through every stage in the same order, at roughly the same age, regardless of individual variation (Piaget, 1962). This formalisation made the theory testable and generalisable, but at a cost: it abstracted away from precisely the individual variation and cultural embeddedness that Montessori's framework was designed to capture. This tension is articulated at a deeper level in contemporary research. Bich and Skillings (Bich & Skillings, 2022) argue that stage-based accounts of development implicitly adopt what they call "adultocentrism": the improper fixation on the adult form as the true or proper form of the organism, which injects a forward-looking teleology into all developmental processes and reduces every prior stage to a merely preparatory condition. Against this, they propose that development should be understood as the maintenance of organisational viability at each moment: there are no intermediate stages defined by their relation to some later achievement, only successive self-maintaining organisations, each with its own integrity. Montessori's piani di sviluppo anticipate this position precisely. The second divergence concerns the role of the material environment. Piaget recognised that the child constructs knowledge through action on objects, and that even early representations are grounded in action. But he treated the environment as essentially interchangeable: any sufficiently rich physical setting would support normal cognitive development, and specific contexts had minimal influence on a developmental sequence that unfolds regardless (Babakr et al., 2019). Even in his later revisions (Piagethttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/SFcJ 1976) , his account does not attend to the specific design of the material environment, the particular affordances of specific objects, or the relationship between artefact structure and cognitive outcome. For Montessori, as we have shown in Section 2, these were the theoretical core. This is not to claim a rigid one-to-one mapping between materials and cognitive outcomes. Montessori's materials typically afford several forms of engagement at once, as when the map and geometry insets train geographical or geometrical understanding while simultaneously preparing the hand for holding a pen (A. Lillard, 2008; A. S. Lillard, 2016; Montessori, 2012 Montessori, , 2013)). Rather, specific material structures incline toward specific forms of cognitive processing, without precluding others. This resonates with later anthropological research on how everyday culturally structured activities guide cognitive development (Rogoffhttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/sD0y 2003) , an activity for which a kitchen is, tellingly, always present in the Montessori classroom. The environment is not interchangeable: its specific material structure is constitutive of, and affords the unfolding of cognitive development (Montessori, 2023).

The third divergence is the separation of intelligence from action. Piaget begins with the sensorimotor intelligence of the infant reachinghttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/tTEZ grasping, and manipulating (Piaget, 2008) , but his developmental sequence is structured as a progressive interiorisation and abstraction of action. At the formal operational stage, intelligence is defined by its independence from reality content: by the capacity for hypothetical reasoning that holds "regardless of the intrinsic truth or falseness of the premises" (Ibid.: 42). The child's body and the material world drop out of the definition of intelligence at precisely the moment it reaches its mature form. Piaget's constructivism thus harbours a rationalist endpoint: the ideal cognitive agent is one who has successfully freed thought from its embodied, material origins. That this constitutes a structural tension within Piaget's own framework, rather than an external misreading, is suggested by the fact that even sympathetic reconstructions of his theory acknowledged "the ever-growing divorce between action and cognition" as a live problem in the field (Lourenço & Machado, 1996) https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/TzJM 158).

From the perspective of contemporary research, this rationalist endpoint is precisely what an organisational account of development rules out. Smith and Thelen's (Smith & Thelen, 2003) re-analysis of Piaget's own A-not-B paradigm is exemplary: what Piaget treated as evidence of a discrete stage transition, i.e., the infant's emerging knowledge of object permanence, turns out to depend on posture, wrist weight, delay duration, and the history of prior reaching. Knowledge, on this account, is not a structure the child acquires but an emergent property of the child's real-time interaction with a specific material environment (Ibid.: 346).

Montessori's framework contains no equivalent rationalist endpoint. The piani di sviluppo do not culminate in abstract logic but in an increasingly refined capacity for engaged, purposeful action in the world: what she called normalizzazione [normalisation] https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/4Ahv term is admittedly imperfect in both Italian Author's Manuscript Submitted to History of Psychology and English, carrying connotations of social conformity that Montessori explicitly rejected. For herhttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/OKKk normalisation is a biological concept: the restoration of the organism's natural autoregulatory rhythms (Foschi, 2012) , "biologicamente prevista" [biologically anticipated] in every developing human being (Pecori, 2024). She describes every organism, plant or animal, as endowed with a construction plan, guaranteed by self regulatory processes (Montessori, 2023). This reminds of Maturana and Varela's autopoietic theory (Maturana & Varelahttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/zRck 2012) , and anticipates the organisational accounts of development proposed by Bich and Skillings (2024). The child who has reached maturity in Montessori's sense is not the one who reasons about hypotheticals but the one whose autoregulatory processes have let free to develop and whose intelligence and action have become fully integrated within their living context: concentratedhttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/Rhcw self-directed, and adaptively responsive to the affordances of the material and social environment (Carrig, 2025) , "adaptive as foreseen in nature for all species" (Pecori, 2024) 16 . This integration is not the endpoint of a process that begins elsewhere, but the culmination of a developmental trajectory in which intelligence and action were never separated to begin with. Already in the infant, Montessori insists, the activity of the senses is not passive reception but active engagement: the infant is "un cercatore attivo nell'ambiente" [an active seeker in the environment], and the impressions absorbed become "parte integrante della sua psiche" [integral part of its psyche] (Montessori, 2023: 150). The continuity between the infant's sensory exploration and the mature child's concentrated, self-directed work is not a developmental achievement in Piaget's sense, and it does not require the transcendence of embodiment. It is, instead, the progressive expression of what Montessori considered to be La mente del bambino: Mente assorbente [The Mind of the Child. The Absorbent Mind] (Montessori, 2023): the child's distinctive capacity to take in, internalise, and be transformed by the cultural and material environment it inhabits. This is an embodied, enactive account of infant cognition, from the first sensory impressions of infancy through to the fully integrated intelligence of the mature piano di sviluppo.

4.3 The contemporary revisitation

The three differences identified in the preceding section are precisely the points on which developmental cognitive science has most substantially revised the Piagetian framework in recent decades, arriving at positions that Montessori's work had already staked out.

Dynamical systems accounts have proposed that cognitive capacities emerge from the continuous interaction of the child's body, motor activity, and environment, rather than from the maturation of discrete internal structures. Material engagement theory, as we discussed in Section 1, has argued that the specific material structure of the environment is not a contextual variable but a constitutive factor in cognition and that artefacts are not neutral props but active participants in cognitive processes that shape the abstract capacities through concrete interaction (Malafouris, 2013 (Malafouris, , 2019 (Malafouris, , 2020)). 4E cognition (Paolo et al., 2017) and predictive processing have consistently shown that perception and action are intelligent operations from the earliest stages of development (Ciaunica et al., 2021; Ciaunica & Crucianelli, 2019; Author's Manuscript Submitted to History of Psychology https://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/BiwO+E5Jc+s98E the infant is not a passive recipient of sensory data waiting to construct intelligence, but an active agent generating and revising predictions through embodied engagement with its environment.

Each of these moves converges on the picture that Montessori had articulated from observationhttps://paperpile.com/c/7a2BJu/7fKY practice, and participation (De Jaegher & Di Paolo, 2007) , without the field having recognised the convergence. That the recognition has been slow is itself a consequence of the misreading traced in this paper. Montessori was classified as an educator, her work was read through a pedagogical lens, and the theoretical framework she had developed was encountered, where it was encountered at all, in a form that made its scientific content difficult to see. The convergence is nonetheless real, and its documentation is the principal contribution this paper has attempted to make.

Conclusion

This paper has argued that Maria Montessori was a scientist of the developing mind whose work belongs in the history of cognitive science, as a coherent theoretical framework, grounded in a specific and traceable intellectual genealogy, that addresses the problems cognitive science is only now formalising with the precision they deserve. Montessori did not call herself a cognitive scientist, and cognitive science did not exist in her lifetime. However, she was pursuing, with rigour and originality, at least some of the problems and methods the discipline would later institutionalise, from a formation that was, in its interdisciplinary range and its commitment to integrating structure and function, brain and behaviour, individual and environment, more thoroughly proto-cognitive-scientific than the formation of many figures who are routinely included in the history of the field.

The misreading that has kept her out of that history, and the history of science in general, is not intellectually justified. It is the product of three historically contingent processes: the transformation of her communicative style under financial and political pressure; the losses incurred in translation and international reception; and the structural exclusion she faced as a woman in a male-dominated academic environment. None of them has any bearing on the theoretical content of her work. Reading her correctly, through her untranslated early publications and the intellectual context of late nineteenth-century Rome, reveals a framework of remarkable coherence and contemporary relevance: an account of cognitive development in which individual variation, designed material environments, and cultural context are irreducible and simultaneous dimensions of a single developmental process.

What the recovery of Montessori's cognitive science offers is not merely a correction to the historical record, though it is that. It is an invitation to read a rich and underexplored body of work, including the untranslated Italian sources on which this paper has drawn, with the theoretical seriousness it deserves. The school, Montessori insisted, is a laboratory. The laboratory has been open for over a century, and only a few seem to have noticed.
