Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Abstract

Reproductive health self-care interventions are growing rapidly in Ghana, yet evidence on the quality-of-care mechanisms that ensure safety for end-users and health professionals remains limited. This study explored how quality of care and safety are experienced and maintained within sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) self-care interventions using a selfcare-specific conceptual framework.

Using an exploratory qualitative approach, underpinned by the Quality-of-Care Framework for Self-Care, we interviewed 29 end-users and 19 professionals, comprising health providers, program managers, digital referral agents, and community facilitators across Accra, Tamale, Cape Coast, and Sunyani. The study spanned six SRHR domains. Data were analysed thematically using Dedoose, grounded in deductive and inductive principles.

Key outcomes include the following: Technical Competency -End-users generally felt empowered to access self-care services despite barriers, while professionals highlighted the need for ongoing capacity strengthening; Client Safety -End-users preferred access to medical eligibility information, but professionals expressed concerns about potential misuse; Information Exchange -Information on family planning self-care services was more accessible than across other SRHR domains; Interpersonal Connection and Choice -Endusers reported more positive experiences with remote services than facility-based care, although professionals faced operational constraints affecting quality; Continuity of Care -Follow-up care was better integrated across health facilities than alternate service delivery points, with periodic stockouts disrupting continuity.

End-users and professionals have adopted interim measures to maintain safety despite policy and institutional inadequacies. However, their sustainability remains uncertain and may pose risks to public health unless quality and safety mechanisms are strengthened.

INTRODUCTION

Quality of care refers to the extent to which healthcare services are delivered in accordance with current professional knowledge, resulting in care that is effective, safe, people-centred, timely, equitable, integrated, and efficient (Busse et al., 2019; World Health Organization, 2018). Over time, it has become central to reproductive health, with the World Health Organisation (WHO) describing it as the 'cornerstone for designing and implementing effective sexual and reproductive programmes' (World Health Organization, 2011a). This underscores its importance in managing both conventional interventions and emerging innovative approaches such as reproductive self-care, which the WHO defines within the broader concept of self-care as the ability of individuals, families, and communities to promote health, prevent disease, maintain health, and cope with illness and disability, with or without the support of a health worker (World Health Organization, 2024).

In sub-Saharan Africa, however, efforts to improve quality of care have been slow and poorly integrated, creating profound cascading impacts on reproductive health outcomes, such as adverse birth outcomes -including maternal and child mortality, discontinuation of contraceptive use, unintended pregnancies and increased morbidities associated with unsafe abortion (Bolarinwa et al., 2024; Liyeh et al., 2025a; Tolossa et al., 2024). These challenges have been driven largely by longstanding health system constraints, including provider capacity gaps, inequitable staff workload and distribution, financial and logistical limitations, and policy inadequacies (Akazili et al., 2020; Kyei-Nimakoh et al., 2017).

Expectedly, the quality-of-care challenges associated with broader reproductive health services are increasingly extending to reproductive health self-care services and products, with early indications observed in Ghana (Amo-Adjei et al., 2025; Bekyieriya et al., 2023; Narasimhan et al., 2024). Multiple studies in Ghana highlight emerging warning signs including limited public awareness of self-care safety mechanisms, increased risk of service and product misuse; provider fears about severe complications due to immature systems, leading to them not performance some self-care services as well as withdrawal of key information from end-users; concerns about providers' knowledge and skills in a fast-paced self-care ecosystem, potentially risking end-user safety; and underlying digital and logistical infrastructure deficiencies that detract from end-user experience and safety (Amo-Adjei et al., 2025; Bekyieriya et al., 2023; Narasimhan et al., 2024).

Although Ghana has developed self-care protocols and guidelines in areas such as selfinjectable family planning, abortion self-care, and HIV self-testing, overall intervention effectiveness risks being undermined by sustained implementation and oversight gaps (Ministry of Health & Ghana Health Service, 2021; National AIDS Control Programme et al., 2022). These concerns are buttressed by a documented history of weak policy enforcement in the country (Agblevor et al., 2023; Ahoto et al., 2021; Aikins et al., 2021; Amon et al., 2024).

Notwithstanding the WHO's strategic bet on self-care interventions as a promising innovation to improve accessibility, any potential benefits are at risk in the absence of robust quality assurance mechanisms (World Health Organization, 2019).

Even when prioritised in the short term, quality assurance for reproductive self-care would require additional layers of planning and execution, presenting further complexity for Ghana and other sub-Saharan African nations (Chaney & Mechael, 2020). As established in WHO's conceptual framework for self-care, quality assurance must extend to encompass digital health technologies and platforms, non-traditional service providers such as pharmacies, and robust data protection mechanisms (World Health Organization, 2023). These elements are not commonly emphasised within conventional health system structures and hence not adequately planned for. These planning and execution demands are further constrained by a major gap in the evidence base specific to self-care interventions. This is in contrast to the vast body of knowledge in the broader reproductive health landscape (Liyeh et al., 2025b; Moucheraud et al., 2022; Pasquier et al., 2024; Tessema et al., 2016). Also, to the best of our knowledge, no publicly available evidence on quality of care in reproductive health self-care in Ghana is underpinned by a self-care-specific framework. Existing literature is often shaped by traditional health care dimensions, commonly comprising utilisation, delivery of care, governance and management, community, and policy (Hanefeld et al., 2017; Quentin et al., 2019). Self-care, on the other hand, takes a pragmatist approach, relying on a confluence of digital and manual solutions to enhance client satisfaction. This level of dynamism does not fully align with conventional quality of care frameworks, underscoring the need for a more adaptive framework, applied through a systematic, contextually grounded, and sustainability-oriented lens, ensuring outcomes remain relevant and effective over time.

The framework adopted for this study, Quality of Care Framework for Self-Care from Population Services International (PSI), addresses this gap by offering a contextually relevant, fit-for-purpose instrument for effectively assessing self-care interventions (Population Services International, 2020). The framework's core domains are adapted from the Bruce/Jain Quality of Care Framework, aligning with proven fundamental elements of quality assurance (Bruce, 1990). It also integrates elements from WHO's conceptual framework on self-care, thereby situating it in a globally accepted format (World Health Organization, 2011b). Importantly, it is strongly modelled around clients, the health workforce, and regulatory systems, allowing for a comprehensive and multilayered investigation, as depicted in Figure 1. Overall, the framework offers a more tailored foundation for our study, eliminating potential risks of contextual misalignment. To mitigate the risk of contextual error and suboptimal resource allocation in Ghana's reproductive self-care programming, this study sought to understand how quality of care and safety are experienced and maintained within reproductive health self-care interventions in Ghana, using a dedicated self-care-specific conceptual framework. The study generated locally grounded and nuanced evidence on prevailing gaps and opportunities from the perspectives of both end-users and professionals, towards strengthening policymaking and programme design.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This study adopted an exploratory qualitative design using semi-structured interviews. The approach is originally designed to generate new knowledge into understudied phenomena, making it well-suited to our goal of exploring the relatively nascent field of reproductive health self-care in Ghana (Reid-Searl & Happell, 2012). Additionally, exploratory qualitative design is inherently dynamic and iterative (Renjith et al., 2021; Swaraj, 2019). This enabled an adaptive, in-depth investigation of participants' experiences across diverse categories in different geographical locations throughout the country. The design and reporting also aligned with guidance from the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) framework (Tong et al., 2007).

Study Setting

The study was conducted across selected health facilities and civil society organisations (CSOs) implementing reproductive self-care interventions in peri-urban and urban locations in Accra, Tamale, Cape Coast, and Sunyani, where such programs are widely implemented.

Extensive consultations were done with reproductive health experts in both the public and private sectors to select sites with relevant characteristics, such as clinical, program and policy experience within Ghana's reproductive health self-care ecosystem, required to address our study objectives. This approach facilitated the inclusion of diverse perspectives across the reproductive self-care ecosystem, as well as participants with varied demographic and professional characteristics, thereby enabling the generation of rich and nuanced insights.

Participant Selection

Sampling

We adopted purposive sampling to recruit participants directly involved in the design, implementation, and evaluation of reproductive health self-care interventions in Ghana. This approach is proven to generate relevant insights and outcomes reflecting the perspectives of stakeholders with the relevant profiles. A total of six (6) reproductive domains were investigated: abortion, cervical cancer, family planning, general SRHR, HIV/STI, and maternal and child health (MNCH). These domains were selected based on an extensive 2024 scoping review conducted in Ghana on available reproductive health self-care interventions (Manu et al., 2025). The domains align with the WHO and IPPF's categorisation of reproductive health services (International Planned Parenthood Federation, 2022; World Health Organization, 2025).

In line with the Quality of Care Framework's framing of QoC channels around clients and health workforce, we included two primary categories of respondents: end-users and professionals (Population Services International, 2020). End-users were defined as women of reproductive age who had successfully used at least one reproductive self-care service within any of the seven reproductive health domains in the past 12 months preceding the interview.

Women were prioritised because all the current reproductive health self-care interventions in Ghana primarily target women, with almost no program offerings exclusively for men (Manu et al., 2025). Professionals were defined as stakeholders involved in the design, coordination, or delivery of reproductive health self-care interventions and services in the country.

Stakeholders with expertise in the aforementioned seven reproductive health domains were prioritised. Based on an initial desk review, four types of professionals were included: Professionals meeting the inclusion criteria were identified first through recommendations from local experts in reproductive health programs, policy, and clinical practice. The lead researcher belongs to national reproductive health expert groups and sought recommendations from therein. Secondly, organisations implementing reproductive health self-care programs were also identified through a 2024 scoping review conducted by the research team (31). End-users, on the other hand, were recruited purposively through health facilities and CSOs identified in the same scoping review. The research team recruited endusers in person at the identified health facilities or virtually after consenting to be contacted.

Participants were excluded if they were not fundamentally associated with reproductive health self-care interventions. Specifically, professionals without a direct affiliation to selfcare programs or interventions were excluded. End-users accessing solely in-person variants of any of the reproductive health services were ineligible. In addition, end-users whose primary service was unrelated to reproductive health self-care were automatically excluded. Furthermore, regarding sample size, we recruited professionals with the necessary expertise across the reproductive domains in focus. Participants spanned the four primary professional categories in focus: health providers, program managers, digital referral agents, and community facilitators. To ensure adequate representation, each reproductive health domain was accounted for through at least one professional to provide relevant insights.

For end-users, recruitment focused on women with direct experience of reproductive self-care interventions across the six domains, allowing the study to explore varied experiences of accessing and using these interventions. Consideration was also given to the geographical and organisational diversity of participants to capture perspectives from different implementation contexts.

Although the sample size was initially informed by established qualitative research literature, formal data saturation was not prospectively assessed because thematic analysis commenced after all interviews had been completed (Kapp et al., 2024; Planned Parenthood Association of Ghana, 2023b). Consequently, the final sample size prioritised the relevance of participants' experiences, the diversity of perspectives, and coverage of the reproductive health domains under investigation.

Data Collection

Data Collection Techniques

Data collection was led by a team of four researchers, with extensive experience collecting data specifically for reproductive health studies across the country. Fieldwork was coordinated by the research lead.

All in-person interviews were conducted either at the health facility or the project office of CSOs, thereby ensuring participants felt safe to participate. Virtual interviews were conducted either on Microsoft Teams (preferred mostly by the professionals) or WhatsApp (preferred only by end-users). On Microsoft Teams, the in-built recording function was used to document the proceedings, while an external recording device was used on WhatsApp, with the respondents' consent across both scenarios. The team supplemented audio recordings with detailed notes, including registering participants who wanted to participate in research output member checking. The average interview duration for professionals was between 30 and 60 minutes, and for end-users was between 20 and 40 minutes. Data collection was conducted from September 1 2025, to November 21, 2025.

Data Collection Tools

The study instruments were informed by the Quality-of-Care Framework for Self-Care, which provides a robust lens for examining quality-of-care considerations in self-care programs. The instruments are publicly available https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/dsutg_v1 as a preprint. The framework is structured into five (5) main domains, extracting insights from multiple perspectives The framework elements were adapted into semi-structured topical guides for both end-users and professionals. Given the professionals comprised sub-categories, the tools were further adjusted to account for all respondents. Slight modifications were made to the prompts in each of the five dimensions to accommodate community facilitators and non-digital self-care products and services, as the framework offered limited consideration for these variables. The tools were pre-tested with both professionals and end-users, and finalised after incorporating all relevant insights, such as shortening the number of items in the health providers' tool to accommodate their busy schedules; simplifying digital terminologies for end-users in the northern zone, who have comparatively lower literacy levels; and pairing questions under the Client Safety and Interpersonal Connection and Choice dimensions, instead of asking them consecutively to minimise duplicative undertones for both end-users and professionals.

Data Analysis

A qualitative thematic technique, underpinned by deductive and inductive analysis, was applied. Sequencing of analytical tasks followed guidance from The Framework Method (Gale et al., 2013). Subsequently, researchers compared codes and streamlined any differences.

All coded data were then charted, still under the study's five core dimensions. Researchers used a combination of code frequencies, deviant or outlier narratives, and pattern-based techniques to generate themes, which were compared among the team and finalised after a consensus was reached. In-depth and nuanced insights were subsequently generated for each theme, ensuring they addressed the study's underlying research questions.

Ethical Considerations

The study obtained ethics approval from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC: 006/06/25) and Maastricht University's Faculty of Health, Medicine, and Life Sciences (FHML-REC/2025/021).

To uphold rigorous ethical standards, the study design posed minimal risk to participants, as confirmed by both approving ethics committees. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, with explicit clarification of their rights to confidentiality and privacy.

Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without penalty or obligation. Participants were not compensated for their participation in the interviews. However, measures were taken to ensure that participation did not impose transportation or communication costs on them.

Although participation offered no direct financial or material benefits, the study provided an opportunity for participants to contribute to a better understanding of quality-of-care considerations in this emerging field, informing future improvements in the design and delivery of reproductive self-care interventions in Ghana.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

A total of 48 respondents were engaged, comprising end-users (29) and professionals (19), and spanning six (6) distinct reproductive health domains. End-users were recruited across the following domains: abortion (n=5), cervical cancer (n=1), family planning (n=5), general SRHR (n=5), HIV/STI (n=8), and maternal and child health (n=5). Professionals recruited included: health providers (n=6), program managers (n=6), digital referral agents (n=3), and community facilitators (n=4).

End-users were distributed across the following age categories: 15-24 years (11 participants), 25-34 years (13 participants), and 35-44 years (5 participants). Participants were also drawn from two geographical zones: the Southern Zone (24 end-users and 15 professionals) and the Northern Zone (5 end-users and 5 professionals). Regarding gender, all end-users were female (n=29), while professionals comprised three (3) males and 16 females.

Themes

The findings are organised around the five dimensions of the QoC Framework for self-care: technical competency; client safety; information exchange; interpersonal connection and choice; and continuity of care.

1. Technical Competency

Technical competency examines end-users' perceptions of service providers' competence and professionals' self-assessments of their own competencies in supporting reproductive selfcare. Across all service areas, end-users generally perceived themselves as sufficiently competent to manage their care when provided with clear information. Competency was most often supported by counselling from service providers or guidance available through digital platforms and product instructions. For some users accessing fully remote services, particularly family planning, package leaflets were the primary source of guidance, as illustrated in the following quote from an end-user: "I did not receive any guidance from a health provider because I was not in contact with any. The package, however, had a leaflet in it which had directions on how to use the pills." -(End-user, Family Planning -Southern Zone).

However, the effectiveness of these resources depended strongly on language accessibility. Written instructions were typically available only in English, which limited their usefulness for some users and constrained opportunities for clarification where no direct provider interaction was available. In these situations, users often relied on alternative sources such as online information or social media to supplement their understanding. Conversely, when services were delivered in languages users understood, through provider interactions, digital referral agents, or tailored messaging, language functioned as a key facilitator of self-care competence. For example, rural MNCH users described how SMS messages in familiar language formats helped them interpret pregnancy symptoms, make nutrition decisions, and adopt recommended care practices. The following quote from an MNCH end-user underscores the main insight: "The [SMS] messages came in two parts-antenatal and postnatal. They were very helpful. During the early stages of my pregnancy, I noticed some symptoms that I thought were illnesses, but the messages explained them. The nutrition content, for example, guided me to make better choices. Later, the postnatal messages taught me how to properly breastfeed and maintain good hygiene while caring for my baby." -(End-user, MNCH -Northern Zone) Provider interaction also influenced users' confidence in their own competence. While most participants perceived providers as knowledgeable and trustworthy, unfriendly attitudes occasionally discouraged further engagement. In some cases, particularly among abortion self-care users, individuals actively verified provider competence through independent research or prior professional knowledge before relying on their guidance, as highlighted in the following quote: "They [service providers and referral point] were skilled. After the service, I remembered that there was some medication (Anti-D) that should have come with my package; I am a student midwife, so I knew about the Anti-D Haemoglobin. I reached out to the contact person, and he explained that the medication I was referring to was given for a gestation at around 28 weeks; mine wasn't. This further convinced me that he knew what he was doing; he was competent." -(End-user, Abortion -Southern Zone).

Furthermore, most professionals reported receiving some training related to self-care service delivery, although opportunities varied across service domains and organisational contexts.

Providers in abortion and HIV/STI services often accessed structured training programs, typically supported by donor-funded initiatives. For example, HIV/STI professionals reported regular participation in capacity-building activities through the Global Fund-supported national program. In contrast, professionals involved in cervical cancer services reported fewer training opportunities, as no dedicated national training structures currently support this domain, as expressed by a provider: "I received training once on cervical cancer screening a few years ago, but it was not specific to self-care. Even that was paid for by my organisation." Some participants indicated that they had to self-fund training unless sponsored by their organisations. Finally, training in other areas, such as family planning, MNCH, and broader SRHR services, was often integrated into routine provider training programs. These domains have incorporated self-care approaches for longer periods, enabling them to be embedded within existing professional development structures. This is illustrated by a service provider working in a CSO-facility: "Many. I can't even count the number of trainings we have had. PPAG [Planned Parenthood Association of Ghana] ensures all providers are trained to deliver quality care.

Complications do occur, but with continuous training and capacity building, risks are minimised." -(Service Provider, Abortion, Family Planning, and General SRHR -Southern Zone).

2. Client Safety

Client Safety was assessed at three levels: understanding of how to use the self-care drugs and services safely, including waste disposal standards; self-assessment of medical eligibility; and accurate determination of side effects or complications.

Across both hybrid and fully remote service delivery options, end-users demonstrated adequate understanding of the safe administration of self-care products (including drugs and hormonal medicines), as well as how to identify and manage side effects and complications.

Knowledge of waste disposal standards was, however, limited, with end-users turning to intuitive practices such as wrapping used sanitary materials in polythene before disposal or disposing of used condoms in bins instead of toilet bowls. For self-administration, end-users reported getting information on dosage and usage from the internet, friends, product leaflets or directly from service providers and digital referral agents, as highlighted by a family planning end-user: "I only received guidance from my friend who recommended the service to me, and not from the supplier. There is also an instruction guide in the leaflet in the package. " -(End-user, Family Planning -Southern Zone) Another notable gap was limited access to medical eligibility information prior to service receipt, which was reported mostly by abortion self-care users. Across MNCH, and cervical cancer domains, no openly accessible platforms existed for users to assess their eligibility for self-care. HIV/STI end-users received eligibility information from community-based facilitators under the national self-care program; however, this information remains largely inaccessible to populations outside the program's target communities. In contrast, family planning services were seen as having a lower barrier to entry, supported by readily available information, while general SRHR services typically required no formal screening. In the words of two end-users: "I think it is common knowledge. I did not need to go and research if I was eligible or not.

Since the unprotected sex I had was still within the 72-hour window, I just knew I had to take the post-pill." -(End-User, Family Planning -Southern Zone) "No, I did not know about the eligibility criteria before accessing the service. I was only told when the service was being explained to me. I would have preferred the option of being able to check first before accessing the service." -(End-User, Abortion -Southern Zone) Furthermore, professionals reported that only abortion and HIV/STI domains had dedicated national guidelines and standard operating procedures that are widely disseminated. Findings also revealed inconsistent use of these guidelines, with providers referencing them less frequently than required. For instance, although allowed under the protocols, most professionals, particularly abortion self-care providers, did not share medical eligibility information with end-users, indicating it was necessary to safeguard user safety. Commonly cited reasons included the potential for misuse and the risk of adverse outcomes, as highlighted by two professionals: "First-time users may not know how they'll react to mifepristone or misoprostol. Some may have undiagnosed conditions like anaemia. In such cases, we may recommend further investigations, like an HB test, before proceeding. Because follow-up isn't always frequent, clients need to be well-informed about risks and referral options." -(Service Provider, Abortion, Family Planning, General SRHR -Southern Zone) "In practice, providers rarely read through the protocol directly. The knowledge they rely on mostly comes from what was discussed during training, and that's what guides them. It's not limited to this facility; it's a general issue. People avoid going back to the manual because it's bulky. During recent facility assessments, I met staff who have worked over 15 years yet admitted they've never seen any SOPs. Even facility heads said the same. Traditionally, SOPs and policies are kept in book form, and people simply don't read them." -(Program Manager, Abortion, Family Planning, General SRHR -Southern Zone)

In addition to standard operating protocols, professionals reported that medicinal and diagnostic products used for reproductive self-care are strictly regulated by the Food and Drugs Authority (FDA). However, the level of compliance varied slightly across service areas. According to respondents, abortion medication and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) are regulated through strict batching and centralised distributing respectively, as corroborated by the quote: "The PrEP is provided at the facility, unlike the self-test kits that you can request online and have delivered to you. PrEP is mainly given at the facility, specifically at the ART [Antiretroviral] center." Professionals also noted that contraceptives and HIV test kits are more widely distributed through multiple channels beyond health facilities, with several unapproved brands in circulation, thereby raising serious public health concerns.

3. Information Exchange

Information Exchange explores areas such as end-user access to comprehensible information, including information on benefits, risks, and side effects prior to receiving service; availability of communication tools for bi-directional exchange; and general health systems that ensure consistent access to relevant information.

Prior to receiving services, end-users for abortion, cervical cancer, MNCH, HIV/STI, and general SRHR reported limited awareness of open-source information channels, constraining access to relevant information. Instead, information was sourced through peer networks or community engagement initiatives targeting specific high-burden communities, as mentioned by an end-user: "I imagine what I would do if I didn't have my friend, who told me about the whole service.

I would have preferred that I can just go online, like Facebook, and search the service, and maybe get a number to call. But there is nothing like. They [health staff] won't even give that you online."

-(End-user, Abortion -Southern Zone).

Digital contact centres were also highlighted by end-users as a prevailing source: "For me, I just sat in my home and went online and ordered it. Within some minutes, it was confirmed." -(End-user, Family Planning -Southern Zone). However, professionals indicated that digital contact centres were either not widely publicised due to funding constraints or were still in pilot phases. In contrast, family planning end-users reported access to multiple information sources, and was corroborated by professionals.

Relatedly, end-users confirmed that language accessibility was generally positive, noting that information from community initiatives and digital contact centres was provided in local languages, as illustrated by an MNCH end-user: "Two things made it easy. First, the messages came in the local dialect, so I understood them well. Second, the calls would keep repeating until I was able to answer, so I never missed the information." -(End-user, MNCH -Northern Zone). However, end-users also called for greater diversity in local languages to reach more communities. Additionally, information on product leaflets was predominantly in English, restricting access for non-speakers.

Professionals reported that the primary bi-directional communication systems across service areas included voice calls, text messaging (predominantly WhatsApp), or a combination of both. These systems were reported to enable continuous interaction between service providers, community facilitators, and end-users. However, the type of system integration within health facilities varied across service areas. Platforms such as SHE+ helpline from Savanna Signatures, and national HIV/STI helpline were integrated into public health facilities, while MarieCall from Marie Stopes, and Yenkasa Contact Centre from Planned Parenthood Association of Ghana, were integrated into their own health facilities. As illustrated by an MNCH program manager: "We didn't reinvent the wheel. We leveraged existing Ghana Health Service materials but redesigned them to be more user-friendly and portable." -(Program Manager, MNCH -Northern Zone). In addition, cervical cancer selfcare programming was not integrated into a formalised helpline infrastructure. Professionals noted that the current mode of operations is directly between service providers and end-users.

4. Interpersonal Connection and Choice

Interpersonal connection and choice examine the extent to which end-users experience dignified, empathetic, and respectful care from healthcare providers.

There were notable differences in end-user experiences depending on the point of entryhybrid or fully remote. Overall, feedback on the in-person component of the hybrid pathway was mixed. Some end-users described encounters marked by unempathetic care, reflecting longstanding client service challenges within health facilities, while others experienced dignified care multiple times. In contrast, most remote end-users reported fewer negative experiences, which may be partly due to the shorter interactions with providers.

End-user experiences also varied across SRHR domains. During facility visits, abortion selfcare users experienced heightened scrutiny by service providers, particularly regarding their age, as well as stigma, which they attributed to prevailing cultural sensitivities surrounding abortion. In the words of an abortion self-care end-user: "At the facility, I was asked why I wanted an abortion, and I told them it was because I wasn't ready for it. They went ahead to ask why I didn't use a protection if I wasn't ready for pregnancy. I felt like I was being judged. They were a bit harsh. This was before she even asked of my age. It's probably because she thought I was underaged. She loosened up a bit after I mentioned how old I was. Once she knew I was older, she was no longer harsh, and she even began to advise me." -(End-user, Abortion -Southern Zone).

Similarly, MNCH end-users described the in-person component of the hybrid self-care service as average, mainly because service providers appeared overwhelmed and could not dedicate sufficient time to each client. In contrast, cervical cancer and HIV/STI end-users reported positive experiences at selected public health facilities, as illustrated in this quote: "I understood everything the provider said because the person was calm and patient with me." -(End-user, HIV/STI -Southern Zone). General SRHR and family planning end-users, who engaged mostly with digital referral agents, also received dignified and respectful care. Furthermore, professionals across all SRHR domains acknowledged existing gaps within the self-care service delivery ecosystem, noting that these are not unique to self-care but mirror broader systemic issues within the health sector. Nonetheless, professionals reported a strong commitment to navigate these constraints using available resources, motivated in part by the benefits self-care offers to the health system, including a welcome reduction in patient traffic at health facilities. For instance, in the absence of dedicated logistics and adequate staffing, service providers and community facilitators often worked overtime without additional compensation and relied on personal devices to sustain remote support, as illustrated in this quote from a service provider: "As of now, I'm the only one available at the facility. But I allow them [clients] to call anytime. I've had calls at 1 a.m. If I've told a client to reach out whenever they need help, then I expect that if they call at that time, it's serious. I also allow texting; so, if I can't answer, they can leave a message, and I respond as soon as possible." -(Service Provider, Abortion, Family Planning, and General SRHR -Northern Zone).

Another core dimension explored was the functionality of systems in ensuring adherence to empathetic and dignified service delivery protocols. Across public health facilities and civil society organisation (CSO) managed facilities, periodic reviews and evaluative exercises were in place to assess and reinforce customer care for end-users. Though inconsistent, professionals were, however, knowledgeable of said systems and required to operate therein.

The finding is illustrated in this quote from a service provider: "We have several mechanisms. First, there's a suggestion box, which is checked weekly. Clients can leave anonymous feedback, which we act upon. All staff are trained in clientcentred communication and taught to avoid judgmental behavior. If a client experiences poor treatment, they can also call the facility head directly to lodge a complaint. For online clients, we share our contact centre number during outreaches and radio discussions. They can call to report any issues, which are then escalated to the head office." -(Service Provider, Abortion, Family Planning, and General SRHR -Southern Zone).

5. Continuity of Care

Continuity of Care explores client access to follow-up support, and the underlying mechanisms that facilitate care continuity.

All the self-care interventions reviewed had integrated, functional follow-up systems, except for family planning. End-users who accessed care through health facilities or digital referral agents received comprehensive guidance on what to do in the event of complications or adverse reactions. This included access to a hotline and clear instructions to return to a health facility or another referral point, depending on their location, as captured in this quote from an end-user: "I have the provider's contact. She calls to check up on me or even to remind me to come in for the drugs." -(End-User, HIV/STI -Southern Zone).

However, most family planning end-users relied on remote self-care options, as cited in this quote: "There was no follow-up system, but I have the supplier's contact." -(End-user, Family Planning -Southern Zone). Family planning end-users confirmed that the contact information provided in the product labelling was intended primarily for marketing rather than for follow-up or post-service support.

Additionally, the self-care programs operated varied approaches to follow-up support. Most family planning end-users received commodities directly from pharmacies with no access to an institutionalised follow-up system. End-users reported a reliance on their peer network and the internet for information on managing any adverse events. For abortion, MNCH, general SRHR, and cervical cancer, end-users reported inconsistencies in provider-initiated followup, with most not receiving post-care support. Service providers, however, confirmed that the fragmented follow-up system was by design, as the protocols do not mandate follow-up, but recommend provider discretion. According to the providers, cases were typically followed up on if the end-user experienced complications or adverse effects during the process, as expressed by a service provider: "Follow-up for [abortion] self-care isn't compulsory. But if a client bleeds for more than a week, she may call. Then we advise her to return for review. Some clients never call back because they're fine. As I mentioned, we do not follow up regularly, but occasionally. We may call to ask, "How did the treatment go? How was the flow? How many pads did you change daily?" But most of the time, they call us. We always give them our contact, but we don't collect theirs, so follow-up is usually on their side." -(Service Provider, Abortion, Family Planning, and General SRHR -Southern Zone).

In contrast, HIV/STI end-users had a stricter system, coordinated typically by service providers. End-users also mentioned being given access to a hotline and direct access to a service provider, if preferred, as highlighted by an end-user: "I was given a follow-up appointment to return to the facility two months after completing my home-based treatment.

During this visit, new laboratory tests are conducted to assess treatment adherence" -(Enduser, HIV/STI -Southern Zone).

Lastly, end-users and professionals both reported persistent challenges with the availability of medication and medical supplies, which significantly affects continuity of care. Particularly in public health facilities and CSO-managed outlets, end-users and providers reported at least one instance of commodity stockout in the past three years. These shortages were attributed to logistical delays at ports, bureaucratic bottlenecks within state agencies, and inconsistent government funding. However, professionals noted that private pharmacies rarely experience such stockouts, as they often access alternative supply chains more efficiently than public health facilities. In the words of one HIV/STI end-user: "For instance, when my pills were getting finished last month, I called the provider (nurse) to inform her that I was going to come around to restock. She informed me that her facility was out-of-stock, and rescheduled my appointment." -(End-user, HIV/STI -Southern Zone).

DISCUSSION

The study explored quality-of-care considerations within Ghana's reproductive health selfcare ecosystem using the Quality of Care Framework for Self-Care, a dedicated self-carespecific framework, focusing on prevailing gaps and opportunities.

The findings showed that policy and governance gaps, such as poor planning and inefficient resource allocation, continue to undermine quality control efforts. Notably, persistent stockouts in public health facilities are redirecting demand to alternative, often unapproved, sources, posing significant risks to end-users. This is buttressed by the recent Demographic and Health Survey, alongside other empirical evidence, which reports a significant rise in demand for and utilisation of reproductive health services, despite limited improvements in health infrastructure (Ghana Statistical Service & ICF, 2024; Obeng et al., 2025). This suggests that end-users are actively seeking care despite enduring constraints and increasingly turning to unregulated outlets, with potentially harmful consequences. For example, Polis et al. (2020), which is the most recent and widely cited statistic on abortion incidence in Ghana, estimated that, of the 200,000 abortions that occurred in 2017, 71% were illegal (Polis et al., 2020). Other studies also report growing access to emergency contraceptive pills outside approved health facilities, often accompanied by broad concerns about misinformation (Gbagbo, 2024; Rokicki & Merten, 2018).

Similarly, licensed chemical sellers and pharmacies, which are key initial contact points for end-users, have faced intense scrutiny in recent times for purveying unlicensed condoms (Guilford, 2022). Although self-care approaches offer transformational advantages, without institutionalised measures to close these quality-control gaps, the innovation falls short of supporting the country's universal health coverage (UHC) agenda. In practical terms, policymakers and regulators must prioritise strengthening commodity regulation, supplychain reliability, and quality assurance mechanisms across both formal and informal delivery channels if self-care interventions are to safely contribute to UHC goals.

Additionally, our findings showed that, while end-users did not have access to medical eligibility information prior to service uptake, most professionals were apprehensive about the risks of openly sharing that information. This stance, however, is in dissonance with the original objective of empowering end-users and facilitating patient-centred care (World Health Organization, 2011b). Studies have shown that end-users are increasingly choosing remote support over in-person care; hence, health care systems should proactively seek to reduce the risks of injury by granting full access to all relevant information, including medical eligibility information (Amo-Adjei et al., 2025; Nai et al., 2022a; Obiri-Yeboah et al., 2017). This would also require developing systems such as simplified eligibility tools and remote counselling protocols that support informed decision-making outside conventional facility settings. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated the safety of self-care, even in seemingly high-risk domains such as self-managed medical abortion, thereby assuaging provider fears and demanding the prioritisation of patient safety (Adu et al., 2023; Amo-Adjei et al., 2025; Gilano et al., 2024; Kapp et al., 2024; Maliwichi et al., 2021; Nai et al., 2022a).

The increasing demand for reproductive health self-care presents an opportunity to scale reproductive care delivery at an unprecedented level. Our findings, alongside other emerging evidence, show substantial benefits for both end-users and the health system, with minimal downsides (Adu et al., 2023; Agbenyo et al., 2022; Amo-Adjei et al., 2025; Behnke et al., 2020; Kapp et al., 2024; Manu et al., 2025; Nai et al., 2022b). As such, recent trends suggest that policy direction, donor funding, and local interest are increasingly converging toward greater investment in reproductive health self-care (Narasimhan et al., 2024; World Health Organization, 2019). However, amid the evident challenges within the local health system, it is unlikely that the system can adequately meet this growing demand from both policy and user-demand perspectives, raising the risk of undermining the full potential of reproductive health self-care in Ghana. A strategic response would be for the health system to recognise the reach and value of alternative health delivery channels, such as pharmacies, traditional birth attendants, and community volunteers, and formalise their inclusion within care delivery systems. This would involve building protocols and guidelines, alongside a national strategic plan for advancing scale. The reach of these alternative providers is vast and expanding far more rapidly than public health facilities, making them an increasingly indispensable component of care delivery (Amedzro et al., 2024; Okai et al., 2019; Pharmacy Council Ghana, 2026). Experiences from other countries in sub-Saharan Africa demonstrate that the successful integration of alternative delivery channels is both feasible and achievable within the Ghanaian context (Amedzro et al., 2024; Lamba et al., 2021; Okai et al., 2019; Thurston et al., 2015). Furthermore, end-users in our study reported generally positive perceptions of the competency of healthcare providers in delivering self-care services. A review of standard operating protocols in Ghana, and globally, indicates that, although the dimensions of selfcare may differ, they are not substantially removed from traditional service delivery models (Ministry of Health & Ghana Health Service, 2021; Munoz, 2024; National AIDS Control Programme et al., 2022; World Health Organization, 2019). For example, family planning counselling and administration remain largely the same across both models, with the main addition to self-care being the need for provider training in managing ongoing remote engagement. Similarly, the medical abortion process is largely comparable, with the key distinction being the additional skills required to assess client eligibility remotely. The counselling and administration processes for HIV, general SRHR, MNCH, and cervical cancer services are also broadly similar across both models. Against this background, the local health system could integratively design a minimum package of competencies that can be scaled nationally, building on existing reproductive health training modules to facilitate quicker adoption. This approach would be more efficient, as it would not require overhauling existing systems and processes, but rather integrating self-care competencies therein, thereby avoiding costly system-wide redesigns and extensive retraining.

In addition, despite the importance of integration as a critical path forward given resource constraints in Ghana, it is equally important for local health systems to recognise the underlying nuances of self-care and avoid systematically conflating it with traditional service delivery approaches (Narasimhan et al., 2020 (Narasimhan et al., , 2024)). Whereas building on existing training modules is essential, such integration must explicitly delineate self-care-specific processes to ensure maximum impact. For example, facility-based abortion care typically requires ultrasound scanning and strict post-abortion protocols, including mandatory follow-up visits.

In contrast, self-managed abortion protocols do not require these procedures; however, our study found that some providers did not adequately acknowledge this distinction. As a result, providers had seemingly transitioned to self-care in principle yet continued to apply facilitybased processes across both models.

Similarly, some health providers in our study reported that a substantial proportion of users of self-injectable contraception still returned to facilities for provider administration, which undermines the core principle of patient-led care. This also risks institutionalising unnecessary layers of in-person provider interaction, particularly within public health facilities, thereby increasing the relative cost of self-care services while further exacerbating the in-person service burden on health facility providers. To mitigate these challenges, standard operating procedures should clearly delineate self-care processes to ensure providers fully recognise and operationalise these differences. Although such distinctions may appear minimal, existing evidence demonstrates that they are highly consequential and transformative for both service delivery and user autonomy (Amo-Adjei et al., 2025; Behnke et al., 2020; Nai et al., 2022a).

Overall, despite the depth of insights generated, important gaps remain in understanding the quality-of -care considerations in integrating alternative delivery channels within the formal health system. Specifically, given the increasing shift toward remote and autonomous care pathways, research is required to examine the safety of digital self-care tools and virtual ecosystems that power reproductive health care. There are currently no regulatory models that address digital safety in reproductive health self-care, raising enormous safeguarding risks in areas such as data protection, misinformation, privacy breaches, and the exploitation of vulnerable end-users. Adequate evidence is therefore needed to ensure self-care interventions primed for scale are safe in the long term.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

The study has some notable strengths, particularly in its design. Adopting the Quality-of-Care Framework for Self-Care enabled a more nuanced exploration of self-care within Ghana's evolving, yet predominantly traditional, health care system. The framework combines critical themes from acclaimed models (Bruce/Jain Quality of Care Framework and Donabedian's Structure-Process-Outcome framework), effectively assessing the complexity and dynamism of all self-care interventions covered in our study (Bruce, 1990; Donabedian, 1988). To the best of our knowledge, this represents the pioneering use of the framework to assess reproductive health self-care interventions in Ghana. This ensured our study was grounded more clearly in relevant self-care constructs, strengthening the uniqueness, relevance, and applicability of findings.

In addition, the study examined the subject from the perspectives of professionals and endusers, ensuring a more comprehensive outlook and aligning with WHO's quality of care recommendations for reproductive health services (World Health Organization, 2011b).

Relatedly, the investigation spanned six different reproductive health services, generating rich and diverse insights across both professionals and end-user groups.

Nonetheless, the study also has some limitations. Although the framework adopted for the study is heavily optimised for self-care, it is somewhat health system-centric and partially underrepresents community-based interventions. As a result, the domains were not adequately primed for exploring key dimensions of community-based awareness mechanisms and nonclinical self-care service providers such as community-based volunteers. We mitigated this by adapting specific prompts about community-focused dimensions of self-care, which were posed to all respondent categories to bring out multiple perspectives. Additionally, despite widespread outreach, fewer cervical cancer end-users were recruited as most declined due to privacy concerns, potentially affecting the transferability of outcomes.

Cervical cancer self-sampling is a relatively small-scale intervention with a small sampling pool; hence, the study was heavily constrained in that regard. This was mitigated by engaging cervical cancer professionals to provide insights that address the primary study objectives.

Lastly, the relatively small sample sizes typically associated with qualitative interviews may have limited the diversity of insights, particularly those obtained from end-users. Although surveys could have facilitated a broader exploration of experiences across a larger end-user population, the pioneering nature of this study warranted an initial emphasis on depth of understanding rather than breadth. Furthermore, reproductive self-care remains a relatively new and sensitive service area within Ghana's cultural context, which may have posed challenges to recruiting a sufficiently large and diverse sample for a survey-based study. As end-users increasingly embrace reproductive self-care, public and private sector actors must work collaboratively to strengthen institutional structures, improve coordination, and establish mechanisms that safeguard both end-users and professionals. Such efforts are essential to ensuring the quality, safety, and sustainability of reproductive self-care interventions and ultimately improving reproductive health outcomes in Ghana.

All interview audio was transcribed using the intelligent verbatim technique, with transcripts sectioned around the core dimensions of the Quality of Care for Self-Care framework, namely, technical competency, client safety, information exchange, interpersonal connection and choice, and continuity of care. Afterwards, transcripts were organised into respondent categories, end-users and professionals, and subsequently reviewed by three (3) experienced researchers, including the lead researcher -IS, MK, and KAM. Interviewer notes were included with the respective transcripts to serve as an approved summary of each conversation. Participants were informed of their right to access their interview transcripts during data collection. However, none requested to review their transcripts before analysis; consequently, transcript-level member checking was not conducted, and the research team proceeded with coding and analysis. In line with deductive principles, a codebook was initially developed by the lead researcher and organised around the five core dimensions of the Quality of Care for Self-Care framework. Thereafter, guided by inductive principles, open coding was done collaboratively by two researchers (MK and KAM) within each core dimension using Dedoose.

CONCLUSION

Quality of care has grown in importance over the relatively short lifespan of self-care interventions in Ghana, but has not commensurated with marked improvements in policy and institutional structures. Major concerns persist regarding limited access to accurate and holistic information, lack of dedicated QoC protocols for most self-care domains, immature operational and service delivery structures at health facilities, and a growing number of unregulated outlets offering unapproved self-care products. Differences between end-users' and professionals' perspectives on quality of care further highlight the need for greater alignment between end-user needs and professional capacity. These challenges are compounded by overstretched public sector actors and fragmented coordination among private sector and civil society organisation actors. Without stronger institutional support and collaborative quality and safety mechanisms, the sustainability of reproductive self-care interventions remains uncertain, with potential implications for public health.
