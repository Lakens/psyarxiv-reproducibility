Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Symptom networks have emerged as a popular framework for characterizing the interactions among psychiatric symptoms.

Estimated from longitudinal ecological momentary assessment (EMA) recordings, they promise individualized insights into psychopathology and its treatment. Yet whether these networks can be robustly estimated from the short, noisy, and irregularly sampled time series typical of EMA studies remains an open question. We address this through the lens of predictive validity: do estimated cross-symptom connections improve forecasts of future symptom states? In a systematic analysis spanning four public EMA datasets covering different symptoms and sampling schemes, we find that they largely do not. In 273 of 313 participants, symptom networks are outperformed by networks that only use every individual symptom's past to predict its future, indicating that estimated cross-symptom connections tend to worsen out-of-sample predictions rather than improve them. This pattern holds across estimation strategies from unregularized Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) to multilevel hierarchical modeling and nonlinear statespace models, suggesting that cross-symptom connections are difficult to identify given the characteristics of real EMA data. Simulation studies support this interpretation, showing that even when ground-truth data is generated by a network model mirroring realistic EMA conditions, cross-symptom structure is often not recovered with sufficient precision to yield a predictive advantage. These findings raise questions about the interpretive framework built on idiographic symptom networks, and suggest that progress may require either measurement designs that make cross-symptom influence more identifiable, or models with less ambitious inferential targets than the recovery of directed cross-symptom effects.

1 Introduction

Emotional states and psychiatric symptoms emerge from an interplay of intrinsic processes, such as interactions among psychological states or symptoms that influence one another, and extrinsic influences via a constant stream of environmental events and social contexts. The network perspective on psychopathology has gained prominence [1], conceptualizing mental disorders as networks of symptoms that causally influence one another. In depression, for instance, insomnia causes exhaustion, exhaustion results in anhedonia, and anhedonia further lowers mood, which in turn disrupts sleep. A network model fit to symptom data, so is the hope, can capture how these self-sustaining cycles emerge. This framing leans heavily on the assumption that internal, symptom-to-symptom dynamics dominate the system's evolution. If this is true, meaningful predictive structure should be extractable from symptom time series alone. If external, unobserved factors dominate instead, the system may fundamentally resist such efforts, and symptom evolution will appear driven by noise [2].

Yet internal dynamics are not directly observable, but we can only infer them from how symptoms covary over time. This inferential problem has been approached in two ways. Early network research relied predominantly on cross-sectional, between-person data, estimating average symptom-to-symptom relationships across a sample. This approach faces well-documented limitations. Estimated networks are often supported by too little evidence for individual edges to be interpreted with confidence [3], and typical study sample sizes yield poor sensitivity for recovering true edges [4]. More fundamentally, interpretations of cross-sectional networks conflate between-person and within-person variation, while psychiatric symptoms exhibit substantial non-ergodicity: population-level averages may poorly predict individual trajectories [5; 6; 7]. A network estimated across people, in other words, need not resemble the dynamics within each individual.

Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA, [8]) offers the promise of capturing symptom evolution in situ, sampling how symptoms unfold in everyday life. The widespread adoption of smartphones has made it feasible to collect dense temporal data per person [9; 10]. Modeled with Vector Autoregressive (VAR) models, EMA data permits estimation of how symptoms at one time point predict symptoms at the next within individuals. These person-specific network models enable simple visualizations [11], and promise individualized insight into symptom dynamics and potential treatment targets [12].

Concretely, a VAR model predicts each symptom's rating at a given timestep from the rating of all symptoms at the preceding timestep. Its diagonal coefficients capture how strongly a symptom persists from one measurement to the next, while its off-diagonal coefficients capture influence between symptoms and are what is drawn as the edge of a symptom network. An Autoregressive model(AR) model is the same model with those off-diagonal terms fixed to zero, therefore each symptom is predicted from its own history alone.

Yet an important question remains largely unaddressed: do these person-specific network models actually "work"? If the premise of the network approach that symptom-to-symptom influence exert substantial causal influence holds, then the cross-symptom links a network model recovers should carry predictive information about a symptom's future. Virtually no study in the idiographic network literature has tested this directly, until recently, where Fechtelpeter et al. [13] found that network models performed significantly worse even than simple baselines, and Zhang et al. [14] show that the time series lengths used throughout the idiographic network literature fall well short of what is required for estimated networks to be adequately powered. These results put into question whether cross-symptom network estimation yields any predictive advantage, motivating the investigation we present here.

We build on [13] methodology, using an expanding window train-test validation (see Fig. 1) across four publicly available EMA datasets [15; 12; 16; 17], covering a broad spectrum spanning symptom dimensionality, sampling frequency, study length, clinical population (anxiety, depression, PTSD, and affect regulation), and preprocessing strategies. We further investigate different network estimation strategies, moving from simple unregularised networks, different regularisation strategies, to more complex hierarchical models and nonlinear state space models. Across this range of datasets, model families, and design choices, we find that models based on crosssymptom structure consistently add no predictive value beyond a symptom's own history, and mostly fail to outperform naive persistence or median forecasts. We argue that our results indicate that this problem might more generally not be solvable by better models, but an intrinsic property of EMA data at realistic noise levels and study lengths, where robust and persistent cross-symptom dynamics (if they exist) are not reliably discoverable in principle.

To gain further insight into these two interpretations (genuine cross-symptom structure surviving regularization vs. convergence on AR), we carried out an additional diagnostic analysis. The autocorrelation structure of symptoms can arise either from a symptom's own persistence (sadness now tends to mean sadness later) or from another symptom's influence (anger now keeps sadness elevated later). When symptoms are strongly correlated with one another, as they typically are in EMA data, these two explanations correspond to highly collinear predictors, so a joint model can only apportion variance between them via structural assumptions such as regularization. As an approximate way to probe this collinearity, we regressed each symptom's own AR(1) history out of its time series and recomputed cross-symptom correlations on the residuals, which indicates how much of the raw cross-symptom correlation could plausibly be attributed to shared autocorrelation rather than direct coupling. Fig. 7 shows that temporal (lag-1) cross-correlations drop substantially after residualization across all four datasets (25-48% reduction), and in three of four, including Rowland, the remaining correlation is statistically indistinguishable from what uncoupled time series of the same length produce by chance. This pattern is consistent with collinearity between cross-symptom and autoregressive signal being one plausible contributor to why cross-symptom terms fail to add predictive value, including in Rowland, where off-diagonal coefficients otherwise survive regularization. Given the approximate nature of this diagnostic, however, we do not treat it as conclusive on its own. We examine the conditions under which genuine cross-symptom terms could in principle be recovered more directly via simulation studies below (Sect.2.3).

2.2 Nonlinear Latent-Space Models Fail To Improve Upon AR Predictions

The failure of linear VAR models might further be attributed to two simplifying assumptions: that symptom dynamics are linear, and that they operate in the directly observed symptom space rather than an unobserved latent space. Both assumptions are routinely questioned in the psychiatric modelling literature, where concepts such as multistability, attractor dynamics, and nonlinear switching between mood states [19] are frequently invoked to motivate more expressive model classes. We therefore ask whether moving beyond linear observation-space models recovers the predictive structure that VAR fails to capture.

To test this, we trained Almost-Linear Recurrent Neural Networks (AL-RNNs, [20; 21]). AL-RNNs generalise linear dynamical systems by allowing a small number of units to implement nonlinear switching between linear subregions, while the remainder of the network remains linear. While there are many candidate nonlinear model classes one might consider, we focus on AL-RNNs for two reasons. First, the P = 0 case recovers a linear state-space model estimated via gradient descent, hence effectively a VAR with different implicit regularisation, providing a direct connection to the model class already examined. Second, increasing P allows us to tune nonlinearity continuously, so the question of whether expressivity helps can be answered systematically. We present an illustrative analysis on the Fisher dataset, since its regular sampling structure directly satisfies the temporal assumptions of sequential recurrent models, but found consistent results with the other datasets as well. We trained AL-RNN on the EMA data, varying the number of piecewise-linear nonlinear units P ∈ {0, 5, 10}.

Unlike VAR models, which are fit in a single step, models of this class are fit iteratively, where parameters are adjusted in small increments, with each pass through the data (a training epoch) reducing the error on the training set. Plotting out-of-sample error against training time thus provides a natural diagnostic for the point at which a model has captured the reproducible structure in the data from the point at which it begins fitting noise, with error on held-out data first falling and then rising again.

Fig. 4 a shows MAE (as before normalised per subject against AR performance (= 1.0)) as a function of how long the model has been trained (training epoch) for all AL-RNN configurations. The turning point arrives almost immediately, where all models quickly peak near AR performance, then deteriorate monotonically with continued training. Crucially, no model at any level of nonlinearity outperforms the AR baseline at any point during optimisation. While more nonlinear models overfit more slowly, their pattern is very similar to that of the linear AL-RNN. Hence whatever an optimally early-stopped model would recover here, does not provide predictive value beyond the symptom's own history.

Fig. 4b reveals the mechanism underlying this trajectory, shown here for P = 0: training loss decreases steadily (Fig. 4c left), yet out-of-sample MAE rises in turn. The Frobenius norm of the transition matrix grows continuously (Fig. 4c right). At initialisation the model quickly fits the diagonal autoregressive structure, at which point it achieves AR-level out-of-sample performance. As training proceeds, off-diagonal weights are filled in, which progressively drive overfitting (decrease in training loss, increase in test loss). Therefore, cross-symptom structure these models learn is, by this measure, sample-specific noise, analogously to the linear case.

While we again cannot exclude that nonlinear models could in principle capture more complex cross-symptom dynamics, without sufficiently strong priors these models face even greater identifiability pressure than VAR models. Finally, the convergence of all estimation strategies toward the same predictive ceiling suggests that the failure of network models is not a consequence of how coefficients are estimated, but of what kind of data they are estimated from. To interpret this, we turn to simulations in which the ground-truth network is known, and ask what a given AR-versus-VAR forecast gap implies about how well that network has been recovered. This step is necessary because in real EMA data the ground-truth connectivity is unknown and comparing forecasts is the only available test.

2.3 Forecast Gaps in the Empirically Observed Range Correspond to Poor Network Recovery in Simulation Studies

To this end we conducted simulations using ground-truth VAR models with autoregressive coefficients drawn to produce lag-1 autocorrelation in the range observed in the empirical data (mean ≈ 0.5), while systematically varying network strength (magnitude of cross-lagged coefficients) and network density (the ratio of real edges vs. number of possible edges in A). We ran the full grid for both 10 and 20 items. For each configuration, we compared AR versus LASSO VAR forecasting performance (see Sect. 4.5.1 for details).

Fig. 5a shows the ratio of AR to LASSO VAR mean absolute error across network strength, density, and time series length, for 10 items (top) and 20 items (bottom). Across most of the grid AR matches or slightly outperforms VAR. A clear VAR advantage emerges only in the upper right region, where the ground-truth network is both comparatively dense and comparatively strong, and even there it requires more than T = 100 observations. With 10 items the spread of forecast ratios is compressed relative to 20 items, but the qualitative pattern is unchanged.

In the simulations the ground-truth network is known, so recovery can be measured directly and related to the forecast ratio. Fig. 5b compares the forecast ratio (AR/VAR) to how accurately the LASSO VAR estimate recovers the true network, measured with three metrics: the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC, [22]) and the Jaccard index [23], both established metrics quantifying agreement between the recovered and true edge sets, and the Frobenius norm of the off-diagonal coefficient error, quantifying how far the recovered edge weights lie from the true ones.

All three track the forecast ratio (r = 0.72, 0.70, -0.74). Within the forecast ratios actually observed in the empirical datasets (≈ 0.88-0.95), all three metrics imply poor recovery, with a median MCC of 0.14 against a chance value of 0, a median Jaccard index of 0.22, indicating that under a quarter of the recovered edges reflect true edges, and a median off-diagonal error norm of 1.34, meaning that the LASSO-VAR edges are further from the truth than no edges at all, which would give a value of 1. The observed AR vs. VAR forecasting performance therefore indicates that even if a true network underlies the data, it has not been successfully recovered. Notably, at T = 100 this remains true even for comparatively strong networks, where both the forecast ratio and the recovery metrics stay poor.

3 Discussion

This paper set out to test whether idiographic VAR-based temporal network models estimated from EMA data demonstrate predictive validity. Across four independent datasets spanning 313 participants and diverse clinical populations and symptom batteries, the answer is consistently negative, where VAR-based symptom network models are outperformed by their own restricted autoregressive special case, i.e. AR models, in the overwhelming majority of participants. This means the estimated cross-symptom connections do not only fail to add predictive value, but actively degrade forecasting accuracy, a signature of overfitting. This finding holds across all tested estimation strategies, from unregularised OLS, LASSO regularisation, stepwise SEM selection, hierarchical estimation, and nonlinear extensions, which all converge to the same predictive ceiling.

The invariance of this ceiling implies that the problem lies in what can be identified from the data rather than in any particular estimation strategy. Our simulation confirms that the empirically observed AR-versus-VAR forecast ratios (≈ 0.88-0.95) correspond to poor network recovery on all three metrics tested, even with a known ground-truth VAR process matched to the empirical autocorrelation. Recovery does improve with denser, stronger networks and longer series, but it shows that the forecasting gaps we observe in practice are consistent with a true network existing yet being unrecoverable at realistic EMA time series lengths and coupling strengths.

Network inference from time series data is a recurring problem across fields, including synaptic connectivity estimated from spike trains [24], and gene regulatory networks [25]. A direct parallel can also be found in functional neuroimaging, where fMRI measures blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal as an indirect proxy for neural activity in each of a set of brain regions, sampled repeatedly over time. The inferential task of estimating directed influence among brain regions is closely related, with the added complication that true couplings operate on a timescale faster than the sampling interval. There, lag-based methods, including VAR-based network models [26], and more expressive approaches such as Dynamic Causal Modelling [27], have been reported to face identifiability problems of their own [28; 29] and to recover directionality only poorly from realistic BOLD data [30; 31], despite regular sampling, negligible missingness, absence of external factors, and longer recordings offering more favorable conditions than those found in EMA data.

Beyond the question of identifiability, our results are also suggestive about what kind of process could be underpinning the data. Predictions are bracketed by the persistence baseline (predicting the next rating by the previous one) at the one-step horizon and the global median at the one-day horizon, which bears on the opening question of whether intrinsic symptom-to-symptom dynamics or extrinsic and unobserved influences dominate the system's evolution. For a process with the martingale property, the current value is the optimal one-step predictor that no function of the observed history improves upon [32]. That symptom trajectories are predicted near-optimally by their own last observed value bounds how much of their short-term evolution can be carried by deterministic within-system coupling at the timescale and resolution captured here. Since EMA data only offers a partial window onto the underlying process, this is also consistent with symptom dynamics being driven by variation these measurements do not resolve, whether environmental events and social contexts absent from the symptom data, or symptom-to-symptom influence operating faster than the sampling interval.

We think these findings have implications beyond the specific models tested here. Much of the network perspective's interpretive weight rests on individual edges: properties such as centrality indices and proposed intervention targets rely on directed coefficients of an estimated transition matrix. We want to emphasize that recovering such coefficients is a considerably harder statistical problem than may be intuitively apparent, and has accordingly surfaced, as outlined before, in other fields where a similar problem has been pursued (e.g. [26]). The conditions under which recovery succeeds (i.e. strong coupling combined with long recordings and low noise) may often not be met by the EMA protocols on which symptom network studies are typically based. Claims about which symptom drives which can therefore rest on parameters that the data frequently cannot constrain, a pattern that persisted across every symptom set and every estimation strategy we tested. Our results do not exclude that a protocol with a different symptom set, substantially fewer variables, denser sampling, or a population with stronger cross-symptom coupling could yield identifiable cross-symptom interactions. What they do suggest is that this problem may be harder than its routine application assumes, which makes it less surprising that no estimator we tried recovered structure that improved prediction. A reasonable prior going forward is that cross-symptom dynamics may not be reliably recoverable from EMA data unless the population, protocol, and dimensionality of the modeled system are chosen with this goal in mind.

We see several ways forward. On the measurement side, new protocols can resolve unobserved factors that currently appear as noise, or increase identifiability by extending recordings and reducing the dimensionality of the modeled system. The limitations of unimodal self-report have contributed to a shift toward multimodal designs incorporating physiological, behavioural, and environmental data [33], alongside explicit efforts to measure the external events and contextual shifts (e.g. social encounters, weather reports etc.) that pure symptom time series models have to absorb into the residual [34]. Efforts to model psychopathology at lower dimensionality than the individual symptom are well established [35], and our results support this on purely estimation-theoretic grounds: Rowland, with the most observations per free parameter, came closest to AR-level performance while retaining non-zero cross-symptom coefficients. Its roughly 240 observations per participant over a month are, however, at the upper end of what EMA protocols currently achieve, and denser or longer sampling increases participant burden [36], so reducing the number of modeled variables is therefore the more tractable of the two routes to identifiability.

Where the measurement strategy cannot be changed, the modeling goal has to be adjusted. Continuoustime stochastic formulations such as Ornstein-Uhlenbeck models [37; 38] shift the target of inference from directed cross-symptom coupling to the characteristics of a stochastic process, usually a much smaller set of parameters. The fMRI literature has emphasized that undirected contemporaneous measures, in particular partial correlations, recover network structure comparatively reliably [26], at the cost of allowing no conclusions about which variable influences which. Our split-half analysis confirms a similar conclusion for the EMA datasets, where contemporaneous networks are substantially more reproducible across different data samples than directed coefficients (Fig. 8).

It is important to be precise about what these results do and do not establish. EMA data remain informative for many purposes [39]: mean symptom levels, intraindividual variance, and treatment-related trajectories are legitimate targets of analysis that fall outside the scope of our critique. Our critique specifically concerns the interpretation of individual cross-lagged coefficients, and their failure to contribute meaningfully to out-of-sample forecasts.

We therefore propose out-of-sample comparison against the restricted autoregressive model as a routine validation step for idiographic network analyses, applicable to symptom sets, populations, protocols, and estimators beyond those examined here. The test is inexpensive, requires no additional data collection, and provides a simple interpretable verification of the validity of cross-symptom coefficients.

4 Materials and Methods

4.1 Conceptual Framework

When assessing that a network model is a good explanation of the data, we must distinguish between two problems: (1) a hidden network exists but is difficult to infer (a problem of identifiability), versus (2) the network framework itself poorly explains what drives the data (a problem of model misspecification). This difference maps onto a broader methodological divide between statistical inference and machine learning approaches to model validation [40], which have been called the "two cultures" of statistical learning [41]. Statistical inference focuses on creating probabilistic models of data-generating processes to test specific hypotheses, for instance, whether a cross-lagged effect differs significantly from zero. The output is a test statistic quantifying confidence that a discovered relationship represents a "true" effect unlikely to result from noise. Machine learning, on the other hand, prioritizes out-of sample prediction without necessarily formalizing an explicit model of underlying mechanisms [42]. In ML, a model that fits training data but fails to achieve the same performance on an independent test set has overfit. For time series data where we lack strong theoretical priors, test set forecasting performance becomes the primary criterion for model validity [32].

EMA time series present a particularly challenging terrain for complex model estimation. Recordings are typically short (weeks to months), sampled sparsely relative to the timescales of psychological processes (although what an optimal sampling rate is remains open to debate [43]), and subject to substantial unobserved external influences, such as events, social context, sleep, and biological rhythms, that collectively drive much of the observed variance. In this setting, a VAR model over p symptoms introduces p 2 free lagged parameters. The number of transitions informing each coefficient is given by (T -1)/(p + 1), against the (T -1)/2 available to an AR(1) model of the same series. Table 1 reports both for the four datasets analysed here, computed on the average available transitions per dataset, including, in Rowland and Geschwind, the exclusion of overnight gaps. The resulting VAR ratio ranges from 4.3 to 12.7, against 17.3 to 57.0 for an AR(1) model on the same series. Conventional regression heuristics of ten to twenty observations per parameter are therefore violated outright in three of the datasets and met only marginally in one. Such counting rules are in any case optimistic here, since they are indifferent to signal strength: in a noiseless linear system the transition matrix is recoverable from a handful of transitions, and once noise is present the binding quantity is not the number of observations per coefficient but the size of each cross-lagged effect relative to residual variance. EMA measurements are unfavourable on exactly this dimension, since sparse self-report of a small item set captures only a fraction of the variance of the underlying process, the remainder of which enters the model as noise. The ratios in Table 1 additionally ignore collinearity among symptom items, which inflates coefficient standard errors, and nonstationarity, which reduces the number of transitions informative about any single parameter value below the number nominally available. From a statistical learning perspective, this creates severe identifiability pressure regardless of which estimation strategy ends up being used, and has been referred to as low statistical power in a network neuroscience context [44]. Crucially, the standard validation approach in the idiographic network literature does not address this problem. Model fit is generally assessed via in-sample criteria such as SEM fit indices (e.g. RMSEA, CFI, [12]). However, these are in-sample criteria quantifying how well a model accounts for the data used to fit it, but provide no evidence that the recovered structure generalizes to new observations. A principled alternative is to evaluate models using out-of-sample forecasting, treating the time series as a sequential prediction problem and asking how well the model can predict the next observation(s) given past values.

A recent study by Fechtelpeter et al. [13] provides the first comprehensive EMA forecasting benchmark. Comparing naive baselines, linear network models (VAR, Kalman filtering), and nonlinear approaches such as recurrent neural network (RNNs) and Transformers across three 40-day micro-randomized trials (N = 145), they found that VAR and Kalman models performed significantly worse than simple baselines such as last-observation-carried-forward, with only nonlinear piecewise-linear recurrent neural networks (PLRNNs) marginally outperforming those baselines 1.

In this work, we systematically extend this validation logic across four publicly available EMA datasets that have been used to make substantive network-theoretic claims [18; 12; 16; 17]. We also provide simulation studies generating synthetic EMA data with known ground-truth networks, confirming whether the failure in forecasting performance also links to the failure in recovering ground truth network parameters.

The system thus switches connectivity as the latent state crosses ReLU boundaries.

4.4 Model Evaluation

4.4.1 Expanding Window Forecasting

Model performance was evaluated using an expanding window design to simulate prospective prediction, based on [13]. Let T s denote the total number of observations for participant s. Beginning with a minimum training period of T min observations (e.g., 4 days × 4 measurements/day = 16 observations), we first fit the model on observations {1, . . . , T train }, 2) generate a one-step-ahead forecast ŷTtrain+1 , 3) expand the training window: T train ← T train + ∆T , 4) repeat until T train + 1 > T s . Here, ∆T is the expansion increment (e.g., 4 observations = 1 day). This procedure yields multiple forecast evaluations per participant while respecting temporal ordering.
