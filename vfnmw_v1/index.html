Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Misinformation and conspiracy theories are often treated as interchangeable, but they have different predictors and respond differently to correction. We identify their differences at the level of the claim itself rather than at what these claims trigger in the mind of the believer. Whereas misinformation claims are, in principle, assessable against a ground truth, conspiracy theories are claims wrapped in an explanation for why evidence is absent or untrustworthy. This semantic criterion avoids conflating bare falsehoods with conspiracy theories while preserving the distinctive structural features of the latter. We show how the wrapper grants conspiracy theories a degree of immunity from disconfirmation and why a claim-level demarcation can inform both theory and measurement.

[div-01]

In July 2026, ahead of the US midterm elections, President Donald J. Trump (once again, Jacobson, 2023; Lewandowsky, Lloyd, & Brophy, 2018) raised concerns about electoral integrity, claiming that FBI files revealed "evidence of alleged fraud by a large-scale voter registration operation in Michigan", and that "even when significant evidence of fraud has been detected, it has been buried and covered up". 1Most readers would label these two assertions as misinformation, conspiracy theories, or both. But which are they? And if officials were involved in the concealment, could evidence ever confirm or reject them?

These questions expose two related problems in research on misinformation and conspiracy theories (CTs), whose definitions are fuzzy, and so are their boundaries (Scantamburlo & Zollo, 2026). For example, in a systematic review of 148 studies on CTs published between 2007 and 2020, Mahl et al. (2022) found that 72.3% referred to misinformation and CTs interchangeably without explicitly defining or differentiating them, and that nearly half (43.9%) invoked several terms simultaneously without delineation. Without a settled definition and criteria for demarcation, operationalizations will inevitably vary, and so will their measurement and estimated prevalence.

In this chapter we attempt to resolve both problems, at least in part, by demarcating misinformation and CTs at the level of claims. Our demarcation rests on a single criterion: a claim's relationship to truth, as determined by its structure and content, deliberately setting aside the beliefs and motives of those who endorse them. We suspect that much of the definitional confusion has arisen because the pragmatic interpretation of claims (what a claim suggests to its audience in context, as opposed to what it actually states) has been absorbed into the definitions themselves. Our demarcation therefore deliberately shifts from a pragmatic to a semantic reading: we classify claims by what they assert, not by what they may lead an audience to infer.

Consider the claims made by President Trump. Taken pragmatically, both imply a conspiracy: that a large-scale, organized operation fraudulently registered voters in Michigan, and that government officials deliberately buried the evidence. Yet the two are structurally different. The first is a factual, checkable claim about the scale and nature of the fraud: taken on its own, it is falsifiable and correctable. 2 The second adds an account of why the first cannot be checked: because a successful concealment leaves no detectable trace, the assertion now explains the very absence of evidence that would otherwise count against it. CTs are thus distinguished from misinformation by this additional content, which, wrapped around a factual claim, makes the overall assertion resistant to disconfirmation: neither missing nor contrary evidence can refute it.

1 Defining misinformation

In a review of 410 empirical studies on misinformation, Scantamburlo and Zollo (2026) found that 39% offer no conceptual definition at all and 10% lack both a conceptual and an operational one; among those that did define the term, definitions varied substantially across disciplines. Different definitions shape what gets counted as misinformation. Stricter vs. broader definitions (e.g., including hyperpartisan content) yield different estimates of the scale of the problem, and have produced two opposed research positions ("minimizing" vs. "alarmist"; see Ecker et al., 2024). A recent EU expert report, however, reframes them as a false dichotomy, noting that the most consequential harm stems from accuratebut-misleading content and elite rhetoric, which are harder to verify (Scharfbillig et al., 2026).

As an umbrella term, misinformation generally includes information that is inaccurate, false, accurate but misleading, or unsubstantiated (Ecker et al., 2022; Scantamburlo & Zollo, 2026). Each of these cases represents a different way a claim relates to a ground truth that is based on the best available evidence from relevant experts at the time.

The first two cases, inaccuracy and falsity, are precision failures to correspond to truth. A claim such as "the population of the USA in 2026 is 320 million people" is inaccurate relative to the official 2026 figure of 340 million, without being blatantly false in the categorical sense. Sufficiently large errors, however, become categorical false claims, such as "the Earth is flat" and "vaccines cause autism". The third case, misleadingness, refers to claims that are not technically false or imprecise yet still produce a false impression in the audience. For example, a Chicago Tribune headline reporting that a healthy doctor died two weeks after receiving a COVID-19 vaccine was factually accurate, yet implied a causal link that had not been established (Allen et al., 2024).

Misleadingness also includes cherry-picking, which consists in selectively citing a factually true claim to support a conclusion that the full evidence would not warrant. In climate-change denial, for example, the claim that "global tem-peratures fell from 2016 to 2018" is not technically false, yet, stripped of context, it creates a false impression about the long-term trend (Cook, 2020). Similarly deceptive are claims so vague that they are uninformative rather than false, such as Barnum statements and pseudo-profound bullshit. Common in mental-health misinformation (Starvaggi et al., 2024), they mislead by framing universally applicable descriptions as if they were individually diagnostic ("if you sometimes find it hard to say no, it may be a trauma response").

Unsubstantiation, another form of misinformation, differs from the previous three in that it lies beyond the true/false dichotomy. An unsubstantiated claim cannot be checked against a ground truth because the relevant evidence has not yet been gathered. A claim such as "placing a halved onion in a room prevents the flu" is neither false nor true, because no evidence exists. Unsubstantiated claims vary widely in prior plausibility (compare the onion claim with the claim that there is an onion in one's neighbor's kitchen), but unsubstantiation is not a permanent condition: evidence can in principle be collected (by checking the kitchen, or by testing onions in clinical trials), and once it exists, the claim can be assessed against the truth.

All these claims are truth-apt: whatever their specific relationship with truth, each can be assessed against a ground truth and is therefore falsifiable. The epistemic gap they create can, in principle, be closed by evidence: a false claim can be replaced with a true one, an inaccurate figure can be updated, a misleading framing can be explained by supplying context, and an unsubstantiated claim can wait for evidence. In each case, nothing in the claim itself prevents evidence from being gathered and weighed. This, as we argue below, is the sharpest difference from CTs.

2 Defining conspiracy theories

As with misinformation, no shared consensus exists on what CTs actually are, and different operationalizations shape what gets counted as a CT. The LOCO corpus (Miani et al., 2021), for example, includes webpages on topics popularly associated with CTs, such as Bigfoot and the deaths of Elvis Presley and Princess Diana. The problem is that it is not obvious what makes these topics CTs rather than merely unsubstantiated stories (Uscinski, 2025). When a theory-grounded definition was applied, prevalence estimates dropped: fewer than two-thirds of Sandy Hook school shooting conspiratorial documents in LOCO (Mompelat et al., 2022) and only one-third of posts in conspiracy-related Reddit forums (Diab et al., 2024) were then labeled as CTs.

Research into the psychology of CTs faces a similar measurement problem. Aside from two notable exceptions that separate the two constructs (Piliousis & Gardikiotis, 2026; Zwolinski, 2024), CTs and misinformation are often conflated. For example, misinformation scales include conspiratorial items (e.g., "Government Officials Have Manipulated Stock Prices to Hide Scandals", Maertens et al., 2023), and CT inventories include unsubstantiated claims without a clear conspiracy allegation (e.g., "Do you believe aliens exist, or not?", Jensen, 2013).

Many definitions of CTs exist and vary substantially in their scope and focus (see e.g., those listed in Samory and Mitra, 2018 and Nefes et al., 2026). A widely cited definition of CTs is that of Douglas et al. (2019), partially borrowed from Keeley (1999), according to whom CTs are "attempts to explain the ultimate causes of significant social and political events and circumstances with claims of secret plots by two or more powerful actors". Later definitions extended the focus from "specific events" to "aspect(s) of reality" (Nera & Schöpfer, 2023), defining CTs as beliefs that "two or more actors have coordinated in secret to achieve an outcome and that their conspiracy is of public interest but not public knowledge" (Douglas & Sutton, 2023, p. 12).

An important disagreement in these definitions concerns the truth value. Some define CTs as false, unnecessary, or unfalsifiable and/or unsubstantiated (see e.g., Brotherton et al., 2013; Sunstein & Vermeule, 2009). Others point out that, as theories, CTs are by definition allegations that may or may not be true (Douglas et al., 2019), awaiting evidence from better-equipped epistemic bodies (Uscinski, 2025). Most experts (79% of 150 surveyed), nevertheless, agree that CTs are a form of misinformation, broadly defined as "false and misleading information" (Altay et al., 2023). Yet some philosophers argue that CTs are not prima facie unlikely and should be evaluated case by case, because some are warranted (e.g., Dentith, 2016). These positions have fueled an ongoing debate between psychologists and philosophers over whether endorsing CTs is irrational (see, e.g., Lewandowsky et al., 2020; Wagner-Egger et al., 2019). Yet, as we argue next, the narrative content of CTs gives them properties that make them highly resistant to disconfirmation and capable of explaining any event.

3 Immunity and portability of conspiracy theories

As Keeley (1999) observed, unlike science, which studies an object indifferent to being measured (e.g., nature), CTs describe a domain whose object of inves-tigation actively obstructs the investigation: "Imagine if neutrinos were not simply hard to detect, but actively sought to avoid detection!" (Keeley, 1999, p. 120). Because a conspiracy is a covert action whose success depends on remaining undiscovered, a perfectly functioning conspiracy leaves no trace, and the absence of evidence is read as a sign of its success. A claim that "there is no conspiracy" is itself read as yet another failure to discover the plot. Contrary evidence is read in the same way: it is reinterpreted as fabricated by the conspirators and, once discredited, leaves the claim with no evidence at all. Missing and contrary evidence are thus rendered by the conspiracy as nondiagnostic, and any claim can be shielded from disconfirmation (or made selfsealing) by wrapping it in a conspiratorial layer that explains both why the evidence for it is missing and why evidence against it cannot be trusted (Nera et al., 2026). We call this layer the conspiratorial wrapper. Figure 1 illustrates the wrapper with the unsubstantiated claim that "placing a halved onion in a room prevents the flu". Read as a bare claim, the absence of evidence ("no clinical evidence exists") and the existence of contrary evidence ("clinical studies show no effect beyond placebo") leave it either unsubstantiated or refuted. Once it is wrapped in the allegation that "Big Pharma buries the evidence for remedies it cannot patent", both evidential states are neutralized: they no longer count against the claim. Because conspiratorial allegations are not constrained by the evidence, they have potentially unbounded degrees of freedom: the wrapper can be applied to any event or aspect of reality, and, for each, it can support multiple, even mutually incompatible, hypotheses accounting for the absence of evidence for the claim it serves. In Figure 1, both evidential states yield the same conclusion that the plot is working; whether scientists are complicit or threatened does not matter, because these logically incompatible hypotheses are rendered compatible by the overarching assumption that the truth is being concealed (Lewandowsky, Cook, & Lloyd, 2018; Nera & Schöpfer, 2023; Nera & Sutton, 2025). In fact, the endorsement of mutually incompatible hypotheses has been reported as a hallmark of CTs (Lewandowsky, Cook, & Lloyd, 2018; Miani, Cruz, & Lewandowsky, 2026; Wood et al., 2012), and the effect appears conspiracyspecific: logically incompatible explanations for fictitious events that lack an explicit conspiratorial wrapper do not elicit mutual endorsement (Miani, Cruz, & Lewandowsky, 2026), suggesting that it is the concealment itself that neutralizes any evidential status.

The onion conspiracy shown in Figure 1, for example, can extend beyond Big Pharma and scientists, incorporating other actors such as instrument manufacturers, government agencies, and the media. Once the conspiracy extends to such a large network of actors, it can be used as a template to explain other wrongdoings. From the concealed curative properties of onions, to Big Pharma's involvement in the development of the coronavirus, and the CIA's role in the JFK assassination, each CT can serve as evidence for other CTs, even if topically unrelated. Indeed, conspiratorial texts accumulate topically unrelated but mutually reinforcing "evidence", linking diverse actors, events, and domains within broader explanatory frameworks (Miani et al., 2022; Tangherlini et al., 2020; Tuters & Willaert, 2022). A similar pattern emerges at the individual level: beliefs in different CTs are positively correlated, and this pattern is better captured by a network of mutually reinforcing beliefs (Williams et al., 2022).

Narrative analyses show that CTs follow a stable template: a small set of narrative roles (e.g., the conspirators, their hidden plan, and their victims) that can be filled with almost any actor or event (Tangherlini et al., 2020; Tuters & Willaert, 2022). For example, Samory and Mitra (2018) found that a few narrative motifs (e.g., "a governmental agency controls communications") recur across otherwise unrelated topics (e.g., the 9/11 attacks, the JFK assassination), while a single event can be framed through different motifs. Anti-vaccination narratives reveal an analogous template, with recurring slots for actors (e.g., Big Pharma), actions (e.g., distributing harmful vaccines), and targets (e.g., children; Introne et al., 2020).

Yet, although topically diverse, conspiratorial discourse relies on a recogniz-able linguistic style. Its vocabulary centers on deception, dominance, power, aggression, and death, with ingroup-outgroup framing, negative emotions, and rhetorical questions (Klein et al., 2019; Miani et al., 2021); phrases such as "the truth is hidden" and "it's a cover-up" are a staple (Recordare et al., 2024). Accordingly, conspiratorial (vs. mainstream) texts are more lexically similar to one another (Miani et al., 2022).

4 A demarcation based on the relationship to truth

Any topic can generate both misinformation and CTs. Unlike CTs, however, misinformation often presents as a bare factual claim, with no recognizable structure that separates a false claim from a true one (e.g., "the Earth is flat" vs. "the Earth is a geoid"). Linguistic markers of misinformation have been identified, but results are mixed (see e.g., Lebernegg et al., 2024). More recognizable are the rhetorical techniques that often accompany misinformation (e.g., emotionally manipulative language, false dichotomies), and learning to spot them improves resilience against misinformation (Roozenbeek et al., 2022). However they manipulate a claim, these techniques do not alter its relationship to truth. CTs, by contrast, are embedded in a specific narrative structure that not only makes them highly recognizable but also shapes their relationship to truth.

The structure of CTs grants them a heightened resistance to disconfirmation. Any strongly held belief resists disconfirmation (Boudry & Braeckman, 2010; Nera et al., 2026), for instance through ad hoc hypotheses introduced after the fact ("prayers did not heal because you did not believe enough"). In CTs, however, that resistance is built into the claim itself: the wrapper states in advance why evidence is missing and why contrary evidence cannot be trusted (whereas the prayer excuse is added only after the failure). Although missing and contrary evidence can still accumulate and make a CT progressively less probable (Grimes, 2016), contrary evidence cannot refute the CT conclusively.

CTs share with unsubstantiated misinformation one trait: the absence of decisive evidence. It is therefore tempting to treat CTs as a sub-class of misinformation. The key difference is in how the epistemic gap can be closed. In misinformation, evidence closes the gap because the claim can be either confirmed or disconfirmed. In CTs, instead, the wrapper renders the absence of evidence non-diagnostic, so the gap stays open.

Note that the wrapper makes the claim resistant to disconfirmation, but not to confirmation. Under a taxonomy based on the relationship to truth, CTs are therefore a distinct class and cannot be included in misinformation. 4 The contrast is sharpest when two claims share an identical factual core: (A) the Earth is flat (B) the Earth is flat and space agencies have faked the evidence Claim (A) is misinformation: it says how the Earth is shaped, and it can be checked against the evidence (it is false).

Claim (B) adds the wrapper, which says why that evidence is not found.

The problem we try to solve is that, on a pragmatic reading, (A) and (B) are equally false and equally conspiratorial. Both stand so far from the available evidence that neither can be held unless one also assumes that the evidence has been corrupted. But if we allow (A) into the class of CTs, then the same classification applies to any claim contradicted by the evidence, such as "homeopathy works", whose acceptance likewise requires assuming that available evidence cannot be trusted. Such a pragmatic inference would thus turn every false or unsubstantiated claim into a CT, and perpetuate the very conflation documented at the start of this chapter.

We avoid this conflation by applying a semantic (vs. pragmatic) reading, thus requiring the wrapper to be present in the claim. In doing so, we prevent bare misinformation ("homeopathy works") from being conflated with a CT ("homeopathy works, and Big Pharma buries the evidence for remedies it cannot patent"). This literal reading also spares us from inferring the beliefs a claim invites, which are external to the claim itself and of little use in a claim-based taxonomy. Note, however, that one limitation of this approach is that restricting the analysis to what claims assert leaves misleadingness outside its reach (e.g., cherry-picking: "global temperatures fell from 2016 to 2018"), since misleadingness is by definition a contextual property.

5 A misinformation-aware definition of conspiracy theories

We suggested a demarcation based on the structural properties of claims: misinformation is a bare factual claim, whereas a CT is a bare claim embedded in a conspiratorial wrapper. We therefore define a conspiracy theory as: a claim wrapped in an explicit allegation that a conspiracy (a secretly coordinated deception against the public interest) accounts for the absence of evidence This definition is compositional: a CT is the conjunction of a bare claim and the conspiratorial wrapper. Without the wrapper, the claim may be false or unsubstantiated, but not a CT. Each component has its own evidential status: 𝐶 = the bare claim (e.g., "homeopathy works") and 𝑊 = the wrapper, i.e., the allegation of concealment (e.g., "Big Pharma buries the evidence for remedies it cannot patent"). 𝐶 may stand in any relation to the evidence, and so may 𝑊: the wrapper can be more or less substantiated (e.g., by documented past cover-ups in the same industry), but as long as decisive evidence is missing, its role in the CT is the same. Because 𝑊 states in advance why evidence for 𝐶 is missing and why evidence against it cannot be trusted, the CT as a whole (𝐶 + 𝑊) becomes resistant to disconfirmation, though not to confirmation.

From this compositional logic, it follows that a wrapper alone is a generic allegation of concealment with no specific claim to be shielded. This logic parallels existing conspiracy scales adding content along a continuum of specificity. For example, generic conspiracist scales largely consist of bare wrappers (Imhoff, 2026), which explain why information is missing without naming what is missing or who is hiding it (e.g., "the official version of the events given by the authorities very often hides the truth", Lantian et al., 2016; "many very important things happen in the world, which the public is never informed about", Bruder et al., 2013). Moving towards higher specificity, the wrapper names an agent and the kind of act it conceals, but still wraps a recurring pattern rather than a specific claim or event (e.g., "the government is involved in the murder of innocent citizens and/or well-known public figures, and keeps this a secret", Brotherton et al., 2013). At the specific end of the continuum, the wrapper is attached to a bare claim about a particular event, which yields a CT in our sense (e.g., "in July 1947, the US military recovered the wreckage of an alien craft from Roswell, New Mexico, and covered up the fact", Swami et al., 2010). In short, a bare claim may be misinformation, a bare wrapper expresses a generic conspiracist belief, and only their conjunction makes a CT.

Our definition requires the wrapper to be explicitly asserted (vs. inferred), keeping classification at the level of claims rather than beliefs and preventing the pragmatic conflation discussed above. Some formulations imply concealment without asserting it. In "the industry doesn't want you to know", a preference for secrecy does not entail that anyone can, or did, actually hide the truth. Under a semantic reading, such formulations are borderline weak wrappers; where the threshold lies is, in our view, an empirical question.

In our definition, the wrapper accounts for the absence of evidence; this is what distinguishes CTs from documented accounts of conspiracies (hereafter, documented conspiracies). Both are claims, but only in CTs does the concealment allegation explain why decisive evidence is missing. When the concealment fails, as in documented conspiracies, and decisive evidence emerges, the allegation no longer explains any absence, and the concealment itself becomes part of what is documented. In 1972, a claim that the White House was covering up its involvement in the Watergate break-in was a CT but once the tapes were released, the CT became a documented conspiracy. Because the wrapper blocks disconfirmation but not confirmation, this is how a CT typically leaves the class: by being confirmed rather than refuted.

Note that among CTs, claims can also be more or less substantiated. A laboratory origin of SARS-CoV-2 is biologically possible and has received serious scientific scrutiny, even though the available evidence favors a natural origin (Lewandowsky et al., 2023); claims about reptilian elites have no such standing. Both, however, are CTs whenever they include a wrapper accounting for the absence of decisive evidence (e.g., that the laboratory's records were concealed).

6 Asymmetries between bare and wrapped claims

Our demarcation predicts a sharp asymmetry in how misinformation and CTs respond to correction. Because misinformation is truth-apt, it creates an epistemic gap that correction can, in principle, close. By contrast, the conspiratorial wrapper keeps that gap open because it explains away disconfirming information in advance. Moreover, the high portability of CTs predicts that debunking itself can be recruited into the conspiracy narrative, filling one of its open slots and absorbing debunkers and fact-checkers as accomplices (Poth & Dolega, 2023; Sunstein & Vermeule, 2009). This idea, however, remains untested. What is empirically supported, instead, is a broader asymmetry: CTs are substantially harder to correct than misinformation (though not impossible; Costello et al., 2024). Directly negating a CT is among the least effective interventions documented in the literature (O'Mahony et al., 2023), and the meta-analytic effect size of CT correction is smaller than that of misinformation correction (Stasielowicz, 2025).

We believe this asymmetry rests on the semantic difference between bare and wrapped claims. Piliousis and Gardikiotis (2026) paired climate-change items with explicit conspiracy content with misinformation items. Conspiracy items were predicted by conspiracy-related constructs such as powerlessness, anthropomorphism, anomie, whereas misinformation items were predicted by political ideology alone. Zwolinski (2024) manipulated misinformation and conspiratorial claims across matched topics and delivered a direct factual correction; after debunking, endorsement of misinformation claims dropped, while endorsement of conspiratorial claims remained unchanged.

If a conspiracy is inferred from a bare misinformation claim, as a pragmatic reading would suggest, bare and wrapped claims should behave alike, because the wrapper would be implied in both. Yet, as these studies show, they have different predictors and respond differently to correction (Piliousis & Gardikiotis, 2026; Zwolinski, 2024). Indeed, denying that climate change is occurring does not necessarily imply a conspiracy: one may simply give more weight to the memory of a cool summer than to scientists' warnings; yet endorsing the claim that the mainstream climate narrative is a malicious plan signals a commitment to both the claim and its wrapper. These findings suggest that respondents do not, by default, read a conspiracy into bare claims.

Further, though indirect, support for this view comes from one of our studies (Miani, Adornetti, et al., 2026), in which participants described the events depicted in a movie ambiguous enough to leave questions unanswered and to invite a conspiratorial interpretation. When their narratives were scored for conspiratorial content using large language models (LLMs), participants higher in conspiracy mentality did not write more conspiratorial accounts, suggesting that, absent an explicit wrapper, even those predisposed to conspiracism did not read a conspiracy into the events.

The distinction between bare and wrapped claims also appears in how LLMs themselves handle content. Bates et al. (2026) prompted six LLMs to rewrite fact-checked false claims and snippets from conspiracy articles in a journalistic style. They found that LLMs were more likely to rewrite the conspiratorial snippets (e.g., "Concerns have been articulated regarding the…") than the fact-checked false claims, which were more often deflected or rebutted (e.g., "There is no credible evidence to support claims that…"). This pattern is tentatively consistent with the structural distinction drawn above: LLM safety mechanisms were less often engaged by conspiratorial framing, possibly because conspiratorial content explains away the absence of evidence rather than asserting a false but testable claim.

7 Testable predictions

The lack of a shared taxonomy of misinformation and CTs has repercussions for both detection and psychometric tools. Testing the wrapper as a core feature of CTs, along with other definitional features (Douglas & Sutton, 2023), is therefore a warranted empirical effort. For example, not all existing conspiracy scales include an explicit wrapper (e.g., "SARS was produced under laboratory conditions as a biological weapon"; Swami et al., 2010). Researchers could cross the presence of the bare claim (𝐶; "vaccines kill"), the bare wrapper (𝑊; "evidence is suppressed"), and their conjunction (𝐶 +𝑊; "evidence that vaccines kill is suppressed"). Such a design (an improvement from the unmatched items in Piliousis and Gardikiotis, 2026) would allow researchers to test whether the wrapper changes endorsement of the same claim, and whether it improves the content and criterion validity of existing scales.

Because a CT is, by our definition, the conjunction of a claim and a wrapper, items measuring CTs are necessarily double-barreled. This is usually considered a methodological flaw, since agreement with 𝐶 + 𝑊 does not reveal which component is endorsed. Yet double-barreled CT items are already common in existing scales (e.g., "In July 1947, the US military recovered the wreckage of an alien craft from Roswell, New Mexico, and covered up the fact"; Swami et al., 2010), and they are unavoidable if CTs, rather than their components, are to be measured. In a fully crossed design, the double-barreled item becomes informative: comparing it with its components separates endorsement of a CT (𝐶 + 𝑊) from endorsement of a bare claim (𝐶) and from generic conspiracist worldview (𝑊).

Paired stimuli can also extend research on motivated cognition. When a belief is challenged, a believer can either engage with the counter-evidence or dismiss its source by recruiting a conspiratorial explanation. In one study (Lewandowsky, 2021), endorsement of conspiratorial explanations of the scientific consensus (in our terms, wrappers) tracked dispositional conspiracism for vaccine safety but political worldview for climate change, consistent with conspiracies being recruited to sidestep an inconvenient fact. Experimentally manipulating ideological threat would test this mechanism causally and shed light on potential conspiratorial backfire effects. For example, after debunking homeopathy, a believer's endorsement of a wrapped claim ("medical experts silence homeopathy's healing effects because homeopathy threatens their power") is expected to increase relative to its bare counterpart ("homeopathy stimulates a healing response"). This increase should occur only after corrections of personally relevant beliefs (e.g., not after "octopuses have three hearts"), reflecting the deployment of a "conspiracy for convenience" (Lewandowsky, 2021).

8 Conclusion

The goal of this chapter was to separate CTs from misinformation, two concepts that are often used interchangeably in research and measurement. We believe the problem of such confusion lies in the pragmatic interpretation of claims. We therefore pitched our demarcation at the level of claims rather than beliefs, on a single criterion: a claim's relationship to truth, as determined by its structure and content. In misinformation, a class of truth-apt claims, the epistemic gap can be closed by evidence. In CTs, instead, the content itself explains why the evidence is missing and reduces any contrary evidence to that same absence, making the claim resistant to disconfirmation and, with it, to correction. Research on interventions supports this distinction. By separating the two, we provided a misinformation-aware definition of CTs, which also distinguishes CTs from documented conspiracies without stipulating that CTs are false: a claim stops being a CT once decisive evidence emerges and its wrapper no longer accounts for any absence. This definition is empirically testable and opens new methodological possibilities.

A final note is warranted on the near-invincible explanatory power of CTs. That CTs render contrary evidence non-diagnostic does not mean that they have the same epistemic status as more plausible explanations, including the accounts they oppose. Resistance to disconfirmation does not mean probably true. In fact, CTs are epistemically more demanding than their non-conspiratorial counterparts. A conspiracy claim does not stand alone but drags with it a system of auxiliary hypotheses (Lewandowsky, Lloyd, & Brophy, 2018; Wagner-Egger et al., 2019), each of which must hold for the theory to survive. The number of people needed to be kept silent makes large-scale conspiracies intrinsically prone to failure (Grimes, 2016), further lowering their prior probability. In short, although a CT can rarely be conclusively refuted, it is, in practice, far less likely to be true than a simpler explanation.
