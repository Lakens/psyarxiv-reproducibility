Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

We derive an exact, sharp lower bound for Hartigan's dip on lattice-supported questionnaire scores and characterise two further identification limits on latent-distribution shape. If 𝑞 (2) is the mass of the second-largest score atom, then 𝐷 ≥ 𝑞 (2) /2. Across nine public collections (2.48 million records; 48 score series), all 48 reject under the diptest calibration at a common 𝑛 = 12 000; 28 lie on the atom floor to four decimals, 34 are within one per cent, and the floor alone crosses the rejection threshold for all 48, with median sample-size crossing 𝑛 * = 539. After within-cell perturbation removes the lattice, 47 series reject in none of 100 replications and one in all. Latent modality is also not invariant under the strictly increasing reparametrisations left free by a nonparametric item-response model; a five-standard-deviation mixture can be transformed to pass the dip test without changing ranks. Finally, fixing a metric does not identify the latent law: under a continuous finite item-response design, a relative-interior observable admits both atomic and atomless mixing distributions. Fisherinformation arc length supplies an item-anchored coordinate, but finite-design identified uncertainty remains.

1 Introduction

An attribute is measured by a questionnaire; the responses are added; the total is examined for structure. Two modes separated by a valley are reported as evidence of two kinds of respondent, one mode as evidence of a continuum. The question is pursued continually: on autistic traits (Frazier et al., 2023), on personality types (Gerlach et al., 2018), and across the psychopathology literature reviewed by Haslam et al. (2012 Haslam et al. ( , 2020)). Those studies use a range of procedures, most of which lie outside the scope of the results below. The inference this paper concerns is narrower: Hartigan's dip applied directly to a questionnaire total, with its rejection read as evidence of distinct latent subgroups, as in Abu-Akel et al. (for example 2019).

We show that this inference is blocked by three distinct limits: the support of the observed score, the freedom to reparametrise the latent coordinate, and the non-singleton identified set left by a finite item design. None is repaired by collecting more respondents. Equivalently, they are three identification requirements for a defensible modality claim: the observable must not make the test reject mechanically, the latent metric must make shape well defined, and the measurement design must identify the feature being interpreted.

One line of research on latent kinds has used tests of distributional shape, whereas psychometric identification theory asks which properties of a latent variable are determined by an item response model.

The two literatures meet at the question considered here: before a mode, a gap or a class is interpreted substantively, both the response format of the observable and the identifying conventions of the latent scale have to be specified. Nonparametric modality inference (Hartigan & Hartigan, 1985; Müller & Sawitzki, 1991; Silverman, 1981) is a theory about samples from distributions the analyst observes directly; the identification results (Lord, 1975; Ramsay, 1996; Rossi et al., 2002; Allman et al., 2009) concern what a finite item set determines about a variable nobody observes. Neither supplies the object in between.

1.1 Positioning and contribution

The paper's distinctive contribution is concentrated in the first link and in what the links establish together. Proposition 1 gives an exact, sharp lower bound for the dip on an arbitrary atomic distribution, identifies the second-largest atom as the binding quantity, and yields a sample-size crossing diagnostic that can be read directly from score counts. In the empirical corpus the bound is not a small correction: 28 of 48 score series sit on it to four decimals, 34 lie within one per cent, and at the common sample size the floor alone crosses the test calibration for every series. The remaining results show why moving from the total to an estimated latent density does not, by itself, rescue the intended inference.

The scale indeterminacy behind that second step is classical. Lord (1975) showed that an itemresponse model leaves the latent scale free under common increasing transformations; Ramsay (1996) gave the corresponding geometry in response-probability space, developed computationally by Rossi et al. (2002). We use that result to make the consequence for modality explicit, not to claim a new reparametrisation theorem. The finite-design geometry is classical in the same sense: manifest probabilities in latent-trait models were characterised by Cressie & Holland (1983) and Holland (1990), while the convex-hull and finite-support results are mixture geometry due to Lindsay (1983 Lindsay ( , 1995)). General truncated-moment results give still broader non-uniqueness and absolutely continuous representations (di Dio, 2019 (di Dio, , 2025)). The contribution here is to put these pieces on one item-response observable map and separate two distinct questions: whether latent shape is well defined in a coordinate, and whether the resulting feature is identified by a finite design. This fixed-𝐽 question is different from the 𝐽 → ∞ identification results of Douglas (2001), He (2024) and Grønneberg (2026), and connects naturally to partial-identification work in psychometrics (Grønneberg et al., 2020; Moss & Grønneberg, 2023).

None of this disputes what sum scores can establish about ordering. Monotonicity conditions can make a sum score stochastically order respondents with respect to a latent trait (Grayson, 1988; Hemker et al., 1997; Van der Ark, 2005), and a strictly increasing reparametrisation preserves such ordering while changing distributional shape. The results are therefore compatible with the recent defence of sum scoring on ordinal and predictive grounds (Sijtsma et al., 2024); the target here is the use of score-distribution shape as evidence about latent shape. Likewise, restricted latent-class and latent-trait models can be compared when their restrictions make their manifest sets differ (Haertel, 1990; Markon & Krueger, 2006); our claim concerns the unrestricted mixing law conditional on a common response curve.

For the dip itself, ties and discreteness have long been recognised as practical issues (Hartigan & Hartigan, 1985; Henderson et al., 2008; Johnsson et al., 2017). The diptest documentation records 1/(2𝑛) as the minimum in its small-sample and discontinuity discussion; for an empirical distribution with 𝑛 distinct values, Proposition 1 yields the same half-atom floor because all atoms have mass 1/𝑛 (Maechler & Ringach, 2025). The proposition generalises that mechanism to arbitrary atomic masses, identifies the second-largest atom as the binding quantity, and establishes sharpness. Recent Z-Dip work separately addresses sample-size-dependent calibration and the increasing sensitivity of the classical dip test to fixed non-null departures, standardising the statistic relative to its continuous-null distribution (Di Martino et al., 2025). Such standardisation does not remove a strictly positive population dip induced by lattice support. Corollary 2 therefore combines a familiar consistency phenomenon with the structural fact supplied by Proposition 1: a finite score distribution with at least two positive-mass values has a population dip bounded away from zero.

1.2 The argument in four steps

Each step closes an escape route the previous one opens.

One. Applying the dip directly to a questionnaire total confounds the intended shape question with the total's lattice support (Section 2). The obvious escape route is to estimate the latent distribution instead, as Woods & Thissen (2006), Woods & Lin (2009) and Li & Lee (2026)

do.

Two. That is not sufficient: the shape of an estimated latent distribution is a property of the coordinate in which it is written, and an unrestricted item-response model does not select that coordinate (Section 3). The next escape route is to anchor the coordinate.

Three. An item set can supply such an anchor, for example through information arc length. Fixing the coordinate removes the reparametrisation equivalence class, but leaves a second one: distinct mixing distributions can have the same barycentre on the now-fixed response curve (Section 3).

Four. The atomic part of a mixing law survives the reparametrisations of step two, but at every relativeinterior observable of a continuous finite response curve the same manifest distribution admits both atomic and atomless representations. At fixed finite 𝐽, more respondents estimate the same observable more precisely without selecting a member of this fibre (Section 4).

Remark 2 (Atomicity and the taxonic question). A taxon in Meehl's sense is a class of respondents alike in kind. 7 Discussion

What conclusions about latent kinds and continua remain defensible once response format, parametrisation and finite measurement are accounted for? The three limits above can be read positively as identification requirements: they state what an analysis must supply before a claim about latent modality has a well-defined target and support in the observable data.

A dip rejection on a scale score is not evidence for latent classes. By Corollary 2 the rejection probability tends to one as the sample grows for every nondegenerate finite score law satisfying its premise, irrespective of latent modality; by Section 6.1 it is what all 48 score series produce, what 47 cease to produce once the lattice is removed, and what the calibrated crossing diagnostic leads one to expect for every one of them at a median of 539 respondents. The bound is computable from the score distribution without fitting a model, so a researcher can check directly whether the lattice lower bound by itself forces rejection under the test calibration, and separately how close the observed dip lies to that floor.

Modality of a latent density is meaningful only relative to a stated metric. Theorem 3 is not a caution about estimation error: the class of models observationally equivalent to any given one contains members whose latent density has any modality one specifies, with every respondent in the same rank position. A reported bimodality is a joint statement about the population and about a coordinate the analyst chose, usually by adopting a parametric link without comment.

An item-anchored coordinate can make shape well defined, but does not restore identification. Once the item-response functions are specified, an item-anchored coordinate (Fisher-information arc length here, surprisal-based alternatives elsewhere (Wallmark & Wiberg, 2026)) is invariant to smooth increasing reparametrisation of the latent coordinate; the cost is that the resulting statement is a property of the pair (attribute, item set) rather than an instrument-free population shape. Fixing it does not make the latent distribution identified. A researcher may assume a finite latent-class model and, under suitable rank/category conditions, identify its mixing proportions and class-specific response probabilities; what the unrestricted data will not do, at a relative-interior observable covered by Proposition 5, is adjudicate the finite-class assumption.

A finite instrument identifies only a set of latent distributions. This is where the practical message departs from the usual one: At fixed finite design and manifest distribution, identified uncertainty about a latent-distribution feature need not vanish as the respondent sample grows. Reducing it requires a more informative observable design or additional model restrictions, not merely more respondents.

Increasing the sample from 12 000 to 1.02 million estimates the observable distribution more precisely, but does not by itself shrink the fibre or the identified range; what distinguishes the designs in Online Resource 1, Appendix E is their length and response format.

Therefore a claim about latent classes requires assumptions, design information and sensitivity analysis, not a large sample and a shape test. For a dip analysis of a questionnaire score, a minimum report is the lattice floor 𝑞 (2) /2, the ratio 𝐷 𝑛 /{ 𝑞 (2) /2}, and whether the floor alone forces rejection under the stated calibration. A claim about latent shape additionally has to state the response model and latent metric that make shape meaningful, and distinguish assumptions that fix the scale from assumptions or design information that identify the mixing law.

Two scope restrictions bear directly on how these conclusions should be used. Proposition 1 is about the dip statistic, and the covariance-based procedures accounting for most of the corpus reviewed by Haslam et al. (2020) are not addressed; their behaviour on lattice-supported inputs is worth establishing, since Walters & Ruscio (2009) showed in simulation settings that summing ordered items can degrade their performance without identifying the mechanism isolated here. And the perturbation result concerns totals rather than traits: in the score series studied here, the observed dip departures are largely accounted for by the lattice mechanism; this is not evidence that the corresponding latent distributions are unimodal. Further limitations are set out in Online Resource 1, Appendix F.

Three developments would settle more: sharp bounds for identified ranges of modality-relevant functionals; a cross-instrument study using common respondents and a flexible latent-density model to assess the practical size of item-relative metric effects; and an analysis of what the covariance-based taxometric procedures estimate under (5).

8 Conclusion

Applying Hartigan's dip test directly to a questionnaire total does not establish whether a latent attribute is categorical or dimensional. First, the total is lattice-supported, so the dip has an atom-induced floor that can itself cross the rejection threshold as the sample grows. Second, latent modality is coordinatedependent under the monotone reparametrisations left free by a nonparametric item-response model. Third, fixing a coordinate does not identify an unrestricted mixing law at finite 𝐽: every relative-interior observable covered by the continuous-curve result is compatible with both atomic and atomless latent laws. An item-anchored coordinate can make a shape statement well defined, but identified uncertainty then remains a property of the finite design, observable and functional, not something respondent sample size alone removes.

B.3 Defects in the distributed files

Four are reported here rather than silently corrected. The last is consequential.

First, the archive documentation for the BBC collection records implausible ages, with respondents recorded at 100 and above, to 130, attributed to a coding or input error; eligibility was 18 and over. Ages outside [18, 99] are treated as missing.

Secondly, the IPIP-NEO-120 file contains 619 150 records against the 𝑛 = 410 376 reported by Gerlach et al. (2018). The exclusion rule accounting for the difference is not stated in that paper and is not reconstructed here; comparisons with that work are therefore restricted to the BBC collection.

Thirdly, the EQSQ codebook describes a four-point response scale but lists three category labels, apparently a formatting fault. The number of categories is taken from the data and is four.

Fourthly, and materially, the EQSQ file does not contain the responses its codebook describes. It holds 125 columns: sixty labelled E1 to E60, sixty labelled S1 to S60, the derived scores EQ and SQ, and three further fields. The two item blocks are identical in every cell of all 13 256 records: the file contains one sixty-item response block written twice. Because every statistic of the second block equals the corresponding statistic of the first, the duplication produces no anomaly in any summary and is detectable only by direct comparison of columns.

Which block survived is recoverable, the derived scores predating the duplication: EQ and SQ differ in 13 024 of 13 256 records, which is impossible had both been computed from identical inputs. Regressing SQ on the Baron-Cohen item scores of the surviving block entered in both keying directions returns coefficients of exactly one for thirty-nine item and direction pairs and zero for the remaining eighty-one, and reproduces SQ without error in every complete case. The surviving block is the systemising items and the key applied by the survey is recovered exactly: twenty items forward, nineteen reversed, twenty-one unscored. The published SQ-60 scores forty items; this administration scores thirty-nine. The collection is retained as a sixty-item systemising measure. The derived EQ has no items behind it and is not used. We are aware of no published analysis of this file; any treating the two blocks as two instruments administered to the same respondents would be affected.

The distributed files also differ in conventions their documentation does not record. Five of the six tab-separated files carry no quoting convention and contain free-text fields holding literal double quotes, so a parser applying a default quote character silently displaces fields on the affected rows; the sixth quotes every field. Missing cells are marked by the literal string NULL. Both are handled explicitly at import rather than by relying on type inference, the consequence of not doing so being a shifted response matrix rather than an error.

B.4 Collections considered and excluded

Two requirements did most of the excluding.

Item-level responses. Several collections distribute scale totals or subscale composites rather than item responses. The phenotypic releases accompanying the ABIDE (Di Martino et al., 2014) and ADHD-200 imaging collections are of this kind: they carry, for instance, a Social Responsiveness Scale total and its subscale composites, but not the item responses from which those were formed. A total is precisely the statistic whose properties the paper concerns, so a collection distributing only totals cannot be used to examine what totals do.

No bilateral agreement.

The Healthy Brain Network distributes item-level responses together with clinician-assigned diagnoses, which would have supplied the clinical anchor this corpus lacks, but requires an executed data use agreement. A number of collections of Autism-Spectrum Quotient responses are described in publications whose availability statements specify release on request to the corresponding author; we did not make such requests, so that the analysis can be reproduced from the sources in Table S1 without correspondence. And the repository accompanying one published factor-analytic study of the Autism-Spectrum Quotient contains the analysis code, which reads four named data files, but not the files themselves; the associated availability statement anticipates release, which appears not to have occurred.

The consequence is stated rather than left to be inferred. The corpus contains no clinically diagnosed sample and no administration of the Autism-Spectrum Quotient. Its coverage of autistic traits rests on one self-report instrument carrying a self-reported diagnosis indicator and one systemising measure carrying none. A reader interested in the substantive question should treat the empirical base as illustrating the argument rather than addressing that question.

C Procedure and response quality C.1 Scoring and keying

Responses were read into an integer matrix per collection with each file's non-response code mapped to missing, and the observed range of categories checked against the codebook.

Published scoring keys were applied wherever an external key was available. As an independent audit, for every scale the leading eigenvector of the item correlation matrix was oriented so that a majority of loadings were positive, and items with negative loading were flagged for reversal as 𝐾 + 1 -𝑦. The derived direction agreed with the published key wherever one exists. For sources without an external key, the derived direction was applied. Empirical atom masses and dip values for those scales are therefore conditional on that keying convention; the atom-bound proposition itself is not, because any finite keyed sum remains lattice-supported. One of the 47 scales audited by this procedure retains an item with a tiny negative corrected item-total correlation after keying (𝑟 = -0.004). The EQSQ operational score uses its separately recovered scoring rule and is not part of this key-direction audit.

The BBC collection is a special case. It is flagged in the analysis registry as already keyed in the deposited form and is therefore not passed through the derived-reversal audit. As an empirical check on that convention, unreversed item sums correlate 1.000 with the depositor-provided scores for each of the five domains. The analysis therefore treats the deposited item responses as already keyed. This correlation check supports the convention used for scoring but does not establish the depositor's preprocessing independently, and no claim of exact equality is made.

Scale scores are sums over complete responses: a respondent missing any item of a scale receives no score for it rather than a partial sum, so that all scores lie on a common range.

C.2 Completeness and response quality

The nine collections imported to their documented dimensions, all response ranges agreeing with their codebooks, and comprise 2 478 614 records. Missingness is below 1.3 per cent everywhere.

Completeness varies with instrument length and bears on what follows, since scale scores require every item of a scale. The proportion answering every item ranges from 47.3 per cent for the 300item IPIP-NEO to 100 per cent for the DASS and BBC collections. Invariant responding is rare where computable: the 99th percentile of the longest run of identical consecutive responses is between 9 and 31, and between 5 and 249 respondents per collection answered identically throughout. The statistic is not computable in the same form for collections whose item files are treated as already keyed before the present scoring step.

Two external indicators of response quality were recovered. Of NPAS respondents 28.5 per cent, and of DASS respondents 13.1 per cent, claimed to know at least one of three non-existent words; median peritem latencies are available for two collections. Neither is used in the analyses reported, being recorded for the careless-responding component a fuller treatment would require.

The NPAS collection asks whether the respondent has ever been diagnosed with an autism spectrum disorder; 1 586 of those answering, or 6.27 per cent, report a diagnosis. The instrument recruits on nerd-adjacent content, so that proportion is not an estimate of prevalence, and the sampling fraction differs by trait level.

C.3 Modality, granularity and reparametrisation

The dip statistic (Hartigan & Hartigan, 1985; Hartigan, 1985) was computed on each score series. Two further quantities were recorded per scale: the mass of the second largest atom, which the atom bound makes the binding one, and the dip recomputed after adding noise uniform on (-1 2 , 1 2 ), which refines ties without reordering distinct scores and removes the lattice. Because that perturbation is random, the test applied after it is a random test and a single application reports one realisation; it was therefore repeated 100 times per scale and the distribution of the outcome recorded, a scale being counted as rejecting only if it rejects in a majority of its perturbations.

Two properties of the uncorrected operation were also recorded: the statistic recomputed at a common sample size across collections, separating it from the 𝑛 at which it happens to be available; and one distribution resampled at increasing 𝑛, exhibiting the dependence of the rejection rate on sample size at constant shape.

Where a scale continued to reject, the modal interval was inspected and the perturbed test repeated, over the same 100 replications, on respondents more than one response category from either boundary. The association between 𝑞 (2) and the number of items summed was summarised by a descriptive log-log regression across scales. For each score series the atom-bound crossing point was found numerically as the smallest 𝑛 for which √ 𝑛 𝑞 (2) /2 exceeds the 0.05 critical value, using the same interpolation of the diptest package's qDiptab table as dip.test.

The reparametrisation families are 𝑔 𝑠 (𝑧) = Φ(𝑧/𝑠), with 𝑔 ′ 𝑠 = 𝜑(𝑧/𝑠)/𝑠 maximal at 𝑧 = 0, and ℎ 𝑎 (𝑧) = sinh(𝑎𝑧)/𝑎, with ℎ ′ 𝑎 = cosh(𝑎𝑧) minimal there; the two move the density in opposite directions and ℎ 𝑎 → id as 𝑎 → 0. They are used for exposition and are not claimed to be the transformation implicit in any published analysis: they are members of the class left free by the observable map, and their sufficiency to reverse a conclusion is the point at issue.

E The finite-design computation

Section 4 of the main text uses this computation only as an illustration that a local distributional functional can remain partially identified at finite questionnaire-sized designs. The full levels are given here, together with the restrictions and numerical audits that qualify them. The restrictions are stated first so that none of the design-screen numbers is mistaken for a bound on modality.

E.1 What is computed, and four restrictions

The functional records central mass, not a gap. Ψ is the mass placed in a central interval and 𝑞 is generated by a standard normal reference law. A latent law placing much less mass there need not be bimodal: the deficit could arise from scale, from skewness, or from mass displaced just beyond the interval. The computation therefore demonstrates partial identification of a local central-mass feature. It does not compute the identified range of a modality or gap functional, and we do not claim a bounding relation between the two, none having been established.

The observable is the total score, which is not sufficient. For graded-response items the patterns (0, 2) and (1, 1) share a total but have probabilities whose ratio depends on 𝜃; the same holds for binary items whenever discriminations differ. Matching the score distribution imposes fewer constraints than matching the pattern distribution, so the ranges are coarsened bounds. Appendix E.4 measures the size of that effect directly.

The polytope discretises the fibre from within. The latent scale is discretised on a grid, and the polytope is the restriction of the fibre to grid-supported laws, so the computed value understates the truth.

Over the audited refinement sequence the differences approximately halve as the grid doubles, consistent with first-order convergence; the numerical audit below quantifies the remaining deficiency.

The item parameters are a convention. They match 𝐽 and 𝐾 and are not estimated from the collections, so the figures characterise design dimensions rather than the archived instruments.

It is not obviously so: Walters & Ruscio (2009) report, from a simulation study of covariance-based procedures using no such convention, that accuracy was highest with four or more ordered categories, the same threshold reached here by a different route.

E.6 Numerical validity

Of 106 range calculations evaluated, 104 produced an accepted pair of endpoints and two did not, both at 𝐽 = 320. Each calculation requires at least two solver calls, so the number of linear programmes is larger. Endpoints are accepted only when the returned weights reproduce the complete observable, including the one response-cell equality omitted from the programme as redundant, to within 10 -6 ; the largest such residual among accepted rows was 9.0 × 10 -8 . Observable maps were verified at every design to have non-negative entries and columns summing to one, and the information function used to define the arc-length metric was verified against a central-difference derivative and against the closed form for a binary item. The two calculations that did not produce accepted endpoints were both at 𝐽 = 320, on which see below.

The latent grid is coarsened as the design lengthens. Adjacent columns of the observable map become nearly indistinguishable on a fine grid for a long instrument and the solver stalls; the behaviour at 𝐽 = 320 is described below. Each of the three transitions of the resulting rule was audited by solving the same design on both adjacent grids, with the design chosen so that the two grids differ. All three solved on both. Ranges differed by between 2.8 and 4.8 per cent, and the deficit by between 0.18 and 0.51 percentage points, in the direction the discretisation argument predicts, the finer grid giving the larger range in every case. Those figures measure local sensitivity to the grid transition; they do not bound the error relative to the continuous fibre. The largest deficit change, 0.51 percentage points, is the tolerance carried to the design screen.

Small coefficients are dropped, and at long designs this matters. The operative threshold is 10 -9 : coefficients below it are discarded whatever value is requested, and on the longest designs a large share of the matrix entries falls below it. Raising the threshold by two orders of magnitude, to 10 -7 , leaves short designs almost unchanged and moves long ones appreciably: the relative change in the computed range is 0.03 per cent at 𝐽 = 10, 𝐾 = 5 and 0.20 per cent at 𝐽 = 26, 𝐾 = 5, but 8.3 per cent at 𝐽 = 60, 𝐾 = 5 and 4.6 per cent at 𝐽 = 40, 𝐾 = 7. The larger figures exceed the grid sensitivity reported above. The computation at long designs is therefore not insensitive to where the coefficient threshold sits, and the reported ranges carry that uncertainty in addition to the discretisation one. The direction is not perfectly monotone: the higher threshold gives a slightly larger range at 𝐽 = 26, 𝐾 = 5 and smaller ranges in the other three audited designs. We therefore treat the comparison as a sensitivity diagnostic rather than as a one-sided bound.

The audit of support truncation is a nested one, and it measures numerical uncertainty rather than a support effect. The support is truncated at [-5, 5]. Truncation removes candidates from the feasible set, so a wider support cannot narrow the identified range, but only if the observable, the objective and the reference value are held fixed. Recomputing the reference law on each support would change all three, the discretised normal being renormalised over a different interval, and the fibres would then not be nested; a wider support could return a narrower range for reasons that are not numerical error.

The reference law is therefore computed once on the working grid and embedded in the wider grids with zero weight outside it. Because the grid spacing is held constant at 0.00417, each wider grid contains the working one exactly, so the observable, the objective vector and the reference value are identical across supports by construction; both the containment and the invariance of the observable are asserted at run time rather than assumed. Under binary designs the range then increases monotonically with the support, from 0.38177 at half-width 5 to 0.38294 and 0.38334 at 6 and 7, as it must.

Across the four instrument-matched designs the largest increase on a wider support is 1.5 × 10 -4 and the largest decrease 5.8 × 10 -3 . Because the feasible sets nest, a decrease cannot be a support effect, so that figure is a direct measurement of numerical error in the computed range. It is of the same order as the grid sensitivity reported above, and is a third quantity the reported ranges carry. No substantial expansion was observed on any wider support, so truncation at the working support does not appear to constrain the extremising law materially; the non-monotone decreases place the numerical uncertainty at roughly the order of the grid audit.

The audit uses the objective fixed in the parametric coordinate. The arc-length objective is not audited: its interval is defined as a fraction of a total arc length that the support itself changes, so the two effects would not be separable without a further convention.

Designs at 𝐽 = 320 display non-monotone solver behaviour. Solved on grids of 1201, 601 and 301 points, the finest and the coarsest return while the intermediate one does not, and the two values returned differ materially. Solver success at these lengths is therefore not monotone in the grid resolution, and the values that are returned are strongly grid-dependent. These designs are excluded from the design screen and from every fitted quantity, and are reported as a demonstration of that behaviour rather than as estimates.

E.7 The interval in the information metric

The arc-length proposition identifies information arc length as a parametrisation fixed by the item set, whereas the computations above fix the interval in the coordinate of the assumed parametric family. Both were therefore computed on the same fibre, differing only in the objective vector: the observable map, the reference law and the grid are identical, and the interval in the second case is the central twenty per cent of the design's total information arc length. The information used to define that coordinate is the full item-response Fisher information even though the identified-range calculation deliberately coarsens the observable to the total score; the metric anchors the item-response curve and does not add pattern-level constraints to the score-only fibre. F Limitations

Five restrictions deserve scrutiny, in addition to the three stated in the scope subsection of the main text.

First, conditional independence. It is the standard assumption of the field and it is an assumption. It is also the one under which the reparametrisation argument is cleanest: a violation introduces dependence among responses that is not absorbed by any transformation of 𝜃, and would in principle carry information about the metric. Whether residual dependence is informative enough to restrict the reparametrisation group in practice is open, and would be a constructive route to fixing the metric that does not require a convention.

Secondly, monotonicity of the item response functions. The invariance theorem uses it only to check that the transformed model remains in P; the observational equivalence in (i) holds without it. Relaxing monotonicity therefore widens rather than narrows the indeterminacy.

Thirdly, the empirical work is confined to sum scores. This is the literature's practice and is not an endorsement of it: the whole of the lattice section of the main text is an argument against the practice, and a reader who concludes that the response patterns should be modelled directly has taken the intended point. The sum score is analysed because it is what the findings under examination were computed from.

Fourthly, the perturbation removing the lattice is a diagnostic and not an estimator. It shows how the rejection classification changes when ties are refined; together with the exact atom bound, it diagnoses Figure S4: Identified ranges for deterministic designs matched to the item and category dimensions represented in the empirical corpus. the practical role of lattice discreteness. It does not estimate the distribution of the underlying variable, and the near-unity 𝑝-values it returns should not be read as evidence of unimodality in any object other than the perturbed score.

Fifthly, the dip is one modality statistic among several, and only the dip is analysed here. The atom bound is a statement about the dip, and we have not established whether the excess-mass or criticalbandwidth statistics have an analogous lattice pathology. A bandwidth-based procedure may be protected by construction, its kernel bandwidth typically exceeding the lattice spacing, but we have not shown this and do not rely on it.
