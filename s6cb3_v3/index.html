Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

In large-scale educational assessments, students' academic outcomes can evolve over time due to various background variables. For instance, parents' education level, a common moderator, has been found to have time-varying effects on children's test scores, leading to heterogeneous academic trajectories. To model these varying trajectories, parametric models like hierarchical linear models (HLMs) are commonly deployed. While these parametric methods are interpretable, their predictive capacities have not been rigorously compared to those of more flexible non-parametric approaches. We present the results of systematic simulation studies and empirical analyses comparing six parametric specifications to two Bayesian non-parametric methods, Bayesian additive regression trees (BART) and its semi-parametric extension with varying coefficients (VCBART). Our findings indicate that the BART-based alternatives can (1) achieve out-of-sample prediction close to the best-specified HLM, with standardized out-of-sample errors within 0.002 of the best parametric model in every simulation condition; (2) provide a reasonable amount of interpretability and uncertainty quantification; and (3) run about 20 to 200 times faster than the most saturated parametric model. This work contributes to the existing literature by demonstrating that Bayesian treed regressions are viable and feasible alternatives to benchmark HLMs for predicting heterogeneous trajectories across time.

Bayesian Treed Regressions for Heterogeneous Test-Score Trajectories in Large-Scale Educational Assessments Introduction Background

In large-scale educational assessments, students' test scores are often heterogeneous at individual levels across time. Exploring the dynamic nature of students' learning patterns as well as their background characteristics that shape their diverse educational development can offer helpful insights into the efficacy of pedagogical strategies and educational policies. Typically, we estimate these trajectories with parametric statistical models in which the heterogeneity in test score trajectories is captured by individual-level parameters. This is a common phenomenon in large-scale educational assessments. For instance, a large body of previous longitudinal studies has shown that children's academic outcomes have time-varying slopes, due to the joint effects of multiple factors at different levels (e.g., Spilt et al., 2012; Ladd and Dinella, 2009; McNeish et al., 2020; Nestler and Humberg, 2022). Parental education, as one of the common background characteristics in education studies, is shown to have significantly varying effects on children's developmental outcomes across time (e.g., Cheadle and Amato, 2011; Dubow et al., 2009; Bae and Wickrama, 2015; Hao and Yeung, 2015; Potter and Roksa, 2013). In particular, Potter and Roksa (2013) conclude that children whose mothers have graduate degrees tend to have higher test scores at all grade levels, compared to those whose mothers have high school degrees, using the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study Kindergarten cohort (ECLS-K) data. With the same empirical data used in Potter and Roksa (2013)'s study, Figure 1 illustrates the variation in the trajectories of test scores for children as a function of their mothers' educational background. First, Figure 1a indicates that the mother's education makes a difference in the average reading scores, and the regression slope for the time measures is evidently not constant over time, indicating the existence of varying coefficients. To depict how individual characteristics yield different test trajectories given certain levels of background covariates, Figures 1b and 1c show the test scores for individuals whose mother has the lowest (coded as 0) and the highest educational attainment (coded as 8) respectively. We see that children's test scores are more similar to each other when their mothers have the same education levels.

In addition, the heterogeneity in children's academic performance can also be attributed to the unique random variability of each individual. This implies that when we model the varying coefficients of the time measures, two components should be taken into consideration: the dependency among students that may cause their scores to be related or similar to each other, and the individual idiosyncrasies that make their learning pattern distinct and independent from others.

The benchmark methods used in social and behavioral sciences are hierarchical linear models (HLMs), which treat individuals as a cluster to model the growth of test scores across time. Previous studies have indicated that HLMs can effectively obtain adequate statistical inference and provide interpretability for the data (e.g., Cece et al., 2020; Borges et al., 2021). Applications to longitudinal assessment data are plentiful; besides Potter and Roksa (2013), the studies of McNeish (2017), Rittle-Johnson et al. (2017), and McNeish et al. (2020) offer representative examples.

A larger body of related (a) (b) (c) approaches, such as growth curve modeling and multilevel structural equation modeling, pursues similar goals with different parameterizations (e.g., McNeish and Matta, 2018; Hoffman, 2015; Fine et al., 2019; Wang and Maxwell, 2015).

In his "Two Cultures" paper, Breiman (2001) describes two cultures of statistical modeling. The first culture assumes a data-generating model of a known form and estimates its parameters, which is how the parametric HLMs above operate. The second culture treats the data-generating process as a black box and approximates it with a flexible algorithm that learns the patterns from the data. This study compares the two cultures on the prediction of heterogeneous test score trajectories.

Motivation

The first culture comes with some potential drawbacks. First and foremost, to attain accurate prediction, the HLMs are supposed to be correctly specified. This has always been a challenging task as the model should be flexible but rigorous enough to capture the underlying patterns of the data, while being parsimonious enough to avoid overfitting, which means inferences obtained from a specific sample can be generalized to the population or other samples. In addition, there are a large number of model selection methods that can be considered such as information criteria (e.g., AIC) and regularization methods (e.g., LASSO). This necessitates researchers to carefully choose appropriate selection methods since different strategies will lead to different final choices of model, which potentially bring in biased statistical inference. Second, complex hierarchical model structures may fail to converge due to issues such as singularity, as manifested by near zero or negative variances, encountered using frequentist approaches (Snijders and Bosker, 2011; Huang and Anderson, 2021). To reiterate, in this study, we first tried to replicate the results of Potter and Roksa (2013) with frequentist HLMs fitted in lme4 (Bates et al., 2015). For both outcomes, every model that incorporates moderator effects terminated in a boundary (singular) fit, with the estimated correlations between the random intercept and the random time slopes pushed to the boundary of the parameter space. Bayesian estimation with proper priors avoids this issue, since the priors keep the variance components away from the boundary. However, both approaches still require the researcher to specify the model correctly. Lastly, modeling varying coefficients or heterogeneous time trends can be computationally intense, utilizing a large amount of memory and time. Given these challenges, we consider Bayesian non-parametric methods that learn the mean function from the data under default priors. That is, Bayesian additive regression trees (BART) and varying coefficients BART (VCBART). Essentially, we compare the predictive performance obtained from the hierarchical models, BART, and VCBART through both simulation and empirical studies.

Aim and Contributions

The general goal of this study is to implement an alternative approach that can (1) effectively address these limitations and (2) maintain a comparable level of predictive performance as the benchmark HLMs. We chose Potter and Roksa (2013)'s work as a starting point to provide a concrete example for the comparisons between baseline HLMs and Bayesian ensemble learning, and our study is not restricted to a single dataset: we employ six simulation conditions and empirical studies with two response variables (reading and math scores). This work contributes to the existing literature in four aspects.

First, given the complexity of large-scale assessment data, we want to avoid the potential model misspecification issues that are intrinsic to parametric modeling strategies, while maintaining the same level of predictive accuracy as benchmark HLMs; the BART-based methods make minimal parametric assumptions in their default set-up (e.g., Chipman et al., 2010; Hill et al., 2020). Second, large-scale data call for robust uncertainty quantification, and we evaluate the calibration of each method's prediction intervals rather than assuming it. Third, VCBART offers the ability to quantify uncertainty using probabilistic intervals and provides a certain level of interpretability for the effects of the time measures, and we examine its estimated coefficient functions directly. Lastly, to make the methods easy to use in practice, especially for applied researchers, BART and VCBART are relatively straightforward to implement, and they are more computationally efficient than the saturated parametric models. The purpose of this paper is not to argue which culture, parametric or non-parametric, is philosophically correct. Instead, we intend to provide a detailed comparison of benchmark hierarchical parametric models and nonparametric machine learning methods in terms of their predictive performance, when the effects of the time measures vary longitudinally in hierarchical clustered data.

This section also introduces the concepts of complete pooling, no pooling, and partial pooling, which are commonly used in Bayesian statistics and are also gaining traction in social science research (Gelman et al., 1995). 1. Complete pooling ignores the hierarchical clustering structure and treats every observation as part of a single grouping cluster. For instance, students' test scores can be denoted as y i ∼ N (µ, σ 2 ), where µ is the overall average test score across all schools and σ 2 is the variance. This approach assumes all the schools' effects are identical, analogous to a regression with only a global intercept and no group-specific parameters.

2. No pooling treats each group as entirely separate, estimating parameters for each group independently. That is, student test scores for each school j can be denoted as y ij ∼ N (µ j , σ 2 j ), indicating each school has its own mean and variance. Unlike the complete pooling model, this allows for different intercepts across groups but ignores the dependency between groups, and no information is shared.

3. Partial pooling is a compromise between complete pooling and no pooling, allowing information to be shared across groups while still acknowledging group-level differences.

That is, y ij ∼ N (µ j , σ 2 ), µ j ∼ N (µ 0 , τ² ). In this case, each school has its own mean, but these means center around an overall mean µ 0 with between-school variance τ².

This approach corresponds to the mixed-effects model, where both an overall mean function and group-specific deviations are considered.

Our preference for the pooling notations is due to their flexible hierarchical modeling capabilities, better uncertainty quantification through posterior distributions, and their ability to provide more comprehensive insights for the cluster structure. Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART)

BART, proposed by Chipman et al. (2010), has gained wide popularity as an "off-the-shelf" ensemble method in statistics and computer science. Generally speaking, BART approximates the unknown f in function (c) by the sum of M independent binary regression trees. Figure 2 illustrates the structure of a binary regression tree, which is the building block for BART. Starting from the root, each regression tree splits based on the thresholds of the inputs, and the corresponding value at each node is the expected value of the outcome variable, µ i (see Figure 2a). In other words, the tree partitions the sample space into several sub-regions (see Figure 2b). While a single tree provides a basic partitioning of the sample space, BART takes this concept further by combining multiple such binary trees, each acting as a "weak learner", to create a robust predictive model. Essentially, BART employs a sum-of-trees approach where each regression tree is intentionally kept shallow by using strong regularizing priors. This ensemble approach of combining multiple "weak learners" contrasts with those methods relying on a single deep tree that can suffer from instability (Gey and Poggi, 2006). Bayesian backfitting MCMC is used to iteratively build these trees. In each iteration, BART proposes changes to the tree structures including growing and pruning, then updates the terminal node parameters. These proposals are accepted or rejected based on how well they fit the data, subject to the prior constraints. This ensemble learning process allows BART to capture complex, non-linear relationships while maintaining computational feasibility and avoiding overfitting. A more detailed discussion of BART can be found in Chipman et al. (2010) and a review paper by Hill et al. (2020). BART and its extensions have been applied to several different domains such as economics and public health (e.g., Prüser, 2019; Scarpone et al., 2020), due to its flexibility in adapting to different data structures as well as its capacity for accurate prediction. VCBART is one of the elaborations of BART for varying coefficient settings.

Next, we use the R packages BART (Sparapani et al., 2021) and VCBART (Deshpande et al., 2023) to run BART and VCBART respectively. VCBART runs four chains of 1,000 burn-in and 1,000 retained iterations each, matching the 4,000 retained draws of the parametric models; BART runs a single chain with 1,000 burn-in and 1,000 retained draws, its package default. To be precise, we perform BART and VCBART with the same inputs as in models 4, 5, and 6.

Convergence diagnostics (potential scale reduction factors and effective sample sizes) for the Stan-fitted models are reported in Appendix B. The coding scripts can be found at the GitHub repository (blinded for review).

In general, the prediction errors grow as more moderator effects enter the true DGP, and the noisy conditions (2, 4, and 6) show larger errors than their constant counterparts (1, 3, and 5). Model 1 (complete pooling) is the exception, since it ignores both the moderators and the individual variation, so it underfits once either enters the DGP. Models 2 (no pooling) and 3 (partial pooling without moderators) go in the other direction and obtain the smallest training RMSE. These two models estimate a separate set of coefficients for each student, so they fit the training data closely.

The testing set tells a different story. Model 2 fails in every condition because it does not share any information across students. A student absent from the training set has no estimated coefficients, so the model falls back on the prior for the prediction. As a result, its out-of-sample SMSE equals one, the same value we would obtain by predicting every score with the training mean. Model 3 avoids this failure because its hierarchical structure shrinks the individual coefficients toward a common population mean, which gives a sensible prediction for a new student. Model 3 still ignores the moderators, so once the moderator effects enter the true DGP, its out-of-sample errors become as large as those of model 1.

When it comes to out-of-sample prediction, the BART-based methods obtain comparably small RMSE as the best parametric model ( model 5 in conditions 4 and 6. They are therefore most competitive when the moderator set is small or when the individual variation dominates, which is the setting most empirical assessment data resemble. All of these differences are far larger than the Monte Carlo standard errors in Table 3. By investigating Figure 3, we can find that the in-sample intervals of all the methods are generally close to or above 95%, with model 2 at one because its intervals are extremely wide. As for the out-of-sample coverage, the hierarchical models with moderators (models 5 and 6) stay at or above the nominal level in every condition, overshooting it in the constant conditions. BART shows the clearest shortfall, with outof-sample coverage falling a few percentage points below the nominal level in the noisy conditions, while VCBART stays closer to it. Conclusion, Limitations, and Discussion

This study compared the benchmark parametric models and the BART-based methods on the prediction of heterogeneous trajectories of test scores. Across the six simulation conditions, which crossed the moderator structure with the presence of individual variability, the BART-based methods obtained RMSE and SMSE comparable to the best parametric models. In the empirical studies, their performance was comparable to the saturated model 5, with BART ahead for reading scores and model 5 ahead for math scores. The BART-based methods offer several advantages. They do not require the researcher to pre-specify the model correctly, and their default settings predict well.

They also run much faster than model 5. VCBART further provides a reasonable degree of interpretability through its estimated coefficient functions. For large-scale longitudinal studies, we therefore consider the BART-based methods practical alternatives to the standard parametric models.

The results also offer guidance on the choice between the two tree-based methods.

In the simulation conditions with moderator effects, VCBART predicted as accurately as BART or better, and its out-of-sample coverage was higher. VCBART also returns the estimated coefficient functions, which BART does not. When the form of the time trend is known, such as the quadratic polynomial in our setting, VCBART provides prediction on par with the best parametric model together with interpretable time coefficients. BART remains attractive for its shorter computing time and for settings where the form of the time trend itself is uncertain.

A well specified parametric model such as model 5 remains a strong choice in some settings. It provides direct estimates and credible intervals for the moderator effects Γ p , which the tree-based methods do not, so it suits studies whose goal is formal inference on these effects, and it remained the most accurate method for math scores in our empirical study. Reaching this accuracy, however, requires several careful steps. The researcher must choose the form of the time trend, decide which moderators affect which coefficients, include the individual random effects, and check convergence. Our results show that missing any of these steps costs predictive accuracy, as models 4 and 6 illustrate. The BART-based methods reach nearly the same accuracy without these steps and at a much lower computational cost. We therefore suggest the saturated parametric model when formal inference on the moderator effects is needed and the specification can be built with care, and the BART-based methods when prediction is the main goal.

Uncertainty quantification deserves a separate qualification. The out-of-sample coverage of BART and VCBART fell below the nominal 95% level in the empirical study, by roughly three to nine percentage points depending on the method and outcome. As implemented here, neither method includes a student-level random effect, so their prediction intervals for a new student omit the between-student variance that the hierarchical models propagate. Two remedies appear feasible: incorporating a random intercept into the tree model, or calibrating the intervals on a held-out fold. We leave both for future work.

Regarding prior selection, our strategy was to standardize the outcome and align the priors of the parametric models with the defaults of BART and VCBART, which places comparable shrinkage on all methods (see Appendix A). For applied researchers we consider this a sensible starting point: the defaults of Chipman et al. (2010) were designed for standardized outcomes, and matching them avoids giving any method an advantage through more diffuse or more concentrated priors.

There are several limitations of this study which can serve as motivations for future research. Our analysis is constrained by two primary assumptions that may not be universally applicable. The first is that we assumed the time measures, the moderators, and the function f are known. In fields where domain expertise is limited, the roles of these variables might be less clear, possibly necessitating exploratory variable selection techniques. The second assumption is related to the normality assumption of our data. In scenarios where data are not normally distributed, employing other prob-ability distributions like Poisson or Bernoulli might be more effective. In addition, the models operate at the student and occasion levels and do not include a school level. This choice preserves comparability with Potter and Roksa (2013) to explore a wider range of analytical tools, and we hope it inspires further methodological development, including computational tools that make these methods more accessible to practitioners in large-scale assessments.

Appendix Appendix A: Prior Calibration

All methods are fit after standardizing the outcome to mean zero and unit variance within the training set. On this scale, the VCBART default places a prior standard deviation of 0.5/ √ M on each tree's contribution to a coefficient function, with M = 50 trees, so the implied marginal prior on each coefficient function has standard deviation 0.5.

The N (0, 0.5 2 ) priors on π p in models 1, 2, and 4 match this marginal scale directly. In models 3, 5, and 6 the same marginal scale is split between the two levels: the N (0, 0.4 2 ) prior on β p0 and the half-normal N + (0, 0.3 2 ) prior on σ p0 imply a marginal standard deviation of √ 0.4 2 + 0.32 = 0.5 for π pi , since 0.4 2 + 0.32 = 0.5 2 . The residual variance σ 2 receives the inverse-gamma prior Inv-Gamma(ν/2, νλ/2) with ν = 3 and λ chosen so that the prior places probability 0.9 above the standardized residual variance, following the default calibration of Chipman et al. (2010); the same setting is used by the BART and VCBART packages. The moderator effects Γ p receive N (0, 0.5 2 ) or N (0, 0.4 2 ) priors on the same standardized scale, as listed with each model. These choices place comparable shrinkage on all methods and give none of them an advantage through more diffuse or more concentrated priors.
