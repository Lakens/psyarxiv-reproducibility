Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Recent studies have investigated preferences and perceptions towards face-to-face and digital psychological interventions. Understanding the potential differences in perceived risks between face-to-face and digital services as well as associations with preferences and intentions, can be worthwhile.

We investigated differences in perceived likelihood of being found out and perceived severity of face loss between face-to-face psychological interventions and digital psychological interventions. We also tested the associations between differences in perceived risks and differences in intentions or preferences towards different types of interventions, and investigated the potential moderation of self-construal, regulatory focus, and cultural background.

We conducted a multi-regional study with 645 college students in Hong Kong China, India, United Kingdom, and Canada. We performed t-tests, correlation analyses, and moderated multiple regressions, while combining with alternative statistical methods to examine robustness and consistency of findings across methods.

We found that people generally perceive face-to-face interventions to have higher likelihood of being found out and severity of face loss compared to digital interventions, with medium to large effects. While no significant association was found for the difference in perceived likelihood of being found out with the difference in intervention preferences and help-seeking intentions, higher perceived severity of face loss for face-to-face intervention was associated with lower preferences and intentions for face-to-face intervention relative to digital interventions. Such associations are moderated by self-construal but in the opposite direction to our hypotheses, in which people with stronger independent self-construal had stronger negative associations between difference in perceived severity of face loss and difference in preferences or intentions.

Our findings contribute by identifying differences in perceived risks between face-to-face and digital interventions as well as association with preferences and intentions. We speculate the surprising self-construal moderation findings may be due to higher reliance on personal feelings and higher prioritization for self-consistency among more independent people, and call for further studies testing such mechanisms. We encourage further much-needed investigations into various forms of perceived risks and potential associations with face-to-face or digital mental health intervention preferences, intentions, and decisions, while examining individual and cultural factors.

Introduction

While face-to-face interventions have long been the primary mode of delivering therapies for people with depression, digital psychological interventions have been shown to be effective for people with depression and have proliferated in the recent decade [1]. In meta-analyses, digital psychological interventions have shown effects roughly equivalent to that of face-to-face interventions [2, 3] and are highly scalable, which can increase access to effective services. However, their uptake remains far from sufficient to meet the substantial demand. The two types of interventions differ in their advantages, disadvantages, benefits, risks, facilitators, and barriers [4] [5] [6] [7] [8]. Notably, digital interventions tend to be more accessible and involve minimal, if any, financial costs, whereas face-to-face interventions are generally less accessible and usually more costly [9, 10]. That said, face-to-face interventions may be perceived as more effective because of the human connection and the non-verbal social-emotional cues available in person [9, 11]. A growing body of user-centered research has investigated how potential service users perceive face-to-face and digital psychological interventions and their preferences or decisions [12] [13] [14].

Although some studies with Western samples have found that a higher proportion of people prefer face-to-face over digital interventions [15] [16] [17] [18] [19], people generally perceive greater helpseeking stigma toward face-to-face interventions than toward digital interventions [20].

Although most people generally prefer face-to-face interventions to digital interventions, younger individuals, people with higher attachment avoidance, and people with higher levels of education tend to hold more positive attitudes toward digital interventions [12, 15, 16, 21]. In particular, individuals who perceive digital interventions as accessible and convenient are more likely to endorse them [22]. Help-seeking preferences and perceptions may also differ across cultures. In many parts of Asia, access to face-to-face interventions is highly limited because the number of qualified mental health service providers is disproportionately small relative to the overall population [23, 24]. Digital psychological interventions, which are generally more accessible, may therefore help address this issue. In addition, help-seeking stigma has been found to be significantly higher in Asian cultures than in Western cultures [25] [26] [27] [28]. Specifically, Asian individuals may have greater concerns about face loss, as seeking help for depression may reflect negatively not only on themselves but also on their families and close ones [25] [26] [27] [28]. Given these cultural differences, it may be important to investigate how the perceived risks of being found out and of face loss may affect individuals' preferences for face-to-face versus digital interventions and their intentions to seek these interventions, and to explore potential cultural differences.

Risk perception is multidimensional and among which it can be operationalized along perceived probability and perceived severity [29, 30]. In the present study, we examine the perceived probability of being found out and the perceived severity of face loss, as perceived probability and perceived severity are potentially critical factors underlying people's preferences, judgments, and choices [31] [32] [33]. In general, when perceived risks (probability and/or severity) are high, people may adopt risk-reduction or avoidance strategies by choosing the option with lower perceived risk [34] [35] [36]. For perceived severity, we focus specifically on the severity of face loss if one is found out. Perceived severity of face loss can be defined as the subjective magnitude of negative consequences to the social reputation, social image, and social worth that an individual hopes to maintain [37] [38] [39] [40] [41] [42]. This construct is particularly relevant because face loss concerns may predict less favorable attitudes toward mental health help-seeking and less help-seeking behavior [37, 43, 44].

Many studies worldwide have shown that both mental illness stigma and help-seeking stigma can deter people with mental health challenges from seeking help [6, [45] [46] [47] [48]. In cultures where more people hold interdependent self-construals or are more concerned about face, these stigmas are particularly likely to threaten not only one's self-image but also the social reputation of one's close associates, especially family members [25, 42, [49] [50] [51]. Consequently, when seeking help for mental health challenges, many people may be likely to worry about the risks of being "found out" and to consider how severe the reputational consequences could be (e.g., face loss for themselves and their families). These individuals may consider and estimate these risks (likelihood and severity) when making preference judgments and forming intentions regarding the use of face-to-face versus digital psychological interventions.

Because digital psychological interventions, especially those that are self-guided, can be accessed privately in a location that individuals consider safe, many people may perceive them as carrying lower risks of being found out than face-to-face psychological interventions, which must be accessed in person in locations where other people may be present. Previous studies have found that perceived stigma toward people who seek help through face-to-face interventions is higher than toward those who seek help through digital interventions [20], and that perceived stigma is associated with loss of face [39]. Thus, we hypothesized that people may perceive more serious consequences if family members or others find out about their use of faceto-face interventions than about their use of digital interventions. Given that some studies have found loss of face concerns to be associated with reduced help-seeking [37, 43] (but see [52] for findings in the opposite direction), it is reasonable to conjecture that people may perceive lower risks for digital interventions than for face-to-face interventions. Furthermore, perceived risks have been found to be associated with lower preferences and behavioral intentions to seek help [53]. Therefore, we expected a negative association between relative risk perception (in probability and severity) and relative preferences and intentions in seeking digital and face-toface interventions. As mentioned, earlier studies, mostly with Western samples, found higher preferences for face-to-face interventions relative to digital interventions, and this may be due to higher perceived effectiveness of face-to-face interventions over digital interventions [15, 18, 19]. Thus, people's preferences for and intentions in seeking face-to-face or digital interventions may be compounded by other factors. Moreover, the associations between risk perception and preferences may also be moderated by individual and cultural differences, such as regulatory focus and self-construal.

According to Higgins's Regulatory Focus Theory [54], humans have two types of regulatory focus -prevention focus, which concerns safety and security, and promotion focus, which refers to striving for advancements and growth. People with a predominant prevention focus are more concerned about safety when making decisions and more vigilant about potential dangers in their judgments and behaviors [55]. Building on findings linking prevention focus to lower risk tolerance [56, 57], we speculated that people with a predominant prevention focus may be less willing to tolerate the higher perceived risks of face-to-face interventions. Specifically, we hypothesized that the negative associations between perceived risk of, and preference for, faceto-face interventions over digital interventions would be stronger among people who are predominantly prevention-focused. In contrast, the associations between differences in perceived risk and differences in preference may be weaker or minimal among people with a predominant promotion focus, as they generally focus more on potential gains and may tolerate higher perceived risks when making judgments and choices [56].

Previous studies have shown that people in Asian societies tend to be more prevention-focused, whereas people in Western Europe and North America tend to be more promotion focused [58, 59]. This difference may reflect the more collectivistic cultures in Asia, where people emphasize responsibilities and obligations and may favor prevention-oriented goals, compared with the more individualistic cultures in the West, where people strive for individual aspirations and growth and may favor promotion-oriented goals [58, 59]. Given this cultural difference, we hypothesized that the association between differences in perceived risks and differences in preferences or intentions for face-to-face versus digital interventions may be stronger among people in Hong Kong and India than among those in Canada and the United Kingdom.

In addition to regulatory focus, we also examine the potential moderating effects of interdependent and independent self-construal across the four cultures in the present study [60].

Self-construal can be defined as the constellation of feelings, thoughts, and behaviors regarding how people understand the self in relation to others [60] [61] [62]. People with higher independent selfconstrual are more focused on demonstrating uniqueness and individuality in their thoughts, traits, goals, and actions, whereas people with higher interdependent self-construal have a self that varies more across contexts and are more concerned about relationships, status, roles, and the "appropriateness" of behaviors [60]. People with higher interdependent self-construal are more concerned about face when making mental health-related decisions such as help-seeking [25, 50]. Because these individuals consider face more important, we speculate that among more interdependent people, higher perceived risks of being found out and losing face when using face-to-face interventions are more likely to translate into lower preferences for, and intentions to seek, face-to-face interventions relative to digital interventions. In contrast, among more independent people, the perceived risk of being found out and the perceived severity of losing face when using face-to-face interventions may be less likely to deter them from seeking such help, as they care less about face and more about autonomy in decision-making [63].

At the cultural level, past studies have shown that people in Asia are, on average, more interdependent, whereas people in the West are, on average, more independent [60, 64, 65].

Therefore, it would be reasonable to expect stronger associations between risk perception and preferences or intentions among participants in Hong Kong and India, and weaker associations among participants in Canada and the United Kingdom. However, given cultural changes in selfconstrual, including increasing individualism and independence in some regions [66, 67], and mixed findings on cross-cultural differences in self-construal (see [68] meta-analysis), we do not rule out the possibility that cultural differences in self-construal may be minimal and that culture may not moderate the association between risk perception and preferences or intentions.

For people in Hong Kong China and India (Asian regions), the (negative) association between ai) difference in perceived likelihood and aii) difference in perceived severity with bi) difference in preference and bii) intention would be stronger when comparing to people in the United Kingdom and Canada (western regions). Method

The dataset analyzed is part of a large-scale cross-sectional survey on help-seeking through digital and offline psychological interventions [28]. Approximately 800 participants through subject pools at 4 universities from 4 cultural contexts (one university in Hong Kong China, one university in Canada, one university in India, one university in United Kingdom) were first recruited based on convenience sampling. Six-hundred and forty-five participants passed our validation checks and are included in analyses. The validation check consists of five simple questions. A sample item is "please indicate strongly disagree for this question". The responses were counted as valid when participants correctly answered all five validation questions. The mean age of participants included was 21.25, with a standard deviation of 4.65.70.40% of participants are women whereas 27.26% of participants are men. 0.62% of participants are transgender, 1.09% of participants prefer not to disclose their gender, and 0.62% of participants prefer not to specify their gender.

Measures

Perceived Likelihood of being Found Out

Participants completed four questions regarding perceived likelihood (any integer from 0% to 100%) of digital and face-to-face psychological interventions. These items included perceived likelihood of being found out by family members through undergoing face-to-face intervention, which is compared with perceived likelihood of being found out by family members through undergoing digital self-guided psychological intervention. We also included perceived likelihood of being found out by other people (not family members) through undergoing face-to-face intervention, which is compared with perceived likelihood of being found out by other people through undergoing digital self-guided psychological intervention.

Perceived Severity of Face Loss

Then they completed 6 questions regarding perceived severity of face loss with 0-10 point Likert scales (0 = not at all,10 = very serious). The first 3 questions in the following are averaged as composite values for perceived severity for face-to-face interventions (α = 0.86) whereas the last 3 questions in the following are averaged as composite for perceived severity of digital interventions (α = 0.86): 1) Perceived severity of personal face loss if family members find out the involvement in face-to-face psychological intervention.

2) Perceived severity of personal face loss if other people find out the involvement in faceto-face psychological intervention.

3) Perceived severity of family face loss if other people find out the involvement in face-toface psychological intervention.

4) Perceived severity of personal face loss if family members find out the involvement in digital psychological intervention.

5) Perceived severity of personal face loss if other people find out the involvement in digital psychological intervention.

6) Perceived severity of family face loss if other people find out the involvement in digital psychological intervention.

Preferences

The survey also consists of one question on the extent they prefer face-to-face intervention and another preference question for digital intervention, with 0 to 10 Likert scales (0 = Not at all,10 = Highly prefer). A difference in preference score for face-to-face intervention versus digital intervention is calculated (preference of face-to-face intervention -preference of digital intervention), in which a positive score implies stronger preference for face-to-face intervention and a negative score implies stronger preference for digital intervention. Similar scoring approaches have been adopted in health preference studies [77].

Intentions

They also completed 3 questions (1 = Strongly disagree,6 = Strongly agree) on intentions of seeking help through face-to-face intervention (α = 0.91) and 3 questions for digital intervention (α = 0.95). Similar to preference, a difference in intention score is computed (behavioral intention for face-to-face intervention -behavioral intention for digital intervention), with a positive score implying stronger intention for face-to-face intervention and a negative score implying stronger intention for digital intervention.

The survey also consists of individual difference scales on regulatory focus and self-construal.

Regulatory Focus

The survey consists of the 11-item Regulatory Focus Questionnaire by Higgins et al. [78], which measures participants' motivational orientations (prevention focus versus promotion focus). The scale consists of 11 5-point 1 likert scale items (6 items on promotion focus, M = 3.14, SD = 0.62, 5 items on prevention focus, M = 3.25, SD = 0.79, score = mean of promotion focus -mean of prevention focus, in which a positive score implies the individual is predominantly promotion focus whereas a negative score implies the individual is predominantly prevention focus), with α = 0.67 in our sample. An example of a prevention-focus item is "Not being careful enough has gotten me into trouble at times.", whereas an example of a promotion-focus item is "I feel like I have made progress toward being successful in my life.". A past study showed that this scale had adequate internal consistency, adequate stability (test-retest reliability), and better predictive validity than other regulatory focus scales available in the literature [79].

Self-Construal

Participants also completed the 10-item Self-Construal Scale short-form by D'amico and Scrima as it is much shorter than Singelis original scale [81] (24 items) to reduce burden for the participants. Also, D'amico and Scrima [80] scale has adequate goodness of fit, has the same internal consistency as the original scale, and outperforms the original scale in concurrent validity [80].

To compare perceived likelihood of face-to-face versus digital intervention, we conducted a Shapiro-Wilk Normality tests and paired-sample t-tests with Jamovi. Discussion

Comparison of Perceived Risks for Face-To-Face versus Digital Interventions

Our study found support for a substantially higher perceived likelihood of being found out for face-to-face intervention than for digital intervention, with a large effect size. We also found strong effects with Wilcoxon rank tests, implying that the differences are robust when ranking within-pair differences. This difference in the perceived likelihood of being found out is understandable, and participants' risk perceptions may, to some extent, reflect actual risks. As undergraduate students, participants are likely aware of, and concerned about, the chances of being found out by others (e.g., professors, classmates, or friends) when walking to the university counseling or therapy center, especially at universities where the location of the therapy center is widely known. In contrast, some students can access digital mental health platforms more privately on their smartphones and computers, so the actual or perceived likelihood of being found out is lower. However, access to private space varies across individuals (see [4] for a review). Future studies investigating the actual likelihood of being found out among participants who have undergone, or are currently undergoing, digital versus face-to-face interventions would be worthwhile. Such studies could test whether these differences in perceived risks align with actual risks or reflect overestimations of risk. In addition, it is uncertain whether these face-to-face versus digital gaps in perceived or actual risks generalize to the working population and older adults. Most people do not have access to the free face-to-face counseling services available at universities, and the locations of therapy centers are less likely to be known by people who know the potential service users.

Another form of perceived risk is the perceived severity of face loss if others find out. Our results also showed a significantly higher perceived severity of face loss if others found out that participants were undergoing face-to-face intervention. These findings are consistent with those of a previous study that found higher perceived stigma for face-to-face intervention than for digital intervention [20]. Perceived severity of face loss (but perhaps not perceived likelihood) appears to be conceptually and empirically associated with perceived stigma [39, 49], as both face loss and stigma concern social image and reputation [37, 51, 97]. Although we did not investigate the possible mechanisms, the higher perceived severity of face loss for face-to-face intervention may arise because people generally believe that face-to-face interventions are for people with more severe mental illnesses. In contrast, digital interventions may be perceived as more suitable for people with mild mental health issues or life challenges. The severity of mental health challenges appears to be associated with the severity of stigma [98]. We speculate that this partly explains why the perceived severity of face loss, which is positively associated with perceived stigma, is higher for face-to-face intervention. Furthermore, because our study focused on self-guided digital intervention rather than video psychotherapy or counseling, the perceived severity of face loss for digital intervention may have been lower than it would be for guided intervention. This is because self-help may be perceived as more acceptable and as carrying relatively lower stigma concerns [99]. Future studies are needed to test this speculation.

Perceived Risks, Preferences, and Intentions for Face-To-Face versus Digital Interventions

Are relative perceived risks (i.e., the perceived risk of face-to-face interventions minus the perceived risk of digital interventions) associated with preferences and intentions for face-to-face versus digital interventions? Interestingly, we found a significant association between the perceived severity of face loss if found out and differences in intervention preferences and intentions. However, we found no significant association between the perceived likelihood of being found out and intervention preferences and intentions. The significant association of preferences or intentions with the perceived severity of threat (face loss in this context) is consistent with studies on the link between perceived severity and intention or behavior in the broader health behavior literature [33, 100], including the mental health help-seeking literature In contrast to the significant findings with perceived severity as the predictor, the minimal association (rs < 0.03, not significant) between the difference in perceived likelihood and preferences or intentions was unexpected and inconsistent with the broader literature on perceived likelihood and health behavior [102]. One possible explanation is that "being found out" is not necessarily detrimental to one's intention to seek help, whereas "losing face" is often a negative consequence that people seek to avoid. Risk of losing face may therefore have more influence on the decision to seek help. It is possible that some people are not worried about the chances of being found out, perhaps because of lower face concern and more accepting professors, classmates, and friends. However, this explanation is speculative. Future studies may conceptually replicate these findings with different samples in different settings and examine potential moderators.

Potential Moderators in the Association between Perceived Risk and Preferences or Intentions

In the previous section, we discussed the association between perceived risk and preferences or intentions in general (i.e., on average). However, these associations could vary depending on one's cultural characteristics. Contrary to our hypothesis, which predicted a stronger association between perceived severity and preferences or intentions among more interdependent people, we found that the association between the perceived severity of face loss and intention (and possibly preferences) was stronger among people with higher independent self-construal. One possible explanation is that people with higher independent self-construal have less need to justify their preferences or choices to others, so they may rely more on personal feelings when forming preference judgments and making choices [103]. Although earlier work tended to theorize risk perception as a purely or predominantly cognitive process [104], some studies in the past two decades have found positive associations between affective feelings and risk perception in some contexts [105, 106]. When participants answer questions about the perceived severity of face loss, which may be very emotionally uncomfortable, they may plausibly rely more on their feelings, especially if they are predominantly independent. Because such risk perception is partly associated with feelings, it may then relate more strongly to preferences and behavioral intentions among independent people [103].

Our findings are also conceptually consistent with studies showing a stronger association between concern about (or perceived severity of) environmental problems and pro-environmental behavioral intentions or behavior in more individualistic societies [107, 108]. This may be because people with stronger independent self-construal consider personal attributes (including the perceived severity of an issue) more important. Personal attitudes are also more closely aligned with behavioral intentions or behaviors among people who are more individualistic or have higher independent self-construal [107] [108] [109]. People with stronger independent selfconstrual prioritize consistency, whereas consistency may matter less to people with lower independent self-construal [110]. In the context of our study, consider people with stronger independent self-construal who perceive a higher risk of face loss for face-to-face interventions than for digital interventions. For these people, preferring or intending to engage in digital interventions would be more consistent with their risk perception. In contrast, among people with lower independent self-construal, the association between the perceived severity of face loss and preferences or behavioral intentions becomes weaker or nonsignificant, possibly because they care much less about consistency. Our explanations are speculative and require replication or follow-up studies. Further research can also examine the potential mechanisms, including mediating or moderating variables such as reliance on feelings [103] and self-consistency [110].

Apart from self-construal, we found no support for the hypothesized moderation of regulatory focus. We based our hypothesis on research into the association between regulatory focus and risk tolerance in financial and physician decision making [56, 57], but not preferences or behavioral intentions for help-seeking in mental health contexts. It is also important to recognize that the association between regulatory focus and risk taking is not as straightforward as previously assumed. Some studies have found that some prevention-focused people may be more willing to take risks when they need to restore the status quo after experiencing financial losses [111, 112]. However, whether such effects are generalizable to mental health intervention contexts is uncertain.

Although self-construal moderated the association, we found no support for a moderating effect of culture (Asian regions: Hong Kong and India; Western regions: Canada and the United Kingdom) on the association between perceived severity and preferences or intentions. Findings were relatively consistent across regions: all four regions showed significant associations between differences in perceived severity and differences in preferences. This may be because the differences in self-construal between regions did not even reach significance. Surprisingly, participants from the United Kingdom and Canada scored higher on interdependent self-construal than on independent self-construal. This finding was unexpected and contradicts some earlier studies and claims about cultural differences in self-construal and individualism/collectivism [60, 64]. One explanation may be the particular characteristics of participants at the Canadian and British universities where we collected data. Another explanation is that cross-cultural differences in self-construal are relatively weak and inconsistent across samples, as shown in Levine et al. 's [68] meta-analysis of cross-cultural selfconstrual research and in more recent studies [113]. We also note that ethnic minorities (non-Caucasian participants, including some Asian and African participants) made up a substantial proportion of our Canadian (30.99%) and British (29.29%) samples. However, our exploratory analyses (see Supplementary Table S5) found no significant differences in self-construal between Caucasian and Asian participants.

Across different regions, a larger proportion of participants were predominantly preventionfocused and interdependent, yet more than 35% of participants in each region were predominantly promotion-focused and independent (see Supplementary Tables S5 and S6).

Given this diversity in individual characteristics (e.g., self-construal) within each region, researchers and practitioners may focus more on individual differences than on cultural background. Individual differences in self-construal appear to play a more meaningful role in explaining the associations between differences in risk perception and differences in preferences or intentions.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Research Directions

One of the strengths of our study is that multi-regional data increases the generalizability of the findings. Cross-regional studies on digital mental health versus in-person intervention preferences or perceptions are rare. We encourage more such studies to test whether findings generalize and how they vary across cultures. Because our findings contradict some crosscultural studies on self-construal, future studies could examine the potential role of culture in the association between perceived risk and preferences or intentions, using more representative or more diverse samples.

Regarding generalizability, because our participants were college students, it is uncertain, and possibly limited, how far our findings extend to the general population. As discussed earlier, the perceived likelihood of being found out when using face-to-face interventions may be higher in campus settings, because many people who study or work at a university likely know where its counseling or psychotherapy center is located. Future studies could examine whether, among working adults and older adults, the perceived likelihood of being found out and the perceived severity of face loss differ substantially between face-to-face and digital interventions.

Our work takes a pioneering step by comparing the perceived risks of face-to-face and digital mental health interventions, measuring risk as both likelihood and severity. However, we did not examine other operationalizations of risk perception. Studies comparing perceptions of, and preferences for, face-to-face and digital mental health interventions rarely examine risk perception. In contrast, various operationalizations of risk perception are frequently studied in judgment and decision making, social psychology, and health psychology [106, 114, 115]. We hope our study will inspire research on other operationalizations of risk perception (e.g., intuition-based perceived vulnerability and affective risk perception [114]). We also hope it will inspire research on the perceived risks of other potential threats associated with engaging in faceto-face versus digital interventions (e.g., professional or occupational risks, risks of adverse effects, and privacy risks). Such research may help explain individual differences in preferences for, and choices between, digital and face-to-face interventions.

Furthermore, we found that self-construal moderated the association between differences in perceived severity and differences in preferences or intentions. However, the direction of this effect was opposite to what we expected, and its mechanisms require further investigation. Such effects may be due to differences in reliance of feelings on behavior and prioritization for selfconsistency between people with different levels of independent self-construal. Because our study did not measure these variables directly, future studies may try to replicate our findings and examine potential mechanisms. They may do so by including additional mediating or moderating variables, notably self-consistency [110] and reliance on feelings [103].

Implications and Future Directions

Pending further research investigations before widespread implementations, our findings may have potential implications for designs of future decision aid for in-person vs digital interventions [14] and mental health practitioners when working with individuals with higher perceived severity of face loss, who had experienced severe face loss, and/or people who are more independent. For these individuals, face loss and the perceived risk of face loss may discourage them from continuing to see a psychotherapist and from disclosing comfortably during therapy sessions. Psychotherapists and psychotherapy researchers may explore ways to address concerns regarding the negative consequences of face loss and the perceived risk of face loss, for example through strength-based approaches [39]. It is possible that mitigating the experienced and the perceived severity of face loss may result in higher attendances or engagements in psychotherapy, but further research is needed to examine this possibility before applying in the real-world. In addition, decision aid for face-to-face and/or digital mental health support seeking decisions [14], may consider incorporating face loss concerns, actual, and perceived risks, as potential attributes in the design, going beyond effectiveness and limitations.

Our findings may therefore also have implications for the growing literature of mental health communication and promotion[116] [117] [118] [119] [120] [121], pending further studies that may involve refinements of messages. To address the concerns of people who perceive a very high risk of face loss in face-to-face interventions, practitioners, mental health promoters, and researchers may test whether digital mental health platforms are promoted more effectively when messages emphasize that using these platforms in private, personal spaces carries a lower risk of face loss or less concerns of face loss.ConclusionTo conclude, our work contributes to the growing literature of perceptions and preferences for psychological interventions, with novel, strong and cross-regionally generalizable findings of substantially higher perceived risks, in terms of both likelihood and severity, through face-toface psychological interventions compared to digital psychological interventions. Furthermore, higher perceived severity for face-to-face interventions compared to digital interventions is associated with lower preferences or intentions for face-to-face interventions compared to digital interventions. Such association is moderated by independent self-construal, with stronger association among more independent individuals. More studies are needed to investigate the mechanisms of such surprising findings. Finally, we call for further studies extending on our pioneering study on perceived risks by examining different forms of perceived risks of digital and face-to-face interventions and their associations with preferences, intentions, and decisionmaking of interventions, while considering individual characteristics and cultural factors.
