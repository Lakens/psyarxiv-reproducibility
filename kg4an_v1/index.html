How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Personality traits are long-established correlates of occupational success. Recently, researchers have also begun to examine the role of social, emotional, and behavioral (SEB) skills in key life domains. Conceptualized as functional capacities rather than characteristic tendencies, SEB skills represent what individuals are capable of doing when wanted or needed. The present research examined how the five major skill domains assessed by the Behavioral, Emotional, and Social Skills Inventory (BESSI) relate with consequential work criteria and outcomes. Specifically, using a demographically representative sample of 1,064 working adults, we tested preregistered links of SEB skills with work performance, attainment, attitudes, intentions, behaviors, interests, and values. SEB skills related robustly with these criteria and outcomes, with different skill domains showing distinctive relationships. For example, Self-Management Skills were linked with self-perceived performance; Social Engagement Skills with occupational attainment; Cooperation Skills with prosocial interests and values; Emotional Resilience Skills with positive work attitudes; and Innovation Skills with creative interests and values. Short forms of the BESSI retained considerable predictive power, making them highly suitable for applied contexts. These findings highlight the key role of functional capacities in occupational experiences and provide a foundation for integrating SEB skills into personnel selection, career coaching, and organizational research.

Putting Social, Emotional, and Behavioral Skills to Work: SEB Skills Are Robustly Linked with Occupational Attainment, Attitudes, Behaviors, Interests, and Values

People's career trajectories, from the jobs they select to their performance and ultimate success, are shaped by many factors. Beyond technical knowledge, these factors also include interpersonal and self-regulatory capabilities. These latter characteristics, sometimes colloquially termed "soft skills," are increasingly recognized as important determinants of life success, because they relate with criteria and outcomes ranging from academic achievement and vocational interests to workplace attitudes and performance (Heckman & Kautz, 2012). To bring conceptual clarity to this domain, researchers have recently organized these capabilities under the framework of social, emotional, and behavioral (SEB) skills. Specifically, SEB skills are defined as a person's capacities to maintain social relationships, regulate emotions, manage goaldirected behaviors, and learn from novel experiences (Soto et al., 2022). By focusing on functional capacities, SEB skills capture what individuals are capable of doing, beyond what they typically do. Understanding these skills is important for both organizational science and applied practice, because they provide a framework for conceptualizing and assessing the capabilities that individuals need to succeed in their careers.

Despite the growing recognition of SEB skills, empirical research investigating their specific implications for the workplace is still emerging. While a robust literature demonstrates that general personality traits relate with occupational success, researchers have only recently begun to systematically examine the distinctive role of SEB skills (Chen et al., 2024; Soto et al., 2022 Soto et al., , 2024)). Consequently, the present research addresses two fundamental questions. First, do the major domains of SEB skills relate with a broad array of consequential work criteria and outcomes? To answer this question, we examine how SEB skills relate with an extensive set of indicators, including occupational performance and attainment, work attitudes, workplace intentions and behaviors, vocational interests, and work values. Second, are these skill-outcome links retained when assessing skills with brief measurement tools? To address this, we explore whether the specific links and overall predictive power of SEB skills are retained when using short-form assessments, which are often preferred in applied organizational contexts.

Conceptualizing and Assessing Social, Emotional, and Behavioral Skills

Social, emotional, and behavioral skills represent the functional capabilities that enable individuals to navigate interpersonal, intrapersonal, and task-directed challenges. Historically, researchers and practitioners have proposed a dizzying array of frameworks for 21st century competencies, noncognitive skills, soft skills, durable skills, and related constructs (Berg et al., 2017). However, recent taxonomic work (Abrahams et al., 2019; Casillas et al., 2023; OECD, 2015; Soto et al., 2021 Soto et al., , 2022;; Walton et al., 2021) has consistently found that many specific SEB skills can be organized in terms of five major domains: Self-Management Skills (used to effectively pursue goals and complete tasks), Social Engagement Skills (used to actively engage with other people), Cooperation Skills (used to maintain positive social relationships), Emotional Resilience Skills (used to regulate emotions and moods), and Innovation Skills (used to engage with novel ideas and experiences).

Notably, these five SEB skill domains closely resemble the widely studied Big Five personality traits in terms of their behavioral referents (John et al., 2008). Both Self-Management Skills and trait Conscientiousness center on task-and goal-related behaviors; Social Engagement Skills and trait Extraversion on agentic social behaviors; Cooperation Skills and trait Agreeableness on prosocial behaviors; Emotional Resilience Skills and trait Emotional Stability on behaviors to regulate negative affect; and Innovation Skills and Openness to Experience on cognitive and behavioral responses to novelty. It is therefore unsurprising that traits and skills relate substantially with each other, while still remaining conceptually and empirically distinguishable (Ringwald et al., 2026; Soto et al., 2022 Soto et al., , 2024)). Specifically, traits describe behavioral tendencies (how someone tends to behave, averaged across different kinds of situations), whereas skills more directly capture behavioral capacities (how someone is capable of behaving when they want or need to do so in a particular situation). For instance, an employee may tend to be disorganized in their personal life (low trait Conscientiousness), but also be quite capable of managing their goals, tasks, and time while completing important work projects (high Self-Management Skills).

From an organizational science perspective, the difference between functional capacities and behavioral tendencies resembles the established distinction between maximum and typical job performance (Sackett et al., 1988). Whereas typical performance reflects a worker's baseline, day-to-day output, maximum performance captures what that worker can achieve when they are deliberately maximizing their effort. Thus, personality traits are akin to typical performance, whereas SEB skills are analogous to maximum performance. Maximum workplace performance has distinctive correlates (Beus & Whitman, 2012; Klehe & Anderson, 2007; Marcus et al., 2007; Turner, 1978), suggesting that SEB skills may also be linked with consequential work criteria and outcomes.

Because SEB skills are defined as functional capacities, they should be measured using methods that align with this conceptualization. Traditional personality inventories ask respondents to rate how accurately a series of adjectives or statements describe their typical behavior, thereby capturing characteristic tendencies (i.e., traits). However, measuring functional capacities requires assessing how well an individual can perform specific behaviors, rather than how often they typically enact them (Soto et al., 2021). The Behavioral, Emotional, and Social Skills Inventory (BESSI; Soto et al., 2022) was developed to address this need. The BESSI explicitly prompts respondents to rate their proficiency across a series of 192 specific behaviors.

These ratings are then aggregated into scores indexing the five major SEB skill domains. The BESSI has demonstrated considerable reliability and validity in previous studies using student, community, and online samples (Napolitano et al., 2025; Ringwald et al., 2026; Sewell et al., 2025; Soto et al., 2022 Soto et al., , 2023 Soto et al., , 2024;; Yoon et al., 2024), and was therefore selected to assess SEB skills in the present research.

Traits, Skills, and Consequential Work Criteria and Outcomes

An extensive research literature spanning industrial-organizational and personality psychology has established robust links of personality traits with a broad range of workplace criteria and outcomes. Although there is now emerging interest in the organizational implications of SEB skills, researchers have only recently begun to examine these relationships using the taxonomic framework defined and assessed by the BESSI. Therefore, the primary goal of the present research is to examine how SEB skills relate with a broad range of consequential work criteria and outcomes. Because the major SEB skill domains parallel the Big Five traits in terms of their behavioral referents, the established trait literature provides a strong foundation for predicting how these skills will function in occupational contexts. Specifically, meta-analyses examining personality traits in the workplace, as well as recent studies using the BESSI (Chen et al., 2024; Soto et al., 2022 Soto et al., , 2024)), can be used to derive hypotheses regarding the expected links of SEB skills with consequential work criteria and outcomes, while also acknowledging that the conceptual and empirical distinction between traits as behavioral tendencies and skills as functional capacities introduces some uncertainty concerning these predictions.

Regarding occupational attainment and job performance, extensive meta-analytic research demonstrates that personality traits are meaningful correlates of both objective career success and day-to-day productivity. For example, trait Conscientiousness and Extraversion often show modest positive associations with higher earnings, occupational prestige, and promotional advancement (Alderotti et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2005; Vella, 2024). Similarly, trait Conscientiousness is recognized as a general correlate of job performance across occupations, while Extraversion relates with performance in specific roles, such as management and teamwork-intensive positions (Barrick et al., 2001). Because SEB skills represent the functional capacities that individuals deploy to navigate workplace demands, multiple skill domains may relate with these outcomes. We therefore investigate whether Self-Management Skills, which encompass capacities for goal and task regulation, and Social Engagement Skills, which include capacities for group leadership and persuasive communication, are the most relevant skill correlates of occupational attainment and self-perceived job performance.

Turning to work attitudes, foundational meta-analytic reviews demonstrate that personality traits relate with how employees experience and evaluate their work environments.

Low trait Emotional Stability serves as a vulnerability factor that predisposes individuals to job burnout, whereas Extraversion protects against this negative outcome (Swider & Zimmerman, 2010). Trait Conscientiousness, as well as the corresponding skill domain of Self-Management, consistently relate with job satisfaction (Chen et al., 2024; Judge et al., 2002). Both Conscientiousness and Openness to Experience show distinctive relationships with work engagement (Fukuzaki & Iwata, 2022), whereas all of the Big Five traits other than Openness are moderately linked with organizational commitment (Choi et al., 2015). These findings suggest that specific SEB skill domains should provide the functional capacities needed to navigate workplace stressors and build rewarding occupational experiences. We therefore hypothesize that (a) Self-Management Skills will positively relate with job satisfaction, because the capacity to systematically execute work tasks should facilitate the attainment and enjoyment of workplace rewards; (b) Self-Management and Innovation Skills will positively relate with work engagement, because these capacities enable workers to maintain disciplined effort and adapt proactively to job demands; (c) Emotional Resilience and Social Engagement Skills will negatively relate with burnout, because emotional resilience protects against the exhaustion associated with chronic stress, while social engagement prevents psychological distancing and depersonalization; and (d) multiple skill domains (Self-Management, Social Engagement, Cooperation, and Emotional Resilience) will positively relate with organizational commitment, reflecting the varied pathways (such as achievement, relationship-building, and effectively coping with challenges) through which employees build strong psychological attachments to their organizations.

Regarding work behaviors and intentions, previous meta-analytic research has summarized how personality traits relate with employees' discretionary actions and turnover intentions. For example, the tendency to engage in positive organizational citizenship behavior relates modestly with multiple trait domains (Chiaburu et al., 2011). Conversely, counterproductive work behavior represents actions that harm the organization or its members, and relates distinctively with low Conscientiousness and Emotional Stability, as well as the corresponding skill domains of low Self-Management and Emotional Resilience (Berry et al., 2007; Chen et al., 2024). Similarly, for the cognitive process of withdrawal, specifically turnover intentions, low Emotional Stability serves as the primary trait correlate of intentions to quit (Zimmerman, 2008). The parallels between behavioral tendencies and functional capacities suggest that key SEB skills should relate with how well employees navigate opportunities and challenges in the workplace. We specifically hypothesize that Self-Management and Emotional Resilience Skills will relate negatively with counterproductive work behavior, because the capacities to regulate impulses and manage negative emotions can help prevent harmful actions in response to workplace stressors. Moreover, we hypothesize that Emotional Resilience Skills will relate negatively with turnover intentions, because the capacity to cope with work-related stress protects employees against the emotional exhaustion that typically precipitates the desire to leave an organization. Finally, because citizenship encompasses a wide array of prosocial and proactive behaviors, we explore whether multiple SEB skill domains show modest, positive relations with organizational citizenship behavior.

As for vocational interests, meta-analytic work has mapped meaningful connections between personality traits and preferences for particular occupational environments. Most notably, this work aligns the Big Five traits with Holland's typology of realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising, and conventional interests (Barrick et al., 2003). For example, trait Openness to Experience is linked with investigative and artistic interests, reflecting a shared emphasis on intellectual curiosity and creativity. Moreover, trait Extraversion relates with enterprising interests, whereas Agreeableness relates with social interests, demonstrating that energetic and cooperative dispositions fit with work environments requiring persuasion and interpersonal support, respectively. Indeed, recent research with student samples has found links between these interests and the corresponding SEB skills domains (Soto et al., 2022 (Soto et al., , 2024)). We therefore expect that specific SEB skill domains will align with vocational interests among working adults. Specifically, we hypothesize that (a) Innovation Skills will relate positively with investigative and artistic interests, reflecting the capacity to engage with novel ideas and complex abstraction; (b) Cooperation Skills will relate positively with social interests, because the capacity to maintain positive relationships is important for roles focused on helping and collaborating; and (c) Social Engagement Skills will relate positively with enterprising interests, given that the capacity to actively lead and persuade others is a key asset in competitive business environments.

Regarding work values, the extant literature shows that personality traits are systematically linked with the personal values that individuals are most motivated to pursue.

Drawing on Schwartz's universal values framework (Schwartz, 1992), this research indicates that the Big Five traits map predictably onto broad motivational dimensions. For example, selfenhancement values align with trait Extraversion, conservation values with Conscientiousness, Self-Transcendence values with both Agreeableness and Openness to Experience, and openness to change values with trait Openness and Extraversion (Parks-Leduc et al., 2015). In the workplace, these general motivational goals manifest as specific work values, such as altruism, prestige, autonomy, and security (Cable & Edwards, 2004)

The Importance of Brief Assessments in Applied Contexts

Beyond the conceptual hypotheses regarding links of SEB skills with work criteria and outcomes derived above, there is also the practical question of how to measure SEB skills in applied settings. Although comprehensive skill inventories like the BESSI can robustly assess individuals' functional capacities, the demands of industrial-organizational research and realworld organizational practice often dictate a need for brevity. In applied contexts, such as largescale employee surveys, selection batteries, and talent development programs, practitioners often face constraints regarding administration time and respondent attention. Lengthy assessments require employees to take time away from their primary work tasks, and can also induce survey fatigue that compromises data quality and reduces completion rates. Thus, in applied settings, brief assessments may capture essential skill domains without imposing undue burdens on workers (e.g., Stanton et al., 2002). However, such measures necessarily sacrifice some reliability and validity for gains in efficiency, and these tradeoffs are most pronounced for extremely brief assessments (Credé et al., 2012; Soto & John, 2019).

To meet the need for efficient assessment of SEB skills, Sewell and colleagues (2025) recently developed and validated three short forms of the BESSI. Although the original, 192item BESSI is a comprehensive measure of functional capacities, its length can preclude use in time-restricted research and applied contexts. Therefore, Sewell and colleagues (2025) used conceptual and empirical criteria to construct 96-item, 45-item, and 20-item versions of the inventory. The BESSI-96 reduces administration time to approximately eight minutes, while the BESSI-45 and BESSI-20 require only about four minutes and two minutes to complete, respectively. Importantly, analyses of multiple independent samples across two languages and cultural contexts indicated that these short forms retain the five-domain structure and strong reliability of the full inventory. Moreover, they preserve the vast majority of the original measure's predictive power, ensuring that researchers and practitioners can efficiently assess individuals' SEB skills.

Building on the initial validity evidence for these short forms, it remains important to determine whether their robust psychometric characteristics extend to relations with consequential work criteria and outcomes. Therefore, a secondary goal of the present research is to explore whether links of SEB skills with work criteria and outcomes are retained when using the BESSI short forms. By systematically comparing the overall predictive power and the pattern of skill-outcome associations across the full-length inventory and its abbreviated versions, we aim to provide guidance for researchers and practitioners who wish to use efficient measurement tools in applied workplace settings.

Participants were 1,064 full-time working adults in the United States. Quota sampling was used to ensure that the sample was approximately representative of the working adult population in terms of age (9.4% ages 18-24, 25.8% ages 25-34, 24.8% ages 35-44, 20.2% ages 45-54, 19.8% ages 55 to 73, M = 41.43, SD = 12.77), and balanced in terms of sex (50.1% male, 49.9% female). Measures

Social, Emotional, and Behavioral Skills

Self-Management, Social Engagement, Cooperation, Emotional Resilience, and Innovation Skills were assessed using the Behavioral, Emotional, and Social Skills Inventory (BESSI; Soto et al., 2022). This measure's 192 items are brief descriptions of behavior (e.g., "lead a group of people," "show up for things on time," "stay calm in stressful situations").

Participants rated how well they could perform each behavior on a five-point competence scale ranging from1 = not at all well to5 = extremely well. To compare skill-outcome relations and overall predictive power across longer and shorter versions of the BESSI, we scored the five major skill domains from the full-length inventory, as well as from the item subsets included in its 96-item, 45-item, and 20-item short forms (BESSI-96, BESSI-45, and BESSI-20; Sewell et al., 2025). In the present sample, McDonald's omega reliabilities on the full-length BESSI were .98 for Self-Management, .95 for Social Engagement, .94 for Cooperation, .97 for Emotional Resilience, and .94 for Innovation Skills. Omega reliabilities ranged from .90 to .95 for the BESSI-96, from .81 to .92 for the BESSI-45, and from .68 to .83 for the BESSI-20.

Current Occupation, Attainment, Managerial Level, and Earnings

Participants responded to questions from the American Community Survey (U.S. Census Bureau, 2024) to assess their current type of employment (e.g., private-sector employee, government employee, self-employed), kind of business or industry, main occupation, and most important activities or duties. They then responded to questions assessing their managerial level, number of hours worked per week, and income earned from this job. Managerial level was coded as1 = do not supervise employees,2 = supervise employees who are not managers or supervisors,3 = supervise other managers or supervisors. Earnings were scaled to dollars per year and then natural log-transformed to reduce positive skew.

Occupation and occupational attainment were coded using the O*NET Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) system, based on responses to the survey questions assessing type of employment, kind of business or industry, main occupation, most important activities or duties, and managerial level. Coding was independently performed by a human researcher (the second author) and an artificial intelligence (AI) agent (Google Gemini 3.1 Pro). Discrepancies were then resolved through review and discussion by the first and second authors. There was exact agreement between the human and AI coders in 75.5% of cases (κ = .74) at the SOC twodigit (major group) level, 65.9% of cases (κ = .66) at the three-digit (minor group) level, 60.8% of cases (κ = .61) at the five-digit (broad occupation) level, and 56.1% of cases (κ = .56) at the eight-digit (detailed occupation) level. Regarding discrepancies, 55.2% were resolved by adopting the human-suggested code and 44.8% by adopting the AI-suggested code. These agreement rates are very similar to those observed between expert human coders, suggesting that AI tools can supplement but not necessarily replace human coding expertise (Putka et al., 2023; Russ et al., 2016).

After finalizing the eight-digit occupation codes, the O*NET job zone for each occupation was recorded as an index of occupational attainment. Job zones represent groups of occupations that are similar in terms of the amount of education, related experience, and on-the job training needed to successfully perform the work (2 = very little to some preparation,3 = medium preparation,4 = considerable preparation,5 = extensive preparation).

Perceived Job Performance

Self-perceived job performance was assessed using the In-Role Behavior scale (Williams & Anderson, 1991). This measure's seven items are statements (e.g., "I adequately complete assigned duties," "I neglect aspects of the job that I am obligated to perform") that participants rated on a seven-point agreement scale ranging from1 = disagree strongly to7 = agree strongly.

In the present sample, perceived job performance scores had an omega reliability of .76.

Work Attitudes

Job satisfaction was assessed using the Michigan Organizational Assessment Questionnaire-Job Satisfaction Subscale (MOAQ-JSS; Cammann et al., 1983)

Work Behaviors and Intentions

Organizational citizenship behavior was assessed using the Organizational Citizenship Behavior Checklist-10-Item Version (OCB-C-10; Spector et al., 2010), counterproductive work behavior using the Counterproductive Work Behavior Checklist-10-Item Version (CWB-C-10; Spector et al., 2010), and turnover intentions using the Michigan Organizational Assessment Questionnaire-Turnover Intention Subscale (MOAQ-TIS; Cammann et al., 1983). The OCB-C-10 items (e.g., "took time to advise, coach, or mentor a co-worker," "offered suggestions to improve how work is done") and CWB-C-10 items (e.g., "purposely wasted your employer's materials or supplies," "complained about insignificant things at work") are descriptions of behavior that participants rated on a five-point frequency scale ranging from1 = never to5 = every day. The three MOAQ-TIS items are statements ("I often seriously consider leaving my current job," "I intend to quit my current job," "I have started to look for other jobs") that participants rated on a seven-point agreement scale ranging from1 = strongly disagree to7 = strongly agree. In the present sample, omega reliabilities were .86 for organizational citizenship behavior, .85 for counterproductive work behavior, and .94 for turnover intentions.

Vocational Interests

Realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising, and conventional interests were assessed using the O*NET Mini Interest Profiler (Mini-IP; Rounds et al., 2016). This measure's 30 items are brief descriptions of work-related tasks (e.g., "build kitchen cabinets," "develop a new medicine," "manage a department within a large company") that participants rated on a fivepoint preference scale ranging from1 = strongly dislike to5 = strongly like. In the present sample, omega reliabilities were .80 for realistic interests, .81 for investigative interests, .83 for artistic interests, .73 for social interests, .75 for enterprising interests, and .74 for conventional interests.

Work Values

Work values reflecting self-transcendence (altruism and relationships with others), selfenhancement (pay and prestige), conservation (security and authority), and openness to change (variety and autonomy) were assessed using the Work Values Survey (WVS; Cable & Edwards, 2004). This measure's 24 items are job characteristics (e.g., "forming relationships with coworkers," "salary level," "being certain of keeping my job") that participants rated on a fivepoint importance scale ranging from1 = not at all important to5 = extremely important. In the present sample, omega reliabilities were .89 for altruism, .93 for relationships with others, .89 for prestige, .85 for pay, .90 for security, .83 for authority, .85 for variety, and .85 for autonomy.

Results

Preliminary Analyses of Age and Gender Differences

Descriptive statistics for the BESSI skill domains, as well as correlations with age and gender, are presented in Table 2; descriptive statistics and correlations between all study variables are presented in Supplemental Table S1. These results indicate that older adults tended to score slightly higher on Self-Management, Social Engagement, and Emotional Resilience skills, whereas younger adults scored higher on Innovation Skills. Regarding gender, women tended to score slightly higher on Cooperation Skills, whereas men scored higher on Emotional Resilience Skills. However, all of these correlations were small in size (absolute r ≤ 0.10), indicating relatively modest demographic differences in SEB skills.

Links of SEB Skill Domains with Work Criteria and Outcomes

Our primary, preregistered analyses tested links of SEB skill domains with consequential work criteria and outcomes. Specifically, we (a) computed zero-order correlations of skills with criteria and outcomes, (b) computed partial correlations controlling for age and gender, (c) regressed each criteria or outcome on the set of five skill domains (to control for overlap between the skill domains themselves), and (d) regressed each criteria or outcome on the five skill domains, age, and gender. The partial correlations and regression coefficients controlling for age and gender are presented in Table 3; additional results, including zero-order correlations, regression coefficients without demographic controls, and confidence intervals, are presented in Supplemental Table S2.

Supporting the relevance of SEB skills for work criteria and outcomes, 25 of our 28 preregistered skill-outcome hypotheses were statistically significant (p < .05) across all four sets of analyses. Moreover, an exploratory search identified a total of 40 skill-outcome links that were consistently significant at an even stricter threshold (p < .001) across all analyses. We therefore focus our interpretation on these highly robust skill-outcome links.

Regarding occupational attainment and self-perceived performance, Social Engagement Skills related consistently with occupational attainment (β = .19 when controlling for gender, age, and the other four skill domains), managerial level (β = .43), and earnings (β = .20), while Self-Management Skills related robustly with perceived performance (β = .52). As for work attitudes, Emotional Resilience Skills related consistently with job satisfaction (β = .28), organizational commitment (β = .20), work engagement (β = .22), and low burnout (β = -.41); work engagement was also linked with Social Engagement Skills (β = .24).

Concerning work behaviors and intentions, organizational citizenship behavior related with the combination of Social Engagement (β = .31), Cooperation (β = .22), and Innovation Skills (β = .15). Self-Management Skills related with low counterproductive work behavior (β = -.27) and turnover intentions (β = -.19); the latter was also linked with Emotional Resilience Skills (β = -.16).

As for vocational interests, Innovation Skills related robustly with both artistic (β = .75) and investigative (β = .36) interests, Social Engagement Skills related with enterprising interests (β = .50), and Emotional Resilience Skills related with conventional interests (β = .23). Social interests related with the combination of Cooperation (β = .44), Social Engagement (β = .20), and Innovation Skills (β = .16), whereas realistic interests related with Emotional Resilience (β = .25) and Innovation Skills (β = .19).

Regarding work values, Cooperation Skills related robustly with the self-transcendence values of relationships with others (β = .53) and altruism (β = .50); the former was also linked with Social Engagement Skills (β = .27), whereas the latter was linked with Innovation Skills (β = .19). Social Engagement Skills related consistently with the self-enhancement values of prestige (β = .47) and pay (β = .23); the latter was also linked with Self-Management Skills (β = .24). Self-Management Skills related consistently with the conservation values of security (β = .41) and authority (β = .19); the former was also linked with Cooperation Skills (β = .20), whereas the latter was linked with Social Engagement Skills (β = .36). Finally, Innovation Skills related consistently with the openness to change values of variety (β = .32) and autonomy (β = .22); the former was also linked with Social Engagement (β = .19) and Cooperation (β = .15) Skills, whereas the latter was linked with Self-Management Skills (β = .19).

Predictive Power of the BESSI Short Forms

Beyond these primary analyses, we also conducted exploratory analyses investigating the extent to which the BESSI short forms (BESSI-96, BESSI-45, and BESSI-20) reproduced the skill-outcome links and overall predictive power demonstrated by the full-length BESSI skill domains. To do this, we repeated the four sets of analyses described above (zero-order correlations, partial correlations controlling for age and gender, and regressions with and without demographic controls) for each of the BESSI short forms. We then computed vector correlations (i.e., the correlation between the set of five coefficients for the full-length BESSI domains vs. a set of five short-form domains) to index the consistency of skill-outcome associations, and computed explained-variance ratios (i.e., R² when predicting a work criterion or outcome from a BESSI short form divided by R² when predicting from the full-length BESSI) to index the BESSI short forms' predictive power relative to the full-length measure. The similarity vector correlations and explained-variance ratios from these analyses are presented in Table 4, and additional results are presented in Supplemental Tables S3 to S5.

Regarding consistency in the pattern of skill-outcome associations, when averaged across the 25 work criteria and outcomes, vector correlations comparing the set of five skill domain coefficients between the full-length BESSI and BESSI-96 averaged greater than .99 when comparing the zero-order correlations, regressions without demographic controls, and regressions with demographic controls; they averaged .98 when comparing the partial correlations controlling for age and gender. For the BESSI-45, the vector correlations averaged .98 when comparing the zero-order or partial correlations, and averaged .99 when comparing the regressions with or without demographic controls. Finally, for the BESSI-20, the vector correlations averaged .97 when comparing the zero-order correlations, .94 when comparing the partial correlations, and .96 when comparing the regressions with or without demographic controls. Of the 40 skill-outcome links that were highly significant (p < .001) across all four sets of analyses for the full-length BESSI, 38 remained highly significant across all analyses for the BESSI-96, all 40 were highly significant for the BESSI-45, and 37 were highly significant for the BESSI-20.

As for the overall predictive power of the short forms, when averaged across work criteria and outcomes, the BESSI-96 skill domains retained approximately 97% of the full-length BESSI's predictive power when excluding demographic controls, or 98% after including such controls. The BESSI-45 skill domains retained approximately 94%, and the BESSI-20 about 85%, of the full-length measure's predictive power, regardless of whether demographic controls were included or excluded.

Taken together, these results indicate that the pattern of links of SEB skill domains with work criteria and outcomes is very similar between the full-length BESSI and its short forms. Moreover, they show that the BESSI-96 and BESSI-45 skill domains retain almost all of the full-length measure's predictive power, whereas the BESSI-20 shows a more substantial reduction in predictive power.

Discussion

The present findings support two key conclusions. First, SEB skills relate robustly with a broad range of consequential work criteria and outcomes, with different skill domains showing distinctive relationships with different criteria and outcomes. Even when evaluated against a strict standard of evidence (p < .001 across four sets of analyses), Self-Management Skills related consistently with self-perceived job performance, low counterproductive work behavior and turnover intentions, and valuing pay, security, authority, and autonomy. Social Engagement Skills related consistently with occupational attainment, managerial level, earnings, work engagement, organizational citizenship behavior, social and enterprising interests, and valuing relationships with others, pay, prestige, authority and variety. Cooperation Skills related consistently with organizational citizenship behavior, social interests, and valuing altruism, relationships with others, security, and variety. Emotional Resilience Skills related consistently with job satisfaction, organizational commitment, work engagement, low burnout and turnover intentions, and realistic and conventional interests. Finally, Innovation Skills related consistently with organizational citizenship behavior, realistic, investigative, artistic, and social interests, and valuing altruism, variety, and autonomy. Notably, every one of the 25 consequential work criteria and outcomes examined here was consistently linked with at least one SEB skill domain, and each skill domain related with a distinctive set of criteria and outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of social, emotional, and behavioral skills for understanding the occupations that people choose to pursue, their reasons for doing so, and their performance, attainment, attitudes, intentions, and behaviors once on the job.

A second key conclusion is that when predicting work criteria and outcomes from the five major SEB skill domains, the BESSI-96 and BESSI-45 short forms preserve almost all of the full-length BESSI's predictive power, as well as the distinctive patterns of skill-outcome relations. The BESSI-20 also preserves the general pattern of skill-outcome relations, but with a more substantial reduction in overall predictive power.

Broader Implications for Research and Practice

These findings have important implications for both researchers and practitioners interested in SEB skills and occupational success. For example, by linking social, emotional, and behavioral skills with work performance, attainment, attitudes, intentions, behaviors, interests, and values, the present findings further extend the nomological network of SEB skills into the workplace (Chen et al., 2024). Whereas previous studies of SEB skills have predominantly focused on adolescent and young adult student populations (Napolitano et al., 2025; Soto et al., 2023 Soto et al., , 2024;; Yoon et al., 2024), the present research examines such skills among working adults.

It therefore advances our knowledge regarding the lifelong importance of SEB skills across life domains and developmental periods. Moreover, establishing the relevance of these capabilities for working adults also highlights a need for future research to investigate whether and how SEB skills relate to functioning in other adult contexts, such as family dynamics, community involvement, health, and quality of life.

A second key implication concerns personnel selection and assessment. Traditionally, research and practice in this domain have focused on personality traits as correlates of occupational success (Barrick et al., 2001; Judge et al., 2002). However, the present findings indicate that SEB skills also relate with a wide array of consequential work criteria and outcomes. This aligns with previous scholarship in industrial-organizational psychology that has examined the correlates of maximum performance, which is analogous to SEB skills as behavioral capacities (Beus & Whitman, 2012; Klehe & Anderson, 2007; Marcus et al., 2007; Sackett et al., 1988). However, work on maximum performance has often relied on specific, resource-intensive, task-based performance assessments, whereas the present findings show that efficient, broadband skill inventories such as the BESSI can be used to reliably and validly assess capacities across multiple domains of social, emotional, and behavioral functioning.

Incorporating SEB skill assessments to evaluate functional capacities could thus provide researchers and practitioners with a more comprehensive evaluation of individuals' potential in the workplace.

A related implication pertains to career coaching and vocational guidance. The present findings suggest that individuals with different SEB skill profiles gravitate toward different occupations, derive meaning and value from different kinds of workplace rewards, and succeed in different ways. For example, individuals with highly developed Innovation Skills may be drawn to investigative and artistic careers, placing special value on the autonomy and variety afforded by this work. In contrast, workers with strong Social Engagement Skills may show a preference for enterprising roles, highly value occupational prestige, and consistently achieve higher levels of managerial attainment and earnings. Asset-focused career coaches, as well as vocational training and professional development programs, could draw on these insights to guide individuals into career paths that align with their key strengths. Doing so would help promote job fit, increase job satisfaction, decrease voluntary turnover, and promote objective career attainment (Barrick et al., 2003; Cable & Edwards, 2004).

A focus on functional capacities could also help inform workplace training and development programs (Day et al., 2014). Whereas personality traits are generally conceptualized as stable dispositions, SEB skills represent learned capacities that can be developed and refined over time. Such skill development can accumulate naturally over time, and can be further catalyzed by interventions that incorporate deliberate practice and targeted feedback (Soto et al., 2026). The skill-outcome links identified by the present findings can thus provide an evidence-based roadmap for employee development (Lacerenza et al., 2017). For example, to help promote organizational citizenship and positive social relationships, an organization could design a training program aimed at developing Social Engagement and Cooperation Skills in newly formed project teams (Tannenbaum & Salas, 2020). The program might involve guided exercises that require employees to practice clear communication, active listening, and group decision-making in simulated work scenarios, followed by targeted feedback from teammates and facilitators. As another example, a program targeting Emotional Resilience Skills could help reduce burnout in frontline customer service representatives. These employees might participate in role-playing scenarios with real-time coaching feedback to help develop their capacity to regulate stress, manage anger, and maintain composure during challenging interactions. By focusing on the development of particular SEB skills, organizational training initiatives could help employees build the functional capacities needed for success in their roles.

A final noteworthy implication concerns the efficient assessment of SEB skills in applied workplace settings. Organizations often need to minimize survey administration time to manage organizational costs and prevent respondent fatigue, thereby maintaining high completion rates and data quality (Stanton et al., 2002). The present findings demonstrate that short forms of the BESSI, especially the BESSI-96 and BESSI-45 (Sewell et al., 2025), retain nearly all of the domain-level predictive power observed for the full-length inventory. Thus, organizational researchers and practitioners can use these brief measures to efficiently evaluate a broad range of social, emotional, and behavioral capacities without imposing undue time burdens on respondents or excessively sacrificing validity (Credé et al., 2012; Soto & John, 2019). The BESSI short forms could be readily integrated into large-scale screening processes, talent audits, or employee surveys.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

The present research has several notable strengths, such as recruiting a large and demographically representative sample of working adults, using a multidimensional framework to assess five major domains of SEB skills, and examining an extensive array of consequential work variables. Thus, the present findings provide a robust map of how social, emotional, and behavioral skill domains are linked with key work criteria and outcomes.

However, these findings should also be interpreted with some important limitations in mind. For example, the present research employed a cross-sectional design. Because all data were collected at a single point in time, we cannot establish temporal precedence or causal relations of SEB skills with work criteria and outcomes. Intuitively, it seems likely that deploying key functional capacities in the workplace can lead to high job performance, positive work attitudes, and other outcomes. However, reciprocal processes are also plausible, such that occupying a challenging, prestigious, or deeply rewarding role at work could shape an employee's capabilities and self-perceptions over time (Denissen et al., 2014; Wille & De Fruyt, 2014). Therefore, longitudinal research that tracks SEB skills and work outcomes is an important next step to map developmental trajectories, and to distinguish between directional selection and socialization effects.

A second important limitation is the reliance on self-report measures of SEB skills and many work criteria and outcomes. This introduces the risk of common method variance, which could artificially inflate observed skill-outcome associations (Avolio et al., 1992; Spector, 1994).

Moreover, measuring SEB skills through self-evaluations captures how well individuals believe they can perform specific kinds of behaviors, rather than directly observing their competence levels. The present research partially mitigated concerns about common method bias by analyzing some more-objective indicators of occupational success (such as earnings, managerial level, and attainment coded from job descriptions), and by focusing on findings that remained robust across regression analyses controlling for overlap among the skill domains. However, future research is still needed to replicate and extend the present findings using alternative data sources (Connelly & Ones, 2010; Oh et al., 2011). For example, incorporating supervisor or peer ratings of SEB skills, job performance, and workplace behaviors, alongside objective human resources data like actual turnover or verified salary, would provide a stronger test of skilloutcome links.

A third limitation is that although our findings show that SEB skills relate with many consequential work criteria and outcomes that have been previously linked to personality traits, they should not be interpreted as indicating whether SEB skills or personality traits provide incremental validity over each other. Based on previous findings in predominantly student populations, we would expect that SEB skills and personality traits often show similar relations with work criteria and outcomes, but can also show unique associations and incremental validity (Chen et al., 2024; Ringwald et al., 2025; Soto et al., 2022 Soto et al., , 2023 Soto et al., , 2024;; Yoon et al., 2024).

However, additional research that assesses both SEB skills and personality traits is needed to directly test this hypothesis in the workplace context.

A final limitation concerns the composition and cultural context of the present sample.

Although our quota sampling approach successfully yielded a demographically representative cohort of full-time working adults in the United States, the present findings may not fully generalize to other populations or cultural contexts. For example, skill-outcome associations may differ among workers who hold part-time jobs. Moreover, cultural norms could influence the development of some SEB skills, as well as the implications of these skills for individuals' career paths and eventual workplace success (He et al. 2019; Salgado, 1997). Therefore, future research is needed to test whether the implications of SEB skills for work criteria and outcomes generalize across other populations and cultural contexts.

Conclusion

In sum, the present research demonstrates that social, emotional, and behavioral skills are robustly linked with consequential work criteria and outcomes. By conceptualizing and operationalizing these characteristics as functional capacities, researchers and practitioners can advance the understanding and prediction of workplace success. The present findings indicate that assessing an individual's capacities to manage goals and tasks, engage socially, cooperate with others, regulate emotions, and learn from novel experiences can provide key insights into their potential for work performance, attainment, attitudes, intentions, behaviors, interests, and values. Moreover, brief assessments of SEB skills can efficiently measure these capacities without imposing undue burdens on respondents or organizations. Mapping general trait-value connections ontoCable and Edwards' (2004) model of work values, we therefore hypothesize that (a) Cooperation and Innovation Skills will relate positively with the self-transcendence work values of altruism and relationships with others, reflecting the capacities to engage with broader ideals and prosocial connections; (b) Social Engagement Skills will relate positively with the self-enhancement values of pay and prestige, because the capacity to assert influence is important for securing resources and status; (c) Innovation and Social Engagement Skills will relate positively with the openness to change values of variety and autonomy, reflecting the capacities to navigate novel experiences and self-direct agentic behavior; and (d) Self-Management Skills will relate positively with the conservation values of security and authority, because the capacity to regulate goal-directed behavior likely aligns with desires for consistency and structured hierarchies.

SEB skill domains relate with a comprehensive set of consequential work criteria and outcomes. Moreover, because organizational research and practice often dictates time-restricted assessments, it is important to establish whether links of SEB skills with criteria and work outcomes are retained when using brief measures. To address these needs, the primary goal of the present research was to test links of SEB skills with consequential work criteria and outcomes. Specifically, we examined how Self-Management, Social Engagement, Cooperation, Emotional Resilience, and Innovation Skills relate with key indicators of (a) occupational attainment and self-perceived performance, (b) work attitudes, (c) work behaviors and intentions, (d) vocational interests, and (e) work values in a demographically representative sample of full-time working adults. Based on the previous research reviewed above, we preregistered 28 hypothesized links of SEB skills with work criteria and outcomes; these hypotheses are presented in Table , organizational commitment using the Organizational Commitment Scale (OCS;Urban et al., 2023), work engagement using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-Ultra-Short Version (UWES-3; Schaufeli, 2019), and burnout using the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory (OLBI;Demerouti et al., 2001).

The three MOAQ-JSS items ("All in all, I am satisfied with my job," "In general, I like working here," "In general, I don't like my job"), three OCS items ("I am willing to work harder than I have to in order to help the firm or organization I work for succeed," "I am proud to be working for my firm or organization," "I would turn down another job that offered quite a bit more pay in order to stay with this organization"), and six selected OLBI items (e.g., "It happens more and more often that I talk about my work in a negative way," "I always find new and interesting aspects in my work") are statements that participants rated on a seven-point agreement scale ranging from1 = disagree strongly to7 = agree strongly. The three UWES-3 items ("At my work, I feel bursting with energy," "I am enthusiastic about my job," "I am immersed in my work") are statements that participants rated on a seven-point frequency scale ranging from1 = never to7 = always. In the present sample, omega reliabilities were .93 for job satisfaction, .82 for organizational commitment, .89 for work engagement, and .81 for burnout.

Taken together, these findings indicate that SEB skills relate robustly with a broad range of consequential work criteria and outcomes, and that different skill domains show distinctive relationships with different criteria and outcomes. Most notably, (a) Social Engagement Skills relate consistently with occupational attainment, (b) Self-Management Skills with self-perceived performance, (c) Emotional Resilience Skills with work attitudes, and (d) people with different skills are interested in different kinds of jobs and value different aspects of their work.
