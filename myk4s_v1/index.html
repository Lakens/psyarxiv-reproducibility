Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

Acquiescence (ACQ), the tendency to agree regardless of item content, can differ across groups and thereby mimic or mask measurement non-invariance. Model-based recursive partitioning (MOB) combined with factor-analytic models (e.g. EFAtree, CFAtree) is increasingly used to detect non-invariance across many covariates, but whether the detected heterogeneity can be attributed to its correct source is unknown. In a Monte Carlo simulation (432 conditions, 500 replications each), we compared EFAtree and CFAtree with their randomintercept (RI) versions under four data-generating conditions: groups differed in neither source, in ACQ only, in the content loadings only, or in both. We also propose a block-wise efficient score test that separates instability in the RI variance from instability in the content loadings by projecting out all other parameters. All trees held the nominal Type I error rate. When groups differed only in ACQ, trees without an RI factor mistook the difference for metric non-invariance under unbalanced keying or missed it under balanced keying, whereas the RI trees detected it regardless of keying. Applied to the true grouping variable, the standard block test, which selects a block from the fully decorrelated scores, attributed purely substantive non-invariance to ACQ in 79% of replications, more often in larger samples. The efficient test kept this misattribution near the nominal level (.06) and attributed heterogeneity correctly at the attainable maximum.

With one content factor, EFA-and CFA-based trees produced identical results. In an empirical example with the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (N = 26236), the RI variance differed across countries but not across age groups or genders, and even across countries it was only one of several unstable parameter blocks, alongside the intercepts and the factor-mean and factorvariance directions. Before a split is interpreted as measurement non-invariance, its source should be examined with an RI factor and efficient block-wise tests.

Introduction

Acquiescence (ACQ) is the tendency of respondents to agree with items on a scale without paying attention to their content (Jackson & Messick, 1958 , 1961). This type of response behavior compromises the psychometric quality of research data (Rammstedt & Farmer, 2013; Rammstedt & Kemper, 2011). To prevent ACQ, Nunnally and Bernstein (1994) and DeVellis and Thorpe (2022) suggested using reversed items (or negatively worded items [NW]) together with non-reversed items (or positively worded items [PW]). The fundamental problem with approaches to modeling or preventing this response behavior is that they cannot distinguish whether the participant genuinely possesses this trait to a high degree or simply exhibits a high level of participation tendency (Ferrando & Lorenzo-Seva, 2010).

The most used method for controlling ACQ bias is the use of mixed-worded scales consisting of PW and NW items. Researchers do not have a clear consensus on whether the number of these items should be balanced or unbalanced, but Rosenberg's Self-Esteem (Rosenberg, 1965), Core Self-Evaluations Scales (Judge et al., 2003), and the revised Life Orientation Test (Vecchione et al., 2014) are balanced, while the Undergraduate Learning Burnout Scale (Zeng et al., 2020) and the Psychological Sense of School Membership Scale (Goodenow, 1993) have an unbalanced structure. The advantages and disadvantages of balanced or unbalanced designs need to be considered. Furthermore, the combined use of PW and NW items for ACQ bias control also prevents participants from exhibiting 'speed bump' behavior and filling out the scale too quickly (Józsa et al., 2017; Seng Kam & Meyer, 2015). However, the combined use of PW and NW items may cause the scale to deviate from its expected factor structure (Danner et al., 2015; Rammstedt et al., 2013; Soto et al., 2008). In line with this, many researchers have reported that scales developed with both PW and NW items contain two separate dimensions (Kam & Meyer, 2015; Weijters & Baumgartner, 2012; Woods, 2006). In other studies, researchers have reported that a bifactor model including one general factor and two method factors for PW and NW items separately (Gu et al., 2015), or one general factor with a method factor for NW items (DiStefano & Motl, 2006; Tang et al., 2023), may be appropriate for these mixed-worded scales. Findings indicate that the combined use of PW and NW items has a clear effect on the factor structure. Furthermore, ACQ bias may further blur these solutions (Rammstedt et al., 2013).

Measurement Invariance

International large-scale assessments (ILSAs) are frequently conducted to enable crosscultural and cross-country comparisons (e.g., the Programme for International Student Assessment [PISA]). In such studies, measurement invariance (MI) must be established before any cross-cultural comparison can be meaningfully interpreted (D' Urso et al., 2023; Davidov et al., 2014; Meitinger et al., 2020; Odell et al., 2021; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Wurster, 2022).

Comparisons drawn in the absence of MI may yield biased conclusions, as observed differences can reflect measurement artifacts rather than genuine group differences (D' Urso et al., 2023).

Nevertheless, some researchers have questioned whether full MI is a strict prerequisite for valid comparisons (Robitzsch & Lüdtke, 2023).

Several approaches are available for testing MI. In the following section, we describe two factor-analytic approaches-confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and exploratory factor analysis (EFA)-along with their tree-based extensions in detail.

Confirmatory factor analysis with multiple groups (MGCFA) is one of the most commonly used methods for testing MI. In this approach, MI is evaluated across four nested steps: configural, metric, scalar, and strict (residual). These steps are conducted sequentially, with each model compared to the preceding one. If model-data fit deteriorates as additional equality constraints are imposed, MI is considered to be violated (Schoot et al., 2012). The comparison of nested models is typically based on changes in various fit indices (Kline, 2023).

There is ongoing debate regarding which fit indices should be reported and which cut-off criteria best indicate non-invariance. One widely used recommendation is a threshold of ΔCFI ≤ -0.01 for the Comparative Fit Index (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002). In addition, several criteria have been proposed for the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (ΔSRMR) and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (ΔRMSEA). For further details, see Chen (2007), Rutkowski and Svetina (2014), and Putnick and Bornstein (2016).

Despite its widespread use, MGCFA has several limitations for testing MI. First, when multiple covariates are present in a dataset, MI must be tested separately for each one, which can be time-consuming; moreover, analyzing each covariate independently ignores potential interaction effects (e.g., gender × region). Second, as the number of groups increases, multiple-CFA to Test MI comparison problems may lead to incorrect identification of the source of non-invariance (Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). Third, MGCFA is not well suited to handling continuous covariates.

These limitations have motivated the development of alternative approaches. For example, Asparouhov and Muthén (2014) introduced the alignment optimization method, which accommodates a large number of groups but allows only a single categorical covariate. Similarly, Bauer (2017) proposed moderated nonlinear factor analysis, which enables the inclusion of both categorical and continuous covariates.

In recent years, research on MI has increasingly shifted from CFA-based toward EFAbased approaches, which offer several important advantages. In CFA models, model misspecification is typically addressed through modification indices, which require iterative respecification and re-estimation. EFA models, by contrast, allow cross-loadings to be freely estimated and readily incorporated. Even when configural invariance holds, constraining crossloadings to zero in multifactorial CFA models may be overly restrictive, whereas EFA-based methods can interpret differences in cross-loadings as evidence bearing on metric invariance.

CFA-based approaches also require strong a priori assumptions about the latent structure (Brown, 2015). Although EFA relies on prior knowledge to some degree as well, it is more flexible in capturing the structure underlying the data: rather than restricting items to load only on theoretically expected factors, it permits the examination of alternative loading patterns, thereby providing a more practical framework for evaluating configural and metric invariance. For further details, see Dolan et al. (2009) and De Roover and Vermunt (2019).

In ILSAs, groups are often defined by multiple covariates, both continuous and categorical. However, EFA-and CFA-based approaches typically test MI for a single covariate at a time, potentially overlooking other relevant covariates and their interactions.

The MOB algorithm proceeds in three main steps (Zeileis et al., 2008): 1.

A model is fitted to the full dataset, and its parameters (e.g., factor loadings, inter-factor correlations, and the log-likelihood) are estimated.

2. Parameter stability is evaluated using structural change tests for each covariate. If one or more covariates exhibit statistically significant instability (after Bonferroni correction), the covariate with the smallest p-value is selected as the splitting variable.

3. The procedure is repeated recursively until (a) no further significant splits are detected, (b) a pre-specified maximum tree depth is reached, or (c) the sample size within a node falls below a predefined threshold (Sterner & Goretzko, 2023). This algorithm offers several advantages over traditional multigroup approaches such as MGEFA and MGCFA. First, it accommodates continuous covariates without imposing arbitrary cut-off points. Second, it can detect interaction effects among covariates: for example, after an initial split by gender, subsequent splits may reveal region-specific differences, thereby identifying non-invariance driven by an interaction (e.g., gender × region).

In recent years, tree-based extensions such as EFA-Tree and CFA-Tree have attracted increasing attention for investigating measurement invariance and identifying sources of heterogeneity (Bulut et al., 2024; Goretzko et al., 2026; Goretzko & Sterner, 2025; Sterner et al., 2024; Sterner & Goretzko, 2023; Straub et al., 2025). In these studies, covariates such as age, gender, region, education level, immigration background, and marital status have been used to define subgroups and assess MI, typically under the assumption that invariance violations arise from observed grouping variables.

However, ACQ bias may represent an unobserved source of non-invariance (Sterner et al., 2026). As discussed earlier, ACQ bias can distort the estimation of latent constructs by systematically influencing item responses, inter-item relationships, and item means.

Understanding the sources of ACQ is equally critical. Although its underlying mechanisms are not fully understood, prior research has linked ACQ to personality traits such as agreeableness (Hibbing et al., 2017) and optimism (Kam & Zhou, 2014), early literacy practices (Bulut, 2021), cultural influences (Bulut et al., 2024; Rammstedt et al., 2013), and subgroup-specific tendencies within the same culture (D' Urso et al., 2023). Moreover, certain groups may be particularly prone to ACQ (Van Vaerenbergh & Thomas, 2012), and its magnitude may vary across countries and cultures (Rammstedt et al., 2013). Because ACQ bias clearly influences the factor structure, failing to model it makes it impossible to determine whether the results of multigroup comparisons reflect ACQ bias or genuine group differences. Sterner et al. (2026) grouped the potential sources of ACQ into three main domains: (a) respondent characteristics (e.g., agreeableness, age, gender), (b) survey characteristics (e.g., the presence of positively and negatively worded items, survey mode), and (c) cultural characteristics (e.g., social norms). Importantly, interactions among these factors may further exacerbate the effects of ACQ.

Taken together, ACQ bias can contaminate the invariance structure of latent constructs across subgroups and act as a hidden source of non-invariance. Explicitly modeling and controlling for it is therefore essential for determining whether observed group differences reflect substantive variation or methodological artifacts (Sterner et al., 2026). Existing CFA-and EFAbased approaches typically rely on global model specifications and assume homogeneous parameter structures across individuals or groups. Tree-based approaches differ fundamentally in that they explicitly model parameter instability across covariates. Whereas random-intercept (RI) models aim to control method effects through a global correction (e.g., by assuming equal unstandardized factor loadings on a method factor), tree-based methods can identify subgroups with distinct parameter structures. This makes them particularly well-suited to detecting heterogeneous ACQ patterns and interaction effects that may remain undetected under traditional approaches.

Present Study

The present study examines whether tree-based factor-analytic approaches, in standard and random-intercept (RI) form, detect group differences in ACQ and, more importantly, whether they attribute measurement heterogeneity to its correct source. When groups differ in acquiescence, a model without an RI factor can absorb the method variance only into its content parameters, so a difference in response style may be mistaken for metric non-invariance, a concern raised by D' Urso et al. (2023). Conversely, when an RI factor is modeled, a genuine difference in the content loadings could be absorbed partly by the RI parameters. Separating the two sources requires a design in which they are manipulated independently. We therefore crossed four data-generating conditions (full invariance, non-invariance in ACQ only, non-invariance in the content loadings only, and both) and complemented the tree-based tests with block-wise parameter-instability tests that examine the RI variance and the content loadings separately.

Four methods are compared: EFA-Tree, RI-EFA-Tree, CFA-Tree, and RI-CFA-Tree.

With a single content factor, the exploratory and confirmatory specifications are alternative parameterizations of the same covariance and mean structure, so the comparison of central interest is between each tree and its RI counterpart; the exploratory variants are reported for completeness.

A Monte Carlo simulation crossed the type of heterogeneity with the number of groups, the sample size per group, the number of items, the proportion of reversed items, the proportion of groups in the first cluster, the magnitude of the content-loading difference, and the level of the ACQ loadings. The methods were evaluated on Type I error, power, the rejection rates of the block-wise tests and the resulting attribution rates, and the recovery of node-specific factor loadings (Tucker's congruence coefficient, bias, and root mean squared error [RMSE]). The study addresses the following research questions: RQ1: Do tree-based methods, with and without an RI factor, keep the Type I error rate at the nominal level when groups do not differ, although ACQ is present? RQ2: How well do the standard and RI versions of these methods detect group differences that arise from ACQ only, from the content loadings only, or from both? RQ3: When heterogeneity is detected, do block-wise parameter-instability tests attribute it to its correct source (RI variance vs. content loadings), and does this depend on how the block test is constructed?

Aim

This study aims to compare tree-based factor-analytic methods with and without an RI factor in (a) controlling the Type I error rate when ACQ is present but equal across groups, (b) detecting group differences in ACQ and in the content loadings, and (c) attributing detected heterogeneity to its correct source.

We conducted a Monte Carlo simulation study manipulating the following factors: • Number of groups: GROUPN = {3, 6, 10}.

There is not a common number of groups when we examine cross-cultural research. However, ILSAs were administered in 81 regions for PISA 2022, and the World Values Survey (Wave 7) was conducted in 66 regions. Reflecting this diversity also entails significant data collection costs. For studies without extensive funding, we aimed to mimic smaller numbers of groups to represent small-to medium-scale cross-cultural research.

• Sample size per group: NOBS = {500, 1000}. For each group, we have manipulated the sample sizes corresponds to the recommendations in the literature for factor analytic estimates of accuracy in self-report studies (Fabrigar et al., 1999) • Content-factor loadings: FLOAD = 0.70 in cluster 1, an "excellent" loading in the terms of Comrey and Lee (1992). • Number of variables: NVAR = {6, 10}. We examined the number of items at two levels.

Cross-cultural comparisons of six-item scales (Lins de Holando Coelho et al., 2020; Wongpakaran et al., 2020; Bekteshi et al., 2015); ten-item scales are frequently used (Erčulj & Mrevlje, 2023; Michaelides et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2011) • Proportion of NW items: BALANCE = {0.33, 0.50}. The ratio of NW items to the scale length varies, but the evidence for the validity of balanced scales is stronger (Alvarado-Leiton et al., 2026). In this context, we examine the ratio of NW items in the simulated dataset at two levels. When examining NVAR x BALANCE, for 6-item conditions, there are 2 NW items (6 x0.33 = 1.98) and 3 NW items (6 x0.50 = 3); and for 10-item conditions, 3 items are NW (10 ×0.33 = 3.33) and 5 items are NW (10 ×0.50 = 5).

• Proportion of groups in cluster 1: PROP_GROUP = {0.33, 0.50}. The first rounded number of groups (GROUPN × PROP_GROUP) formed cluster 1 and the remaining groups formed cluster 2. For example, with GROUPN = 3, cluster 1 contained one group when PROP_GROUP = 0.33 and two groups when PROP_GROUP = 0.50. Cluster 1 carried the higher ACQ loadings (themes 2 and 4) and the higher content loadings (themes 3 and 4).

• Type of heterogeneity: TYPE = {invariance, ACQ only, substantive only, both}, referred to as themes 1-4 (Table 1). In theme 1, the two clusters had identical population parameters; in theme 2, only the ACQ loadings differed (0.30 in cluster 1 vs. 0.15 in cluster 2); in theme 3, only the content loadings differed; and in theme 4, both differed, in the same cluster.

• ACQ loading level: RILOAD = {0.15, 0.30}. In themes 1 and 3, the ACQ loadings were equal in both clusters and set to this level; in themes 2 and 4, they were fixed by design at 0.30 (cluster 1) and 0.15 (cluster 2).

• Distractor covariates: three covariates unrelated to the data-generating process were generated for every respondent, following Sterner and Goretzko (2023): cov1, an ordinal variable with ten equiprobable levels (deciles of a standard normal draw); cov2, a binary variable; and cov3, a five-category variable. A continuous cov1 was not used because, with up to 10,000 respondents, testing all of its candidate split points was computationally prohibitive.

Factor loadings were not fixed at single values. In each replication, one uniform offset per item, uj ~ U(-0.05, 0.05), was drawn for the content loadings and another for the ACQ loadings, and added to the level of the item's cluster. Content loadings therefore followed U(0.65, 0.75), U(0.50, 0.60), or U(0.35, 0.45) for the levels 0.70, 0.55, and 0.40, and ACQ loadings followed U(0.10, 0.20) or U(0.25, 0.35) for the levels 0.15 and 0.30. Because the offsets were shared by all groups, groups within a cluster had identical population parameters, so that theme 1 was an exact null condition, and the differences between clusters equaled exactly MAGN for the content loadings and 0.15 for the ACQ loadings. Reversed items had negative content loadings and positive ACQ loadings, and all items were analyzed in their original direction. Only unidimensional content structures were simulated, and skewness was set to zero. The data were generated with functions adapted from the latentFactoR package (Christensen et al., 2022), in which the ACQ factor is added as a method factor to the population correlation matrix, so that the item variances are 1 in both clusters.

A full crossing of all factors would create cells in which a factor has no effect (e.g., MAGN under invariance, or RILOAD in themes 2 and 4, where the ACQ loadings were fixed by design). These redundant cells were excluded, leaving 24 base cells (GROUPN × NOBS × NVAR × BALANCE) × 18 theme-specific cells = 432 conditions: 96 for theme 1 (RILOAD × PROP_GROUP), 48 for theme 2 (PROP_GROUP), 192 for theme 3 (MAGN × RILOAD × PROP_GROUP), and 96 for theme 4 (MAGN × PROP_GROUP; Table 1). Estimand and targets

The estimands were (a) the probability that a tree splits on any variable, on the grouping variable (GROUP), or on a distractor covariate; (b) the probability that a block-wise parameterinstability test rejects the stability of the RI variance or of the content loadings across GROUP; and (c) the node-specific content loadings. The targets were the nominal α = .05 for splits and block tests when the tested parameters did not differ between groups, a probability of 1 for the detection of a manipulated difference, and the node-specific population loadings for recovery.

Methods

All models were estimated by maximum likelihood (ML) in lavaan (Rosseel, 2012), treating the five-point items as continuous. ML and robust ML (MLR) yield identical point estimates and casewise scores; the robust corrections of MLR concern standard errors and fit statistics, which neither the trees nor the block tests use.

Estimators for ordinal data were not used because the score-based tests rely on casewise loglikelihood scores, which are available for ML. Trees were grown with model-based recursive partitioning (MOB; Zeileis et al., 2008) as implemented in partykit (Hothorn & Zeileis, 2015), using a lavaan fitting function that follows the same score-based logic as the EFAtree package (Sterner, 2026). At each node, the stability of all free parameters was tested against the four partitioning variables (GROUP, cov1, cov2, cov3) with a Bonferroni-corrected α = .05; GROUP was treated as an unordered and cov1 as an ordered factor. The minimum node size was ten observations per free parameter (180 to 310 observations, depending on the model and the number of items). For the EFA-Tree, the unidimensional model was specified as a single-factor EFA block. In all models, the content factor was identified by fixing its variance to 1, so that all loadings were freely estimated, and item intercepts were included, so that the EFA-and CFAbased trees tested the same parameters.

EFA-Tree and RI-EFA-Tree.

For the RI-EFA-Tree models, a similar procedure was followed. In addition to the substantive factor, a method factor representing ACQ bias was incorporated. The factor loadings of the ACQ factor were fixed across items, and the correlation between the substantive factor and the ACQ factor was constrained to be orthogonal (i.e., fixed at 0.00). In the RI-EFA-Tree model, the unstandardized factor loadings of the items on the ACQ factor were fixed at 1.00. The variance of the RI factor was freely estimated.

CFA-Tree models were specified as a unidimensional CFA with the same estimator, identification, mean structure, and tree settings as the EFA-Tree. With one content factor and no cross-loadings, the EFA and CFA specifications are equivalent, so the two trees can differ only through numerical aspects of estimation.

For the RI-CFA-Tree models, a similar procedure was followed. In addition to the substantive factor, a method factor representing ACQ was modeled. The factor loadings of the ACQ factor were fixed across items, and the correlation between the substantive factor and the ACQ factor was constrained to be orthogonal (i.e., fixed at 0.00). In the RI-CFA-Tree model, the unstandardized factor loadings of the items on the ACQ factor were fixed at 1.00. The fixed loading specification for the ACQ factor followed the conventional RI formulation. Variability in ACQ effects was introduced at the data-generating stage rather than at the model-estimation stage, allowing evaluation of how well RI-based approaches recovered heterogeneous method effects. As in the RI-EFA-Tree, the RI variance was freely estimated.

A split shows that some parameters differ between subgroups, but not which ones. To examine the source of heterogeneity, we tested the stability of two parameter blocks of the full-sample model across GROUP and across each distractor covariate: the RI variance (RI models only) and the content loadings. These tests apply the score-based tests of measurement invariance (Merkle & Zeileis, 2013; Wang et al., 2014) to a subset of the parameters and were computed alongside the trees, not as part of them. The standard block test decorrelates the full score matrix with the inverse square root of its outer-product information matrix and then retains the columns that belong to B, as with the parm argument of sctest function in strucchange package (Zeileis et al., 2002). Under parameter stability, LM follows a χ 2 distribution with |B|(C -1) degrees of freedom.

Both tests used α = .05 without correction across blocks. Attribution was scored for the RI models in each replication: it was correct when the RI-variance test rejected if and only if the groups differed in ACQ (themes 2 and 4) and the content-loading test rejected if and only if they differed in the content loadings (themes 3 and 4). A rejection for a block whose parameters did not differ between groups was counted as misattribution. Because such a block rejects at the nominal rate, the attainable maximum of correct attribution is about .95 in themes 2 and 3, .952 ≈ .90 in theme 1, and close to 1 in theme 4.

The projected score is Neyman's (1959) efficient score for the tested block, that is, the part of its score that is orthogonal to the scores of the remaining parameters. With normal-theory ML, the expected Hessian depends on the data only through their first two moments, so the expected information used in the projection is appropriate for five-point items as long as the mean and covariance structure is correctly specified; departures from normality affect the variance of the projected scores, which the robust estimate takes into account. Two limits of this first-order argument should be noted. If the model is misspecified, the observed information is a natural alternative for the projection. If groups differ in their item means, the expected covariance scores differ between the groups even when the covariance parameters are equal, so tests of covariance-structure blocks should then be computed after centering the items within the levels of the covariate. Neither situation arises in the simulation, in which the item means did not differ between groups; both are examined in the empirical example.

Performance Measures

The methods were evaluated on three groups of measures. Detection: in theme 1, the proportions of trees that split on any variable, on GROUP, and on a distractor covariate (Type I error); in themes 2-4, the proportion of trees that split on GROUP (power) and the adjusted Rand index (ARI) between the terminal nodes and the true clusters. Splits on a distractor covariate were counted as Type I errors in all themes; in themes 2-4 these are per-tree rates that include the tests in the child nodes. Attribution: the rejection rates of the two block-wise tests on GROUP and the rate of correct attribution defined above. Recovery: Tucker's congruence coefficient, bias, and RMSE of the node-specific content loadings.

Findings

We first report convergence and admissibility, then Type I error (theme 1), the detection of heterogeneity (themes 2-4), its attribution, and the recovery of the factor loadings. With one content factor, EFAtree and CFAtree, and riEFAtree and riCFAtree, made identical GROUP-split decisions and gave identical block-test results in every condition; the largest differences were .004 in the rate of distractor splits and .012 in bias (EFAtree vs. CFAtree). All four trees are reported in the tables and figures; because their results coincide, the text refers to CFAtree and riCFAtree, and every statement applies equally to EFAtree and riEFAtree.

Convergence and Admissibility

All trees and all full-sample models converged in every replication. Improper node solutions were rare: at least one node of riCFAtree had an improper solution in 0.3% of the replications on average and in at most 10% in the smallest conditions (three groups of 500 respondents and six items; 2.1% on average there vs. 0.2% elsewhere), whereas CFAtree had almost none (at most 0.2%). Full-sample riCFA solutions were proper in at least 99% of the replications in every condition, and the excluded nodes contained at most 3.2% of the observations. riCFAtree produced at least one estimation warning in 2.8% of the replications on average and in up to 44% in conditions with ten groups of 500 respondents, six items, unbalanced keying, and a .30 loading shift; these replications were retained unless a node solution was non-convergent or improper.

Detection of Heterogeneity

When groups differed only in acquiescence (theme 2), riCFAtree split on GROUP in 96.1% of the replications (Table 6, Figure 3); its power fell below .80 only with three groups of 500 respondents and six items (.64-.77). Recovery of the Factor Loadings

Figure 5 shows the keying-aligned bias, RMSE, and Tucker's congruence of the node-specific content loadings. riCFAtree attenuated the loadings slightly and uniformly (bias -.034 in themes 1 and 2 and -.030 in themes 3 and 4), with the same value for positively keyed and reversed items, RMSE between .030 and .053, and Tucker coefficients above .99 in every condition. This constant attenuation reflects the treatment of five-point items as continuous and did not depend on sample size.

CFAtree estimates depended on keying balance. With balanced keying, the bias was similar for both keying directions (about -.04). With unbalanced keying, the two directions diverged: positively keyed items were nearly unbiased or overestimated (-.004 to .012, averaged within themes), whereas reversed items were strongly attenuated (-.100 to -.123), reaching .033 and -.176 in themes 1 and 3 when the ACQ loadings were .30. Averaged over both directions, these errors largely cancel (overall bias -.036, compared with -.031 for riCFAtree), which is why bias is reported by keying direction. In these conditions the RMSE of CFAtree reached .139 and Tucker's congruence fell to .946, although no improper solutions occurred; the large errors therefore reflect the misspecification of a model without an RI factor rather than estimation problems.

Real Data Example

All eight trees detected heterogeneity (Figure 7, Figures S1-S3, Table S1), and all node solutions converged and were proper. With one content factor, EFAtree and riEFAtree produced exactly the same partitions as CFAtree and riCFAtree, with node-specific loadings that differed by less than .00001, both with and without the intercepts; the following therefore applies to both pairs.

The trees that tested the intercepts split first by age (18-30 vs. 31-65; p < .001) and then by country, separating India and the Philippines from the Western countries among the younger respondents and Great Britain from the other countries among the older respondents; the trees without an RI factor had 15 and those with an RI factor 16 terminal nodes. The trees without intercepts first separated India and the Philippines from the Western countries (p < .001) and then split by age and gender (9 and 11 terminal nodes). These partitions reproduce those obtained earlier with EFA-and CFA-based trees, which differed in the same way, indicating that the earlier contrast was due to the intercepts. Content-factor loadings were lower in the nodes comprising Indian and Filipino respondents (mean |λ| = .61-.62) than in the Western nodes (.71-.74), and the mean standardized RI loading was higher (.26-.34 vs. .17-.22 in riCFAtree).

Country-specific riCFA models gave the same picture: standardized RI loadings of .27 (India) and .33 (the Philippines) against .14-.20 in the Western countries, and mean content loadings of .62 against .70-.73.

Discussion

This study examined whether model-based trees with and without a random intercept detect group differences in acquiescence and attribute measurement heterogeneity to its correct source.

Three findings stand out. First, when groups differed only in acquiescence, trees without an RI factor either mistook this difference for metric non-invariance or missed it. With unbalanced keying, CFAtree split on GROUP in about two thirds of the replications and its content-loading block was rejected in more than three quarters, so that a pure response-style difference appeared as a difference in the content structure; with balanced keying, the same difference was practically invisible to the tree (power .02). Second, riCFAtree detected acquiescence differences with high power regardless of keying and kept the Type I error rate at the nominal level when ACQ was present but equal across groups. Third, attributing a detected difference to its source required an appropriate block-wise test: the efficient test attributed heterogeneity correctly at its attainable maximum, whereas the standard approach of selecting a block from the fully decorrelated scores attributed up to 99% of purely substantive non-invariance to acquiescence, and did so more often as the sample size and the number of groups increased.

The dependence on keying follows from how ACQ enters the data. In the data-generating model, a larger ACQ loading adds roughly the same positive amount to the covariances of all item pairs, regardless of keying, while the item variances remain unchanged. With unbalanced keying, a one-factor model absorbs part of this shift into its loadings, inflating positively keyed and attenuating reversed loadings, so that the ACQ difference appears as metric non-invariance. With balanced keying, the shift is not captured by the content parameters: the content-loading block of CFAtree was rejected at close to the nominal rate (.068), so the pseudo-true loadings hardly differed between the clusters and the tree had nothing to detect. Both patterns are consistent with the observation that acquiescence distorts factor solutions in a keying-dependent way (D' Urso et al., 2023; Ferrando & Lorenzo-Seva, 2010; Savalei & Falk, 2014). The RI factor captures the shift directly through its variance, which explains why the power and the estimates of riCFAtree did not depend on keying balance. The failure of the standard block test has a related cause. In the RI model, the scores of the RI variance and of the content loadings are correlated, because a uniform change in the loadings also changes the average covariance among the items.

Decorrelating all scores and then selecting a block mixes the blocks, so that a difference in the loadings leaks into the test of the RI variance, the more so the more information the data contain.

Projecting out the remaining parameters before testing removed this leakage without a loss of power: the efficient test rejected the loading block more often than the standard test when the loadings did differ (.999 vs. .805 in theme 3).

The empirical example, based on a scale with balanced keying, shows how the block-wise tests change the interpretation of a tree. All trees detected heterogeneity, and the RI loadings were considerably higher in the nodes comprising Indian and Filipino respondents (.26-.34) than in the Western nodes (.17-.22), a pattern consistent with cross-cultural research showing that acquiescence is weaker in more individualistic and more economically developed countries (Johnson et al., 2005; Rammstedt et al., 2017; Smith, 2004). The block-wise tests, however, located acquiescence differences only between countries: the RI variance did not differ between age groups or genders, and the first split on age in the trees that tested the intercepts was dominated by the direction of a latent mean difference. The country difference in the RI variance survived centering the items within countries and the use of the observed information. Even between countries, acquiescence was one of several sources, together with differences in the factor mean and variance and in the intercepts. This agrees with the simulation, in which a pure acquiescence difference under balanced keying was nearly invisible to trees without an RI factor; that such trees nevertheless split by country first points to the additional differences that the block tests identified. In empirical data, a split should therefore not be read as evidence of measurement non-invariance, let alone of acquiescence, before its source has been examined.

Implications for Researchers

Several practical implications follow. First, when a scale contains both positively and negatively worded items, analyzing the items in their original direction with an RI factor is necessary for interpreting tree-based invariance results: without it, a group difference in acquiescence can appear as metric non-invariance (unbalanced keying) or remain undetected (balanced keying).

Second, neither a split nor a rejection of the loading block in a model without an RI factor should be taken as evidence of substantive non-invariance when the groups may differ in acquiescence.

Third, the source of a detected difference should be examined with block-wise tests that project out the remaining parameters, as the efficient test does; testing a subset of the fully decorrelated scores (e.g., the parm argument of strucchange::sctest) can attribute substantive differences to acquiescence, and larger samples make this error more likely, not less. Because the trees fix the factor mean and variance in every node, group differences in these latent parameters (impact) also produce splits; separating their directions from the loading and intercept blocks, as in the empirical example, prevents impact from being read as non-invariance. Fourth, researchers should report node-specific estimates, including the RI loadings, alongside the tree; report the bias of loadings by keying direction, because errors of opposite sign cancel in the average; and decide a priori whether item intercepts are to be tested, because including them changes the splitting variables, as the empirical example illustrates.

Finally, in large samples score-based tests detect trivial differences, so interpreting primarily the upper-level splits and using stricter stopping rules, such as a larger minimum node size or a lower significance level, remains advisable.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, ACQ heterogeneity was operationalized as a difference in the ACQ loadings, that is, in the variance of the RI, with item variances held constant and without group differences in mean acquiescence. Real differences in acquiescence may also raise item variances and means, which trees without an RI factor could detect through the residual variances and intercepts; the finding that balanced keying hides acquiescence differences from such trees is therefore specific to this covariance-only form. Second, only one content-loading level in the reference cluster (.70) and two keying ratios (one third and one half reversed items) were studied; misattribution may be larger with weaker loadings were not examined.

Third, substantive non-invariance shifted all loadings by the same amount, which is close to a difference in factor variance; partial non-invariance affecting only some items may be harder to separate from acquiescence. Fourth, with a single content factor the exploratory and confirmatory trees coincide, so the study cannot speak to the advantages of EFA-based trees in multidimensional structures. Fifth, five-point items were analyzed as continuous by ML, which produced a constant attenuation of the loadings of about .03. Sixth, in theme 4 both differences affected the same cluster, so attribution could be assessed only with the block-wise tests, not with the tree structure; moreover, the efficient block test, although it held its nominal level throughout, has not been examined beyond the present design. Finally, in the empirical example the four-point items were treated as continuous, the results are based on a single dataset with a highly unequal distribution of respondents across countries, and the decomposition of the loading and intercept blocks was checked only in a small simulation with continuous items; its directional parts are not specific (e.g., a uniform intercept shift cannot be distinguished from scalar non-invariance that affects all items alike).
