Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

The striking constraints of some human cognitive processes stand in stark contrast to the near limitless capability of others. While we can acquire and flexibly use vast amounts of information, the amount we can process at any one time is often stiflingly limited: for example the number of items we can hold in working memory or the number of tasks that can be performed at once. Here, we integrate ideas from information theory, cognitive science, and neuroscience to offer a unified account of why processing is often so limited. We argue that this reflects a fundamental tradeoff between generalization-how effectively existing representations can be used in novel settings-and how many distinct representations can be processed in parallel. Representations that best promote strong forms of generalization -a characteristically human cognitive strength -come at the expense of surprisingly strict limits in the number of items that can be processed at once, an equally characteristic human weakness. We refer to this as the "curse of generalization." We formulate this first in information-theoretic terms, and then in process models, including a neural network model of classic tasks used to demonstrate strict limits in human processing capacity. This tension offers a potential explanation for a range of phenomena -from performance on the tasks on which we focus, to representational learning and skill acquisition more broadly -as well as the performance of modern machine learning architectures that exhibit generalization capabilities comparable to humans.

1 Introduction

Although humans exhibit striking information processing capabilities, they can often also be strikingly limited. Most famously, humans can actively maintain only a handful of items in mind at one time, an observation familiar from both everyday experience and classic laboratory experiments. For example, it has long been recognized that we are generally limited to remembering only about 7 randomly presented digits at a given time (e.g., Hayes, 1952), and even fewer when rehearsal and chunking are prevented (Cowan, 2001). Remarkably, however, the causes of this limit remain poorly understood. While the resources necessary to carry out human cognitive function are clearly finite -e.g., storage space (Amit, 1989), processing time (Cheyette and Piantadosi, 2020), and the precision of neuronal signaling (Ma et al., 2014) -it is unclear why limits in these resources alone should impose such severe cognitive constraints, especially given the precision with which we can process information in many instances, the tremendous volume of information we can store and retrieve from longer term memory (Brady et al., 2008), and the vast resources available to the human brain (over 100 billion neurons, that consume approximatelyfoot_0 3 of the body's energy). Furthermore, it is unclear why these strict constraints are so similar across diverse forms of representation and types of cognitive processes. This diversity was the focus of George Miller's classic article (1956), in which he observed that the limit to the number of items -seven plus or minus two -that can be held in immediate memory (now more commonly referred to as "short term" or "working memory") is remarkably similar to limits in the amount of information -about 2.5 bits -that can be processed in other tasks, including perceptual ones such as matching a tone to one of several reference tones (Gravetter and Lockhead, 1973; Nizami, 2010; Pollack, 1952), or estimating the number of items in a visual display (Cheyette and Piantadosi, 2020; Kaufman et al., 1949; Mandler and Shebo, 1982).

The similarity of these constraints intrigued Miller, but he was perplexed by several factors: the disparity of information and tasks involved; the fact that seven "items" can carry substantially more than 2.5 bits of information (e.g., a decimal digit carries more than one bit of information); and the lack of any principles or processing mechanisms that could explain the relationship between these constraints. Accordingly, he could not convince himself that the prevalence of the "magical number seven" was more than a "Pythagorean coincidence," a provocative conclusion that has continued to attract attention in the decades since (e.g., Shiffrin and Nosofsky, 1994).

Here we reconsider Miller's conclusion, by identifying a fundamental principle of information processing that puts the use of overlapping, or shared representations in tension with the use of distinct, or separated representations 1 . We show that this tension, coupled with the value of shared representations for generalization, can explain the severe processing limits observed across disparate cognitive domains -limits that fall consistently within range of Miller's "magical number seven" -when the tasks involved require generalization, and may provide a normative basis for the role of serial processing and cognitive control that are signature characteristics of human cognitive function in many settings.

1.1 Argument Summary

Our argument is as follows: In general, increasing the complexity of a representational system (quantified as the number of bits available) increases the amount of information the system can represent; that is, its expressivity. In principle, this can also increase the amount of information that can be actively processed and kept distinct at the same time -a quantity that we refer to as processing capacity, in alignment with the question that interested Miller. However, increasing complexity is not the sole determinant of processing capacity; critically, it is also strongly impacted by the structure of the representations and, most importantly, how they relate to one another. Our primary observations are that: i) there is a fundamental tension between generalization and processing capacity; ii) this tension depends on the presence of uncertainty in the decision process (as elaborated below); iii) the nature of this tension is such that optimizing representations for generalizationthat is, the use of shared, or similar representations to represent correspondingly similar items -limits their ability to be reliably distinguished, thereby constraining the number of items that can be processed at the same time and thus limits processing capacity.

In this article, we formalize and examine this tension between generalization and processing capacity. We build on the well-established tradeoff in information theory between compression and accuracy-grounded in Rate-Distortion Theory (RDT) and the Information Bottleneck Shannon, 1959; Tishby et al., 2000-and on its relationship to principles of generalization. In prior work, the capacity constraint motivating the need for compression is generally taken as given, though it remains unclear why capacity should be so severely limited in the first place. This question arises at two levels: what constrains expressivity, the precision with which a single item can be represented (e.g., Sims, 2018); and, more broadly, what constrains processing capacity, the number of items that can be represented and kept distinct at the same time (Bays & Husain, 2008; Cheyette & Piantadosi, 2020; Ma et al., 2014; Sims, 2016). The two are coupled. Shepard's classic work formalized the principles of generalization for any single item in terms of the geometry of an internal representational space (Shepard, 1987), and Sims (2018) extended this account to RDT, showing tinction between these is closely related to Shepard's (1991) distinction between "separable" and "integral" dimensions. However, the alignment between these distinctions requires clarification, that we provide in Section 2 that Shepard's functional form of generalization follows from optimal coding under a capacity constraint (Sims, 2018). In short-term memory, the precision of each item declines as set size grows (Bays & Husain, 2008; Ma et al., 2014)-a signature of limited processing capacity, standardly interpreted as a fixed resource divided among items. Here, rather than assuming that multi-item capacity is set by intrinsic resource constraints (e.g., structural or metabolic), as in traditional theories of attention, working memory, and cognitive control (Anderson, 1983; Cowan, 2001; Posner & Snyder, 1975; Shiffrin & Schneider, 1977), we consider a reversal of the logic: if representations are optimized for generalization, to what extent can this account for the constraints on processing capacity? Specifically, we show that optimizing representational compression for generalization imposes remarkably strict constraints on processing capacity -ones that align with the constraints observed in tasks that involve the processing of novel stimuli and thus rely on generalization, including those considered by Miller (1956). We suggest that this tension between generalization and processing capacity, and the severity of the attendant effects, reflects a fundamental principle of its own, that we refer to as the Miller-Shepard Law. We formalize this relationship using two kinds of tasks: an identification task, requiring selection of an item in a reference set that is identical to a probe, that evaluates the ability to distinguish among items; and a generalization task, requiring selection of the item most similar to the probe that is not identical to any item in the set, and thus relies on similarity-based representations. We quantify the impact that the degree of similarity among representations (parameterized as a generalization gradient) and number of items in the reference set (as a measure of processing capacity) have on performance in each type of task.

We find a robust and strikingly severe tension between generalization and processing capacity in the identification and generalization tasks. Importantly, we find that this tension appears only when there is uncertainty in the decision process used to identify an item and/or relate it to others: With no uncertainty, both identification and, perhaps surprisingly, generalization are optimized by the narrowest possible generalization gradient, an explanation for which we provide with the formal analysis in Section 3.4. However, with even vanishingly small amounts of uncertainty, the effect of the generalization gradient on performance aligns with intuition: for identification, the narrowest gradient remains best. However, for generalization the relationship becomes non-monotonic: too narrow and there is insufficient generalization, too broad and everything is treated as the same, with an intermediate yielding the best generalization performance. We find that this holds across multiple potential sources of processing uncertainty, including additive decision noise, superposition of representations, and dynamic retrieval processes. Critically, we find that -owing to the log-log relationship between generalization and identification -the optimal value of the gradient for generalization is consistently associated with Draconian constraints on identification performance and thus processing capacity, restricting the latter to levels within range of the ∼2.5-bit limits famously observed by Miller.

Based on these observations, we argue that the severity of constraints in the tasks on which Miller focused was because, by design, they demanded the use of generalizable representations to process novel stimuli, which by construction involve representational sharing that, in turn, limits the number of items that can be actively represented and kept distinct at the same time. More generally, we conclude that the severe capacity limits observed in performance on tasks designed to probe the mechanisms responsible for flexibility of processing (such as attention, working memory and cognitive control) emerge not from the resource limitations intrinsic to such systems (e.g., a fixed biological limit on storage capacity or energetic requirements per se), but because the representational compression required for generalization, and the flexibility it affords, are in fundamental tension with the ability to preserve exact information.

The remainder of the article is structured as follows: First we present the argument in a more extended form, providing additional background -including its relationship to the foundational work in information theory, semanticity and generalization on which it is based, and an illustrative example of the problem. (We elaborate the constructs, and the assumptions on which they are based, in Appendix E). We then provide a formal derivation of the relationship between generalization and processing capacity in information theoretic terms, focusing on 1-dimensional stimuli. We follow this with a brief summary of results using a simple process model (described more fully in Appendix B) to simulate performance on the identification and generalization tasks with 2-dimensional stimuli, that are required to explore the effects of compositionality but that are intractable to analysis using the formal model. Finally, we describe a mechanistic (neural network) model, parameterized using neurobiological and psychophysical data, to simulate the tasks originally considered by Miller (1956). This directly demonstrates the applicability of the principle to those settings, with a system that is subject to superposition-based distributed representations and dynamic retrieval as the source of uncertainty in decision making. In the General Discussion, we consider how our information theoretic treatment aligns with Miller's original formulation of the problem; more broadly how the tradeoff between generalization and processing capacity relates to other foundational constructs in the cognitive literature, such as the theory of complementary learning systems, and how our approach may provide a normative account of cognitive control and its association with serial processing; and, finally, the implications this work has for the understanding and design of artificial neural networks.

2 Shared versus Separated Representations and the Tension Between Generalization and Processing Capacity

In this section we lay out in more detail the conceptual argument that motivates the subsequent formal analyses and computational experiments, illustrating key ideas with a concrete example taken from a visual working memory paradigm.

2.1 Shared Representations and Generalization

We begin by assuming that agents seek to maximize both the scope and accuracy with which they can represent the world. In principle, this can be done by exhaustively assigning a separate, dedicated representation to every possible state of the world, that specifies all of its features. This is sometimes referred to as "conjunctive coding" (e.g., see Figure 7, and aligns with Shepard's 1991 notion of integral dimensions. In informationtheoretic terms, this amounts to maximizing the complexity, or "expressivity" of the representational system. However, this is of course impossible in the extreme. Agents have limited time for learning, finite resources for representation and computation, and even evolution cannot anticipate all possible eventualities. In this respect, agents face the famous curse of dimensionality (Bellman, 1957). This has led investigators in statistics, cognitive science, neuroscience, and modern machine learning to search for algorithms that can efficiently acquire representations from past experience that can be applied to the broader class of experiences expected in the future. This ability to extend the use of existing representations to represent new information -i.e., generalization -reflects a form of compression (i.e., increasing the amount of information in new settings without increasing the complexity of representation). Although not all forms of compression necessarily support generalization (for example, randomly deleting parts of a signal), here we are interested in forms of compression that do. Building on Rate-Distortion Theory (RDT; Shannon, 1959) and its application to efficient coding in psychology and neuroscience (e.g., Barlow, 1961; Friston, 2010; Linsker, 1988; Sims, 2016 Sims, , 2018;; Tishby et al., 2000; Zaslavsky et al., 2018), as well as work in cognitive science on the nature of representations required for generalization (e.g., Fang and Sims, 2025; Malloy and Sims, 2025; Shepard, 1987), we argue that the mature human cognitive system, through evolution and/or learning over the course of development, relies on the use of shared representations as a form of compression that supports generalization -that is, as a solution to the optimization problem of maximizing information in new settings.

Cognitive scientists have long highlighted two ways in which natural systems exploit representational sharing to capture structure in the world, and thereby optimize generalization, through the use of: (i) semanticity in which representations preserve the similarity structure in the world (Shepard, 1987); and (ii) compositionality, in which novel or unfamiliar states are represented through the re-combination of previously acquired represen-tations (Fodor and Pylyshyn, 1988; Shepard, 1991; Smolensky, 1990) foot_1 . Together, these support generalization: Semanticity assigns similar representations to states of the world that are similar along perceptually and/or behaviorally-relevant dimensions, allowing new states that are similar to previously experienced ones to elicit similar interpretations or responses, respectively. Compositionality allows the system to represent never-before seen states through the composition of previously acquired representations. Together, semanticity and compositionality reflect a form of representational sharing, in which representations acquired from states of the world that have been previously experienced can be extended to represent a broader class of states that have not yet been experienced. In this respect, they support generalization, and the characteristic flexibility of human cognitive function that this affords.

2.2 Separated Representations and Processing Capacity

While the acquisition of shared representations supports generalization, it necessarily comes at the cost of the ability to distinguish different states. Insofar as representational sharing reflects a form of compression (discussed below), this cost can be formalized using RDT as a reduction in accuracy, or "distortion," due to the loss of information that comes from compression. From this, a Pareto front can be derived in the tradeoff between compression and distortion, defining a maximum achievable accuracy for a given amount of compression. This is exemplified by the Information Bottleneck (IB; Tishby et al., 2000), which derives such a Pareto front in the amount of information preserved with respect to some task-relevant variable (e.g., a category label) as a function of compression. This is generally considered with regard to the processing of a single signal, or representation. Here, we are concerned with the effect that compression has on how many representations that can be kept distinct and processed at the same time (i.e., in parallel). As noted above, we refer to this as processing capacity -in alignment with the long tradition of interest in this feature of human cognitive function, initially brought into focus by Miller -and analyze the extent to which the distortion introduced by compression, in the service of generalization, constrains processing capacity.

2.3 Generalization and Shared versus Separated Representations

To the extent that compression introduces distortion one might ask why, or under what conditions, it might be advantageous for representations to be compressed? This has typically been considered with respect to resource constraints that limit representational complexity in artificial systems (e.g., code length), and the metabolic costs of representational precision or speed of processing in biological ones (Cheyette and Piantadosi, 2020; Wilken and Ma, 2004; Zheng and Meister, 2025). Here, we consider another potential advantage of compression: generalization -that is, the effectiveness with which existing representations can be used to process new information, formalized as the amount of information preserved when representing novel information. As noted above, semanticity and compositionality are forms of compression that support generalization. For example, the form of compression (or "distortion function") used in Tishby et al. (2000) to demonstrate the IB can be thought of as a form of semantic coding (classification); and Sims (2018) shows that the exponential similarity gradient proposed by Shepard (1987) as a Universal Law of Generalization can be derived from RDT as an optimal form of compression. However, as a form of compression, RDT tells us that semanticity must therefore also be associated with distortion and thus, as seen through the lens of the IB, a limit on the ability to accurately distinguish between and identify individual items. In this article, we formally address this tradeoff between compression in the service of generalization and the costs it imposes on identification accuracy, and the dramatic impact that this has on processing capacity.

Our analyses lead to two critical observations: (i) with any amount of uncertainty in the decision process used to evaluate items, there is an intermediate amount of compression (i.e., representational sharing) that optimizes generalization; (ii) that amount of compression heavily constrains the ability to accurately identify individual items which, in turn, radically limits processing capacity (i.e., the number of items that can be processed at the same time); we refer to this as the "curse of generalization."

2.4 Illustrative Example

To make the problem concrete, consider the visual short term (or working) memory task, diagrammed in Figure 1A, that is used paradigmatically to study capacity limits in human information processing. In this task, colored squares appear at various positions in a display, followed by a delay, and the participant's task is to determine whether the color of an item at a particular location has changed. Performance on this task degrades precipitously when the display contains more than 4 or 5 items (see Figure 13D) -that is, within the constraints of the magical number 7 (in this case, minus two). visual display with colored squares and, following a delay, is shown either the same display or one in which the color of one of the squares has been changed at a probed location, and asked to respond whether that item is the same or different from the original display. (B) Encoding of the information in the display as a compositional coding of semantically structured representations: Each item in the display is represented through the rapid formation of associations between feature values represented semantically along each independent feature dimension (i.e., color and position), and the display is represented through the full set of associations.

Figure 1B shows one possible scheme for how such a task might be performed. Each node corresponds to a representational element, with a pattern of these used to represent a feature value semantically along one of the task-relevant dimensions, and a different set of nodes dedicated to representing features along each of the two dimensions. Importantly, in this scheme there are no nodes for combinations of feature values across dimensions: Nodes used to represent features along one dimension do not carry any information about features along the other dimension, and thus can't be used to represent the specific combination of color and location associated with a particular item. In other words, the representations are both semantic and compositional: Representations of features along each dimension are similar for similar features (e.g., red and orange), each item is represented as a combination of its feature value along each dimension (i.e., its color and position) by associating ("binding") the representations of those features, and the display is represented as the set of associations representing each of the items in the display. The formal structure of such semantic and compositional representations is shown graphically in Figure 7, discussed in Section 3.5, and at greater length in Appendix E.1).

The use of such representational structure, and in this example compositionality in particular, confers substantial flexibility. By representing each item as a combination of representations along different dimensions, any possible combination of feature values along those dimensions can be represented; similarly, representing displays through the combination of items allows any possible combination of items to be represented. This scheme can also be acquired much more efficiently, since it involves learning only the representations for feature values along each dimension, which are shared across representations of different objects (i.e., combinations of features), rather than learning a separate, dedicated representation for every possible object. Accordingly, compositional representations scale additively in the number of features, rather than multiplicatively and, in this way, mitigate the curse of dimensionality. The representational sharing induced by semanticity (i.e., across features within dimensions) confers additional advantages for generalization, consistent with Shepard's 1987 Universal Law of Generalization, a connection that we will elaborate further in Section 3.2.

Importantly, representational sharing -whether due to semanticity and/or compositionality -introduces the risk of interference and confusion. For example, compositionality makes it impossible to identify an item by its color if its color is the same as another item, exactly because the representations of a given feature are shared across objects. This is exacerbated by semanticity, in which similar feature values are represented similarly along a given dimension (e.g., red and orange versus blue). This is because similarity of representation, by construction, reduces the distance between similar representations, which makes them less distinguishable (i.e., more likely to be confused), so that even items with different feature values (e.g., red vs. orange) may now be confused. Such similarity structure reflects a "softer" form of representational sharing (e.g., through partial overlapfoot_2 ) that, like compositional coding, introduces the risk of confusion. This is consistent with a large empirical literature on similarity-based interference in short-term memory (e.g., Lewis, 1996; MacLeod, 2024). Thus, while supporting generalization, representational sharingwhether compositional and/or semantic -limits the reliability with which items can be distinguished from one another. This, in turn, invokes the curse of generalization, limiting how many items can be represented distinguishably at the same time, thus constraining processing capacity. In the sections that follow, we provide a formal, information theoretic characterization of this tension between generalization and processing capacity, that we argue may explain the pattern of constraints in human cognitive function that perplexed George Miller.

Before doing so, however, it is important to note at the outset that the tension can be managed in at least two ways: by increasing representational complexity, through the allocation of dedicated, separated representations for individual items (i.e., conjunctions of features); or by imposing serial processing. The former can occur through forms of representational learning that transpire over longer time frames (e.g., consolidation, automatization, or chunking - Anderson, 1983; McClelland et al., 1995; Miller, 1956; Musslick et al., 2023; Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977), and can account for the near limitless capability people have for acquiring new knowledge and skills (i.e., increasingly their representational complexity) and that can also increase processing capacity. However, these come at the cost of time (passive exposure, rehearsal, practice, etc.), and cannot account for the rapid, flexible, and effective processing of novel states, which require the use of shared representations that generalize. The other way to overcome the risk of interference posed by shared representations is to limit how many are processed at once and, in the limit, to serialize processing. 4 From this vantage, serial processing can be viewed as acquiescing to the curse of generalization in exchange for its benefits. That is the domain of function on which we focus in this article.

3 Information-Theoretic Analysis

To cast the problem in information-theoretic terms, we assume that the knowledge required to perform a task is encoded by representations of the relevant information: observed states of the world are converted to internal representations (encoded) that are accessed for processing (in channels) to generate (transmit) a task-relevant inference and/or action (decoded) as the response. Here, we are interested in how the structure of the internal representations impacts how much information from the original source can be transmitted to generate the task-relevant response(s) at a given time. Specifically, we consider the degree of compression with which information is represented, implemented as the breadth of a generalization gradient, and focus on two critical factors: i) the effectiveness of generalization that can be achieved by a given degree of compression; and, given that degree of compression ii) the impact that this has on identification accuracy, and its consequences for processing capacity (i.e., how many distinct items can accurately be processed at the same without interfering with one another). We begin by reviewing foundational work in information theory, and in particular RDT and the IB, that formalize the tradeoff between compression and identification accuracy, typically with respect to the transmission of a single signal. We then extend this, focusing on the impact that compression -in the form of semantic and compositional representations -has on the relationship between generalization and identification, and in turn how this impacts performance when multiple items must be processed at once (i.e., processing capacity).

3.1 Accuracy and Compression: Rate-Distortion Theory (RTD) and the Information Bottleneck (IB)

The focus of RDT (Shannon, 1959) is on the relationship between the compression of representations (i.e., the reduction in their complexity) and the accuracy of information transmission that those representations support. Compression can be quantified in terms of the mutual information between a signal presented to the system and its encoding (i.e., the representation of that signal). Accuracy is quantified in terms of a distortion function, that defines a loss with respect to some task. The task can be as simple as conveying the signal itself or, more generally, as a mapping from a set of possible signals to corresponding responses, and distortion is quantified by some measure of mismatch between each signal and the task-relevant response for that signal given its representation. If there is any redundancy among representations (i.e., more than one representation for a given signal), then compression can be lossless (i.e., achieved without any distortion, or loss of accuracy). This was the focus of early work on efficient coding in neuroscience and cognition (Barlow, 1961). Absent such redundancy, then compression leads to distortion. Where there is distortion, it can be mitigated by increasing the complexity of the representations, allowing them to carry more information and thus more accurately map each signal to its task-relevant response. However, more complex codes require higher transmission rates, since more information must be transmitted per signal. RDT provides a formal framework for capturing this tradeoff, and the optimization of this tradeoff defines a Pareto front that describes the maximum accuracy that can be expected for a given distortion function and level of representational complexity (or, conversely, the minimum distortion achievable for a given level of compression; see vertical plane of Figure 2). The IB (Tishby et al., 2000) represents a special case of this problem, in which the distortion function corresponds to the difference in the value of some variable associated with a signal (e.g., the assignment of a category label to some object) and the value of that variable predicted by the encoding of the signal. Considerable empirical evidence supports the idea that human information processing often approaches the Pareto optimal relationship between compression and accuracy as specified by the IB within the RDT framework (Sims, 2018; Zaslavsky et al., 2018).

While RDT, and IB as an example, formalize the tradeoff between accuracy and compression, neither provides a normative account of the extent to which compression should be favored over accuracy when these are in tension (i.e., when there is no redundancy in the representations; though see Shamir et al., 2010, for a treatment of IB for finite samples). Under such conditions, compression may nevertheless be viewed as advantageous, or at least worth the cost of distortion, for at least two reasons. One is that it lessens representational demands, and thus is less physically costly. This may offset the cost of distortion, or even pose an upper limit on complexity, and thus a lower bound on compression.

Some theories have suggested that such costs can explain the upper bound on processing capacity observed in human cognitive functions such as working memory (e.g., Ma et al., 2014; Wilken and Ma, 2004). Here, however, we focus on compression that reflects another factor: representational structure that supports generalization.

As illustrated above, compositional and semantic representations both involve forms of representational sharing that support generalization, and can be thought of as forms of compression. In this article, we formalize the effects of such representational structure on generalization (operationalized in terms of similarity judgments), and the consequences these have both for more traditional measures of performance (operationalized as signal identification) and processing capacity (i.e., identification in multi-item settings, that quantifies the number of items that can be represented simultaneously while being kept distinct).

Specifically, we show that under reasonable assumptions and conditions, levels of compression (e.g., imposed by semantically structured representations) that optimize generalization are associated with amounts of distortion that limit identification. Critically, we show that such constraints in identification have dire consequences for processing capacity (i.e., identification in multi-item settings), limiting this to the range observed by Miller. We refer to this relationship between generalization and processing capacity as the Miller-Shepard Law. Below, we provide the foundations for our operationalization of generalization, grounded on classic work in cognitive science.

3.2 Semanticity and Compression: The Universal Law of Generalization

Understanding the relationship between representation and generalization is one of the most fundamental and longstanding goals in cognitive science. Perhaps the most definitive and influential treatment of this is Shepard's (1987) Universal Law of Generalization. This asserts that information should be represented in a form that is monotonically related to similarity structure in the world that is relevant to how the agent should respond to such information. Importantly, this allows the specific metric properties of internal ("psychological") representations to vary from those of the physical world, so long as they capture the same similarity structure; such metricity is generally referred to as semanticity. Specifically, the law asserts that "the probability that a response. . . to any stimulus will generalize to any other is an invariant monotonic function of the [psychological] distance between them. .. [and] to a good approximation, this probability of generalization decays exponentially with this distance" (Shepard, 1987; p.1317). Based on these principles, Shepard was able to derive psychological metric spaces (i.e., of internal representations) that could predict with remarkable accuracy empirical data concerning human psychophysical judgments, all of which exhibited strikingly reliable exponential gradients of generalization. Intuitively, the generalization gradient can be thought of as reflecting a form of representational sharing, in the sense that representations overlap to the extent that they lead to similar responses, while dissimilar representations are more distinct, or separated. From an information-theoretic perspective, the generalization gradient implements a form of compression that supports generalization, and aligns with human psychophysical judgments.

3.4 Decision Noise and the Optimization of Generalization

Intuitively, one might expect that there should be a "sweet spot" for the generalization gradient. This can be seen in the limits: at one extreme, eliminating the generalization gradient (and therefore any compression) effectively renders all representations equally separated from one another (e.g., orthogonal "one hot" codes), precluding the use of similarity for generalization. At the other extreme, if the gradient is too broad, or flat (maximum compression), then all representations overlap entirely with one another, so that no distinctions are possible. Thus, generalization should be best served by some intermediate width of the gradient. Here, we address this quantitatively, by examining the impact that width of the gradient (i.e., rate or decay of the exponential) has on performance on a simple similarity judgment task that we use to operationalize generalization. Somewhat counterintuitively, we find that as long as the generalization gradient has a finite decay rate and is continuous, and there is no uncertainty in the decision process, then performance on the generalization task is monotonically improved by narrowing the generalization gradient (see dashed line in Figure 3A below), and performance is best for the narrowest possible gradient (i.e., infinitely small compression). However, introducing any amount of noise into the decision process induces a non-monotonicity, that captures the intuition above, with a well-defined optimal width of the gradient. We demonstrate these effects below, and characterize their relationship to accuracy of performance on an identification task, and their relationship to set size, that undergirds the Miller-Shepard Law.

Identification and generalization tasks. We begin by examining how the decay rate of the exponential generalization gradient impacts performance in an identification and a generalization task, and how this interacts with set size. The former replicates the structure of tasks commonly used to assess limits in processing capacity (e.g., Luck and Vogel, 1997; Miller, 1956); we use the latter to assess generalization under conditions as similar to the identification task as possible. Both tasks have the same basic structure: an agent is presented with and required to represent a set S of N stimuli that are drawn from a superset of task-relevant stimuli; the agent is then required to select an item from S based on a probe and a prompt. In the identification task, the probe is drawn from S, and the agent is prompted to select the item in S to which it is identical. Again, this is the design commonly used to assess capacity limits, by varying the set size N . Critically, in the generalization task, the probe is specifically chosen to not be in S, and the agent is prompted to select the item that is most similar to the probe (and hence its properties are most likely to generalize to the probe; Shepard, 1987).

The foregoing analysis indicates that accuracy of both identification and generalization are optimized by using the narrowest gradient -i.e., least compressed (or most separated) representations -possible. However, as noted earlier, it is commonly assumed that less compression -i.e., greater complexity -is costly in terms of representational resources (e.g., code length or precision), and that this may sometimes favor compression at the expense of accuracy. Zaslavsky et al., 2018 provided evidence of this within the context of the IB formulation for the domain of color naming; and Sims, 2016 provided evidence of a similar effect in perceptual judgment and working memory tasks similar to those addressed by Miller (1956), suggesting that empirically observed generalization gradients (which, in contrast to the analysis above, are not minimal) may reflect capacity constraints in representation (that is, physical limits to representational complexity). Decision uncertainty and non-monotonicity of generalization performance. The analysis described above assumes that the encoding, representation, and decoding of information -and any computations based on these -are free of any noise in the decision process, and that any constraints on performance (i.e., distortion) are attributable to the representations themselves (i.e., compression). However, surprisingly and significantly, adding noise to the decoding process itself qualitatively alters the effect of β on generalization error; specifically, it induces a non-monotonic relationship between com-pression and generalization, comporting with the intuition suggested above (and by others; e.g., Shamir et al., 2010; Sims, 2016), that there is an intermediate amount of compression that optimizes generalization (implemented here as the exponential decay rate β).

In summary, these analyses show that decreasing noise has a relatively weak impact on raising β * and I * i , and thus on ameliorating the impact that optimizing for generalization has in limiting identification accuracy.

Interim summary of the analyses.

In aggregate, the results in this section show that any noise in the decision process induces non-monotonicity in the relationship between the generalization gradient and generalization performance, with an optimum in the former that maximizes the latter. Furthermore, the analyses reveal that even for vanishingly small amounts of noise, optimizing for generalization is associated with dramatic decreases in identification accuracy that, in turn, is greatly amplified as set size increases. Sims (2018) noted that "adaptive behavior requires perceptual generalization and discrimination abilities that are finely calibrated to the costs of perceptual error" (p. 652). Here, we show that, assuming there is any uncertainty in the decision process, then such calibration must face the draconian effect that optimizing for generalization has on reducing identification accuracy, and the effect that this has on reducing processing capacity as the size of the set of items that must be discriminated increases. This is consistent with the evidence that people favor generalization in tasks that require it, despite the constraints on processing capacity that this imposes -tasks such as those considered by Miller, and that we consider in more detail with a neural network model below (see Section 4). Finally, we note that the analyses above were restricted to a generalization gradient (i.e., semanticity) as the form of compression, involving features that varied along a single dimension. They did not take account of compositionality, another form of structure that uses compression for generalization, but assumes that items can be represented by features that vary along more than one dimension. Compositionality is commonly exploited in experimental tasks used to study processing constraints, and thus we extend our treatment to address this in the sections that follow.

3.5 Semanticity and Compositionality

The foregoing analyses focus on an exponential generalization gradient -consistent with Shepard's Universal Law of Generalization -as a form of compression. This represents a form of semanticity, in which representations are organized in a metric space. However, as discussed earlier in the Illustrative Example, compositionality is another form of structure that is also commonly considered to be important for learning and generalization. One way to think about the relationship between these is as different forms of covariance structure, or representational sharing (see Figure 7): Semanticity reflects covariance (sharing) among the features within a given dimension (e.g., the representations of red and pink are more highly correlated than red and blue); while compositionality reflects the absence of covariation among features across dimensions (e.g., colors are not correlated with positions).

4 Neural Network Model of Processing Limitations

We extended the abstract model above in three ways. (1) We committed to a gridlike code -a well-known similarity-preserving code in biological systems -and show that the same pattern of effects holds in this setting. (2) The results also show that the need to transiently bind separate features from otherwise independent dimensions, imposed by compositional representations, can induce uncertainty (via superposition) that, when operating over correlated grid-like codes, is sufficient to create a tradeoff without any external decision noise. (3) We evaluated the generalization/identification tradeoff when the decision process is determined by the iterative dynamics of a Hopfied-Ising model, rather than a static argmax decision rule. We begin by providing an analysis of these factors in the model, relating these to the formal analysis presented in the first part of this paper. We then show that when the model is used to simulate the tasks considered by Miller 1956, it reproduces the empirical observations that were the focus of his inquiry.

Coding Assumptions.

The model used compositional representations (Figure 12A), constructed from features represented independently across dimensions, but represented semantically within each dimension (as schematized in Figure 7). The latter resembled grid-like codes observed in medial entorhinal cortex (Dordek et al., 2016; Hafting et al., 2005; Stachenfeld et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2015). For example, to represent a display of colored shapes at particular locations, as in the illustrative example and Figure 1, an object's location would be represented as a pattern of activity over nodes representing sine waves of different frequencies and phases that jointly encode the value of the location variable (Bicanski and Burgess, 2019), and likewise for color. This specific choice of codes was motivated by both theoretical and empirical considerations. Theoretically, prior work has shown that grid codes exemplify representational efficiency (Chandra et al., 2023; Fiete et al., 2008; Stachenfeld et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2015), minimizing the number of variables necessary to cover the space (Wei et al., 2015) while promoting generalization (Frankland et al., 2019; Mondal et al., 2023; Whittington et al., 2020). Empirically, grid-like codes have been found in a variety of species, including humans, to represent spatial as well as non-spatial dimensions such as sound (Aronov et al., 2017), olfactory stimuli (Horner et al., 2016), 2D visual arrays (Bicanski and Burgess, 2019), and abstract conceptual dimensions (Constantinescu et al., 2016). Here, we assumed that task-relevant features in classic tasks used to reveal limits in information-processing capacity -such as color and number -could be reasonably and usefully represented in this way as well.

Specifically, we assume each stimulus s i is encoded as a grid-like code ξ(s i ) constructed from M modules of P phase offsets each, with frequencies related by a geometric scaling ratio λ. The generalization gradient e -β d(s i ,sp) maps onto the code-space kernel: with the gradient width β ↔ α controlled by the scale ratio λ between successive modules (Figure 9) 11 All inference regarding s i and s p is mediated by this representational space; in particular, the "psychological distance" that governs both generalization and identification is the code-space distance 1-cos(ξ i , ξ p ), not the native distance d(s i , s p ) directly.

Figure 9A shows that a grid-like code captures the principles adduced above: points closer in the native space (e.g., a spatial array) are separated by fewer bits in the coding space. Furthermore, this function decays approximately exponentially. This is not unique to grid-like codes, but instead a common property of similarity-preserving factorizations of a domain. However, it demonstrates that the codes we employ align with the assumptions of the abstract model. Importantly, the slope of the generalization gradient can be modified by manipulating the grid-scale parameter λ, which controls the size and rate of change of a grid-cell's firing field (Figure 9B-C).

Uncertainty via Grid-Cells & Superposition.

In both the abstract model and the grid code model considered thus far, uncertainty was introduced at the decision stage as an additive noise constant ∆. However, binding of representations required by the use of compositional codes introduces another potential source of uncertainty -one that enters earlier in the processing stream via the superposition of patterns. For example, we assume that representing colors at particular locations (as in the Illustrative Example; see Figure 1) requires dynamic binding of the color and position representations for each item. Moreover, metric codes are necessarily correlated-nearby stimuli must have similar codes for effective generalization -and so the patterns partially interfere. Uncertainty via Grid-cells, Superposition, & Dynamic Retrieval.

Finally, we consider yet another potential source of uncertainty in the neural network model, that arises as a consequence of retrieval dynamics and thus, analogous to ∆, is intrinsic to the decision process itself. Specifically, when the settling process of the recurrent network: is applied asynchronously until convergence, it becomes sensitive to the correlational structure of the stored patterns.

Unlike retrieval via selection of the stored item most similar to h, here, an early bit flip toward a neighboring attractor may continue in that direction with each update. Correlated patterns-optimized for generalization-have overlapping attractor basins, so it is possible for the dynamics to settle to a neighboring pattern rather than the target. This has direct consequences for processing capacity.

When the patterns in a memory set are uncorrelated, increasing the number of nodes in a simple auto-associative memory increases the the number of distinct associations (i.e., bindings) that the system can distinctly represent at the same time at the classic rate, given by: L 2 log 2 L (McEliece et al., 1987) or the frequently-cited approximate 0.138L (Hopfield, 1982). However, even relatively small correlations (e.g., r ∼ 0.01) dramatically limit this increase, which in turn limits processing capacity in settings that rely on such bindings to associate the features belonging to each individual item in a display. As in the abstract model, processing capacity is heavily constrained by the amount of correlational structure in the representations, with even modest increases in correlation dramatically restricting capacity. For example, if λ is fixed at the √ e (Wei et al., 2015), we find that the model gridcodes generated above have an average between-pattern correlation of 0.14. Figure 11B shows that for a correlation of r = 0.14, even if network size is increased by three orders of magnitude (e.g., from 32, the size used in the simulations reported below, to 32,000), the number of items that can be stored and successfully retrieved remains within the range of Miller's "magical" number 7 ± 2.

To summarize, in this section, we have extended the abstract model to incorporate plausible similarity-preserving codes and sources of processing uncertainty in biological systems, showing that: (1) the trade-off between generalization and identification derived for the abstract model above emerges naturally from variation of grid-coding scaling; (2) the binding mechanism itself introduces uncertainty in readout, distinct from the independent and additive decision noise of the abstract model, but with a similar effect; and (3) the retrieval dynamics of an autoassociative memory induce an effect similar to decision noise (∆) used in the abstract model, that leads to even more aggressive limits in processing capacity.

4.1 Neural Network Implementation

Processing Capacity: the MEME Model. As outlined above, we adopt two core assumptions to model performance: (1) grid-like compositional codes bound through Hebbian association; and (2) the inferential dynamics of autoassociative memory for retrieval. In the model, grid cells project to a memory buffer W (Equation ( 16)) implemented as a simple recurrent neural network Hopfield, 1982, with nodes corresponding to coding elements in the embedding layer (e.g., grid cells). Each item in a display is defined by a combination of feature values along several dimensions (such as color and spatial location), so that every possible item can be represented compositionally as a unique pattern of activity z(x) over the nodes of the network. For a given context c (e.g., a trial of a task), conjunctive representations are formed by binding the codes for each item's task-relevant feature values, together with the current context and corresponding response, through rapid Hebbian weight updates that are specific to and reset for each new context. 12,13 The Hebbian weight update minimizes an energy function over the nodes corresponding to the feature values of each item and its context (Figure 1B). 4.2 Empirical Application to Behavioral Performance

We applied the model outlined above to the three tasks highlighted by Miller (1956): immediate (or "working") memory; absolute perceptual identification; and numerosity judg-ment. Though these three tasks operate over different time scales and different domains of information, in each the task-relevant input-output mappings (x c → y c ) require mediation by conjunctive codes (z c ) that, we assume, were not already present in the system's existing set of codes (i.e., they involved novel or unfamiliar pairings of features across dimensions). They must therefore be constructed compositionally, as combinations of existing codes along each dimension, such as of colors and locations or reference tones and their corresponding labels.

For each task, we constructed a network with sufficient representational "expressivity" to uniquely encode any pair of feature values an agent might need to distinguish, along each dimension, in that task. We formally quantify expressivity as the joint entropy of the collection of nodes in the network (H(z 1 , . . . , z L )) when feature values at sampled at random along each dimension (See Appendix C.2 for details). For example, if we assume an agent can distinguish the location of any pair of N equally probable points in a 2D spatial array, we would require a representational scheme with a joint entropy of k = log 2 N bits of information. Although the most efficient codes for doing so would be random vectors assigned to each code in network with L nodes, where L = k, (as in the classic analyses of auto-associative networks), such codes would fail to support generalization, given that they are not similarity-preserving.

Instead, to implement similarity structure, guided by the properties of grid-like coding described above as well as empirical psychophysical data, we implemented a coding scheme that provided the desired joint entropy of ∼ k bits for each dimension. In practice, this means that L > k. For example, for a 2D spatial display of 9.8° × 7.3°, as in Luck and Vogel (1997), we use published work on Vernier acuity (Frisén and Frisén, 1981; Westheimer and Beard, 1998) and estimates that agents could likely distinguish approximately 715,400 spatial locations (see Appendix C.2 for additional details). If each of these spatial locations is equiprobable, an observation that a stimulus is located at a particular spatial position would carry 19.47 bits of information. We therefore seek a grid-like coding scheme that scales 8 frequencies at √ e and has a representational capacity of ∼19.47 bits. We find that a binarized grid-code with 32 nodes (8 frequencies x 4 phases) has a joint entropy of ∼19.5 bits and an average between-pattern Pearson correlation of r ∼ 0.14, and use such a code to represent spatial position in our simulations. We use a similar reasoning procedure to derive grid-codes for other dimensions (e.g., color, number, pitch).

Although these are estimates and vary from dimension to dimension, we note a few general points about the model before describing specific results: First, joint entropy of 10-20 often appears sufficient to represent each dimension-specific distinction agents can make. Although this quantity gets larger for high dimensional semantic representations, it is worth noting that a code with a joint entropy of 265 bits would permit the unique identification of each atom in the universe (assuming 10 80 atoms) -this means that systems with even seemingly modest joint entropies have considerable representational capacity. Second, in this formalism, as semantic precision increases (higher joint entropy, narrower generalization gradient β), so does expected working memory capacity, as has been observed empirically (Alvarez and Cavanagh, 2004; Brady and Störmer, 2022). Third, and most critically, as shown above, with even small amounts of correlational structure in the code, processing capacity is expected to remain strikingly low, near Miller's magical number. Here we provide a brief summary of the empirical findings, and our models' account of them. In the Appendix, Section C.1 we provide additional details concerning model implementation and simulations of the experimental tasks.

Immediate (Working) Memory.

Figure 13 shows the model for a classic cued change detection paradigm, variants of which are widely used in the study of visual working memory (e.g., Bays, 2015; Bays and Husain, 2008; Luck and Vogel, 1997; Sims et al., 2012; Wilken and Ma, 2004), and that we used to evaluate the capacity of what Miller referred to as immediate memory. In its simplest form, the task involves a brief presentation of a visual display containing a number of items (e.g., shapes of different colors at various locations), followed by a delay (∼1000 ms), and then a second version of the display in which a target item is spatially cued, and the participant must respond by indicating whether the color of that item has changed (see Figure 1A). Both the number of items in the display (set size) and, critically, the combination of feature values for each item are varied randomly across trials. Absolute Identification.

We used the same model architecture to address capacity limits observed in the absolute perceptual identification tasks considered by Miller (1956). In such tasks, participants were first presented with a reference set of auditory stimuli (e.g., tones) and associated ordinal rankings along a single underlying dimension (e.g., frequency). Then, throughout the experiment (usually lasting around an hour, though in some cases, weeks; see Shiffrin and Nosofsky (1994)), they were presented repeatedly with test stimuli drawn from the reference set in random order, and tested on the ability to identify the corresponding rank. For example, the reference set might have consisted of nine tones evenly spaced between 1,000 and 5,000 Hz and their corresponding ranks (e.g., 1 for 1000 Hz, 2 for 1500 Hz, etc.), for which the participant should have responded "2" to a test tone of 1500 Hz. Numerosity Estimation.

Finally, following Miller (1956) admonition, we "cannot leave this general area" (p. 90) without considering limitations in the ability to judge the number of items in a visual display.

Participants show fast and nearly perfect numerosity reports when the number of items is small -referred to as the "subitizing" range -but are increasingly slow and error-prone as that number increases past some limit (Kaufman et al., 1949; Mandler and Shebo, 1982). Early results put the limit at about 6 (Kaufman et al., 1949), in the range of Miller's 7 plus or minus 2, though decades of subsequent work has found a limit often closer to 3 or 4 (Cheyette and Piantadosi, 2020; Mandler and Shebo, 1982; Revkin et al., 2008; Trick and Pylyshyn, 1993), with variation depending on features of the experiment (e.g., Cheyette and Piantadosi, 2020; Revkin et al., 2008).

To simulate performance of this task, items in the display were embedded in the model as binary patterns over nodes once again using a grid-like code comprised of frequencies and phase, in this case to code for position and number (Figure 15A). The position codes were parameterized to cover the size and resolution of the 2D spatial display, and the integer codes were chosen to span the range of numerosities sampled (e.g., 1-12), and that constituted the range of possible responses in the task.

Note that this task differed from the preceding ones in that all of the information necessary to perform the task was available in the display at the time of the response, without the memory requirements imposed by the tasks considered above. Nevertheless, we assumed it required the same form of structured (i.e,. generalizable) codes and binding mechanisms to represent the arbitrary (i.e., novel) pairing of the number of items and their positions used in the task. Accordingly, the model's representation of the display was generated as follows: For each of the N items in the display, item i ∈ {1, . . . , N } was selected randomly without replacement from the set of remaining Ni items, the code for its position was added to the sum of the codes for the preceding Ni items, that sum was paired with the code for the number i, and the pair was stored (bound) in the weights of the associative memory. This provided the model with a set of numerosity representations for the display, each of which was the conjunction of a number code with a representation of the simultaneously embedded positions for the corresponding number of items. The model was tested by encoding the display as described above, then presenting it as input and allowing the network's activity state to settle, and finally taking the representation of the number code with the shortest distance to the settled state as the network's response regarding the number of items in the display (Figure 15B).

Similar to the simulations of the other two tasks, error rates increased abruptly for displays with 3 or 4 items, closely matching the empirical data for human participants (see Figure 15C-D). In addition to error curves, we computed an estimate for response times (RTs) as the mean number of updates required to reach equilibrium. The qualitative form of RT as a function of set size closely tracked empirically observed RT curves (e.g., Cheyette and Piantadosi, 2020; Kaufman et al., 1949; Mandler and Shebo, 1982; Revkin et al., 2008; Trick and Pylyshyn, 1993): For displays of 3 items or less, RTs were almost flat, after which they increased monotonically (Figure 15D-E). The slope over the increase appears slightly more pronounced for the Hopfield network than for humans in the range tested, though the qualitative form remains the same. Future work may build on these modeling assumptions to more rigorously test alternative model-fits using available datasets.

5 Discussion

5.1 Summary

The constraints on perceptual processing and immediate memory capacity, famously referred to by Miller (1956) as the "magical number 7," have largely remained a mystery. This might be considered even more perplexing, given that similar constraints seem to extend beyond tasks involving perception and memory, to ones involving other functions that have been equally foundational in the study of cognition and the brain, including attention, multitasking capability and cognitive control (Posner and Snyder, 1975; Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977; Treisman and Gelade, 1980). Here, we have identified a potential source of these constraints from an information-theoretic perspective, in terms of a fundamental tradeoff between the value of compression, in the form of structured representational sharing that affords generalization, and the constraints that this imposes on processing capacity -the ability to accurately distinguish those representations when processed at the same time. We have shown that, in the presence of any decision noise: a) even modest amounts of compression, that support generalization, dramatically restrict processing capacity; b) this is observed for psychophysically and neurally informed types of representational structure and processing mechanisms; and c) this account can provide a unified, normative explanation of the restrictive constraints on performance observed across the range of tasks that puzzled Miller (1956).

In the remainder of this article we consider several questions raised by this account: i) How does it relate to the information-theoretic treatment of capacity limits presented in Miller's paper? ii) How does it relate to previous applications of information theory to learning and representation in cognitive science and neuroscience? iii) How does it relate to prominent accounts of capacity constraints in working memory and attention? iv) How do the mechanisms for transient binding postulated by, and central to our models relate to standard psychological and neural mechanisms thought to be responsible for the rapid formation of arbitrary associations? v) To what extent can our account explain similar capacity constraints in other cognitive capabilities, such as multitasking and cognitive control? vi) How can these constraints be overcome? vii) How do our observations relate to seemingly similar issues in statistics, machine learning, and the use of neural network architectures in artificial intelligence? Miller (1956) article was perhaps the most influential one to call attention to the severe limits on immediate memory (now referred to more commonly as short-term or working memory), that he indelibly imprinted in most people's minds as the magic number 7. Oddly, it is known mostly for that observation alone -though he assiduously referred to those who originally made it (Hayes, 1952; Pollack, 1953) -rather than for the observation that consumed him, and actually motivated him to refer to the number as magical: the diversity of settings and forms in which the same constraint appears. This led him to surmise "that there may be something deep and profound behind all these sevens, something that is calling out for us to discover it." Miller was inspired by the initial surge in applications of information theory to perception and memory following Shannon's seminal 1948 paper, and intrigued by the prospect of using information-theoretic measures to solve the mystery of "all the sevens."

5.2 Relationship to Miller's Analysis

In his effort to solve the mystery, Miller focused on a set of tasks that had been used to assess the channel capacity of human cognition as an information transmission system. Accordingly, he sought to quantify the capacity of memory and perceptual identification in these tasks in terms of the amount of information transferred, measured in bits. He accepted this as a reasonable measure of capacity for perceptual processing (i.e., performance in the absolute identification and numerosity tasks), but ran into trouble when applying it to immediate memory. There, he noted that the number of bits transmitted seemed to vary widely depending on the items to be remembered. For example, decimal digits carry about 3.3 bits each, so remembering seven digits (e.g., a birth date) actually amounts to storing 23 bits of information; and English words carry about ten bits, so remembering seven English words amounts to storing about 70 bits, and so on. This led Miller to conclude that the constraint on immediate memory is not the absolute amount of information conveyed by the memories, but rather the number of familiar items that must be remembered, which he referred to as "chunks." Without a similar construct for perception, and without a formal characterization of a chunk, he expressed suspicion that the constraint of 2.5 bits for perception and the seven chunks for immediate memory was "only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence."

The work we present here suggests that the disparities Miller observed across tasks may be understood, at least in part, by taking account of representational sharing that reflects correlations among the features of items. Much like Mathy and Feldman (2012) and Nassar et al. (2018), we suggest that such correlations provide a formal grounding for what Miller referred to as a "chunk" (and that we refer to as an "item"): a pattern of covariation over a set of feature values along one set of dimensions (e.g., color and shape) that is shared over a range of values along other dimensions (e.g., spatial or temporal; see Appendix, Section E.1). This embraces objects in a display (such as in Figure 1A), but also the digits of a birthday, or the letters of a word. Crucially, we show that the pervasiveness of the ∼2.5 bit constraint can be explained by taking account of how representational structure affects identification error, and how this interacts with the number of items that can be distinguishably represented at the same time.

From this perspective, Miller's chunks can be viewed as conjunctive codes (separated representations) dedicated to particular items, that have been acquired through repeated exposure to the correlations among feature values that define those items. While such codes are not representationally efficient (in information-theoretic terms, they have high complexity), the lack of correlations among them increases their distinctiveness, allowing them to be used at the same time without risk of interference (identification errors), and thus increases the number of items that can be processed at the same time -i.e., they afford higher processing capacity. Conversely, when such conjunctive codes dedicated to individual items are not available -that is, when the items must be represented as combinations of feature values for which the correlations have not previously been experienced,or at least learned -then compositional codes (a form of representational sharing) must be used that support generalization. However, the structure of such codes is a form of compression, associated with distortion (identification errors) that limits processing capacity. These are exactly the conditions imposed by the tasks on which Miller focused. Furthermore, the formal analyses and simulations we presented indicate that these conditions are subject to a remarkably consistent constraint of approximately ∼2.5 bits of processing capacity, and that this can be traced to a strikingly strong asymmetry in the effects of representational structure (i.e. compression) in the service of generalization, versus processing capacity: Even the modest amounts of representational sharing required to promote generalization have draconian effects on processing capacity.

In sum, inspired by Miller's information-theoretic approach, and his observations of a provocative similarity in processing constraints among tasks that probed perception and memory, we arrive at a conclusion that differs from his, but one that we suspect he may have appreciated: The similarity in constraints does indeed reflect "something deep and profound behind all these sevens:" a relationship between representation and processing in which compression in the service of generalization comes at a disproportionate cost to processing capacity. This defines an envelope of performance within which any information processing system must operate, that we have dubbed as the Miller-Shepard Law. We have shown that, with even a modest premium placed on generalization, processing capacity is constrained -in the number of independent items that can be processed at once -to the number that so intrigued Miller. However, if it is truly a general principle, it should apply not only to the particular phenomena that intrigued Miller but, more broadly, to the wealth of related data and models that have accrued in the nearly seven decades since his landmark article. In the remainder of this Discussion, we consider some of these connections.

In contrast to such a fixed architectural constraint, we argue that limits to processing capacity arise from the representational structure demanded by generalization: codes optimized to generalize are necessarily correlated, correlated codes interfere when they must be kept distinct, and this limits the number of such codes that can be processed at the same time. Critically, the tasks most commonly used to assess attentional and/or working memory capacity use novel or unfamiliar stimuli (e.g., arbitrary combinations of features such as position, color, shape, and/or time of occurrence), precisely in order to probe the flexibility of processing for which attention and working memory are thought to be responsible. In other words, by design, they involve stimuli that require the use of generalizable representations. By our account, it is precisely this requirement that imposes the capacity limit. This provides a normative account of capacity limits in terms of a fundamental representational tradeoff, rather than a fixed architectural constraint.

At the same time, our account is generally well-aligned with the idea that the limit is closely associated with attentional function. For example, the associative binding mechanism in the models we used to simulate Miller's tasks -that allowed the system to flexibly represent arbitrary combinations of features -implemented precisely the binding operation assigned to attention by the Feature Integration Theory of attention Treisman and Gelade, 1980. Seen in this way, the capacity of the attentional store (e.g., Cowan, 2001) and the capacity of the focus of attention may reflect the same quantity. What we believe our account adds is a proposed answer to what determines that capacity: the similarity structure of the codes being bound, which is required for generalization, rather than a fixed architectural constraint. This proposal is also consistent with Miller's principle of chunking, which affords greater capacity through the use of previously-formed associations (e.g., between a word and its constituent letters) to combine elements into distinguishable representations (the word). In such cases, the capacity constraint is largely determined by the representational level at which the combinations are less familiar (e.g., an arbitrary sequence of word combinations vs. a sentence); at that level, once again, codes are required that afford generalization (e.g., compositional codes that bind each word or letter to a position); however, as we have argued, because such codes are less distinguishable they are subject to capacity constraints. This view is consistent with observations by Halford et al. (2005 Halford et al. ( , 2007) ) that capacity is limited not by the number of elements held but by the number of relations among them that must be simultaneously represented, with a limit of roughly four variables. In the present framework, each argument of a relation is a feature code that is bound into a single representation. The limit on relational complexity and the limit on set size may be a single constraint on how many codes can be superimposed before interference renders them indistinguishable.

Resource theories and target confusability competition. Another influential account of capacity limits in working memory attributes this to a fixed resource that is distributed among the items currently held in memory, so that the precision with which each is represented declines continuously as load increases (Bays and Husain, 2008; Ma et al., 2014; Wilken and Ma, 2004). On this view there is no discrete capacity limit, and the resource is typically identified with the achievable activity of a neural population, perhaps bounded by divisive normalization (Ma et al., 2014), and/or metabolic constraints. Schurgin et al. (2020) recent model of visual short term memory meaningfully complements this tradition by considering what makes individual memories confusable in the first place. The authors show that a signal detection model with a single memory-strength parameter -target confusability competition (TCC) -accounts for continuous-report, n-AFC, and change-detection data across set sizes, delays, and encoding times, in tasks closely related to those considered here. TCC is closely related to our abstract model: in both, a probe elicits a graded similarity signal across candidate items, noise is added, and the maximum is selected. Critically, in TCC the similarity among response alternatives follows an independently measured exponential function rather than being linear in stimulus space. TCC takes that function as given, consistent with its derivation from efficient-coding principles (Sims, 2018), and estimates d ′ separately for each condition; the resulting decline of d ′ with set size follows a power law that the authors note is broadly consistent with resource theories, without committing to a mechanism.

Our current work bears on two issues both accounts leave open. First, what determines the width of the similarity function (and hence processing capacity)? We show that when the width is optimized for generalization, identification performance is constrained to be low, which in turn limits processing capacity. Second, what mechanism produces the decline with set size? Resource theories hold that a fixed pool is shared among N items, so each is represented less precisely. We attribute this decrease in precision to representational interference: N correlated codes superimposed in a common substrate corrupt one another, so each is retrieved less reliably. On this view, representational sharing determines the severity of the constraint: optimization for generalization accounts for the strikingly restrictive effects on load beyond what may be attributable to the cost of precision alone. We take the success of TCC across tasks and memory systems as convergent evidence that similarity structure -not a fixed store -is the primary determinant of capacity. How the structural constraint interacts with the cost of precision is an important direction for future work.

5.4 Relationship to Rate-Distortion Theory

We began our analysis by reviewing RDT, and then used it as framework within which to formally characterize the relationship between semanticity, as a form of compression, and processing capacity. Here, we briefly summarize the contributions we believe our analyses make to the use of RDT for studying cognitive function, and potentially interesting future directions in which this could be taken. In Section A.4 of the Appendix we consider the formal relationship between our analyses and the Information Bottleneck as an instance of RDT).

RDT is concerned with the effects of distortion on the transmission of information. Distortion -defined in terms of differences between the source and decoded signals -can be due to perturbations of the signal during transmission, usually referred to as noise, or more structured forms of perturbations (e.g., representational) that reduce the complexity of the representations used to encode the data, referred to as compression. The IB (Tishby et al., 2000) focuses on forms of compression that are imposed by some objective (e.g., classification error). This has been used to derive a Pareto front in the relationship between compression and performance (e.g., Zaslavsky et al., 2018), that specifies the maximum achievable level of performance for a given level of compression. In a related vein, Sims (2018) used RDT to derive the exponential similarity gradient proposed by Shepard, 1987 Universal Law of Generalization as the optimal form of compression for generalization. However, while that identifies the optimal form of compression, neither that derivation nor the IB specify the amount of compression that is optimal. In both analyses, increasing complexityreducing compressionmonotonically improves performance. Accordingly, empirical evidence for compression in human cognitive function (e.g., Zaslavsky et al., 2018) is usually taken to reflect a capacity constraint, either imposed by a fixed limit on representational complexity (e.g., Sims, 2016), or the result of a tradeoff against some physical cost (e.g., metabolic) incurred by precision(i.e., complexity; Bays and Husain, 2008; Wilken and Ma, 2004). Importantly, the underlying analyses all assume that compression is the only source of distortion, and that all other processes are lossless (i.e., noise-free). The work we present here indicates that if another source of distortion is considered -decision noisethe relationship between compression and generalization is qualitatively changed from being monotonic to non-monotonic, such that: a) an intermediate amount of compression can be identified that is optimal for generalization; b) that optimum is associated with considerable degradation in the ability to accurately identify individual items, and thereby processing capacity (see Figure 2). This relationship appears with a remarkably small (virtually any) amount of decision noise. This interaction between decision noise and compression is a potentially important extension of RDT, offering a normative account of how generalization may drive compression, in addition to considerations of capacity noted above.

Our findings, along with those of Sims (2016), contribute to RDT in another potentially important way: by extending analyses of the effects of compression on the processing of individual signals, to the processing of multiple signals at once -what we have referred to more generally as processing capacity. Here, we found that, when compression is optimized for generalization, the restrictive effects observed on identification are rapidly amplified as the number of items that must be processed (set size) is increased. At the least, this has value for applications of RDT to cognitive science and neuroscience, where such capacity constraints are widely observed, such as those that were canonized by Miller. However, as we discuss further on, these are also likely to be as important to the design of artificial agents that more closely approximate the flexibility and efficiency of human cognitive function.

These contributions suggest potential new lines of work, that may help further refine our understanding of the relationship between compression and generalization. One is suggested by how we operationalized generalization. We did so by contrasting a generalization task with an identity task. Both involved the selection of an item in a test set that best matched a probe. However, they differed in the nature of the match -identicality or similarity -which was manipulated, across the two tasks, by how the probe was sampled. In the identity task, it was sampled from the test set itself; that is, "within distribution." This can be thought of as modeling circumstances in which existing knowledge (the test set) has all the information needed to perform the task (identify the probe), and no generalization is required because the probe was chosen to be in the test set. In contrast, in the generalization task, the probe was chosen to not be an item in the test test; that is, "out of distribution." This can be thought of as modeling circumstances in which generalization is required: existing knowledge must be represented in a form that shares as much information as possible with the distribution of possible probes, without actually (exactly) representing any of them. Sampling the probe from a uniform distribution of items outside the test set demanded the most extreme form of generalization. Importantly, the two extremes that we used define a continuum, in which the need for generalization grows with the disparity between the distribution of existing representations and that of the items that will be encountered in the future (i.e., from which a "probe" may be drawn). How the effects we have reported here vary when the need for generalization is varied along this continuum remains an open and interesting question. This question connects to recent work on variable-rate encoding, in which the rate -and so the achievable precision -differs between representations (Hedayati et al., 2022) and can be adapted to the agent's circumstances (Nagy et al., 2025). Our framework suggests one normative driver of such variation: the differential demand for generalization across circumstances, with greater demand favoring intermediate rates and thus more representational sharing.

Similarly, our analyses assumed a particular, and relatively simple form of loss (or objective) closely related to ones used in previous work (e.g., categorization; Zaslavsky et al., 2018). The extent to which our findings extend to other forms of loss, tuned to more complex forms of structure (e.g., sequential and/or as hierarchical) that are characteristic of other domains (e.g., natural and formal languages) remains another open question. Both of these questions present interesting and potentially important directions for future research.

Finally, it is worth noting that a central tenet of RDT is that, for any given distortion, there is a commensurate increase in the complexity of the code that can compensate for the loss. For example, as suggested by the Illustrative Example presented at the beginning of this article, this increase in complexity can be achieved by using dedicated conjunctive codes for each combination of features to avoid the loss (i.e., avert the "binding problem") imposed by the use of compositionally compressed codes. More generally, the tension between generalization and processing capacity is a reflection of this principle, wherein the learning of dedicated (e.g., conjunctive) representations decreases identification error, and thereby increases processing capacity -a consideration that we turn to below (and in greater detail in Section E of the Appendix) as a way of formalizing the effects of consolidation, automatization, and "chunking" noted above. However, as noted there, these processes take time, and thus cannot contribute meaningfully to the rapid and flexible processing of novel states -i.e., generalization -that are required for the kinds of tasks on which Miller focused. From the perspective of RDT this suggests that, using identification error as the loss, it should be possible to quantify the increase in representational complexity that would be required to compensate for the distortion introduced by forms of structure that support generalization (such semanticity and compositionality). Doing so could usefully leverage RDT to analyze and characterize the kinds of representations that improve processing capacity in a given domain; that, in turn, could provide a framework for formalizing our understanding of the processes underlying consolidation, automatization and chunking, that we consider further below.

5.5 information-theoretic Accounts of Learning and Representation

Our work builds on a longstanding and influential tradition of theoretical work in cognitive science and neuroscience, inspired both by statistical physics and information theory (Barlow, 1961; Friston, 2010; Linsker, 1988) suggesting that the brain implements efficient coding to maximize information preservation, given an agent's resource constraints. Here, we argue that by extending this approach to take account of representational structure -viewed, through the lens of RDT, as a form of compression -can help explain an apparent paradox in human cognitive function: on the one hand, the remarkable range of information over which it can operate, as well as the flexibility it exhibits in responding to novel information; and, on the other, the equally striking constraints in processing capacity it exhibits in some settings. Most previous work has focused on the former -that is, optimal coding that maximizes the ability of the system to come to accurately represent as much information as efficiently as possible. Furthermore, it has focused largely on probabilistic structure, wherein optimal coding is achieved by adapting representational complexity (e.g., code length) to the frequency of events. The objective of such forms of optimization is to minimize identification error -the frequency with which the selected code is not identical to the correct one. Here, we have pointed out that an equally important goal of learning is to represent the similarity structure of the world, the objective of which is to minimize generalization error -the distance of the selected code from the correct one. This follows from Shepard (1987) Universal Law of Generalization, and relies on the use of representational sharing and attendant compression. Here, we operationalize generalization error as a form of compression, and thus its tension with the traditional focus on minimizing identification error and, accordingly, the optimization of generalization in tension with the optimization of processing capacity. We have shown that this tradeoff can explain the conditions under which cognitive function is constrained -viz., when it relies on the use of representational sharing, in the form of semantically and/or compositionally structured codes, to process novel items -and the remarkable severity of these constraints even when the amount of structure introduced to the codes is relatively modest.

5.6 Relationship to Other Accounts of Generalization and Processing Constraints

Our account is closely related to other influential accounts of generalization and how this relates to constraints on processing, that we consider below. Importantly, however, while all of these formalize the tension between generalization and the ability to identify individual items and/or discriminate them from others, none of these lines of work -nor, to our knowledge, any others -directly analyze the consequences that this has for the number of items that can be processed at once, that we refer to as processing capacity, and that we propose as a principled account of Miller's (1956) observations.

Generalization: Gaussian Process models.

An elegant series of analyses by Wu and colleagues, exemplified by Wu et al. (2021), show how generalization-formalized within the Bayesian framework of Gaussian Processes-can arise from learning, and how this can explain behavioral performance in a range of cognitive tasks (Ten et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2021). Furthermore, they show how this formulation relates to Shepard's (1987) Universal Law of Generalization, and how it can be used to specify generalization gradients across representations (Wu et al., 2021 (Wu et al., , 2025)). However, like Shepard's original formulation (as well as others that have been derived from it, such as Sims (2018) and the information theoretic accounts we discussed above, e.g., Tishby and Zaslavsky, 2015), Gaussian Process models do not provide a normative account of the degree of generalization that is optimal. Rather, generalization gradients, as well as parameters controlling memory effects (e.g., recency and surprise prioritization) are inferred from the data (Ten et al., 2025) and compared against working memory capacity scores from other tasks. This leverages the tension between generalization performance and capacity constraints for model specification, but it does not explain it. Here, we formalize this tension: we show how optimizing for generalization can explain the commonly observed severity of capacity constraints.

That said, coupling our observations with the approach taken by Gaussian Process models points to interesting and potentially important directions for future research. For example, how do factors associated with learning (distribution of samples, learning rate, etc.) impact the extent to which a system converges on optimal generalization gradients, and to what extent is this associated with changes in capacity constraints as would be predicted by our account. Furthermore, the work of Wu and colleagues (Ten et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2021 Wu et al., , 2025) ) address generalization performance in more complex decision making tasks (e.g., explore-exploit tasks on graphs, rule learning, and function extrapolation in continuous spaces) than we consider, raising the question of how our normative account of the relationship between generalization and capacity constraints may extend to these more complex task domains.

Generalization versus discrimination in neural representations.

Another influential line of work has addressed the tradeoff between generalization and discrimination in the context of neural representations (Barak et al., 2013). In this work, generalization is operationalized as the ability to respond to items as a function of their similarity to a prototype, closely aligned with Shepard's Universal Law of Generalization (Shepard, 1987) and the generalization task used in the work we present in this article. Similarly, the operationalization of discrimination-as the ability to assign correct category labels to items-aligns closely with the identification task we used to quantify processing capacity, as well as measures of accuracy used in work on the Information Bottleneck (Tishby et al., 2000; Zaslavsky et al., 2018, also see Appendix A.4). Barak et al. (2013) show that moving representations of items farther apart in neural activity space improves discrimination but impairs generalization, consonant with our analyses as well as the tradeoff between accuracy and compression the Information Bottleneck (e.g., see Figure 2). Interestingly, their formulation of this tradeoff also exhibits a non-monotonicity in the generalization gradient (see their Figure 9) with an optimum that is associated with relatively poor discrimination. Our analyses suggest that the non-monotonicity should reflect some form of uncertainty in the inference processes. Furthermore, and critically, our treatment extends the analysis of the relationship between generalization and discrimination from single items to the consequences it has for processing multiple items at once, thus bringing the effects of coding and generalization into contact with the capacity limits discussed by Miller (1956). More generally, it would be valuable to formally relate the coding framework of Barak et al. (2013) to our framing of the problem in information theoretic terms. This would provide a valuable bridge in our understanding of the principles of neural coding to limits in processing capacity, that may be relevant to the precision-load tradeoff we turn to below. It may also provide theoretical guidance for the analysis of brain imaging data, by providing quantitatively precise predictions about the relationship between patterns of brain activity (e.g., correlation among neural representations) and limits to processing capacity (measured behaviorally).

5.7 Relationship to Neural Mechanisms for Associative Memory

The ability to rapidly associate (bind) compositional representations to process novel items plays a central role in our account, that we assume is supported by some form of associative memory. This raises two closely related questions: How do such associative mechanisms relate to traditional cognitive constructs, such as episodic memory (EM) and working memory (WM); and what neural mechanisms might be responsible for these?

Rapid associative binding is a hallmark of EM, widely believed to be supported by the hippocampus (e.g., McClelland et al., 1995), and perhaps other structures such as the cerebellum (Musslick et al., 2023; T. W. Webb et al., 2024). The mechanistic model we presented, using a recurrent neural network and Hebbian learning, aligns with models of such structures (e.g., Hasselmo and Wyble, 1997; K. A. Norman and O'Reilly, 2003; Spens and Burgess, 2024).

Furthermore, one of the most influential theories of EM function, the Temporal Context Model (TCM; Howard and Kahana, 2002), asserts that entries in EM are accompanied by a temporal code, usually assumed to be a slowly drifting signal, that makes it possible to identify when an event occurred and its temporal relation to other events. At the same time, this introduces characteristic patterns of memory errors arising from correlations in the temporal code, for which there is extensive empirical support (e.g., Sederberg et al., 2008). The presence of such structure in EM is consistent with its role as a mechanism for rapid associative binding of structured representations, and the costs that this incurs, that are central to our account.

Alternatively, it has been proposed that rapid associative binding may occur among representations actively maintained in WM, whether using similar Hebbian mechanisms (Oberauer et al., 2012), phase coupling among oscillating representations (Hommel, 2004; Jensen and Lisman, 1998; Roux et al., 2012), short-term synaptic potentiation (Jonides et al., 2008), the online formation of tensor product representations (Smolensky, 1990; e.g., see Appendix, Section C.3), or through projection into a high-dimensional space of actively maintainable random representations (Bouchacourt and Buschman, 2019).

One fundamental difference between EM and WM is the durability of the associations formed among representations: a fundamental feature of EM is that such associations are stable, and remain accessible for considerable periods of time after they are formed (hours to years), a feature that is assumed to reflect their formation through modification of synaptic weights. In contrast, WM is generally assumed to rely on the transient maintenance of information in activity states: associations formed in WM are inaccessible once the corresponding representations are no longer active, presumably after short periods of time (minutes if not seconds; though see Stokes (2015) for the possibility of "activity-silent working memory"). Use of mechanisms responsible for EM versus WM for binding should thus make different predictions about the influence of the associations formed on subsequent processing over different temporal intervals -a factor that has received some attention (Beukers et al., 2021), but remains an important direction for future research. More realistically, it seems likely that both types of mechanisms play a role in binding, and that interactions between them contribute to the efficiency and flexibility of human cognitive function, a possibility that has begun to attract attention both in cognitive neuroscience (Frankland and Greene, 2020; Giallanza et al., 2024) and machine learning (Graves et al., 2014). One particularly interesting possibility is that rapid binding capability, evolved under the pressures for optimization discussed here, provided a platform for the emergence of symbolic computation in the brain, an idea that has also begun to attract attention in cognitive neuroscience and machine learning (T. W. Webb et al., 2021 Webb et al., , 2024)).

5.8 Relevance to Language Processing

Language is perhaps the most widely recognized domain in which there is pressure for compositionally structured representations that support generalization (Fodor and Pylyshyn, 1988). Therefore, based on our considerations, this should be closely accompanied by extreme limits in capacity when multiple similar representational codes must be processed simultaneously. Such limits were first pointed out by George Miller and Noam Chomsky (Chomsky and Miller, 1958), who observed that we seem to have striking limits in our ability to process syntactically well-formed sentences that require the maintenance and discrimination of phrases with highly similar structure, such as in self-embedded clauses (e.g., "the mouse the cat the dog bit chased hid"). Modern accounts suggest that the need to manage long-distance dependencies -syntactic, semantic and referential relations that may stretch across dozens of words or even sentences -requires that linguistic representations be actively maintained in working memory which, coupled with similarity-based interference among those linguistic representations, give rise to the severe capacity limits observed in language processing (Gibson, 1998 (Gibson, , 2000;; Lewis, 1996; Lewis et al., 2006).

What we add here are analyses that indicate such capacity limits were inevitable given the pressures for generalization in language.

The challenge of reliably handling long-distance dependencies in natural language led directly to the development of architectures such as the Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), and large language models (LLMs) based on them. Such models provide a useful test of the extent to which the tension between generalization and processing capacity is a general principle. On the one hand, they have massive numbers of parameters, suggesting that resources for representation and processing should not be a strong source of constraint. On the other hand, they were designed to handle the online processing of long-distance dependencies, and trained under heavy pressure for generalization, suggesting that they should make use of structured codes. By our account, these factors should together induce strict constraints on processing of multiple items at the same time, similar to the constraints on processing capacity observed in humans. Remarkably, there is growing evidence that this is the case, when such models are tested under conditions that elicit such constraints in humans: They have difficulty processing sentences involving self-embeddings (with their attention patterns reflecting this; Ryu and Lewis, 2021), and with tasks requiring the simultaneous processing of multiple items composed of arbitrary combinations of features (Campbell et al., 2024). We return to the latter where we consider the relevance of our work to artificial systems in greater detail further below. Our analyses also align with theories about the trajectory of representational structure over the course of language development, that we return to in the section below, where we discuss how learning interacts with the tradeoff between generalization and processing capacity. All of that said, language involves forms of structure (e.g., sequential and hierarchical) that are substantially more complex than the ones we have examined in this article. While it stands to reason that generalization will rely on some form of similarity expressed at those higher levels of structure, and that this will require representational sharing that imposes processing constraints similar to the ones we have examined here, evaluating this remains an important challenge for future work.

5.9 Relevance to Automaticity and Control

In this article we have largely adhered to Miller (1956) focus on capacity constraints in tasks that involve perception and/or memory, constraints that we suggested above may extend to language processing. Another domain of cognitive function in which similarly restrictive constraints have been observed is in multitasking capability and the allocation of cognitive control. Here, the constraints are usually framed in terms of a foundational distinction in cognitive psychology, between automatic and control-dependent modes of processing (Posner and Snyder, 1975; Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977). Control-dependent processing is characterized as highly flexible and general, but subject to interference and therefore limited to inefficient serial processing. In contrast, automatic processing is deemed less prone to interference and can support efficient parallel processing, but requires extensive practice to achieve and is more rigidly task-dedicated. The same distinction -sometimes referred to as "dual process" theory" -has been made in other fields using other terms, such as System 1 and System 2 in behavioral economics (Sloman, 1996), model-free (habitual) and model-based (deliberative) in machine learning and neuroscience (Daw et al., 2005; Doya, 1999; Sutton and Barto, 1998); and in terms of "thinking fast and slow" in the lay literature (Kahneman, 2011).

The constraints on control-dependent processing have traditionally been attributed to reliance on a centralized, general purpose mechanism that is responsible for the flexibility of human cognitive function, including its ability to respond effectively to novel situations (D. A. Norman and Shallice, 1986), but that is capacity-limited and restricted to the serial execution of processing, akin to the central processing unit (CPU) of a traditional computer (Pashler, 1988; Posner and Snyder, 1975; Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977). However, a longstanding alternative -"multiple resources theory" (Allport et al., 1972; Navon and Gopher, 1979) -suggests that processing is distributed, and that constraints arise not from reliance on a "central bottleneck" imposed by a capacity-limited control mechanism, but rather when different processes must compete to use the same set of shared local resources. This has received growing support from computational analyses (e.g., Meyer and Kieras, 1997; Musslick et al., 2023), in which "local resources" are sets of representations required to perform a given task. In these models, interference arises when different tasks (e.g., naming the color versus shape of a stimulus) rely on a shared set of representations (e.g., phonological codes), but those tasks require different representations within the set that must compete for activation at the same time. This has lead to a formulation of the distinction between control-dependent versus automatic processing in terms of a tradeoff between, respectively: i) the shared use of compositional representations, that can be acquired efficiently and flexibly reconfigured to perform novel tasks, but subject to interference that poses the demand for serialization of processing imposed by control mechanisms; and ii) the formation of separated, conjunctive representations (of stimuli and associated responses) that are less subject to interference that allows parallel execution (i.e., multitasking) and thus less demand for control, but require time and effort to acquire and are more rigidly task-specific (Musslick et al., 2023).

This interpretation can be viewed as another expression of the information-theoretic principles we have identified here; viz., the tradeoff between generalization afforded by shared (i.e., compressed) representations, and processing capacity afforded by separated (i.e,. more complex) ones: Control-dependent, serial processing can be viewed as an adap-tation by the cognitive system required to mitigate the potential for interference incurred by representational sharing required for generalization. From this perspective, the constraints in capacity so intimately associated with control-dependent processing can be viewed as reflecting its purpose" rather than a shortcoming: the imposition of serial processing to avert the risk of interference (i.e., identification errors) incurred by the use of representations that support generalization. In short, control mechanisms may have co-evolved with the ability to learn shared representations as a condition for their use. This framing offers a unified account of capacity constraints, that spans perception, working memory, and tasks that rely on cognitive control, that provides a potentially valuable framework future empirical work in these domains. It also provides a formally rigorous, and potentially normative framework within which to consider how and when such constraints may be overcome, that we turn to next.

5.10 Overcoming Capacity Constraints

5.10.1 Conjunctive Codes: Recoding, Automatization and Consolidation

Recoding. While there has been considerable work in cognitive science and neuroscience that focuses on representational learning and its relationship to generalization (Fang and Sims, 2025; Frankland et al., 2019; Franklin and Frank, 2020; T. W. Webb et al., 2021; Whittington et al., 2020), considerably less has focused on its relationship to limitations in processing capacity. As we have noted, this relationship is brought into sharp relief by tasks that involve novel or unpredictable stimuli -including those on which Miller focused. However, as noted just above, there are also many conditions under which people exhibit remarkable processing capacity, evidenced by parallel processing capabilities, from perception (e.g., identifying a face in a crowd) to action (e.g., talking and driving, no less the vast array of vital functions carried out in parallel by the brain outside of awareness). We assume that this relies on the use of dedicated conjunctive representations that have been acquired over the course of evolution, early in development, or through repeated experience. Insofar as such representations diminish the likelihood of interference, acquiring and using them is an effective strategy for increasing processing capacity. Miller referred to this as "chunking" (e.g., representing a string of digits as a single date, or a string of letters as a single word).

Where possible, such recoding (i.e., identifying an existing conjunctive representation that corresponds to its individually presented constituents) is a well known mnemonic device, that can produce dramatic increases in capacity, such as remembering an arbitrary string of 100 digits (Ericsson and Simon, 1980).

Automatization. Importantly, however, the acquisition of new representations of this sort requires long term learning mechanisms to generate purpose-specific conjunctive codes, by forming enduring associations among existing codes that co-occur with sufficient frequency. This is what drives the process of automatization that occurs with practice in skill acquisition (Cohen et al., 1990; Logan, 1985; Musslick et al., 2023; Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977). The framework we have described provides a formally rigorous, normative approach to understanding this canonical trajectory: from capacity-limited, control-dependent performance early in training, that exploits the flexibility of shared representations for generalization, to a more efficient, automatic, parallel form of processing and multitasking capability that comes from the formation of dedicated conjunctive (i.e., separated) representations with time and practice. It also bears striking parallels to observations and theory regarding representational learning, which is often framed in terms of statistical learning and consolidation, where the same principles may apply.

Consolidation. Learning patterns of correlation among features has long been used to explain the semantic structure of representations acquired by neural networks (e.g., Hinton, 1986; Rumelhart and Todd, 1993) and, accordingly, as a model of human semantic memory (e.g., Giallanza et al., 2024; McClelland and Rogers, 2003). We assume that such processes, building on and in addition to the process of evolution, give rise to representations with generalization gradients and dimensional structure that support the simple forms of semanticity and compositionality considered in this article. However, as noted repeatedly, such forms of statistical learning require a relatively slow rate of adaptation to insure that they capture reliable structure in the environment and thus fail, on their own, to explain the rapid forms of adaptation characteristic of human cognition (e.g., McCloskey and Cohen, 1989). Complementary Learning Systems (CLS) theory was introduced to address this issue, proposing that statistical learning in semantic memory (SM) is complemented by the rapid formation of arbitrary associations in EM (McClelland et al., 1995). Here, we assume that such functionality supports the binding of (i.e., the rapid formation of associations among) compositionally structured codes, and thus the "online" formation of conjunctive representations required to represent novel objects. As discussed above, whether this relies on the specific mechanisms of EM proposed by CLS, or others with comparable functionality remains an important and open question.

Importantly, however, CLS also proposes another role for EM: consolidation: the use of associations rapidly formed in EM among previously unassociated representations in SM, to forge new, more stable representations in SM that capture these associations (Kumaran et al., 2016; McClelland et al., 1995). This is assumed to rely on processes such as active rehearsal and/or passive replay that drive statistical learning mechanisms in SM to form these new associations. Such processes thus support the formation of new, dedicated conjunctive representations corresponding to the ones formed from the binding of compositional representations in EM. At the same time, this is thought to occur in a manner that preserves as much existing structure as possible (e.g., by sampling representations that have distinct associations in those that are replayed). Such preservation of existing structure may require narrowing the generalization gradients (i.e., reducing compression) of the newly formed representations with respect to the existing ones that are to be preserved. In either case -the formation of new, dedicated conjunctive codes, and/or the potential narrowing of generalization gradients -consolidation can be viewed from an informationtheoretic perspective as increasing representational complexity. From the perspective of this article, it provides a means of increasing processing capacity through the use of dedicated representations that are less subject to interference. More generally, this suggests that consolidation -the transition over time, and with rehearsal and replay, from flexible but arbitrary associations formed in EM to the richer, domain-specific representations that form semantic knowledge (the representation of meaning and its use in inference) -can be viewed as a process that is homologous to automatization -the transition over time, and with practice, from flexible, control-dependent processing to more efficient, automatic processing that relies on procedural knowledge (skills acquired in the service of action).

Finally, it is also important to note that the use of rapid associative mechanisms for binding of compositional representations -whether for generalization (the primary focus on in this article) or consolation and automatization (as discussed just above) -should be subject to limitations similar to those observed for EM. For example, while the storage capacity of EM is generally assumed to be extremely large, if not unlimited, the number of independent sets of associations (e.g., among features of an item) that can be either stored or retrieved at the same time is constrained by the same factors of compositionality (the binding problem) and semanticity (proactive interference) as those we have considered in this article.

Relationship to Optimal Coding. The information-theoretic framework we have described offers a unified, formally rigorous approach to understanding the effects of recoding ("chunking"), as well as the trajectories in representational learning (consolidation) and skill acquisition (automaticity) from flexible forms of processing to more efficient ones, through the formation and use of dedicated, purpose-specific conjunctive representations. By reducing representational sharing, these reduce the risk of interference, and thus increase processing capacity. Importantly, the frequency-dependent nature of the learning mechanisms involved accord with a central tenet of optimal coding in RDT: that the complexity of a representation should be reduced with the frequency of its use. Dedicated conjunctive codes can thereby be viewed as less complex codes (a single representation, rather than the composition of several) for frequently encountered conjunctions. This has recently been applied to provide a normative account of dual process theory in terms of the minimum description length principle (Moskovitz et al., 2024). Here, we propose that this can be extended beyond the traditional assertion that frequency should impact code length (i.e., complexity), to the idea that frequency should also impact the structure of the codes themselves, and how they relate to one another: new representations should be dedicated to frequently experienced conjunctions of features (i.e., ones that are correlated, comprising specific items) that are not only less complex, but also are less similar to (i.e., share less with and are further from) from those used to support the processing of novel items with which they might be confused. Note that, while such dedicated conjunctive (i.e., separated) representations may be less complex, as additional (and redundant) representations, they increase the representational complexity of (i.e., the number of representations in) the system as a whole. This observation warrants two comments. First, to the extent that such dedicated conjunctive representations can be used compositionally to flexibly represent new, higher level constructs (e.g., individual animals -as dedicated representations of specific conjunctions of features -can be used compositionally to flexibly represent higher-level constructs, such as a circus or a zoo), and thus reflect a form of compression at a higher level of analysis. Thus, more generally, consolidation and automatization can be viewed as the formation of dedicated representations for high-frequency correlations among representations at lower levels of analysis that can serve one or both of two purposes: i) the separation of those representations from ones with which they might otherwise be confused, to increase processing capacity for their use (akin to "chunking"); and/or ii) their use as elements for compositionally representing higher level novel, or less frequently experienced constructs.

Second, this relationship between frequency-dependent reduction of complexity at one level (in accord with RDT), and its potential to support compression at another level, closely resembles a similar relationship between item versus system level complexity in theories of language development (e.g., Nowak et al., 2000). There, it is hypothesized that the system begins, under pressure for expressivity, by developing dedicated representations for high-frequency items (often "irregular" forms; Kirby, 2001). However, as it is exposed increasingly to structured information (e.g., regular forms), and under the pressure for compression (imposed by the need for communication over limited bandwidth channels), it develops generalizable (e.g., compositional) representations that, while they are more complex (e.g., compared to dedicated conjunctive representations of items), reduce overall system complexity. Our analyses assumed that such representations had already formed, and focused on the constraints in processing capacity to which they are subject -constraints that are amenable to their use over limited bandwidth channels. Here, we note that the processes of consolidation and automatization may reflect an additional (perhaps later) phase of acquisition, during which highly frequent and/or useful items, even if they can be represented using generalizable codes, may be assigned simpler codes (again, in accord with RDT), that have the effect of increasing processing capacity. Interestingly, these phases of acquisition align broadly with observations made about the progression of representations over the course of training in neural networks, including the learning of regular and irregular forms of verb conjugation (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1985), the phenomenon of "grokking" (in which "memorization" is followed by generalization; e.g., Power et al., 2022), and the transition from flexible ("in-context") forms of processing to more efficient, dedicated ("in-weight") forms of processing (e.g., Singh et al., 2023), as well as recent information-theoretic analyses of such learning trajectories of learning (Conklin et al., 2026; Tishby and Zaslavsky, 2015).

Collectively, these considerations strongly suggest that compression and generalization must be considered at multiple levels of analysis, and with respect to different forms of use. The effort to frame these considerations in formal, information-theoretic terms may provide a useful foundation for a more detailed, and normative understanding of how and when learning can be used to optimize the tradeoff between item versus system level complexity and, more generally, between generalization and processing capacity, that we turn to next.

5.10.2 Optimization of the Tradeoff between Generalization and Processing Capacity

The information-theoretic perspective on consolidation and automatization discussed above suggests that they present an optimization problem of their own: Replay, rehearsal and practice all allow people to actively manipulate the frequency with which they process different information, selectively increasing exposure to particular patterns of correlations relative to their incidental likelihoods in the environment. In so doing, they allow people to actively regulate the tradeoff between generalization and processing capacity in particular domains of function, the optimization of which involves at least three closely related factors: the utility of dedicated conjunctive representations in a given domain; the time (and corresponding opportunity costs) it takes to learn these; and the temporal horizon over which that utility obtains. Characterizing the specific forms of utility that drive these processes is beyond the scope of this article. However, the general problem can be cast in the form of an intertemporal choice with respect to maximizing the corresponding utility: When confronted with a novel or unfamiliar task, should an agent: a) rely on the flexibility of currently available generalizable representations, that can support immediate performance in novel environments, and thus maximize utility over the short term, but that foregoes the opportunity to accrue the advantages of more dedicated representations over the longer term; or b) invest in learning dedicated representations in order to accrue those advantages as soon as possible, but at the cost of the time (and attendant opportunity costs) this will incur over the shorter term. Recent work has begun to address this question in the context of skill acquisition, multitasking capability and planning (e.g., Agrawal et al., 2021; Petri et al., 2021; Ravi et al., 2020; Sagiv et al., 2018). The current framework could be used to generalize this approach to perceptual and inference processes, which may have relevance not only for understanding the profiles of human performance, but for the design of artificial systems, a concern that we turn to next.

5.11 Relevance to Work in Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence

Progress in machine learning has produced remarkably successful systems that, in many instances, exhibit generalization abilities comparable to that of humans (e.g., in tests of analogical reasoning; Hu et al., 2023; T. Webb et al., 2023). This suggests that they have acquired structured forms of representations -and, in particular, representational sharingthat support this capability for generalization. According to our account, this predicts that despite their massive resources (processing units, parameters), these models should nevertheless be subject to constraints in processing capacity similar to those observed for humans when called upon to process novel information in parallel (i.e., without the adjunct of serial processing). 15 Consistent with this idea, it has been observed that the distributional properties of the data on which transformers are trained is, not surprisingly, important in determining what information is stored in the structure of the weights, and what information relies on dynamic construction of activity states, often referred to as "in context" learning (Chan et al., 2022). More specifically, it was found that weight-based learning occurred for high frequency items involving stable correlations in the data; whereas large numbers of rarely occurring classes and/or items, the meanings of which were dynamic rather than fixed across samples, tended to rely on in context learning. These observations align well with the idea that stable, frequently encountered forms of correlation will, with sufficient exposure, be assigned dedicated conjunctive codes and become incorporated into the system's set of existing semantic codes, with weights dedicated to the relevant correlations. This is comparable to the processes of consolidation and automatization discussed above. In contrast, items encountered less frequently (i.e., involving more "novel" correlations) will rely on dynamically constructed compositional representations formed from existing semantic codes; that is, the correlations will be represented using rapid binding mechanisms (in this case, in activity states; i.e., WM) similar to those used in the models described in this article. These observations also reinforce the prediction made by our analysis, that the amount of information transformers can process using "in context" learning should be limited in ways similar to humans when they are forced to process information involving novel items comprised of correlations infrequently (or not) experienced during training.

As noted earlier, this may already be evident in large machine learning models. For example, one recent study investigated the extent to which existing vision-language models (VLMs; such as GPT-4v) can reliably distinguish among simultaneously presented items, using the types of tasks and stimuli that have been used to characterize capacity constraints in humans, including some we have considered in this article. That study found that VLMs consistently exhibited constraints in processing capacity as restrictive as those observed for humans forced to rely on rapid parallel processing (Campbell et al., 2024). This is consistent with the supposition that VLMs rely on the learning of shared representations that are useful for generalization, and the generality of the principle that such representations should be associated with restrictions in processing capacity. Interestingly, there is growing evidence that, when these models are augmented with mechanisms for serial processing (e.g., fine tuning on pointing, and/or use of "chain of thought"), they are better at performing such tasks, again consistent with human performance and the role of control-dependent serial processing in overcoming the constraints imposed by representations that are structured for generalization.

Finally, in a separate line of work in machine learning, there have been extensive studies seeking ways to improve the generalization performance of neural networks. These have shown that training networks on several related tasks in ways that encourage the discovery of shared structure -referred to variously as "multitask training" (Baxter, 1995; Bengio et al., 2013; Caruana, 1997) or meta-learning (Finn et al., 2017; Hospedales et al., 2021; Santoro et al., 2016) -can substantially improve performance on novel tasks that share similar structure. Critically, however, networks in these paradigms are only ever trained or asked to perform one task at a time. Recently, the effects of such learning have been interpreted in terms of the acquisition of compositional representations, that favor efficiency of learning through generalization, but that this comes at the cost of dramatic restrictions in multitasking capability -the ability perform multiple tasks at the same time (Musslick et al., 2023) -an effect that is largely invariant to the size of the network (Petri et al., 2021). Again, this is consistent with the principle that, whereas structured representations support generalization and therefore more efficient learning, this comes at the expense of processing capacity -in this case, the ability to simultaneously represent the information needed to perform multiple tasks in parallel. As noted above, recent work has begun to consider ways in which the tradeoff between generalization and processing capacity can be optimized, using both formal analysis of abstracted networks in simple task settings (e.g., Petri et al., in press; Sagiv et al., 2018), and using deep learning in the context of more complex and realistic tasks (e.g., Ravi et al., 2020).

These findings may have significance for the design of artificial systems. Most work to date has focused on generalization: how artificial systems can achieve the efficiency of learning and flexibility afforded by generalization, and exhibited by humans. It is widely assumed that this will require imbuing models with more powerful inductive biases for discovering structure, whether in their training curricula (e.g., Marinescu et al., 2024) and/or their architecture (e.g., T. W. Webb et al., 2024), in order to optimize the bias-variance tradeoff. As discussed above, one way to frame this is the need to form dedicated representations of high frequency items that can be combined (i.e., compositionally) to represent as many low frequency items as possible. However, while optimizing this tradeoff remains a major challenge for the design of artificial systems, the work we have presented suggests that, even with complete success in this effort, the design problem faces another tradeoff: inductive biases that maximize generalization will carry a commensurate cost in parallel processing capacity. That is, they will be subject to the curse of generalization. This, in turn, will require the incorporation of control mechanisms needed to serialize processing, to mitigate the risk of interference from simultaneous use of representations optimized for generalization, in just the way the human brain seems have solved this problem though the use of control-dependent processing.

This tradeoff between flexibility but inefficiency of serial processing imposed by generalizable representations, and the efficiency but more narrowly constrained applications of parallel processing afforded by task-dedicated representations is comparable to a similar tradeoff faced in traditional computational architectures, between highly flexible components (e.g., interpreted procedures and general-purpose processors) and purpose-dedicated ones (e.g., compiled procedures, drivers and custom chipsets). It is intriguing to consider the possibility that this reflects yet another expression of the information-theoretic principles considered in this article. Whether or not this is so, the tension is a fundamental one for any adaptive system that must function in the real world. While flexibility and generalization are clearly assets in non-stationary, unpredictable environments, so is the need to simultaneously encode and meaningfully distinguish among multiple sources of input, and/or efficiently execute multiple independent tasks in parallel -capabilities that are likely to be critical for artificial autonomous agents in naturalistic environments. It is clear that the human brain has developed mechanisms to adjudicate this tradeoff and, as discussed above, to adapt both its behavior and its structure over time to do so as a function of experience and need. We hope that our consideration of the information-theoretic principles underlying this tradeoff will help advance the understanding of how this tradeoff is managed by the brain, as well as the design of artificial agents that can do so as effectively as humans.

5.12 Conclusion

In this article, we provided a formal characterization of a fundamental tradeoff faced by any information processing system between: generalization -the effectiveness with which previously acquired representations can be used to process new information, that is advantaged by representational sharing (a form of compression), but that comes at the cost of potential inference introduced by such sharing; and processing capacity -the amount of information that can be processed at the same time, advantaged by the use of maximally separated (i.e., dedicated conjunctive) representations, but that comes at the cost of the time and effort required for their acquisition. We presented an analysis of this tradeoff in an abstract, information-theoretic form, and then demonstrated that the same principles are exhibited in both a simple process model, and a neurally-inspired mechanistic model of tasks that have been used to characterize human information processing capacity constraints in perception and memory. Finally, we argued that the same principles may apply in other domains of cognitive function that exhibit similarly restrictive constraints on processing capacity, such as language, multitasking performance and control-dependent processing, and that they appear to be expressed in artificial systems as well.

Our account follows a long tradition of work that assumes natural agents place a premium on generalizable representations, both for the efficiency they afford during learning, and the flexibility they afford in novel settings. Our analyses explored the properties of such representations in relatively simple formulations of the problem, and for relatively simple tasks that have been used in the laboratory. The extent to which these findings themselves generalize to more complex forms of structure present in the world, and tasks that must make use of that structure, remains an open question. However, it seems likely that similar effects will be observed in such realistic environments, given the statistical complexity of the natural world, and the value of responding flexibly and effectively within it. At the same time, it is obvious that processing capacity can also be of considerable value, and it is clear that humans actively invest the effort to achieve this when the demand is sufficiently frequent and the consequences sufficiently valuable, through processes such as consolidation and automatization. The information-theoretic approach we have presented offers a unifying, formally rigorous framework for understanding these processes, as the formation of purpose-dedicated, conjunctive representations that reduce the similarity with (i.e., increases the distance from) other codes with which they may interfere.

In their commentary on Miller's "magical number 7 plus or minus two," Shiffrin and Nosofsky (1994) (pg. 360) noted: ". . . the possibility that there may exist some deeper connection may have contributed to the fame of this article, despite Miller (1956) convincing and amusing arguments to the contrary. If there is a deep connection, it continues to escape the field: The dominant theories and paradigmatic approaches in these two domains have gone their separate ways, with little, if any, cross talk."

The work we have presented here strives to bring together these different approaches, and converges on the conclusion that tempted Miller, but that he ultimately (though perhaps coyly) rejected: the consistency of capacity limits in the range of the "magic number 7" is not a coincidence. Rather, we suggest that it is a reflection of a fundamental tradeoff between the value of generalizable representations in processing novel stimuli, and the cost this extracts in processing capacity when called upon to do so. This tradeoff between generalization and processing capacity defines an envelope of performance that finite information processing agents can hope to achieve. A.4 Formulation of Identification and Generalization Tasks with respect to the Information Bottleneck

Here we elaborate on the connections between the information bottleneck (IB) and the tasks we use to operationalize and analyze the tension between the identification and generalization.

As described in the section on Task Design under Simple Process Model, a trial in each task involved sampling a set of stimuli S from a much broader space of possible stimuli; presenting a compressed representation of a probe that was drawn either from S (identification task) or a different stimulus in the same space from which S was drawn (similarity task); and responding either by identifying the member of S corresponding to the probe (identification task) or that was closest to it (generalization task). Both of these can be viewed as forms of a categorization tasks, in which the compressed probe needs to be assigned to the correct category, i.e. the corresponding stimulus in S; and the construction of an encoder that performs categorization in an "optimal" way can be formalized using the Information Bottleneck (IB) formalism. Thus, it is reasonable to ask how that optimization problem relates formally to the analyses of the tension between identification and generalization presented in this article.

To address this question, we first denote how the IB formalizes the categorization problem. For the identification task, we constructed test sets of various sizes by uniformly sampling from the feature interval [0, 1], and randomly selecting one of the items in the set as the probe. We then quantified the ability to identify the matching item in the set by reporting its index. We measured performance as the mutual information between, on average, the index of the correct response (i.e., an integer from 1, . . . , N for a set size of N ) and the index of the model's response. We computed this mutual information via a numerical simulation that estimated the joint probability distribution over the correct responses X and model's responses Y . Thus, for set size N , the maximum possible information was log 2 N . We explored set sizes up to 256 so that, in principle, processing capacity as measured by this task could be as high as 8 bits.

For the generalization task, we randomly selected test sets of N items from all possible items, sampled a probe item uniformly from the set of all possible items excluding any in the test set, and then quantified the ability to pick which of the N items in the test set was most similar to the probe; that is, the one closest given the distance metric applied to the features. In the simple 1D case, there was no need to specify the form of the distance metric, under the assumption that distance is a monotonic function of the difference between the two features. For the 2D case, we examined several distance functions, as described below. Note, once again, that only the choice of the probe item differed between the two tasks; their structure and performance measures were otherwise equivalent.

We manipulated four primary factors to examine the tradeoff of between generalization and identification performance: 1) the generalization gradient applied to item distances (viz., the exponential decay rate β as described above, that implemented a form of compression); 2) noise in the decision process (viz., the additive term ∆ also described above); 3) set size in the generalization task N g (viz., the number of items against which the probe was compared); and 4) the dimensionality of item representations together with the distance metric used to compare them. Note that varying N g will lead to different optimal generalization gradients that maximize performance for the corresponding set size in that task. We also manipulated set size in the identification task N i (to evaluate processing capacity), but we focus first on results using N i = 256 (shown in Figure B1), as this provided the greatest amount of information available against which to evaluate any observed constraints in performance when compression (i.e., the generalization gradient) is optimized for the generalization task.

While Euclidean distance is a natural mathematical extension of the distance measure used for the 1D case in mathematical analyses and preceding simulations, it makes two assumptions relevant to how psychological distance may be determined in such settings. First, it necessarily assumes that distance is measured between the conjunctive representation of each item (i.e., each as a single, unique point in the 2D space). In our simulations, this conjunctive representation is formed by binding the features associated with each item, either by concatenating those features (simulations above), or through rapid Hebbian learning in an autoassociative (as reported in the next section). Importantly, in both cases we assumed that this binding was temporary (i.e, restricted to the duration of the trial and/ or task), and was between features that were themselves represented along independent dimensions -that is, the binding was over features represented in a compositional form (see Figure 7), rather than the use of a dedicated, stable conjunctive code for those items. This suggests the possibility that, under such conditions, the system may not have learned how to compute distances directly between such (transient) conjunctions, and so must rely on the distances between features along each dimension over which the conjunction is composed -something that it is more likely to have been learned. However, whereas these distances could then be used (with Pythagoras' Theorem) to explicitly compute the Euclidean distance, this seems an unlikely prospect. Alternatively, it seems reasonable to consider the possibility of computing distances independently along each dimension, and using that information to make the relevant judgement. The theoretical observations we report here further suggest that familiarity, and how this interacts with representational dimensionality, may be an important factor. This aligns with the general idea that consistent experience with a particular set of stimuli drives the formation of dedicated conjunctive codes (vs. ones constructed from compositional representations), and that this increases processing capacity. Here, we note that this may extend not only to the conjunctive representations themselves, but to learning of an appropriate distance metric among them.

C.2 Coding Assumptions

Psychophysical work has shown that, under normal viewing conditions, typical human observers can detect spatial displacements up to 0.0167-3 degrees of visual angle (e.g., Frisén and Frisén, 1981; Westheimer and Beard, 1998). We assume a conservative 0.01o as a JND. Using this quantity, we reason that, for a 2D spatial display of 9.8° × 7.3°, as in Luck and Vogel (1997), if one were to begin in one corner of the display and take steps of one JND across the display region, we would obtain approximately (9.8 /0.01 =) 980 across and (7.3 / 0.01) = 730 down, yielding approximately 715,400 distinguishable spatial locations in total. If each of these spatial locations is equiprobable, an observation that a stimulus is located at a particular spatial position would carry 19.47 bits of information. We therefore seek a grid-like coding scheme that scales 8 frequencies at √ e and has a representational capacity of ∼19.47 bits.

To estimate the representational capacity of a possible coding scheme, we care about the joint entropy H of the entire collection (z 1 , . . . , z L ).

Semantic structure.

We assume that the features in each dimension have semantic (metric) structure (see Figure E1, Panel A), that can be expressed as correlations (also see Figure 7). In the analyses and simple process model presented in the main text, we operationalize this as an exponential distance gradient of features along a dimension, such that each feature value can be more or less similar to (distant from) other feature values along the same dimension. In the neural network model, we use empirical estimates of similarity structure among feature values along a dimension to implement the distances that determine semantic structure.

Compositional structure. While we assume that features have semantic, and thus correlational structure within each dimension, we assume that features are uncorrelated across dimensions (see Figure 7). Accordingly, as noted above, the dimensions form a basis set for the full space, such that any state of the system can be represented as a combination of its features along each dimension. We refer to this as compositional representation. Also as noted above, this can be hierarchically structured. For example, we can consider two intuitively meaningful levels of compositionality within the representation of a given state: compositionality of the representation of each item in terms of its feature values, that amounts to the concatenation of its features along each dimension (i.e., for each of the points comprising its location), and reflecting the covariation of its feature values along those dimensions; and the compositionality of items within a given state (e.g., objects in a display), that amounts to the simultaneous representation of (i.e., superposition of the codes for) feature values along each dimension belonging to the different items in that state, and reflecting the covariation of those items in physical space (i.e., the locations spanned by the display) and/or in time (i.e., their co-occurrence during the duration of the display). Accordingly, to the extent that the identities of individual items need to be kept distinct within a state and/or different states need to be kept distinct, then there needs to be some mechanism of associating (binding) the feature values belonging to each particular item and/or the items within a state. This is because compositionally structured representations reflect only the first order statistics of the features involved (i.e., their marginal frequencies), and not higher order statistics needed to represent correlations. For items, this means only the (frequency with which) feature values are present in the state are represented. This is sufficient to identify the coherent covariation of feature values that defines an individual item, but insufficient to keep these distinct for different co-occurring items; the latter requires some associative mechanism for encoding higher order statistics among feature values. The same applies to higher level conjunctions, such as among items within an display.

The work we present points out that tasks used to study constraints in human information processing capacity are usually designed to probe item identification in settings that demand compositional representation -that is, the encoding of states that are novel compositions of items, which may themselves be novel compositions of feature values. This demand reflects the generalizability of compositional and semantic coding, that comes at the cost of processing capacity-that is, strict constraints on the number of items that can be distinguishably represented at the same time (e.g. "set size") -that are empirically observed.

E.2 Representational Structure, Generalization and Compression

Our focus in this article is on forms of representational structure that promote generalization. In information-theoretic terms, this can be thought of as a form of compression (e.g., Sims, 2018). Along similar lines, we focus in particular on the exponential distance function identified by Shepard (1987) Universal Law of Generalization, and quantify its effects using a standard information-theoretic measure applied to a simple generalization task: Given a set of stimuli and a probe not in that set, generalization is quantified as the mutual information between the agent's choice of the closest stimulus to the probe and the one that is actually closest (for more details, see Decision Noise and the Optimization of

Generalization under information-theoretic Analysis).

There are two primary motivations for this approach: 1) to explicitly operationalize generalization as a form of compression, not all forms of which necessarily support generalization; and 2) and quantify its effects on processing capacity. In the following, we consider in greater detail the relationship between this operationalization of generalization, the forms of representational structure outlined above, and standard information-theoretic constructs.

Probabilistic vs. similarity structure. If the world had no structure, then the only way to represent it would be by pairing each state with a unique code. That is, there would be no opportunity for generalization, and the agent would obligately face the curse of dimensionality, manifest in the number of samples (and hence time) required to learn such a code. 23 Fortunately, however, the world does appear to have structure, and thus the curse of dimensionality can be mitigated through the acquisition and use of more efficient forms of coding that support generalization. Traditional work on efficient coding in information theory (e.g., Barlow, 1961; Stocker and Simoncelli, 2006), and its application to learning and representation (Friston, 2010; Linsker, 1988; Tishby et al., 2000), have focused largely on one particular form of structure: the probability distribution over states of the world. Accordingly, they have been driven by a fundamental tenet of information theory: that efficiency of coding can be maximized by optimizing complexity to reflect probabilistic structure. More specifically, the complexity (e.g., number of bits) of a code assigned to a state should be proportional to its surprisal (the negative log probability of its occurrence), so that shorter codes are used to represent more frequent states. There are at least two ways in which this can be thought of as efficient: i) it minimizes the average complexity of the system's representations for a given level of mutual information between those and the potential states of the world, by minimizing the resources (e.g., total number of bits) needed to represent those states given their likelihoods; it maximizes rate which, in RDT, is defined as the number of bits required to transmit a signal on average: using fewer bits for the most frequent signals means more efficient transmission (fewer bits per signal) on average. The latter also has implications for rate in another sense: how much information can be transmitted per unit time, assuming a channel capacity that imposes some requirement for serial processing. The latter bears a close relationship to the construct of processing capacity on which we have focused -how many signals can be reliably represented and transmitted at the same time -although, as noted this is not usually how the problem is framed, at least not in the context of cognitive science, neuroscience or machine learning applications (see Note 1).

The idea that the frequency of a state should impact how it is represented has played a central role in statistically-motivated theories of representational learning. While these do not always formulate the relationship explicitly in terms of complexity or efficiency, they do address how factors such as passive experience, replay, rehearsal and practice -mediated by psychological processes such as consolidation (e.g., Kumaran et al., 2016; McClelland et al., 1995) and automatization (Anderson, 1983; Musslick et al., 2023; Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977) -can lead information that is processed more frequently to be represented more compactly. The same may be true for longer term processes, such as evolution and/or early development. In the Discussion we consider how this relationship between frequency and representational structure might be considered within an information theoretic framework, and in particular with regard to the tension between generalization and processing capacity (see Overcoming Capacity Constraints, and Conjunctive Codes: Recoding, Automatization, and Consolidation). However, our primary focus in this article is not on how the system can most efficiently represent or process the most frequent states, but rather on how it accommodates the need to process the most infrequent states -that is, ones statistically out of the distribution of prior experience -which demand generalization, and how that impacts efficiency.

Infrequent states can be ones comprised of items with novel or unfamiliar features (i.e., semantically unusual states), and/or novel combinations of features or items (i.e., compositionally unusual states). Note, however, that while instances of such states may individually be infrequent, encountering infrequent states as a class may be common, especially in high dimensional, non-stationary -i.e., naturalistic -environments. Accordingly, the effectiveness with which infrequent states can be processed is critical to adaptation. Furthermore, the processing of such states is assumed to rely on mechanisms responsible for the flexibility of processing (e.g., working memory and cognitive control), and tasks used to study these mechanisms are therefore designed specifically to elicit such states -that is, ones that involve the processing of unfamiliar items, such as ones comprised of novel combinations of features (e.g., colors, shapes and positions as shown in Figure 1A). The primary point of this article is to argue that, precisely because these tasks are intended to tap mechanisms responsible for the flexibility of processing, and therefore rely on the capacity for generalization, they necessarily showcase the limits to human information processing capacity that reflect the curse such generalization.

Theories of optimal coding do not generally address this consequence of generalization: they prescribe how to assign codes to individual signals, based on their frequency of occurrence, but not how these should relate to codes used for other signals (e.g., semantically or compositionally). In fact, as formalized by the Singleton Bound (Singleton, 1965), they prescribe the use of codes that are as distinct (i.e. as far) from one another as possible. That is, each state of the world should be assigned a unique code that is as distinct as possible, maximizing the reliability of transmission. We refer to these as conjunctive codes, because they are arbitrary mappings to states that reflect only the unique conjunction of feature values or items that comprise a given state, and bear no systematic relation to one another. However, under the assumption that the world has semantic and/or compositional structure outlined above, it is inefficient to represent the world in this way -both with respect to the efficiency of learning and representation. As noted in the main text, the number of samples required to learn to represent every possible state of the world individually (i.e., as conjunctive codes) grows exponentially with the number of feature values and dimensions in the world. In contrast, the number of samples needed to acquire a strictly compositional code grows linearly with the number of feature values and dimensions. Thus, compositional representations of states can be acquired and represented more efficiently than conjunctive ones; and, once acquired, they can be used more flexibly to represent any possible -but as yet unexperienced -state. Similarly, semanticity allows responses to novel states to be similar to those that share similar feature values along the behavioral relevant dimensions (and, conversely, compressing ones that are not relevantly distinct into a single representation). Together, these maximize generalization -the ability to accurately represent and/or respond appropriately to states that have not previously been encountered, based on existing representations of ones that are similar (Shepard, 1987). However, they come at the cost of the ability to reliably identify distinct individual states, which is the criterion for performance in standard information theoretical analyses (e.g., the mutual information between the transmitted and received code). This is because introducing similarity structure increases the potential for confusion among codes, making them harder to distinguish when that is required. 24 We formalize this tension between generalization and identification in the information-theoretic Analysis of the main text. To isolate the effects of such structure, independent of probabilistic structure, the work we report in this article makes the assumption that all states are equiprobable, and represented within the system with equal complexity. We then quantify the relationship between complexity (or, conversely, compression) and generalization, and ask how optimizing for generalization impacts identification error and, concomitantly, processing capacity.

Furthermore, this should be greatly exacerbated when multiple novel items must be represented at the same timethat is, it should reduce processing capacity. This is evident in the Illustrative Example presented at the beginning of this article (see Figure 1), where representing novel stimuli (e.g., a yellow square next to a green square each in an arbitrary position) requires the use of compositional coding, both for items in terms of their features, and the display in terms of those items. However, this poses a problem: without some mechanism for associating each point in the display, and similarly each item with its corresponding set of features, it would be impossible to determine which particular features belong to a given item -for example, to distinguish it from the case in which the green square was in the upper right corner, and the yellow square was below it. That is, the use of compositional representations restricts the number of items that can be represented and identified at the same time. This has been referred to as the "Binding Problem" in cognitive science (e.g., Treisman and Gelade, 1980). This highlights the limitations of compositionality -that is, the curse of generalizationclassically observed as a cost in the ability to identify the features of a specified item in a display containing multiple items, that increases with the number of items. This cost is observed either as an increase in response time with the number of items, indicative of reliance on serial processing (Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977; Treisman and Gelade, 1980), or a reduction in accuracy under pressure to respond immediately (i.e, enforcing parallel processing) or after a delay (enforcing that the display be encoded in memory). This is because strictly compositional codes (i.e., without any binding) carry information only about the fist order statistics of features in a state (which are present or absent), and not higher order statistics (i.e., their associations with one another, or patterns of covariation). The problem is exacerbated by semanticity, in which the similarity of codes for similar features makes the ability to distinguish such codes more sensitive to perturbation (e.g., if the squares had subtly different shades of yellow). The constraints that such structured representations place on processing efficiency can be mitigated in one of two ways.

Serial processing.

One way to overcome the constraints imposed by structured representations on the simultaneous representation of multiple items is to serialize processing, isolating each item from interference introduced by the others. For example, the classic binding problem can be averted by using eye movements and/or attention to sequentially process items one at a time, focusing on its features in isolation of others. A large and longstanding body of empirical evidence suggests that this is exactly what people do (e.g., Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977; Treisman and Gelade, 1980), and has been variously interpreted as the function of attention (Kahneman and Treisman, 1984) and cognitive control (Botvinick et al., 2001). For instance, in the Illustrative Example presented at the beginning of this article (see Figure 1), by focusing on one location at at time the covariance of red and square can be kept separate from the covariance of green and triangle. However, this comes at the cost of an increase in time needed to process all the items in the display, reducing processing capacity in this case to one. This highlights one of the most important consequences of the Miller-Shepard Law: the tension between between the flexibility of processing afforded by generalization, and the cost in processing capacity that this incurs due to the reliance on serial, control-dependent processing. This, in turn, points to a second, equally important consequence: the need to balance the value of generalization against the value of processing capacity, that can increased through representational modification.

Representational modification. While serial processing can be used to avert the cost of identification error with structured, generalizable codes, another way to do so is to modify existing representations and/ or create new ones. For example, distinctions between features can be made finer along relevant dimensions (by narrowing the generalization gradient) to mitigate interference due to semanticity; and/or by adding new conjunctive representations dedicated to specific combinations of features to mitigate interference due to compositionality. As discussed above, and in the main text (see Conjunctive Codes: Recoding, Automatization and Consolidation in the Discussion), such modifications can occur through representational learning over longer time frames. However, such longterm adaptation can't explain the flexibility people exhibit in representing and processing novel states that, by definition, have not had a chance to exert such pressure on the representations. Rather, over the short term, it requires either rapidly binding features along different dimensions of a compositional code (i.e., to represent higher order statistics), and/or dynamically modifying the precision with which features are represented along a given dimension. The former is the function ascribed to episodic memory by Complementary Learning Systems Theory (McClelland et al., 1995). This aligns closely with binding mechanisms used in the models described in this article. However, our findings suggest that rapid binding can only partially mitigate the constraints on processing capacity imposed by compositionalityowing in large measure to the additional effects of semanticity. In principle, the latter could be mitigated by increasing precision.

However: i) it has been argued that this too is subject to constraints (e.g., Bays and Husain, 2008; Wilken and Ma, 2004); and ii) our analyses suggest that the degree of precision needed to overcome these constraints is extreme (see Figures A3 and B2). Importantly, the constraints on processing capacity observed in these analyses appear to be robust to the forms of generalization, multidimensional representations, binding mechanisms, and relevant model parameters (such as decision noise and set size) we have explored. Nevertheless, whether and how processing constraints vary for other forms of generalization and binding mechanisms, and how this interacts with the dimensionality and compositionality of representations remain important questions for future research, some of which we consider in the Discussion.

C.1 Hopfield-Ising Network

We adopt the statistical framework of computation (i.e., encoding and decoding in the channel above) as minimization of an energy function (Hinton and Sejnowski, 1983; Hopfield, 1982), influential in computational neuroscience (Friston, 2010; Gottwald and Braun, 2020; Hopfield, 1982) as well as machine learning (Hinton and Salakhutdinov, 2006; LeCun et al., 2006; Salakhutdinov, 2015). The "energy" state of a network is a global measure of the agreement between the knowledge stored in the network's parameters (weights) and its current activity values. Here, we consider its utility as an abstract cognitive model, capable of explaining human experimental data in classic cognitive tasks.

As in other frameworks, our model assumes two time-scales of parameters: slow and fast (Hinton and Plaut, 1987; McClelland et al., 1995; Whittington et al., 2020). E.1 Structure of the World

The analyses and models presented in the main text assume that there is structure in the world that our perceptual and representational apparatus have evolved to exploit, and that states of the world can be described in terms of this structure. We elaborate on those assumptions here. Specifically, we assume this structure can be captured by describing the world as points in a multidimensional vector space, with each point described by a vector comprised of a set of subvectors (or "fields"), each of which represents a single feature value along one of the dimensions (including location and time). Furthermore, dimensions are assumed to be orthogonal to one another so that, over the entire space, the feature values along any given dimension vary fully independently of those along any other. 21 Accordingly, the dimensions comprise a basis set for the space, and any point in the space can be compositionally coded as a set of the feature values along each dimension. We further define two types of hierarchically-related subspaces, that are relevant to the information processing demands of physical agents: states, that are for every point in physical space (i.e., along the three dimensions of position) and a given point (or set of points) in time, the feature values along all other dimensions; and items within a state (e.g., objects within a display, or a display within the broader scope of the state), that are comprised of a subset of points in physical space and/or time that share covariance structure across other feature dimensions (e.g., color, shape, etc.) that is independent of any other such subset. For example, an item in Figure 1 of the main text (i.e., one of the colored shapes) can be defined as the locations in the display that all comprise the same shape (square) and share a given color (e.g., yellow), as shown in Figure E1, Panel B. 22 Critically, we assume that all of the feature values of each item, and all the items in a state are represented using the same set of codes. 21 This is of course an idealization, that we use as an extreme to anchor and simply our analyses. As noted in the caption to Figure 7, the real world is likely to involve varying degrees of independence across dimensions; how our analyses interact with relaxation of the assumption of orthogonality across dimensions remains an important direction for future research. 22 The level of resolution (or precision) at which items are represented is of course an important consideration that, in information-theoretic terms, can be considered in terms of complexity; for example, each item (colored square) in Figure E1, Panel B could be represented, at a lower level of resolution, as occupying a single shared position, such as "upper left". As explained further below, we hold complexity constant in our analyses, in order to isolate the effects of correlations in the code, and show that these are qualitatively preserved over reasonable assumptions about complexity. In the Discussion we consider how this relates to work that links capacity limits to a tradeoff between precision and load (Ma et al., 2014; Wilken and Ma, 2004).
