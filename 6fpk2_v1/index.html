Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: The Conversation Questionnaire (CQ) assesses everyday conversational difficulties through items developed with autistic adults. We translated the CQ into German and examined whether its psychometric properties reproduced the original findings.

Methods: Translation, author consultation, and cognitive debriefing with 15 self-reported autistic adults preceded validation in 185 native German-speaking adults. Participants reported an autism diagnosis (diagnosed), suspected or self-identified autism (suspected), or neither. We reanalyzed the original data (n = 266) to provide comparable benchmarks. We approximated the original broader cognitive-ability comparison with nonverbal matrix reasoning and extended validation to the recall of childhood coordination difficulties.

Results: Item response theory modeling closely reproduced two dimensions: difficulty knowing what to say (15 items) and misunderstandings or behaviors affecting conversation (21 items). Only two of 36 items showed shifted loadings, while reliability remained high for both dimensions (.94 and .90, respectively). As in the reanalyzed original data, both dimensions distinguished the diagnosed and neither groups. The suspected group had intermediate average scores, with overlapping individual scores across groups. Correlations with the CQ's Core Language Difficulties and Negative Interaction Style control subscales were stronger for the second dimension, while correlations with reasoning performance were near zero. Extending the original validation, both dimensions correlated strongly with recalled childhood coordination difficulties (r = .64-.71).

Conclusion: These findings support the German CQ as a reliable, community-informed measure for characterizing conversational experiences among autistic and non-autistic German-speaking adults. Our findings also connect conversational experiences to childhood coordination abilities, showing the questionnaire's relevance to broader neurodevelopmental research.

Community Brief

Why is this an important issue? Conversation can be demanding even when others do not notice the effort involved. The Conversation Questionnaire (CQ) was developed in cooperation with autistic adults with a focus on everyday, non-pathologizing statements. The German version makes this measure available to researchers in German-speaking countries. It will allow cross-cultural and international comparisons and contribute to the study of associations between behavioral and brain processes.

What was the purpose of this study?

We translated the CQ and examined whether German responses reproduced the question groupings, score consistency, and autism-status patterns reported in the original study.

What did the researchers do?

Two translators and the first author developed the German wording, consulted the original author, and sought feedback from 15 self-reported autistic adults. We then studied 185 native Germanspeaking adults who reported an autism diagnosis (diagnosed), suspected or self-identified autism (suspected), or neither. We reanalyzed data from 266 participants in the original Englishlanguage study to compare the German findings with the original results. We compared scores between groups and examined associations with the CQ's control subscales, Core Language Difficulties and Negative Interaction Style, as well as performance on visual pattern puzzles, recalled childhood coordination difficulties, sex assigned at birth, age, and education.

What were the results and conclusions of the study?

The German CQ largely reproduced the published question groupings and high score consistency. Scores differed significantly across reported autism-status groups: the diagnosed group had the highest scores and the suspected group intermediate scores. Scores correlated more strongly with Core Language Difficulties than Negative Interaction Style and showed little association with visual pattern reasoning. Adults who recalled more childhood coordination difficulties reported significantly greater conversational difficulties.

What is new or controversial about these findings?

The coordination findings extend earlier English-language research to the two aspects of conversation measured by the German CQ. Associations with Core Language Difficulties and Negative Interaction Style followed the original pattern but were somewhat stronger here. After accounting for age and sex, average scores for misunderstandings or behaviors affecting conversation were highest among participants with lower secondary qualifications or some higher education without a degree. Neither age nor sex was significantly associated with CQ scores.

What are potential weaknesses in the study?

We did not independently verify participants' reports of an autism diagnosis (n = 17). Furthermore, we did not include a secondary measure of pragmatic language nor a broader autism phenotype questionnaire as in the original study.

Background

Conversational difficulties vary in ways that diagnostic status alone cannot capture. Autistic adults may struggle to separate speech from competing sounds or remain focused on clearly audible speech (Bendo et al., 2024). In autistic and non-autistic adults, the Pragmatic Language subscale of the Broad Autism Phenotype Questionnaire (BAPQ) showed stronger associations with self-reported speech understanding, spatial hearing, and sound separation than its social or rigidity subscales (Bang & Igelström, 2026).

Self-reports are essential because observable performance can conceal conversational effort. Autistic, non-autistic, and mixed groups transferred information similarly in a retelling task but experienced the interaction differently (Crompton et al., 2025). Rehearsing and monitoring speech can also conceal difficulties (Hull et al., 2017). Although subscales within the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ), BAPQ, and Comprehensive Autistic Trait Inventory (CATI) provide phenotypic descriptions (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001; Hurley et al., 2007; English et al., 2021), a dedicated questionnaire can examine conversational effort in greater detail. Wilson (2022) developed the Conversation Questionnaire (CQ) from autistic adults' accounts and feedback, retaining everyday statements such as 'I find conversation tiring'. Validation in 312 adults supported two reliable dimensions: difficulty knowing what to say (D1) and misunderstandings or behaviors affecting conversation (D2). Correlations with autistic traits and the Communication Checklist-Self Report's Pragmatic Language scale (CC-SR; Bishop et al., 2009) supported its focus on pragmatic communication. Modest correlations with the CQ's Core Language Difficulties and Negative Interaction Style control subscales, alongside near-zero cognitive-ability correlations, suggested that these experiences were not simply expressions of language errors, intentional disruption, or reasoning ability (Wilson, 2022). A German version will support international comparisons.

We evaluated the German CQ's structure, reliability, and autism-status differences against recalculated original-data benchmarks. The Matrix Reasoning Item Bank (MaRs-IB; Chierchia et al., 2019) assessed nonverbal matrix reasoning in place of the broader International Cognitive Ability Resource (ICAR; Condon & Revelle, 2014). To examine convergent validity, we tested predicted positive associations of CQ scores with recalled childhood coordination difficulties, previously associated with AQ Communication and English CQ scores (Zappullo et al., 2023; Bang et al., 2024). We used the German Adult Developmental Co-ordination Disorders/Dyspraxia Checklist (ADC; Kirby et al., 2010; Meachon et al., 2022). Exploratory regressions jointly examined associations with sex assigned at birth (hereafter sex), age, and education.

Methods

German Conversation Questionnaire

The CQ comprises two conversational dimensions-difficulty knowing what to say (15 items; range 0-30) and misunderstandings or behaviors affecting conversation (21 items; range 0-42)-and two four-item control subscales: Core Language Difficulties and Negative Interaction Style (range 0-8 each; Wilson, 2022). Responses range from 0 (rarely/never) through 1 (in some situations) to 2 (in most situations). We summed the published item groupings; higher totals indicate more frequent endorsement.

Native German-speaking co-authors VP and JB, both with native-like English proficiency, used forward and back translation following World Health Organization (2023) recommendations. VP translated the CQ and resolved semantic and cultural questions with the first author; JB, unfamiliar with the CQ and autism clinically or in research, independently back-translated it. The three reconciled discrepancies. Following the CQ author's clarification of items 4 and 14 (sound selection, speech planning and production) and 37 (grammar), a colleague qualified in speech-language therapy suggested German examples: 'Mifon' (a nonword)/'Mikrofon' ('microphone'; 4), 'Fall' ('fall/case')/'Unfall' ('accident'; 14), and 'Ich bin gelauft'/'Ich bin gelaufen' (incorrect/correct participle for 'I have run'; 37).

Fifteen self-reported autistic adults completed cognitive debriefing through SoSci Survey (Version 3.5.07; Leiner, 2024); ten provided 36 comments across 22 items. Four comments on item 7 questioned whether 'wenn es nur sozial ist' ('when it is just social') meant small talk or excluded socializing as a purpose. After author consultation, we changed this to 'wenn es nur dazu dient, "gesellig zu sein"' ('when it only serves to "be sociable"'). No other item changed.

Participants and procedure

Six Gorilla.sc studies (Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020), conducted March 25, 2025-June 18, 2026, contributed 185 native German-speaking adults to this cross-sectional validation. Recruitment used Prolific (Palan & Schitter, 2018), university mailing lists, the Tübingen autism outpatient clinic, and online support forums. Reported autism status defined three groups: an autism diagnosis (diagnosed), suspected or self-identified autism (suspected), or neither.

For repeat participants, we retained each measure's first administration and earliest nonmissing background responses. Inclusion required complete CQ and childhood ADC responses, an eligible MaRs-IB score, and recorded autism status, age, education, gender identity, and female/male sex. The Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty and University Hospital Tübingen approved debriefing and validation (approval no. 852/2023BO2).

Benchmarks used 266 original-study records with complete 44-item CQ and 16-item ICAR responses and recorded group membership (Wilson, 2022). Wilson's controls had no neurodevelopmental diagnosis and below-threshold autistic-trait and reading-difficulty scores. We lacked these screens, so regrouped the original data as 86 diagnosed, 29 suspected (self-identified only), and 151 neither.

Additional measures

The ADC's ten German childhood items assess recalled coordination difficulties (e.g., handwriting, dressing, ball games; Kirby et al., 2010; Meachon et al., 2022). Responses from 1 (never) to 4 (always) were summed (range 10-40), with higher scores indicating greater difficulty.

The eight-minute MaRs-IB task involved four-choice matrix completion (Chierchia et al., 2019). ICAR-16 comprised four items each on matrix and verbal reasoning, threedimensional rotation, and letter-number sequences (Condon & Revelle, 2014; Wilson, 2022). We scored MaRs-IB ability using Zorowitz et al.'s (2024) calibrated items and parameters (expected-a-posteriori scoring; guessing .25; standard-normal prior). Timeouts counted as incorrect; practice and unreached items were unscored. To limit rapid guessing, we adapted the calibration study's four-of-16 rule to variable test lengths, excluding administrations with ≥25% of scored responses under three seconds.

Statistical analysis

We adapted the original scripts (Wilson, 2022; https://osf.io/uqyt9/) in R 4.5.1.

Using mirt (Chalmers, 2012), we fitted two-dimensional graded-response item response theory (IRT) models to 36 D1/D2 items in each cohort. The models accommodated ordered responses; free loadings assessed item grouping, and oblimin rotation allowed correlated dimensions. Limited-information fit indices (Cai & Hansen, 2013) were the comparative fit index (CFI) and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA); higher CFI and lower RMSEA indicate better fit.

Empirical IRT reliability assessed dimension-specific precision from expected-a-posteriori estimates and standard errors (Chalmers, 2012). Minimum sampled split-half reliability assessed consistency across groupings of all 36 items, using psych's default 15,000 random equal-half partitions (Revelle, 1979).

Pearson correlations assessed associations between dimension totals and control totals, reasoning and childhood coordination, and between dimensions (two-sided, unadjusted tests; Fisher-z 95% confidence intervals). Reasoning scores were MaRs-IB ability and ICAR-16 number-correct totals. We repeated ADC correlations within the neither group post hoc.

Welch one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) compared autism-status groups, accommodating unequal variances and sample sizes. Holm correction covered D1, D2 and both controls in each cohort, plus exploratory German ADC scores. Games-Howell tests compared all three German group pairs for D1, D2 and ADC, adjusting within each outcome (Games & Howell, 1976). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses compared the diagnosed and neither groups (German: 17/132; original: 86/151). Using ROCR (Sing et al., 2005) and pROC (Robin et al., 2011), we estimated areas under the curve (AUCs) with DeLong 95% confidence intervals and selected cutoffs minimizing squared distance from perfect sensitivity and specificity (scores ≥ cutoff positive). Cohort comparisons were descriptive; means had t-based 95% confidence intervals.

Exploratory ordinary least-squares regressions jointly modeled each dimension from sex, age and education. We modeled education categorically, allowing differences without assuming a linear qualification trend. Heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC3) Wald F tests accommodated unequal residual variances and tested education jointly; Holm correction covered six predictor tests across both dimensions (α = .05). Adjusted education means used the cohort's age and sex distribution.

Results

The 185 German participants (52.4% female sex; ages 18-65; mean 33.21 years, SD 11.18) formed diagnosed (9.2%), suspected (19.5%), and neither (71.4%) groups. Gender identity was 53.0% female, 44.9% male, 1.1% nonbinary and 1.1% undisclosed.

Participants held bachelor's degrees (27.6%), master's degrees (19.5%) or doctorates (3.8%); others reported some higher education without a degree (14.1%), higher education entrance qualifications (25.4%) or lower secondary qualifications (9.7%).

German model fit (CFI = .980; RMSEA = .056, 90% CI [.048, .063]) resembled the originaldata refit (CFI = .983; RMSEA = .055). Largest loadings matched published assignments for 34/36 German and 36/36 original items. German items 10 and 41 loaded more strongly on D1 than D2 (.481 versus .426 and .518 versus .440), despite their original D2 assignments. Empirical IRT reliability was .936 for D1 and .897 for D2; minimum split-half reliability was .857 (original benchmarks: .938, .925 and .885, respectively). Dimension totals correlated positively (r = .604, 95% CI [.504, .688], p < .001).

Autism-status groups differed significantly on D1, F(2, 36.27) = 34.29, and D2, F(2, 38.10) = 66.41 (both Holm p < .001). The diagnosed group had the highest scores (D1: M = 19.12, range 3-28; D2: M = 24.06, range 9-32), followed by the suspected group (D1: M = 16.36, range 0-28; D2: M = 18.89, range 4-37) and the neither group (D1: M = 8.18, range 0-27; D2: M = 8.70, range 0-26), with overlapping distributions (Figure 1A). Both diagnosed and suspected groups scored higher than the neither group on both dimensions (all Games-Howell p < .001). The diagnosed-suspected difference was significant on D2 (5.17, 95% CI [0.34, 10.00], p = .033), but not on D1 (2.76, [-2.20, 7.71], p = .372).

Core Language Difficulties followed this ordering, F(2, 31.15) = 5.30, Holm p = .021, whereas Negative Interaction Style did not differ significantly between autism-status groups, F(2, 34.77) = 1.95, Holm p = .158. Original benchmarks showed the same dimension ordering and group differences (both Holm p < .001); however, Negative Interaction Style differed between groups in the English-language data set (Holm p = .031) and Core Language Difficulties did not (Holm p = .236). German AUCs were .873 (95% CI [.776, .970]) for D1 and .952 [.903, 1.000] for D2 (Figure 1B). At ≥17, sensitivity and specificity were 76.5% and 91.7% for D1, and 94.1% and 87.9% for D2. Original benchmarks were .828 [.775, .880] and .879 [.833, .925], with cutoffs ≥16 and ≥18, respectively.

Core Language Difficulties correlated with D1 (r = .401) and D2 (.503; both p < .001); Negative Interaction Style correlations were .164 (p = .025) and .310 (p < .001), respectively. This pattern replicated the original, with descriptively larger estimates (Figure 1C). Neither MaRs-IB correlation was significant (D1: r = .036; D2: -.072), as in the original ICAR benchmarks (.077 and -.011, respectively). Childhood ADC scores differed across groups, F(2, 32.87) = 36.07, Holm p < .001 (Figure 2A). The diagnosed (M = 24.12, range 10-37) and suspected (M = 19.83, range 12-34) groups scored higher than the neither group (M = 14.14, range 10-25; both Games-Howell p < .001); the diagnosed-suspected contrast was nonsignificant (4.28, 95% CI [-0.31, 8.87], p = .071). Pooled correlations were .643 with D1 (95% CI [.550, .721]) and .706 with D2 ([.626, .772]; both p < .001; Figure 2B/C). Within the neither group (n = 132), correlations remained significant (D1: r = .480, 95% CI [.337, .601]; D2: r = .528, 95% CI [.392, .641]; both p < .001).

Adjusted D2 scores differed across education categories, F(5, 177) = 4.63, Holm p = .003, whereas D1 scores did not, F(5, 177) = 1.45, Holm p = 1.000. Adjusted D2 means were descriptively highest for participants with some higher education without a degree (17.6) and lower secondary qualifications (17.0), versus 9.4-11.6 otherwise. Sex and age were nonsignificant for both dimensions (all Holm p = 1.000).

Discussion

The German CQ largely reproduced the original structure (Wilson, 2022) and showed high reliability, supporting its use with autistic and non-autistic German-speaking adults. D1 reliability closely matched the original benchmark; D2 reliability was lower than D1 but remained high. Two items shifted toward D1: item 10, 'Ich analysiere, was andere Personen meinen, weil ich etwas nicht verstehe oder meine ich habe es missverstanden.' ('I analyse what other people mean, because I don't understand or think I've misunderstood.'), and item 41, 'Es gibt bestimmte Dinge, über die ich gerne spreche, aber andere Personen sind selten daran interessiert.' ('There are particular things I like to talk about, but people are rarely interested.'). Uncertainty about meaning or shared interests may leave someone unsure what to say next, connecting both items to D1.

German AUCs were descriptively higher, while cutoffs differed by one point. The smaller diagnosed proportion (9.2% versus 32.3%) may partly explain lower German scores. Recalculated AUCs were slightly below the published screened-control estimates, possibly reflecting the broader comparator and complete-record sample. These sample-based cutoffs need independent evaluation. Wilson (2022) found greater conversational difficulties among non-autistic adults with reading difficulties or elevated autistic traits than among screened controls. Together with overlapping scores across autism-status groups here, this suggests relevance beyond formal diagnosis. The stronger Core Language Difficulties correlations with D2 than D1 in both cohorts may reflect shared demands on message formulation and monitoring; weaker Negative Interaction Style correlations suggest less association with deliberate disruption.

Coordination associations extend English CQ and AQ Communication findings to both German dimensions (Bang et al., 2024; Zappullo et al., 2023). These findings could support Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) research on whether similar conversational experiences arise through shared or distinct functional processes across diagnostic groups (Cuthbert & Insel, 2013; Kozak & Cuthbert, 2016). Motor difficulties may limit participation, while shared planning and monitoring demands could link conversational and motor development. In children, parent-rated motor and social difficulties were indirectly associated through executive functioning and understanding others' intentions (Estrugo et al., 2024), suggesting candidate pathways for behavioral and neural investigation. Near-zero matrix-reasoning correlations resembled the original ICAR findings, despite MaRs-IB assessing a narrower nonverbal ability. Wilson (2022) questioned whether female overrepresentation affected representativeness, but neither dimension was significantly associated with sex in our less skewed cohort. Higher male AQ/CATI Communication and BAPQ Pragmatic Language scores in nonclinical samples suggest questionnaire content matters (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001; English et al., 2021; Ingersoll et al., 2011). However, English et al. (2025) found no significant CATI Communication difference between autistic cisgender men and women, although women reported more camouflaging. Sex patterns may therefore depend on the population.

Camouflaging may conceal overt difficulties (Hull et al., 2017), whereas CQ questions about effort, uncertainty, and fatigue may capture shared experiences despite different conversational strategies. Comparing CQ scores with communication and camouflaging measures could test this interpretation.

Adjusted mean D2 scores were highest among participants with lower secondary qualifications or some higher education without a degree. Difficulties with university group work and supervisor relationships illustrate how conversational experiences may shape educational participation (Gurbuz et al., 2019). Age associations were nonsignificant, whereas older adults reported fewer BAPQ Pragmatic Language difficulties in a larger study (Chopik et al., 2021). Experience may help adults manage conversations more effectively without reducing the effort involved. Longitudinal studies could test this possibility.

The German CQ provides a reliable, community-informed measure of conversational experiences; testing generalizability requires larger, more diverse samples, verified diagnoses, and additional communication measures.
