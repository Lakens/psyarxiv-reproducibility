Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. Abstract

Knowledge of memory and learning processes may inform teachers' educational decisions. However, to our knowledge, no psychometrically validated instrument specifically assesses teachers' procedural knowledge of these processes through realistic classroom scenarios.

This Stage 1 Registered Report aims to develop and validate a scenario-based measure of Italian teachers' procedural knowledge of memory and learning processes.

Approximately 600 Italian teachers, from preschool to upper-secondary education, will be recruited through proportional stratified quota sampling based on sex, geographical area, and school level.

Following two pilot studies and iterative revisions, 30 original items were retained and paired with conceptually matched items to create two intended parallel forms. All participants will complete the 60 multiple-choice items online. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses will be conducted separately for each form in independent subsamples. Measurement invariance across forms will be examined if corresponding factor structures are supported. Internal consistency will be estimated using Cronbach's alpha. Mixed-effects logistic regression will examine associations between itemlevel accuracy and teaching experience, educational attainment, and, among teachers with tertiary qualifications, academic field.

We expect interpretable, replicable factor structures and adequate internal consistency, with measurement invariance providing additional evidence of comparability across forms. Higher accuracy is tentatively expected with higher educational attainment, psycho-pedagogical qualifications, and fewer years of teaching experience.

The study will provide initial evidence on the psychometric properties of the proposed measure. If the findings support its validity and reliability, the instrument could subsequently be used to identify teachers' knowledge gaps and inform teacher education initiatives.

1. INTRODUCTION

Assessing teachers' knowledge of memory and learning processes is theoretically and educationally relevant since they constitute the cognitive mechanisms through which students acquire, retain, retrieve, and transfer knowledge. Research in cognitive and educational psychology has identified several principles that can substantially improve learning, such as retrieval practice, distributed practice, and instructional designs that take into account the limits of working memory and cognitive load (Dunlosky et al., 2013; Kirschner et al., 2006). However, pre-service and in-service teachers do not always possess accurate knowledge of effective learning strategies or memory-related principles and may misjudge the relative effectiveness of different approaches to studying and instruction (Firth, 2021; Halamish, 2018). This misjudgment may partly reflect the counterintuitive nature of some evidence-based learning strategies. Techniques such as retrieval practice, distributed practice, and interleaved practice introduce difficulties that can slow initial learning while improving long-term retention, whereas less effective strategies may produce rapid short-term gains and create powerful illusions of learning (Bjork, 1994; Bjork et al., 2013; Clark & Bjork, 2014). Practical classroom experience alone may be insufficient to correct these misconceptions, as teachers' metacognitive knowledge of evidence-based learning strategies does not necessarily improve with years of teaching practice and, in some studies, more experienced teachers have demonstrated less accurate judgments than novices (Firth, 2021; Halamish, 2018). Limited coverage during initial teacher education may also contribute to these knowledge gaps. For example, Surma et al. (2018) found that distributed practice and retrieval practice were often not fully covered in textbooks and syllabi used in Flemish and Dutch teacher education programs. Addressing these gaps is important because teachers' professional knowledge is considered a core component of instructional quality and has been linked to student learning outcomes in several domains (Baumert et al., 2010; Kunter et al., 2013; Sadler et al., 2013). Measuring teachers' knowledge of memory and learning processes may therefore help to identify strengths and gaps in the knowledge base that informs their educational decisions.

A particularly relevant distinction concerns whether such knowledge is assessed at a declarative or procedural level. Declarative items typically require respondents to indicate their level of agreement with statements presented in isolation, without reference to a specific classroom context, and therefore focus more directly on explicit knowledge or beliefs. By contrast, procedural items require teachers to evaluate how they would act in a specific classroom context in order to support students' learning, thereby focusing on how knowledge is applied to educational decision-making and on self-reported behavioral intentions. This distinction is important because declarative knowledge may be biased by teachers' self image and not always align with how they act or intend to act in educational settings. Consequently, measures based only on explicit statements may not fully capture the knowledge that guides teachers' classroom decisions. For this reason, assessing teachers' knowledge through realistic, scenario-based items may provide a more ecologically valid measure of how knowledge about memory and learning processes is applied in educational contexts (Rousseau, 2021; Sullivan et al., 2021; Tovazzi et al., 2020).

Existing research has assessed teachers' general pedagogical or psycho-pedagogical knowledge more broadly (OECD, 2026; Voss et al., 2011), as well as teachers' and instructors' beliefs, metacognitive judgments, or knowledge about specific memory and learning strategies (Firth, 2021; Halamish, 2018; Morehead et al., 2016; Surma et al., 2022). However, to our knowledge no psychometrically validated instrument is currently available to assess teachers' procedural knowledge of memory and learning processes through realistic classroom scenarios. This gap is important because it hinders investigation of teachers ability to apply evidence-based principles of memory and learning to educational situations that resemble those encountered in classroom practice. The aim of the present study is therefore to develop and psychometrically validate a measurement scale assessing teachers' procedural knowledge of memory and learning processes. The scale will consist of scenariobased multiple-choice items describing educational situations that teachers may encounter in their professional practice, organized into two intended parallel forms designed to assess the same underlying aspects of knowledge through conceptually matched but distinct scenarios.

The development of the present scale originated within a broader research program aimed at investigating Italian teachers' adherence to neuromyths. The main objective of this broader program is to develop measures assessing adherence to neuromyths both when neuromyths are presented as explicit statements and when they are embedded in realistic educational scenarios. Examining the association between neuromyth adherence and knowledge of memory and learning processes is important for evaluating the construct validity of the neuromyth measures and requires an independently validated measure of teachers' knowledge of memory and learning processes. The development of the present scale therefore constitutes the first stage of this broader research program.

Importantly, the relevance of the present instrument is not limited to its future use in research on neuromyths. Teachers' knowledge of memory and learning processes is itself a theoretically and educationally meaningful construct, as it may inform decisions concerning study activities, instructional strategies, feedback, practice, and the organization of learning. A validated measure of this knowledge can therefore be used independently to identify areas of strength and potential knowledge gaps, investigate differences associated with teachers' education and professional training, and evaluate the effects of interventions intended to improve evidence-based educational knowledge.

Findings on procedural knowledge of memory and learning processes suggest that such knowledge may be associated with several teachers' backgrounds and educational variables. First, evidence regarding teaching experience is mixed. Halamish (2018) found that in-service teachers' metacognitive knowledge of evidence-based learning strategies did not increase with tenure and was negatively associated with years of teaching experience. However, this pattern is not consistently observed, as Firth (2021) found that response accuracy in a vignette-based measure of teachers' beliefs about memory was not correlated with years of teaching experience. Second, results from the Teacher Knowledge Survey (OECD, 2026) indicate that teachers' general pedagogical knowledge tends to increase with their level of formal education, although the advantage associated with master's or doctoral degrees is not consistent across countries. Third, teachers' pathway into the profession may also be relevant: teachers whose first qualification included only subject-specific preparation, with minimal or no pedagogical preparation or practical teacher training, tended to show lower levels of general pedagogical knowledge in several countries (OECD, 2026). Taken together, these findings suggest that teaching experience, educational attainment, and academic field are relevant external correlates that need to be considered in the validation of the present scale.

Specifically, the study aims to: (a) investigate the latent structure of the two forms through separate exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses; (b) examine measurement invariance across them; (c) examine external validity by means of associations between performance and selected teacher background and educational variables, including years of teaching experience, educational attainment, and academic field, thereby providing validity evidence based on relations with other variables; and (d) assess the internal consistency of the two forms. 2. MATERIAL AND METHODS

>MATERIAL AND METHODS

2.1. Scale development

The scale was developed through a multi-step process. First, an initial pool of 34 multiple-choice items was created on the basis of theoretical and applied work on memory, learning, and cognitive processes (Baddeley et al., 2021; Cornoldi et al., 2018; Eysenck & Keane, 2015). Items were designed to vary in difficulty and to describe different concrete educational situations that teachers may encounter in their professional practice. Each item was embedded in a specific classroom-related context to assess procedural knowledge, rather than knowledge expressed through abstract declarative statements. Each item included one correct answer and three incorrect alternatives, or distractors, which were also contextualized within the scenario. The initial item pool was then evaluated for clarity by eight independent evaluators recruited without applying any specific educational or professional eligibility criteria. For each item, the evaluators were asked to indicate whether the item and its response options were sufficiently clear. When an item was judged to be unclear, they were asked to identify which aspects were difficult to understand and, where possible, to suggest how the item could be improved. Items were revised based on this feedback to improve clarity.

The revised items were subsequently administered in a first pilot study with 38 pre-service teachers. Responses were analyzed to examine preliminary item functioning. Specifically, item difficulty was inspected by identifying items that were particularly difficult, defined as items with accuracy lower than the chance level of 25% given the four response options per item, and items that were too easy, defined as items with 100% accuracy. The distribution of responses across distractors was also examined to identify alternatives that were rarely selected or clearly less attractive than the others. On the basis of these results, a second round of item revisions was conducted by the authors.

A second pilot study was then conducted with 48 Italian in-service teachers. As in the first pilot study, item difficulty and distractor functioning were inspected by examining accuracy levels and the distribution of responses across alternatives. In addition, exploratory analyses examined associations between overall response accuracy and several teacher characteristics using separate univariate logistic regression models. Among these analyses, educational attainment and years of teaching experience showed associations in the directions suggested by previous literature (Halamish, 2018; OECD, 2026). Given the small pilot sample, the exploratory nature of these analyses, and the substantial overlap among some participant characteristics, these findings were not considered evidence of the scale's validity, but rather preliminary observations to inform about the design of the larger validation study. The results of the second pilot study informed a final round of item revisions.

Finally, 30 items were selected based on item difficulty, distractor functioning, and the researchers' evaluation of item content and theoretical relevance. For each selected item, a conceptually parallel item was created, that is, an item intended to assess the same underlying aspect of knowledge through a different but theoretically equivalent scenario. These parallel items were then evaluated for clarity by a separate group of eight evaluators and refined based on their feedback. This procedure resulted in a final pool of 60 scenario-based multiple-choice items, organized into 30 conceptually related item pairs. The item pool is therefore divided into two parallel forms of 30 items, each form containing one item from each pair. The form comprising the original items will be referred to as the Original form, and the form comprising their conceptually parallel counterparts as the Twin form. An overview of the construct assessed by each item and the corresponding theoretical source is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

2.2. Participants

The study will involve ~600 teachers currently working in Italian schools (see Section 2.3, Sample size justification). Eligible participants will be teachers working at any school level, from preschool to upper-secondary education. Participants will be recruited using stratified quota sampling with proportional allocation, based on the 2024/25 reference distribution of Italian state-school teachers (N = 942,997; source: the Italian Ministry of Education and Merit's Portale unico dei dati della scuola).

Strata will be defined by crossing sex (female, male), geographical area (Northern Italy, combining North-West and North-East; Central Italy; Southern Italy and Islands), and school level (preschool, primary school, lower-secondary school, upper-secondary school), yielding 24 strata. Geographical areas will follow the divisions used by the Italian National Institute of Statistics (Istituto nazionale di statistica, 2025).

The unrounded target for each stratum h was calculated as n h * = n N h N , where n is the target sample size of 600, N h is the number of teachers in stratum h, and N is the total reference population.

Integer recruitment quotas were obtained using the largest remainder method (Hamilton method; Balinski & Young, 2001): each stratum was initially assigned the integer part of its unrounded target, and the remaining places were allocated to strata in descending order of their fractional remainders. This procedure ensures that the quotas sum to exactly 600. Each of the three strata comprising male preschool teachers represented less than 0.04% of the total reference population and received a quota of zero after rounding. The reference population proportions and planned recruitment quotas for each of the 24 strata are reported in Supplementary Material 3.

2.3. Sample size justification

The target sample size of approximately 600 teachers was determined primarily on the basis of the planned assessment of structural validity, the primary objective of the study. This target refers to the final analyzable sample. Incomplete questionnaires are expected to be excluded by the survey provider (Norstat).

The total sample will be randomly divided into two independent subsamples of approximately 300 participants each, with exploratory factor analyses conducted in the first subsample and confirmatory factor analyses in the second (see the Data analysis section for further details). Because the Original and Twin forms each comprise 30 items and their factor structures will be examined separately, each analysis will involve approximately 10 participants per item. This sample size exceeds the criterion specified in the COSMIN Risk of Bias Checklist (Mokkink et al., 2018) for a very good sample-size rating in structural validity studies, which requires at least seven participants per item and a minimum of 100 participants. Although this checklist was developed for systematic reviews of patient-reported outcome measures, its sample-size criterion provides an external methodological benchmark. A participant-to-item ratio of 10:1 has also been described as a commonly used heuristic in exploratory factor analysis (Costello & Osborne, 2005).

Nevertheless, sample size requirements in factor analysis depend on several characteristics of the data and model, including item communalities and the number of indicators per factor (MacCallum et al., 1999; Mundfrom et al., 2005). The planned sample size is therefore considered a pragmatic basis for the initial investigation and independent cross-validation of the factor structures of both forms, rather than a guarantee of adequate statistical power under all possible factor structures.

2.4. Procedure

Data will be collected through an online questionnaire. Participants will be recruited through Norstat (https://norstat.co/), a professional online panel provider. This recruitment strategy was selected to facilitate access to a large and heterogeneous sample of teachers across different geographical areas and school levels, and to allow data collection to be completed efficiently and anonimously.

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Free University of Bozen-Bolzano (protocol number UNIBZ_EDU_2026_08). Before accessing the questionnaire, participants will be presented with an informed consent form describing the aims of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the anonymous collection and processing of responses, and the possibility of withdrawing from the study at any time before submission. After acceptance, participants will then be asked to provide information on relevant sociodemographic, educational, and professional characteristics, including age, sex, region of teaching, school level, subject area taught, years of teaching experience, and highest completed educational qualification. Age and years of teaching experience will be recorded in years. Region of teaching will be re-coded into the three geographical areas defined above. Educational attainment will be classified as upper-secondary school diploma or equivalent, bachelor's degree, master's degree or equivalent, or doctoral degree. Participants with a tertiary qualification will also report the field of study of their highest completed qualification, which will be classified as psycho-pedagogical (psychology, educational sciences, pedagogy, teacher education, or closely related areas) or non-psycho-pedagogical.

Participants will additionally report separate ratings of their perceived knowledge of memory and learning on a scale from 0 to 10, the cumulative number of hours of non-university courses specifically concerning memory and learning processes that they have completed, the approximate number of peer-reviewed scientific articles they have read during the previous 12 months, and, among these, the approximate number specifically concerning memory and learning processes. The two knowledge ratings, course hours, and both article counts will be treated as separate continuous variables. Training undertaken as part of a university degree program will not be included in the estimate of non-university course hours.

After completing these background questions, participants will complete the 60 scenariobased items assessing procedural knowledge of memory and learning processes. The order of all items will be randomized across participants to reduce order effects. In addition, the order of the response options within each item will be randomized to minimize potential response-position biases. The questionnaire will be administered in Italian. The complete questionnaire, including the sociodemographic and professional questions and all items from the Original and Twin forms, is provided in its original Italian version in Supplementary Material 1a and in English translation in Supplementary Material 1b. The English translation is provided to facilitate understanding of the items and does not constitute a validated English-language version of the scale.

Recruitment will be monitored throughout data collection to meet the predefined quotas for geographical area, school level, and sex. Data quality will be assessed through Norstat's standard quality-control procedures. Incomplete questionnaires will not be included in the analytic dataset. Norstat automatically monitors survey completion times and compares individual response times with the project-specific median. Responses completed markedly faster than the other responses in the same study may be flagged and excluded, with ambiguous cases additionally reviewed by Norstat's local panel managers.

2.5. Data analysis

All analyses will be conducted in R (R Core Team, 2023). The psych package (Revelle, 2025) will be used to compute tetrachoric correlations and the KMO measure, perform Bartlett's test, conduct parallel analysis, inspect scree plots, and estimate Cronbach's alpha. The lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012; Rosseel et al., 2025) will be used to conduct EFA and CFA, the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) to estimate mixed-effects logistic regression models, and the tidyverse collection of packages (Wickham et al., 2019) for data manipulation and visualization.

2.5.1 Internal structure: EFA and CFA

The 60-item pool comprises the Original and Twin forms, each containing one item from each conceptually related pair. Because the two items in each pair were deliberately designed to assess the same underlying principle through similar but distinct educational scenarios, responses to paired items may share pair-specific variance beyond that accounted for by the latent factor(s). Including both members of each pair in the same model could therefore violate the assumption of local independence. Accordingly, all EFA and CFA analyses described in this section will be conducted separately for the Original and Twin forms.

To investigate the latent structure, the total sample will be randomly split into two independent subsamples, while preserving the stratification of the whole sample in terms of geographical area, sex, and school level. EFAs will be conducted in the first subsample. Each retained solution will then be tested through a CFA (Jöreskog, 1969) in the second subsample, thereby cross-validating the structures in an independent group of participants. The consistency of the solutions across forms will be evaluated by comparing the number and substantive interpretation of the retained factors and the loading patterns of corresponding items. Before conducting these analyses, the factorability of the item correlation matrices will be examined using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy (KMO; Kaiser, 1970) and Bartlett's test of sphericity. KMO values above .80 will be considered good, whereas values above .70 will be considered acceptable for factor analysis (Kaiser & Rice, 1974). A statistically significant Bartlett's test will be taken as evidence that the correlation matrix is not an identity matrix.

EFA will be used to explore the latent structures and the pattern of relationships between items and latent factor(s), whereas CFA will be used to test the adequacy of the structures identified through EFA. Because the measure is a maximum-performance measure in which item responses will be coded dichotomously as correct or incorrect, tetrachoric correlations between items will be used as the starting point for EFA and CFA. The weighted least squares mean-and variance-adjusted estimator (WLSMV) will be used in both EFA and CFA. The number of factors to be tested will be determined by considering both parallel analysis and inspection of the scree plot. Candidate solutions will then be compared in terms of model fit, pattern of factor loadings, presence of cross-loadings, and theoretical interpretability. The final solutions retained for CFA will be selected based on the convergence of these empirical and theoretical criteria. When multiple factors are extracted, an oblique rotation method such as oblimin will be used, as the latent dimensions of teachers' knowledge of memory and learning processes are expected to be correlated.

Model fit will be evaluated using multiple indices. For the purposes of the present study, models investigated through EFA and CFA will be considered to show acceptable fit when the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) is ≤ .08. RMSEA values ≤ .06 will be interpreted as indicating particularly good fit, consistent with Hu and Bentler (1999). Comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) values ≥ .95 will likewise be considered indicative of good fit, in line with Hu and Bentler (1999). Standardized factor loadings will be examined to evaluate item relevance. A standardized loading of ≥ .30 will be used as the prespecified criterion for adequate association between the item and the factor. Items with loadings < .30 or substantial cross-loadings will be considered for revision or removal. Decisions about item retention or removal will additionally take into account the theoretical alignment and content coverage of the items. Cross-loadings will be considered substantial when an item shows factor loadings ≥ .30 on more than one factor.

2.5.2 Measurement invariance across forms

After the form-specific structures have been investigated and cross-validated as described above, measurement invariance will be examined using a joint confirmatory factor analysis including both forms. This analysis will be conducted only if the preceding analyses support substantively corresponding factor structures. A schematic overview of the planned analytical procedure is provided in Figure 1.

The measurement invariance analysis will be conducted on the full sample after the factor structure and final set of retained items have been established. We acknowledge that this approach does not provide a fully independent sample for the measurement invariance test. However, the joint CFA is substantially more complex than the separate models because it includes both forms simultaneously as well as the residual covariances between matched item pairs. Restricting this analysis to the confirmatory subsample would substantially reduce the available sample size and could compromise estimation stability. If item removal during the preceding analyses results in the exclusion of one member of a conceptually matched pair, that pair will not contribute to the measurement invariance analysis.

Each form will be represented by its own latent factor or set of latent factors according to the structure retained in the preceding analyses, with corresponding latent factors allowed to correlate. Because Original and Twin items are conceptually matched, a residual covariance will be specified a priori for each pair and freely estimated in both models to account for pair-specific covariance not explained by the latent factor(s).

Because item responses will be coded as binary indicators (0 = incorrect,1 = correct), measurement invariance will be evaluated using CFA with categorical indicators and the weighted least squares mean-and variance-adjusted estimator (WLSMV), following the measurement invariance framework described by Brown (2015). Two nested models will be estimated. First, a baseline model will be specified in which the factor structure identified in the preceding analyses is modeled simultaneously for the Original and Twin forms, while the unstandardized factor loadings and indicator thresholds of corresponding items are freely estimated. Second, a measurement invariance model will be estimated in which, for each retained matched pair, the unstandardized factor loading and indicator threshold of the Original item, are constrained to equal those of its Twin counterpart: λ Oi = λ Ti , τ Oi =τ Ti .

Here, λ and τ denote the unstandardized factor loading and the item threshold, respectively. The subscripts O and T identify the Original and Twin forms, respectively, while i indexes the matched item pairs. These equality constraints will be imposed only between matched Original and Twin items; parameters from different item pairs will not be constrained to equality.

The measurement invariance model will be compared with the baseline model using the robust chi-square difference test ( χ diff 2 ) appropriate for WLSMV. Provided that the baseline and constrained models show acceptable overall fit, a non-significant difference test will be interpreted as evidence that constraining the factor loadings and indicator thresholds of corresponding Original and Twin items to equality does not produce a significant deterioration in model fit. A significant difference test will be interpreted as evidence against full measurement invariance.

If full measurement invariance is not supported, modification indices will be inspected to identify noninvariant matched item pairs. For a noninvariant item pair, the equality constraints on its factor loading and indicator threshold will be relaxed together. The number and identity of noninvariant item pairs will be explicitly reported.

2.5.3 Associations with teacher background and educational variables

All regression analyses described in this section will be estimated separately for the Original and Twin forms. To examine whether teachers' procedural knowledge of memory and learning processes varies as a function of selected background and educational variables, item-level accuracy will be analyzed using mixed-effects logistic regression models. For each participant and item, responses will be coded dichotomously as1 = correct and0 = incorrect.

Two primary models will be estimated. The first model (Model A in Table 1) will be estimated using the full sample. The probability of providing a correct response will be modeled as a function of years of teaching experience and educational attainment, with upper-secondary school diploma or equivalent as the reference category for educational attainment. The second model (Model B in Table 1) will be estimated only among participants who have completed a tertiary qualification. In this subsample, the probability of providing a correct response will be modeled as a function of years of teaching experience, level of tertiary qualification, and academic field. Bachelor's degree will be the reference category for level of tertiary qualification, and non-psycho-pedagogical qualification the reference category for academic field. Academic field will not be included in the full-sample model because it is not defined for participants without a tertiary qualification. When the disciplinary classification of a qualification is not straightforward, it will be classified independently by two members of the research team. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion before the outcome data are analyzed.

Because responses are nested within both participants and items, each model will include random intercepts for participants and items. This approach will allow accounting for individual differences in overall response accuracy and for differences in item difficulty. Fixed effects will be reported as log-odds coefficients and corresponding odds ratios (i.e., the exponentiated form of the same coefficients), together with 95% confidence intervals. For continuous predictors and binary categorical predictors, statistical significance will be based on the p-value associated with the corresponding model coefficient, using two-sided tests with an alpha level of .05. For categorical predictors with more than two levels, overall statistical significance will first be evaluated using likelihood-ratio tests; if the omnibus test is significant, specific model contrasts will then be evaluated using the same significance criterion. As sensitivity analyses, both primary models will be reestimated including geographical area, school level, and sex as additional covariates. The primary interpretation will be based on the corresponding unadjusted models, whereas the adjusted models will be interpreted as robustness checks.

Additional exploratory analyses will examine associations between item-level response accuracy and variables that are not included in the primary regression models and for which the available literature does not provide a sufficiently strong basis for directional a priori hypotheses. These variables will include age, school level, subject area taught, geographical area, sex, the two self-reported knowledge ratings, cumulative hours of relevant non-university courses, the total number of peer-reviewed scientific articles read during the previous 12 months, and the number of those articles specifically concerning memory and learning processes.

Each exploratory association will be examined in a separate mixed-effects logistic regression model using the same item-level outcome and random-effects structure specified for the primary analyses. Because research literature has provided contrasting evidence, it is not possible to specify directional hypotheses for these analyses, and their results will be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as confirmatory evidence.

2.5.4 Internal consistency

Internal consistency will be evaluated in the full analyzable sample, separately for the Original and Twin forms. Cronbach's alpha will be computed on the final set of retained items after the exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. If a retained structure is unidimensional, Cronbach's alpha will be computed for its total score. If a multidimensional structure is retained, Cronbach's alpha will be computed separately for each subscale, because alpha is most appropriate when items are intended to measure a single latent dimension. Generally, Cronbach's alpha values ≥ .70 will be considered indicative of adequate internal consistency (Tavakol & Dennick, 2011).

In addition, alpha-if-item-deleted values will be inspected as a diagnostic tool to identify items that may be inconsistent with the remaining items in the corresponding scale or subscale. However, item removal will not be based solely on increases in Cronbach's alpha. Decisions about item retention or removal will also consider factor loadings, cross-loadings, item content, and the theoretical coverage of the construct. 3. EXPECTED RESULTS

We expect the exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses to provide evidence supporting the internal structure of both forms. Given the exploratory nature of the initial stage and of the domain, no strict prediction is made regarding the exact number of factors that will emerge. However, we expect EFA to identify theoretically interpretable factor solutions, characterized by coherent patterns of item loadings, limited cross-loadings, and a sufficient number of items loading substantially on the retained factor(s). We further expect CFA, conducted on an independent subsample extracted from the same population, to support the adequacy of the corresponding solutions, with model fit indices falling within the good or optimal ranges defined in the Data Analysis section. Overall, these results are expected to provide validity evidence based on internal structure. If the form-specific analyses support substantively corresponding factor structures, we expect the joint CFA to support measurement invariance of the unstandardized factor loadings and indicator thresholds across matched Original and Twin items. We also expect performance to be associated with the selected teacher background and educational variables. Specifically, based on previous findings, we expect years of teaching experience to be negatively associated with the probability of providing a correct response, although this prediction is considered tentative because previous evidence is mixed.

In the full-sample model, we expect educational attainment to be associated with item-level accuracy, with participants holding higher completed educational qualifications generally showing a higher probability of responding correctly than participants whose highest qualification is an uppersecondary school diploma.

In the model restricted to participants with a tertiary qualification, we tentatively expect participants holding higher-level tertiary qualifications to show a higher probability of responding correctly than participants whose highest qualification is a bachelor's degree. This prediction draws on findings concerning general pedagogical knowledge, although the reported advantage of higher qualifications is not consistent across countries (OECD, 2026). We also expect participants whose highest tertiary qualification is in a psychological, pedagogical, educational, or closely related field to show a higher probability of responding correctly than participants whose tertiary qualification is in another disciplinary area.

Finally, we expect adequate internal consistency for both forms. If a retained structure is unidimensional, Cronbach's alpha is expected to indicate adequate internal consistency for its total score according to the criterion specified in the Data Analysis section. If a multidimensional structure is retained, Cronbach's alpha is expected to indicate adequate internal consistency for each subscale, while taking into account that shorter subscales may show lower alpha values. These results will be interpreted together with the evidence on factor loadings, item content, and theoretical coverage of the construct.
