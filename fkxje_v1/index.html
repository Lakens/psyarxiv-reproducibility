Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Introduction

Young people today grow up in a digital world that differs substantially from the world in which the adults in their lives grew up. Despite this gap, young people do need guidance from adults in their lives to navigate online risks safely and to benefit from the many opportunities the digital world offers (Classen et al., 2020; Stavropoulos et al., 2021). Support from adults may be especially relevant for neurodivergent youth, including those with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), mild intellectual disabilities (MID), and dyslexia (Layinka et al., 2024), as they face both heightened opportunities and elevated risks in digital environments (Alfredsson et al., 2020; Dündar & Karabıçak, 2022; El-Asam et al., 2023; Eltahir et al., 2025; Kahraman & Demirci, 2018; Stiller et al., 2019).

Youths' digital experiences are affected by commercial, aggressive, sexual, and values-related risks and educational opportunities, digital literacy learning, participation and civic engagement, creativity and selfexpression, and identity and social connection (Livingstone & Haddon, 2009; Staksrud et al., 2009). How young people are affected by such risks and opportunities depends on individual factors such as resilience and coping, as well as broader ecological systems including families, schools and care settings (Livingstone et al., 2016). To benefit from digital media, neurodivergent young people need knowledge and skills. Ribble (2011) describes nine interrelated elements of digital citizenship: digital access, digital literacy, digital communication, digital rights and responsibilities, digital etiquette, digital security, digital law, digital health and wellness, and digital commerce. Together, these elements might give a first conceptual framework to help young people use digital technologies effectively, safely, and responsibly while respecting the norms, values, and rights of others.

Neurodivergent youth are typically supported through multiple systems, including education and healthcare. Healthcare professionals and educators, henceforth referred to as 'professionals', are therefore key social agents in supporting neurodivergent young people in their digital lives (Livingstone et al., 2016). They serve as role models and sources of information, protection and support, particularly for young people who cannot rely on parental guidance. Professionals therefore play an important role in supporting young people to benefit from online opportunities while managing potential risks (Mendoza, 2009; Nikken et al., 2024; Nixon & Halpenny, 2010).

In the focus group interviews, both healthcare professionals and educators elaborated on the types of risks neurodivergent young people encounter. Both groups noted high levels of aggression and hate on social media.

Healthcare professionals observed that online conflicts sometimes escalate to offline violence, and that young people are exposed to harmful trends on platforms such as TikTok. Educators similarly noted cyberbullying and hate texts as prominent risks.

Both groups used a range of tools and approaches when supporting young people, though with some differences in emphasis (see Appendix A Table 7A). Support Challenges

Despite reporting few formal support needs in the questionnaire, both groups described meaningful challenges in their day-to-day work with neurodivergent young people in the focus group interviews.

Collaboration with parents.

Both groups mentioned collaboration with parents as a challenge, though the nature of the challenge differed between the two groups. Healthcare professionals described a situation in which young people are often more open with them than with their parents, leaving parents unaware of online issues and making collaboration difficult. On top of that, when professionals were informed and wanted to share information with parents, they found it difficult to convey the urgency to parents who they felt often did not perceive the risks. On the other hand, healthcare professionals noted that parents frequently asked for more restrictive mediation conflicting with the professionals' own approach.

AH2: "I also had a girl in my counseling program who had posted herself in her underwear on YouTube.

So I called her parents about it, and they didn't seem very impressed. They didn't seem to realize that anyone could see it and that it could be recorded on a cell phone, meaning it could exist forever."

Educators described a somewhat more functional relationship with parents, noting that collaboration is possible when problems arise.

Privacy. Healthcare professionals specifically mentioned young people's desire for privacy as a barrier to support. They suggested young people could be protective of their digital worlds and resistant to professional involvement.

CH2: "Well, if you even come near it, it's gone -so to speak... No, I think it's mainly because they just feel like: this is mine."

Responsibility and time. Educators in particular raised questions about the boundaries of their professional role.

They described feeling that digital support falls outside their core responsibilities, and that they lack the time and specialised knowledge to address more complex digital issues.

E4: "But we are not people who ignore psychological aspects. And you know, of course you can have conversations, but at some point that stops, and I think that as teachers we are increasingly aware that... there is a limit to what we can do, because we can all take on so much and want to solve everything, but that's not realistic. Support Needs

The majority of professionals in both groups reported being able to find answers when they have questions about online issues. Both groups relied primarily on colleagues, though healthcare professionals also drew on therapists and websites, while educators made more use of student support coordinators and assisting staff (Table 5). In the focus group interviews, both groups initially did not spontaneously identify additional support needs. Only after explicit prompting did specific needs emerge.

Healthcare professionals identified three areas where additional support would be helpful. First, they noted the need for more accessible official websites and government services for neurodivergent young people. Second, they identified a need for training and interventions for parents -both to raise awareness of online risks and to help parents develop more nuanced approaches to screen time guidance. Healthcare professionals noted that parents frequently ask for screen time guidelines but felt that a single guideline cannot be appropriate for all children. For neurodivergent young people in particular, they believed the focus should be on managing transitions between activities rather than limiting time per se.

AH3: "I think that with autism, preparing for the end of the game is very important. It's better to give them plenty of warning that they will have to stop gaming in a certain amount of time and to repeat this regularly so that they know when the end is coming and are prepared for it. Third, healthcare professionals identified a need for more support from management and specialists within their organisations. Educators identified somewhat different support needs. They noted that digital tools are helpful but should be better aligned to the emotional capabilities of neurodivergent students. Most strikingly, educators specifically suggested the need for a dedicated digital coach -a younger professional with up-to-date knowledge of the digital landscape -to support them in their work.

Discussion

This study examined how healthcare professionals and educators perceived the digital needs, opportunities, and risks of neurodivergent youth, and how they characterised their own capacities, supporting styles, and support needs in this domain. Professionals agreed that the digital capacities of neurodivergent youth could be strengthened, particularly in relation to applying digital knowledge in everyday situations. Professionals perceived neurodivergent youth as technically capable and often aware of online risks, but reported that young people frequently experienced difficulties translating this knowledge into behaviour. Furthermore, professionals acknowledged that they could not protect young people against all digital risks. Despite initially reporting few unmet support needs, more specific needs emerged when explicitly prompted in the focus group interviews. Key challenges identified by professionals include collaboration with parents, respecting young people's privacy, and questions about professional responsibility.

Capacities of Neurodivergent Youth

Both healthcare professionals and educators perceived the digital capacities of neurodivergent young people as limited across most elements of digital citizenship, with the notable exception of digital literacy, where educators rated students' skills more positively. This suggests that neurodivergent youth are not uniformly lacking in digital competence (they can be technically proficient) but struggle particularly with understanding consequences, recognising risks, and navigating complex or official digital environments.

A particularly important finding concerns the gap between neurodivergent youth's knowledge about digital risks and the subsequent actions that they take. This knowledge-action gap is consistent with previous research, which shows youth are more inclined to engage in risk behaviors in the presence of peers (Powers et al., 2022), though they may aware that their behaviour is dangerous. Kamarudding et al. (2023) have argued that school-based cyberbullying programmes typically yield small effects probably due to the challenge of translating learning into behaviour. This suggests that prevention approaches for neurodivergent young people need to go beyond information transfer and focus more on applied, contextualised learning.

Opportunities and Risks

A striking asymmetry emerged between the two professional groups in how they framed digital media for neurodivergent young people. Both professional groups attached greater importance to online risks than to online opportunities. Yet, during the focus group interviews healthcare professionals elaborated more extensively on opportunities than educators. However, because opportunities were not explored using identical prompts across all focus groups, differences between professional groups should be interpreted with caution.

Nevertheless, the risk-centered emphasis among educators is consistent with broader patterns in the literature. School and policy responses to children's digital lives have historically prioritised safety and protection over empowerment and opportunity-building (Frau-Meigs et al., 2017; OECD, 2023), and digital citizenship education is frequently operationalised as responsible behaviour and online safety rather than as a balanced agenda that equally foregrounds participation, creativity, and critical engagement (Tadlaoui -Brahmi et al., 2022). Our findings suggest that this pattern extends to educators working with neurodivergent young people, potentially leaving a gap in the support these youth receive for realising the positive potential of digital media.

Regarding risks, both groups identified aggression, commercial exploitation, and risks related to values and sexual content as prominent concerns. These risks are not unique to neurodivergent young people, but professionals in both groups suggested that neurodivergent youth may be particularly vulnerable, for example because of difficulties in recognising social cues, saying no, or anticipating consequences. This is consistent with existing literature on elevated online risk for young people with ADHD, autism, and MID (El-Asam et al., 2023; Eltahir et al., 2025). Professionals reported feeling capable of supporting young people around knowledge of risks yet acknowledged that they cannot protect them against risks. This finding might suggest the importance of equipping young people themselves with the skills and resilience to navigate digital risks, rather than relying solely on professional oversight. That is, professionals should adopt an enabling mediation approach in young people's daily online lives rather than attempting to control their online activities (Edelaar-Peeters et al., submitted).

Professionals' own Capacities and Supporting Style

Both healthcare professionals and educators reported feeling reasonably confident in their own digital capacities in the questionnaire, although healthcare professionals reported to be more confident than educators. The difference in confidence between the two professional groups may be explained in part by their different professional orientations. Healthcare professionals approach digital support from a relational perspective, focusing on the relationship with the young person and adapting to their needs. Educators, by contrast, may operate from a more traditional teacher-centered framework, in which the teacher is expected to be the authority on a subject before teaching it (Admiraal et al., 2017; Biggs, 2012; Serin, 2018). In a domain as rapidly changing as digital media, this expectation of authoritative knowledge may be particularly difficult to meet, which could explain why educators feel less confident.

The different professional orientations are reflected in the supporting styles described by the two groups. Healthcare professionals predominantly described an enabling and authoritative approach: having conversations about online issues, learning from young people about online culture, playing games together, and offering unsolicited but relationally embedded advice. Restrictive mediation was used rarely and acknowledged as sometimes necessary but not the preferred approach. Educators, by contrast, described a more mixed picture: their day-to-day approach was broadly enabling, but restrictive measures such as the smartphone ban were also mentioned.

This difference is consistent with broader research on digital parenting and mediation styles, which suggests that enabling and authoritative approaches are generally preferred to restrictive ones in supporting young people's digital development (Edelaar-Peeters et al., submitted; Geurts et al., 2023 , Vossen et al., 2024). A teacher-centered approach aligns with a restrictive authoritarian mediation style, whereas an enabling and authoritative mediation style is associated with a student-centered approach (Admiraal et al., 2017; Biggs, 2012; Glatz et al., 2018; Serin, 2018). Regarding digital support in education, a student-centered approach with coconstructivist practices is preferred -one in which educators do not need to be an authority on the topic but take a coaching role in students' development of knowledge (Admiraal et al., 2017; Biggs, 2012; Tang, 2023).

Support Needs

The specific challenges and needs identified differed somewhat between the two groups, reflecting their different professional roles and contexts.

Responsibility and time.

Educators questioned whether digital education falls within their professional remit and expressed concern about the time and energy required. This question is understandable. However, the Digital Education Action plan now encourages, European member states to promote digital education in schools through clear strategic visions and concrete actions (European Commission, 2020). In the Netherlands, new core objectives for digital education will become mandatory parts of the curriculum from August 2027 (SLO, 2025).

This means that, in the near future, digital education and support will become a formal responsibility of schools and teachers. This is only feasible if educational organisations provide clear guidelines and a student-centered approach enacting these responsibilities, so that educators experience digital education as a shared endeavour rather than an individual burden.

Privacy. Both groups noted that young people's desire for privacy can be a barrier to digital support.

Professionals are not always permitted to monitor or restrict young people's online activities, and young people can be resistant to professional involvement in their digital lives. One way to navigate this barrier is for professionals to maintain an open and transparent social media presence, allowing them to connect informally with young people online and gain a sense of what is happening in their digital world (Hamdiui & van den Broek, 2019). Regularly discussing digital life as part of everyday conversations is another approach that can reduce the threshold for young people to seek support (Knipschild et al., 2025).

Collaboration with parents.

Healthcare professionals in particular found collaboration with parents challenging. Parents were often unaware of online issues, tended to ask for more restrictive approaches than professionals considered appropriate, and sometimes failed to recognise the urgency of situations that professionals had identified. This difficulty in parent-professional collaboration is not unique to the digital domain: research suggests it is a broader challenge in care for neurodivergent youth (Nikken et al., 2024; Mahboub & Yassin, 2024; Westaway, 2023).

Tools and interventions.

Both groups reported using digital tools to support their work, but the selection of tools appeared relatively arbitrary. It would be valuable to support professionals in identifying highquality, evidence-based tools. Currently, only a limited number of evidence-based digital interventions are available (Chaplinska et al., 2026), which makes this a priority area for future development.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings.

First, both the questionnaire and focus group samples were recruited through convenience sampling. In both cases, this may have resulted in samples of professionals who are more engaged or interested in the topic of digital support than average. Additionally, dropout in the online questionnaire was substantial. Although dropout analyses revealed no significant differences between completers and non-completers, with the exception of primary school teachers, selection bias cannot be ruled out. Yet, the findings were combined with data from focus group interviews, providing a deeper understanding of the questionnaire responses.

Second, a discrepancy emerged between educators' questionnaire responses, in which they reported feeling digitally competent, and their focus group accounts, in which they described struggling to keep up with digital developments. This suggests that the questionnaire may not have been well-calibrated to capture professionals' actual experiences of digital competence, and that self-report measures in this domain should be interpreted with caution. Similarly, support needs only emerged after explicit prompting in the focus groups, raising questions about the sensitivity of the questionnaire for detecting unmet needs. These insights seems to illustrate the importance of the mixed method approach, showing that educators might be better able to articulate their needs when they are actively considering and discussing digital opportunities and needs, at the same time one must take into account that the questionnaire is not answered by the same people who participated in the focus group interview.

Third, the broad scope of the study -covering digital capacities, opportunities, risks, supporting styles, and support needs across two professional groups -meant that not all topics could be explored in equal depth in either the questionnaire or the focus group interviews. The mixed-methods design was chosen precisely to provide a broad overview rather than an in-depth examination of any single topic, which was appropriate given the exploratory aims of the study.

Finally, the study focused on neurodivergent youth as a broad category, without distinguishing systematically between different neurodevelopmental conditions. Neurodivergent young people are a heterogeneous group, and findings may not apply equally across all conditions. While comorbidity is common in this group (Godard et al., 2026; Stevens et al., 2016), this does not eliminate meaningful differences between, for example, young people with autism and those with ADHD or MID.

Conclusions

The digital world holds both significant opportunities and risks for neurodivergent youth, and professionals may help them to navigate both. This study provides a first insight into how healthcare professionals and educators perceive the digital needs, opportunities, and risks of neurodivergent young people, and how they characterise their own capacities, supporting styles, and support needs in this domain.

Both groups agreed that the digital capacities of neurodivergent young people can be strengthened in specific areas, though the findings also highlight genuine strengths that should not be overlooked. Professionals described neurodivergent youth as technically competent and generally aware of online risks, while also experiencing difficulties anticipating consequences and acting accordingly. Support should focus less on increasing knowledge and more on helping neurodivergent youth apply existing knowledge in real-life situations.

Both groups reported feeling capable of supporting young people with digital issues in general, yet almost all acknowledged that they cannot fully protect young people against digital risks. This underscores the importance of equipping neurodivergent young people themselves with the skills and resilience to navigate digital challenges, rather than relying solely on professional oversight. The specific support needs of the two groups differed in ways that reflect their different roles. Healthcare professionals reported needing support primarily in collaborating with parents and in developing nuanced, tailored approaches to guidance. Educators reported needing support in understanding their professional responsibility for digital education and in accessing up-to-date expertise, for example through a dedicated digital coach. Both groups would benefit from better access to high-quality, evidence-based tools and interventions for neurodivergent youth, of which currently only a limited number are available.

Finally, when making these conclusions it has to be taken into account that the study relied entirely on professionals' perceptions of neurodivergent young people's digital capacities and needs. These perceptions may not fully reflect young people's actual experiences, and future research would benefit from combining professional perspectives with direct input from neurodivergent young people themselves.
