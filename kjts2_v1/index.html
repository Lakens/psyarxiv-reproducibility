Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Introduction

Ultra-short assessment formats reduce respondent burden, fatigue, and administration time, but psychometric compression is not only an administrative operation. Shortening a measure reduces the number of observations available for content sampling, reliability, and construct representation, and it can weaken the evidence for the intended score interpretation when item reduction is guided mainly by convenience rather than by theory and validation evidence (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014; Messick, 1995; Smith et al., 2000). This concern becomes sharper when compression is combined with nontraditional response formats or stimuli, because the information lost may concern not only content breadth but also the response process that links stimuli to the intended construct (Messick, 1995; Wetzel & Greiff, 2018).

Paired-comparison methods provide one possible response-format alternative. Their psychometric roots lie in Thurstone's comparative judgment model and subsequent pairedcomparison scaling and structural-equation work, and they have also been used in applied preference and decision contexts where graded strength of preference is informative (Bech et al., 2007; Bramley, 2007; Maydeu-Olivares & Böckenholt, 2005; Thurstone, 1927). Modern forcedchoice and paired-comparison models can recover normative trait information under appropriate design conditions, but the format does not guarantee validity by itself; the pair structure, scoring model, and construct representation remain central (Brown, 2016; Brown & Maydeu-Olivares, 2011; Maydeu-Olivares & Brown, 2010).

The present article focuses on graded paired comparisons (GPCs), not strictly binary forced choice. In a GPC item, respondents indicate both the direction and the strength of their preference or self-description. This distinction matters because dichotomizing a comparative response discards preference-strength information, whereas graded responses preserve orderedcategory information that may improve score recovery if the response categories are interpretable (Bech et al., 2007; Brown & Maydeu-Olivares, 2018; Stark et al., 2005). At the same time, graded responses may introduce their own threshold and response-style complications, so the format should be evaluated as a measurement design rather than as a generic improvement over ratings or binary choices (Brown & Maydeu-Olivares, 2018; Wetzel & Carstensen, 2017; Wetzel et al., 2020).

Image-based GPC assessment adds a further layer of design risk. Images can be fast, engaging, and semantically rich, and image-based comparative assessment has shown empirical promise in personality assessment (Hilliard et al., 2022). However, images are high-dimensional stimuli: a photograph can carry target-trait content alongside visual salience, affective tone, familiarity, social desirability, cultural associations, and idiosyncratic preferences. From a validity perspective, the key question is therefore not whether images are appealing, but whether the image-pair response process preserves construct-relevant information and limits constructirrelevant variance (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014; Hilliard et al., 2022; Messick, 1995).

A narrower measurement question must be separated from the broader validity question: how much target-relevant information remains in an ultra-short response pattern? Information recovery refers to the extent to which scores from an ultra-short response pattern recover either a known latent target or an observed benchmark score. It is narrower than validity, because benchmark recovery cannot establish content representation, response-process validity, fairness, or appropriate use by itself; nevertheless, poor benchmark recovery is a serious warning sign for any intended interpretation (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014; Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Messick, 1995). This framing also clarifies the role of supervised prediction.

Cross-validated models can estimate how much benchmark information is recoverable from the response pattern, but they are diagnostic recovery benchmarks rather than psychometric scoring models by themselves (Morris et al., 2019). We therefore use information recovery as the organizing principle for the present article.

Research on comparative measurement shows that item-pair construction and loading configurations affect forced-choice performance (Sun et al., 2024). For Thurstonian IRT, previous work has documented statistical limitations (Bürkner et al., 2019) and derived theoretical bounds on information obtainable from ordinal comparative judgments (Bürkner, 2022). Empirical research has compared graded, dichotomous forced-choice, and Likert formats in terms of psychometric properties and response styles (Zhang et al., 2024), while simulations have examined response categories, ipsativity, and pair composition (Lingel et al., 2025). Ultrashort pictorial assessment also predates this work: the PPTQ-C uses three paired pictorial contrasts per Big Five dimension (Maćkiewicz & Cieciuch, 2016), although a subsequent evaluation reported limited factorial support (Martins et al., 2026). Image-based forced-choice instruments drawing on larger item pools have demonstrated benchmark convergence (Hilliard et al., 2022). What remains less directly characterized is the joint influence of target signal, stimulus properties, pair construction, and systematic nuisance on benchmark recovery from only three graded comparisons. The present study therefore maps conditional benchmark recovery across these design factors, test length, and response format, including cases in which internal consistency increases while target recovery declines.

The article addresses three questions. First, which design factors most strongly determine benchmark recovery in ultra-short GPC assessment? Second, do graded preferences retain more target information than binary paired choices when both are generated from the same latent utilities? Third, do empirical three-pair image-GPC implementations targeting different construct domains exhibit distinct benchmark-recovery profiles despite sharing the same response format and test length? Study 1 answers the first two questions through a factorial Monte Carlo simulation. Study 2 addresses the third question using two brief benchmarks expected to differ in empirical recoverability: the STAI-6 state anxiety short form and the Smartphone Application-Based Addiction Scale (SABAS; Csibi et al., 2018; Marteau & Bekker, 1992; Spielberger, 1983). STAI-6 is a focused state-affective measure designed for brief administration, whereas SABAS is commonly scored as a unidimensional total but covers addiction-component content including salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse (Billieux et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2024; Csibi et al., 2018; Elhai et al., 2017; Mason et al., 2022; Vujić et al., 2024).

Study 1 Simulation Study

Methods

Study 1 evaluated whether ultra-short GPC responses can recover a target trait when construct saturation, stimulus validity, nuisance variance, pair construction, test length, response format, and recovery method are varied. The simulation followed recommendations for simulation studies as evaluations of statistical and measurement procedures by specifying the datagenerating mechanism, manipulated factors, estimands, performance measures, replication structure, and sensitivity checks (Morris et al., 2019). The main design crossed 324 conditions with 1,000 replications per condition. Each replication generated N = 375 persons to ensure the Monte Carlo conditions represented the scale of the intended empirical application, generating three response datasets from the same latent utilities: single-stimulus ratings, graded paired comparisons, and binary paired choices. Holding the latent utilities constant allowed the response formats to be compared without changing the stimulus pool.

The data-generating model included one target trait and one nuisance trait. Benchmark indicators defined the target to be recovered, which operationalized the idea that an empirical ultra-short task is usually judged against an observed benchmark rather than against a known latent trait (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Messick, 1995). Stimulus utilities were generated so that target signal could be strong or weak, and so that a systematic nuisance contrast could be absent, moderate, or strong. Pair construction varied the quality of target contrasts: balanced pairs had clear target direction, asymmetric pairs weakened one side of the target contrast, and contaminated pairs added construct-irrelevant influence. Test length varied from 3 to 24 pairs. The three-pair condition was the focal ultra-short design; longer forms indicated when additional comparisons became necessary (Brown & Maydeu-Olivares, 2011; Smith et al., 2000).

Together, these factors represented the main design routes by which target information can be preserved, attenuated, or replaced by stable construct-irrelevant responding in an ultra-short comparative task (Brown, 2016; Hilliard et al., 2022; Messick, 1995; Morris et al., 2019; Thurstone, 1927). Results

The main result was the design dependence of recovery. Under balanced pair construction and no nuisance variance, three GPC pairs recovered the benchmark strongly when construct saturation and stimulus validity were high, r = .852. Recovery remained above the practical r = .70 criterion when construct saturation was high and stimulus validity was moderate, r = .745. When either construct saturation or stimulus validity was low, three pairs were insufficient and longer forms were needed. With moderate construct saturation and high stimulus validity, six pairs crossed the criterion; with lower saturation or stimulus validity, even 24 pairs often failed to reach it. Figure 1 visualizes this interaction: ultra-short recovery appeared in the high-signal region of the design space, whereas weaker construct or stimulus signal required more pairs and often still failed to reach the recovery criterion.

Nuisance variance created a particularly important warning. For GPC responses, stronger systematic nuisance variance reduced benchmark recovery but increased internal consistency.

Mean benchmark recovery declined from .582 when nuisance strength was zero to .374 when nuisance strength was .45, whereas omega increased from .695 to .895. Thus, reliability could reflect stable construct-irrelevant responding. Pair contamination showed the same pattern: balanced pairs outperformed contaminated pairs in benchmark recovery, despite acceptable reliability under some contaminated conditions. This dissociation is central because it shows that coherent responding is not necessarily construct-relevant responding. Average format differences were smaller than the effects of design conditions. Across all conditions, single-stimulus ratings showed the highest mean benchmark recovery, GPC responses were close behind, and binary paired choices recovered less information. The average benchmark correlations were .493 for single-stimulus ratings, .477 for GPC responses, and .447 for binary paired choice. GPC responses also retained more information than binary choices in favorable regions of the design space, supporting the value of graded preference strength over dichotomized choice. The single-stimulus advantage should be read cautiously because the single-stimulus condition observed two ratings per pair and was an informational reference rather than a burden-equated comparison. Table 2 summarizes the core contrasts and shows that average format differences were modest relative to changes associated with saturation, validity, nuisance variance, length, and pair construction.

Metamodels quantified the same pattern. For benchmark recovery across all response formats, sequential partial eta squared was .812 for construct saturation, .702 for stimulus validity, .661 for pair construction, .648 for nuisance strength, and .445 for test length, compared with .089 for response format. In GPC condition means, the full metamodel explained 96.1% of the variation in recovery; eta squared was .359 for construct saturation, .203 for stimulus validity, .165 for pair construction, .159 for nuisance strength, and .063 for test length. Full metamodel tables are reported in the supplemental materials. The focal simulations supported the same interpretation after decoupling several dependencies in the main design. Target saturation, stimulus loading, nuisance contrast, judgment noise, pair construction, and length each contributed to recovery. Stable nuisance contrast was more damaging than random judgment noise in the tested focal design. The threshold-heterogeneity sensitivity check did not change the qualitative ordering of conclusions across jitter levels. Taken together, these results locate recovery in the information preserved by a particular benchmark, stimulus set, pair design, response format, and test length.

Study 1 Discussion

Study 1 shows why ultra-short GPC assessment should be evaluated as an information-recovery problem. The response format mattered, but the design conditions mattered more. Three GPC pairs can recover benchmark information when the target construct is focused, stimuli are strongly aligned with the target, pair construction is balanced, and nuisance variance is low. The same format can fail when those conditions are absent. The most consequential warning is that internal consistency can increase for the wrong reason: stable nuisance variance can make responses coherent while pulling them away from the target benchmark.

Simulation results alone cannot establish whether real image-based implementations occupy favorable or unfavorable regions of this design space. Study 2 therefore examines two three-pair image-GPC implementations using the same response format and scoring framework but different construct domains. The goal is not to test the causal mechanisms isolated in Study 1, but to assess whether empirical benchmark recovery shows the kind of design dependence predicted by the simulation.

Study 2 Empirical Illustration Methods

Study 2 applied the simulation framework to an empirical image-based GPC dataset administered online through Qualtrics. Eligible participants were recruited through universityrelated networks from a general participant pool aged 16 years or older. A total of 383 participants remained after initial screening, of whom 375 completed all study-measure blocks.

The primary analytic sample comprised 369 participants with complete study-measure and age information. Six additional participants had complete study-measure responses but did not report their age. Because the minimum eligibility age was 16 years and age eligibility could not be verified for these cases, they were not included in the primary analytic sample. Among the 369 participants with complete age data, the mean age was 20.28 years (SD = 5.65; range = 16-64); 239 participants identified as female, 124 as male, and 6 as another gender category.

Participants completed Likert benchmark measures and three-pair image-GPC versions of the same two constructs in a single Qualtrics session. The design was within-person. The order of the Likert and GPC blocks was randomized in the survey flow to control for order effects. No additional exclusion criteria were applied beyond duration, verified-age eligibility, and assessment-block completion criteria. All participants provided informed consent through the online procedure approved for the eligible age range, including 16-and 17-year-old participants.

The empirical study was approved by the Bioethics Commission of the Universitat de Vic-Universitat Central de Catalunya, internal code 308/2024.

State anxiety was assessed with the six-item short form of the State scale of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-6; Marteau & Bekker, 1992; Spielberger, 1983). Problematic smartphone application-based use was assessed with SABAS (Csibi et al., 2018). Although SABAS includes addiction-component content, it was developed and is widely used as a brief total-score screening measure, and later psychometric studies have supported one-factor scoring in adolescent, adult, multilingual, and multi-region samples (Chen et al., 2024; Csibi et al., 2018; Mason et al., 2022; Vujić et al., 2024). The observed Likert totals therefore served as empirical benchmarks.

For each construct, the empirical comparative task consisted of three graded image pairs. Respondents saw two images side by side and answered the prompt, "Which photo best represents your current moment?" Responses used a five-point bipolar preference scale: strongly prefer left, slightly prefer left, both equally, slightly prefer right, and strongly prefer right. Thus, the empirical task was a graded paired-comparison preference task, not a strictly binary forcedchoice task (Brown & Maydeu-Olivares, 2018; Stark et al., 2005). Figure 2 reproduces one administered STAI image-pair item to illustrate the actual stimulus layout and five-category response scale without disclosing the proprietary item set.

Candidate images were compiled from open-access image banks, primarily Pexels, using freely licensed real photographs. Across the two instruments, the initial candidate pool comprised 42 images for STAI and 41 for SABAS, reduced to six final images per measure. Four expert judges reviewed candidate images for semantic equivalence with the corresponding verbal item, clarity, naturalness, and absence of confusing visual elements. The procedure used iterative expert consensus inspired by Delphi-style item-development methods (Diamond et al., 2014; Hasson et al., 2000). Earlier qualitative response-process feedback from preliminary development phases identified stimuli that participants found unclear, too broad, or poorly matched to the intended item content; these stimuli were removed or replaced before the present data collection. The final empirical task is under active proprietary development, so one administered image-pair item is reproduced in the main manuscript and the remaining pairs are described in a supplemental stimulus-audit table.

Unidimensional ordinal factor models for the graded-preference responses were fitted separately for STAI and SABAS in Mplus using ULSMV estimation and Theta parameterization. Each observed pair response was treated as an ordinal manifestation of a latent utility difference, following Thurstonian logic for graded-preference data (Brown & Maydeu-Olivares, 2018; Maydeu-Olivares & Brown, 2010). Because the empirical task had one ordinal response per pair rather than separate utility indicators for each image, the reduced model fixed the oriented pair loadings to the keyed pattern +1, -1, +1 for both instruments. The model estimated four ordinal thresholds for each of the three five-category pair responses, yielding 12 threshold parameters and three degrees of freedom from the 12 thresholds plus three polychoric correlations. Modelderived factor scores, model-implied information, average MAP-score standard errors, and empirical factor-score reliability were compared with the Likert benchmarks.

To test whether the empirical contrast depended on the constrained ordinal factor score, we also computed keyed GPC sum scores, first principal components, pair-level benchmark associations, and descriptive multitrait-multimethod correlations (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). Supervised prediction models were used only as recovery benchmarks. Ridge, LASSO, Elastic Net, and Random Forest models predicted the corresponding Likert total from the three GPC responses under nested cross-validation, with an outer 10-fold loop for performance estimation and an inner tuning loop. Performance was summarized with out-of-sample Pearson correlations and RMSE relative to an intercept-only benchmark.

Results

Benchmark recovery showed the central empirical contrast. The STAI model-derived GPC factor score correlated .70 with the STAI-6 Likert total. Keyed GPC sums and the first principal component recovered essentially the same benchmark signal, r = .71 and .71. For SABAS, recovery was weak across the corresponding diagnostics: the model-derived GPC factor score correlated .18 with the SABAS Likert total, keyed sums correlated .17, and the first principal component correlated .16. Figure 3 displays this descriptive multitrait-multimethod pattern, showing strong cross-method recovery for STAI and weak recovery for SABAS rather than a uniform advantage or failure of the GPC format.

The contrast was robust across flexible recovery benchmarks and pair-level checks. Crossvalidated prediction from the three STAI GPC responses yielded r = .72 for ridge, r = .72 for LASSO, r = .72 for elastic net, and r = .70 for Random Forest, with RMSE values clearly below the intercept-only baseline of 4.25. For SABAS, cross-validated r values ranged only from .18 to .26, and RMSE values were only slightly below the intercept-only baseline of 5.26. Pair-level benchmark associations showed the same pattern: absolute pair-level correlations were .60, .68, and .56 for STAI, compared with .04, .07, and .21 for SABAS.

Reliability and ordinal model diagnostics helped interpret this contrast, but they did not replace benchmark recovery. The Likert benchmarks showed acceptable internal consistency: alpha and omega were .84 and .84 for STAI-6, and .71 and .72 for SABAS. The GPC response patterns diverged more sharply. STAI-GPC showed ordinal alpha = .87 and ordinal omega = .88, whereas SABAS-GPC showed ordinal alpha = .31 and ordinal omega = .32. Thus, the STAI image-pair responses formed a much more coherent keyed pattern than the SABAS image-pair responses.

The constrained ordinal models also differed, although high STAI-GPC reliability should not be interpreted as evidence of exact model fit. For STAI-GPC, the model yielded SRMR = .083, CFI = .917, TLI = .917, and RMSEA = .362, 90% CI [.313, .412]. The SRMR and incremental indices were more favorable for STAI than for SABAS, but chi-square and RMSEA indicated that exact fit should not be claimed. For SABAS-GPC, fit was clearly poorer, SRMR = .150, CFI = .000, TLI = .000, and RMSEA = .381, 90% CI [.333, .432]. This interpretation follows recent cautions that RMSEA can be unstable or misleading in models with very low degrees of freedom, making local residuals, SRMR, and substantive recovery diagnostics especially important (Kenny et al., 2015; Pavlov et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2018).

Study 2 Discussion

Study 2 illustrates the design logic from Study 1 in a deliberately constrained empirical task. The two implementations used the same nominal format, the same number of pairs, and the same broad analytic pipeline, but they preserved very different amounts of benchmark-relevant information. The STAI implementation retained a strong recoverable signal across model-derived scoring, keyed and component scores, supervised prediction, and descriptive MTMM checks. The SABAS implementation did not. This contrast should not be read as proof that state anxiety is inherently compressible or that problematic smartphone application-based use is inherently non-compressible. It shows that two same-format, same-length image-GPC implementations can generate radically different recovery profiles, without allowing the difference to be assigned causally to a single feature of the design.

The SABAS disadvantage may reflect weaker target signal, less coherent pair responses, construct underrepresentation, stronger non-target stimulus influence, or a mismatch between the current-moment image prompt and a benchmark concerning habitual smartphone application use. This ambiguity illustrates the importance of distinguishing response format, construct representation, stimulus properties, pair construction, prompt framing, and scoring when evaluating ultra-short comparative assessments.

General Discussion

This article asked which design factors determine recovery in ultra-short GPC assessment, whether graded preferences retain more target information than binary paired choices, and whether empirical three-pair image-GPC implementations targeting different construct domains exhibit distinct benchmark-recovery profiles despite sharing the same response format and test length. Three contributions follow from the two studies.

The first contribution is that recoverability is a property of the construct-stimulus-design system, not of test length or response format alone. Study 1 showed that three GPC pairs can recover benchmark information when the target construct is focused, stimulus signal is strong, nuisance variance is low, and pair construction is balanced. The same number of pairs failed when those conditions deteriorated. Study 2 showed the same logic empirically: two implementations with the same three-pair image-GPC format produced very different levels of benchmark recovery. This contribution also clarifies terminology. Binary forced choice and graded paired comparison are not interchangeable, because dichotomizing comparative responses discards preferencestrength information that can contribute to recovery (Bech et al., 2007; Brown & Maydeu-Olivares, 2018; Stark et al., 2005).

The second contribution is that reliability can be high for psychometrically undesirable reasons.

In Study 1, stronger systematic nuisance variance reduced benchmark recovery while increasing omega. This pattern is important beyond the specific GPC design studied here: a response pattern can be internally coherent because respondents are reacting consistently to construct-irrelevant stimulus features. Study 2 is consistent with the converse problem as well. The STAI implementation showed both high keyed reliability and strong benchmark recovery, whereas the SABAS implementation showed weak keyed coherence and weak recovery. Taken together, the two studies show why reliability must be interpreted alongside benchmark recovery, stimulus review, pair-level diagnostics, and local fit rather than treated as sufficient evidence that compression has succeeded.

The third contribution is that scoring methods cannot create construct information that is not represented in the responses. In Study 1, cross-validated ridge prediction tracked the same design constraints as simpler recovery indices. In Study 2, regularized linear models and Random Forests recovered the STAI benchmark well but did not rescue the SABAS implementation. Supervised prediction is useful here because it estimates recoverable benchmark information out of sample. It does not establish that the response process is valid, that stimuli represent the intended construct, or that scores are suitable for applied decisions (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014; Messick, 1995; Morris et al., 2019).

The findings translate into practical design guidance. Before committing to a three-pair GPC form, developers should verify that the benchmark is sufficiently focused, candidate stimuli align semantically with the intended item content, pairs are balanced in target direction, and the prompt matches the time frame of the benchmark. A three-pair design is most defensible when pair-level benchmark associations are consistently positive, keyed-pair reliability is not driven by an obvious non-target contrast, local residual diagnostics do not point to a single problematic pair, and cross-validated recovery improves meaningfully over an intercept-only baseline. High alpha, omega, model-implied information, or small factor-score standard errors are insufficient on their own. Increasing test length is most justified when the construct is heterogeneous, stimulus meanings cover only part of the benchmark domain, pair-level associations are uneven, nuisance features are salient, or recovery remains below the intended practical criterion despite acceptable internal consistency.

Several limitations should guide interpretation. Study 1 used a simplified one-target, onenuisance data-generating model. Real comparative assessments may involve multidimensional target constructs, multiple nuisance sources, local dependence, heterogeneous response thresholds, and person-by-stimulus interactions. The focal and threshold-sensitivity simulations reduce some concerns, but they do not exhaust plausible design failures. Study 2 was not a factorial experiment and should not be used to assign a single causal explanation to the SABAS result. Its role is empirical delimitation: it asks whether two very short image-GPC implementations recover their verbal benchmarks strongly enough to be psychometrically informative. The empirical image stimuli were developed through expert consensus and qualitative feedback, but they were not independently validated before deployment. The sample was not demographically broad, and benchmark recovery may differ across age, cultural, linguistic, and educational groups.

Future work should manipulate response format, stimulus modality, test length, and pair construction within the same empirical design. A stronger design would compare single-stimulus ratings, GPC preferences, and binary paired choices using the same stimulus pool, vary the number of pairs, and include independent ratings of stimulus construct relevance and nuisance features. Adaptive or modular comparative designs may be especially useful because not all constructs or stimulus sets should be forced into the same number of comparisons (Wang et al., 2024).

In sum, ultra-short comparative assessment should begin with a design question: does the response pattern contain enough target-relevant information to make validity plausible? When the answer is yes, a short GPC form may recover a benchmark efficiently.
