Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Similarity is fundamental to cognition, shaping both foundational and higher-order processes. Cognitive theories often adopt a geometric approach, where similarity is defined by the distance or angle between objects in a psychological space, successfully explaining many perceived similarity relations. However, the validity of such geometric similarity models has been questioned by diverse empirical challenges, such as violations of the metric axioms (minimality, symmetry, and triangle inequality), the common features effect (common features increasing similarity), contextual effects (similarity relations shifting with the presence of other objects), and nearest neighbor effects (single objects considered as most similar to many others). The present work reviews evidence showing that geometric similarity models can address many of these challenges through psychologically plausible extensions of the model formalizations. Some extensions add parameters within the standard framework, such as feature-specific attention or object-specific biases (e.g., salience or frequency). Others generalize the geometric framework itself, through similarity functions that go beyond distances or angles, or psychological spaces that represent objects as something other than points on a multidimensional map. Each extension addresses specific empirical challenges while maintaining cognitive plausibility. These findings suggest that geometric models, when appropriately extended, provide a robust foundation for understanding human similarity relations and offer a path toward comprehensive theories of higher-order cognition.

Overcoming the Empirical Challenges of Geometric Similarities: A Model-Based Review

Few concepts in cognitive science have been studied as extensively as similarity.

Similarity in one respect tends to predict similarity in others, and the human mind makes use of this correspondence to interact with the world (Goldstone & Son, 2012). For example, people can infer the toxicity of snakes based on their similarity in terms of head shape, pupil shape, and other attributes. Learning about some venomous snakes allows people to generalize and infer that other, similar snakes are venomous too-even without explicitly knowing rules such as that venomous snakes often have triangular-shaped heads and vertical pupils. Similarity underlies both perceptual and semantic cognition: it supports processes such as identification (Luce, 1963; Shepard, 1957), categorization (Kruschke, 2008; Nosofsky, 1986), and judgment (Albrecht et al., 2020; Juslin et al., 2003), but also forms the basis of semantic representations (Hintzman, 1984; Landauer & Dumais, 1997) and higher-order reasoning (Hahn & Chater, 1998), problem-solving (Holyoak & Koh, 1987), and memory (Raaijmakers & Shiffrin, 1981). Understanding how the human mind perceives similarity is therefore crucial for understanding cognition. This work examines a seminal framework of psychological similarity-the geometric similarity framework-and addresses the empirical criticisms it has faced. Specifically, I review evidence showing how psychologically plausible adjustments to formal geometric similarity models can resolve many of these criticisms.

Because similarity is fundamental to cognitive science, various computational models have sought to formalize the underlying cognitive processes (for overviews, see Goldstone & Son, 2012; Hahn, 2014). The present work focuses on geometric models, which assume that the human mind computes similarity between objects based on their locations in a multidimensional mental space (Nosofsky, 1986; Shepard, 1957 Shepard, , 1987)). These models represent each object as a vector of numeric attributes that may correspond to continuous dimensions or to discrete features, depending on how the stimulus domain is represented (Lee & Navarro, 2002). In the Euclidean space defined by these attributes (called psychological space or embedding space), an object is represented as a point whose coordinates correspond to the object's attribute values (see Figure 1a). According to this geometric framework, the locations of objects in psychological space reflects their similarity: Close objects are similar, those far apart are dissimilar. This relationship between spatial representation and similarity is well-supported (see Roads & Love, 2024) and has been successfully used to explain perceptual cognition (Donkin et al., 2015; Hebart et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2016; Juslin et al., 2003; Kruschke, 1992; Navarro & Lee, 2004; Nosofsky, 1984 Nosofsky, , 1986;; Nosofsky et al., 2011; Nosofsky & Palmeri, 1997; Sanders & Nosofsky, 2020; Shepard, 1962a Shepard, , 1962b Shepard, , 1987;; Shin & Nosofsky, 1992; von Helversen & Rieskamp, 2009) and semantic cognition (Devlin et al., 2019; Dumais et al., 1988; Hintzman, 1984 Hintzman, , 1988;; Landauer & Dumais, 1997; Lund & Burgess, 1996; Mikolov, Chen, et al., 2013; Mikolov, Sutskever, et al., 2013; Pennington et al., 2014).

Although highly influential, the geometric similarity framework has faced significant empirical challenges. Research has shown that people can systematically violate the assumptions underlying geometric similarities (e.g., Gati & Tversky, 1984; Tversky, 1977; Tversky & Gati, 1982). In his pioneering work, Tversky (1977) reported multiple empirical phenomena that contradict the core assumption of geometric similarity models, namely that similarity can be modeled within a metric psychological space. These findings have challenged the validity of the geometric framework and led to the development of alternative similarity frameworks (Goldstone, 1994; Hahn et al., 2003; Tversky, 1977), in particular Tversky's feature-matching approach according to which two objects' similarity increases with their common features but decreases with their distinctive features (see below for more details). To this day, the findings of Tversky (1977) are considered a benchmark for any comprehensive theory of similarity (Pothos et al., 2013). The present work addresses these and related challenges, demonstrating that geometric similarity models can overcome many of the criticisms leveled at them through psychologically plausible extensions. The following sections formally introduce the two main geometric similarity models, summarize key empirical challenges, and review evidence showing how these challenges can be resolved.

Overview of Geometric Similarity Models

The geometric framework represents each object I as a point in a psychological space, with coordinates corresponding to its attribute values, I = (i 1 , i 2 , . . . , i N ). Formally, the similarity of object I to object J is a function of their representations, s IJ = f (I, J).

What makes this function geometric is that it depends on I and J only through their spatial relation in psychological space: either the distance between them (distance-based similarity, s dist IJ ) or the angle between them (angle-based similarity, s cos IJ ). Throughout this article, s IJ without superscript refers to either form.

Distance-based similarity dominates in classic perceptual models of stimulus identification, recognition, and categorization (e.g., Nosofsky, 1986; Shepard, 1987), where similarity depends on attribute differences and overall magnitude-objects are more similar the closer they are in psychological space. In contrast, angle-based similarity is more common in semantic models of language and memory (e.g., Hintzman, 1984; Landauer & Dumais, 1997), which often assume high-dimensional psychological spaces where similarity depends on attribute patterns rather than magnitude. Distance-based and angle-based similarities can be seen as two sides of the same coin: Two objects seem similar either because they differ in relatively few attributes or because they share similar patterns across attributes, thus pointing in similar directions in psychological space.

Another way to quantify similarity in geometric terms-particularly popular in semantic modeling of language and concept learning (e.g., Landauer & Dumais, 1997)-is by measuring the angle between the vectors representing two objects in psychological space. The most widely used measure defines similarity as the cosine of this angle.

Formally, the cosine similarity s cos IJ between objects I and J is given by where I • J denotes the dot product of vectors I and J, ∥I∥ and ∥J∥ represent their respective Euclidean norms, and θ is the angle between I and J. Intuitively, cosine similarity measures the directional alignment of two vectors-their dot product captures how closely they point in the same direction, while normalization by their magnitudes ensures that only the angle between the vectors, not their lengths, influences similarity.

Cosine similarity can be computed directly from attribute values without explicitly calculating the angle θ first. Nevertheless, θ can be recovered via θ = arccos s cos IJ . Like distance d IJ , θ can be treated as a metric, satisfying analogous assumptions (see below).

In comparison to distance-based similarity, cosine similarity ranges from -1 (corresponding to a maximal angle of θ = 180 • ) to +1 (an angle of θ = 0 • ), reflecting opposite and identical concepts, respectively (see Fig. 1c). Overview of Empirical Challenges

By relying on the location of objects in a psychological space, geometric similarity models make a series of assumptions that have faced empirical criticism (Goldstone & Son, 2012). One key assumption is that the cognitive system represents objects as vectors of distinct attributes, each assigned a numeric value. Tversky (1977) argued that this so-called dimensional assumption may apply to some objects, like colors or geometric figures, but not to others, like countries or personalities, which might be better represented by qualitative features rather than quantitative dimensions (see the discussion for more details). Moreover, the same object can be represented using different attribute combinations (e.g., a rectangle might be defined by its length and width or by its area and shape), leaving the specific input to the similarity function ambiguous (Tversky & Krantz, 1970). The primary focus of this article, however, lies in the assumptions about how similarity is computed provided that objects can be represented as vectors of attribute values. In particular, geometric models assume metric axioms (minimality, symmetry, and triangle inequality) which restrict the possible similarity spaces (Beals et al., 1968; Krantz et al., 1971; Tversky & Krantz, 1970).

These assumptions have been questioned on empirical grounds using various methods that measure psychological similarity directly or infer it from similarity-based cognition tasks (Daggett & Hout, 2025; Tversky, 1977). Direct measures include pairwise similarity ratings, in which participants judge the similarity of two objects on a scale; free associations, in which participants list the words most similar to a reference word; and choice tasks, in which participants select the most similar stimuli from a small set (including triad tasks, in which participants select the two most similar of three stimuli, or, if one stimulus serves as a reference, which of the remaining two is more similar to it).

Indirect tasks, by contrast, infer similarity from related but distinct choice behavior: in object identification tasks, similarity is inferred from confusion patterns as participants name individual objects, and in same-different tasks, similarity is inferred from accuracy and response times as participants decide whether two stimuli are the same. The underlying idea is that objects often confused with each other or requiring long response times to discriminate are likely to be psychologically similar, whereas dissimilar objects can be told apart quickly and accurately. However, performance in such similarity-based cognition tasks may reflect both properties of similarity itself and additional task-specific processes (e.g., response biases), either of which can produce violations of the assumptions of geometric models-a distinction that recurs throughout this review.

In the following, I will define the key assumptions of geometric similarity models and summarize the empirical criticisms they have faced across various experimental methods and tasks (for a summary, see Table 1 and Figure 4) 5.

Minimality

One assumption of geometric similarity models is minimality (Beals et al., 1968): the distance (or angle) is zero between identical objects and positive between any two non-identical objects.

Formally, d IJ ≥ d II = 0 for any two distinct objects I ̸ = J (Burago et al., 2022; Deza & Deza, 2009) foot_4 . Assuming that similarity strictly decreases with increasing distance, minimality implies that two objects have maximal similarity if they are identical, s IJ ≤ s II = 1, and that self-similarity is constant across objects, s II = s JJ.

While minimality generally holds in direct similarity ratings (e.g., Melara, 1989; Seitz, 2023), it faces some challenges in similarity-based cognition tasks (Keren & Baggen, 1981; Tversky, 1977). In an identification task, Gilmore et al. (1979) found for example that briefly presented "M" and "W" were more often misidentified as "H" (39% and 35%) than correctly identified (11% and 18%), which, if confusions are taken to reflect similarity, implies that their perceived similarity to "H" exceeded their self-similarity (for similar evidence from geometrical figures, see e.g., Nosofsky, 1989). In a same-different task variant, Podgorny and Garner (1979) found that, consistent with minimality, two letters were judged as belonging to the same predefined group more quickly the larger their similarity, with identical letters yielding the fastest responses overall. Yet, notable challenges to minimality still emerged: Some identical letter pairs were judged faster than others (for instance, responses to an "O" pair were about 50 ms faster than to a "W" pair)-a pattern that could reflect either processing difficulty or genuine differences in self-similarity between letters. Moreover, participants even reacted faster to some non-identical letter pairs than to some identical ones (e.g., the pair "C" and "O" was 5 The assumptions are formalized for distances and extend directly to angles between normalized vectors.

Symmetry

Another assumption of geometric similarity models is symmetry (Beals et al., 1968): the distance (or angle) between objects is non-directional, formally d IJ = d JI , directly implying s IJ = s JI .

Symmetry reflects insensitivity to the direction of comparison-the same psychological similarity is formed irrespective of which of two objects serves as subject or referent. Geometric similarity models thus do not differentiate between "the similarity of I to J", "the similarity of J to I", and "the similarity between I and J" (Tversky, 1977).

Although symmetry is reasonable in many situations (Tversky & Gati, 1982), psychological similarity can be asymmetric, be it in pairwise ratings, free associations, or similarity-based cognition tasks. Tversky (1977) argued that many similarity statements are directional, as when a variant is similar to a prototype but not vice versa (e.g., it feels more intuitive to say an ellipse is like a circle than the reverse; see also Asch and Ebenholtz, 1962). Relatedly, participants tend to judge a less prominent object as more similar to a more prominent one than vice versa, both for semantic material (e.g., country names, numbers) and perceptual material (e.g., geometrical figures, colors): For example, rarely presented colors are judged more similar to more frequent ones than the reverse (Polk et al., 2002), and low-intensity colors are judged more similar to high-intensity ones (Epping et al., 2023; Rosch, 1975). Free associations are one of the classic sources of asymmetry data (Nelson et al., 2004). By some estimates, over 85% of word pairs exhibit asymmetric association strengths, with frequent words such as "cat" named as an associate of rarer words like "meow" more often than the reverse (Griffiths et al., 2007). Confusions in identification tasks are often asymmetric as well: In the letter study described above, "M" and "W" were mistaken for "H" in more than a third of cases, but "H" for "M" and "W" in only 6% and 9% of cases (Gilmore et al., 1979), and when one letter includes another (e.g., "F" and "E"), the included letter was mistaken for the including letter more often than the reverse (Tversky, 1977). Similar asymmetries have been reported for faces, with less extreme emotional expressions more often mistaken for more extreme ones than vice versa (Roberson et al., 2007; but see Best and Goldstone, 2019). Taken together, asymmetries-unlike violations of minimality-emerge not only in similarity-based cognition tasks but also in direct similarity ratings and free associations, suggesting that they are not merely a by-product of identification processes.

Triangle Inequality and Corner Inequality

The third metric axiom of geometric similarity models is triangle inequality (Tversky & Gati, 1982): the direct distance between two objects I and J is no greater than their indirect distance via a third object K, formally d IJ ≤ d IK + d KJ (see Fig. 3a).

Testing triangle inequality empirically has proven difficult (Jäkel et al., 2008; Tversky & Gati, 1982): Summing distances across object pairs is meaningless if distances are conceptualized as ordinal scales, and even when treated as interval-scaled, triangle inequality can be trivially satisfied by adding a sufficiently large constant x to all distances (i.e., d IJ + x ≤ d IK + x + d KJ + x for x ≫ 0). To address these limitations, Tversky and Gati (1982) introduced the related corner inequality. In the typical configuration (Figure 3b), I and J occupy opposite corners of a rectangular two-dimensional space, K matches both on a different dimension (forming a corner), whereas a fourth object C lies at a point within the rectangle (e.g., the midpoint between I and J). Corner inequality states that the path between I and J via the corner point K should be at least as long as the path via the interior point C:

Independence of Common Features

Beyond the metric axioms, geometric similarity models assume independence of common features (Gati & Tversky, 1984): a common feature contributes no difference between objects and thus does not affect the Minkowski distance (Eq. 1) or the angle underlying cosine similarity. Formally, d IJ = d I + J + , where + denotes an additional feature shared by both I and J, which translates to s IJ = s I + J + . This assumption implies that geometric models are sensitive only to differences, which increase distance or angle in psychological space and thereby decrease similarity (Table 9.1 in Kruschke, 2008).

Contrary to this assumption, pairwise rating tasks clearly show that common features increase psychological similarity (Gati & Tversky, 1984; Lee & Navarro, 2002; Navarro & Lee, 2002b , 2004; Ritov et al., 1990; Seitz, 2023). Gati and Tversky (1984) found across multiple studies that adding a common feature to verbal stimulus pairs (e.g., a common hobby in two person descriptions) increased similarity ratings by about 4 points on a 20-point scale. For visual stimuli (e.g., the same glasses in two schematic face drawings), the rating increased only by about 1.5 points, possibly reflecting the higher cohesiveness of visual compared to verbal materials (Ritov et al., 1990). Moreover, some features increase similarity when shared but do not decrease it when they differ (Navarro & Lee, 2002b)-for example, learning that two people are twins may increase their perceived similarity, but learning they are not twins need not decrease it. Similarly, Navarro and Lee (2004) collected similarity ratings for pairs of cartoon faces that varied in hairstyle, hair color, and the shape and color of glasses. The feature representation that best accounted for the ratings included a common feature for "unremarkable" faces with conventional hair colors and glasses: Two unremarkable faces gained similarity from sharing this feature, whereas being unusual did not make two faces more similar, because faces could be unusual in many different ways. Beyond direct similarity ratings, common features can also affect higher-order cognition: When deciding which of two objects leads to an event, an additional common feature can increase task difficulty (Galesic et al., 2018; Verguts et al., 2004; M. E. Young & Wasserman, 2002), presumably by drawing attention away from diagnostic distinctive features and thereby increasing the objects' perceived similarity.

Contextual Independence

Geometric similarity models also assume contextual independence (Tversky, 1977): the distance (or angle) between two objects is unaffected by the presence of other objects, formally d IJ = d IJ|K (and thus s IJ = s IJ|K ) for any third presented object K. Contextual independence implies stable similarity orderings across contexts: if s IJ > s IK , this ordering should hold even when a distractor D is added, s IJ|D > s IK|D .

This assumption resembles the independence of irrelevant alternatives principle in multi-alternative choice theory, whereby relative preference between two options is unaffected by other options in the choice set (Rieskamp et al., 2006; Tversky, 1972; Tversky & Russo, 1969).

Nearest Neighbor Bound

Finally, geometric similarity models assume an upper bound for the number of objects that can share the same object as their nearest neighbor (Tversky & Hutchinson, 1986). I's nearest neighbor is defined as the object with the smallest distance to I.

is geometrically impossible that an arbitrarily large number of objects share the same nearest neighbor in a space of limited dimensionality. Known in geometry as the kissing number problem, the maximal number of objects with the same nearest neighbor is still unknown for spaces of various dimensionalities (Jenssen et al., 2018; Musin, 2008; Odlyzko & Sloane, 1979), but for stimuli with one to three dimensions-commonly used in laboratory psychological research (e.g., Ashby & Maddox, 1992; Seitz et al., 2023; Seitz et al., 2026)-it is 2, 6, and 12, respectively, assuming a Euclidean distance. For instance, in a two-dimensional space, at most six objects (corresponding to the vertices of a regular hexagon) can share the same nearest neighbor (the hexagon's center).

Empirical evidence, however, suggests that an object can be the nearest neighbor for more objects than is geometrically possible (Tversky & Hutchinson, 1986; Verheyen et al., 2022). Tversky and Hutchinson (1986) analyzed participants' similarity relations resulting from various experimental tasks (including similarity judgments, free associations, and identifications) across various perceptual and conceptual domains (e.g., colors, sounds, animal names, and sport names). For each data set, they calculated a centrality value, indicating how often one object served as the nearest neighbor for all other objects in the set; in the geometric model, this value cannot exceed 2 (see Newman & Rinott, 1985; Tversky et al., 1983). Centrality values for perceptual stimuli generally fell within this admissible range (M = 1.76, SD = 0.62 across sets; see also Verheyen et al., 2022), but often exceeded it for conceptual stimuli (M = 3.57, SD = 3.06). In particular, generic terms serve as the nearest neighbor for most instances-for instance, in one data set of fruit items, "fruit" was the nearest neighbor for every fruit item except "olive" and "lemon" (Rosch, 1978; Rosch & Mervis, 1975). These findings align with broader difficulties representing pairwise similarity judgments for conceptual objects within a geometric framework (Dry & Storms, 2009). Using free-association data, Griffiths et al. (2007) argued that power-law distributions in the number of word meanings imply that a few highly polysemous words must be near neighbors of many other words-a pattern difficult to accommodate even in high-dimensional spaces (Steyvers & Tenenbaum, 2005). Relatedly, Hodgetts and Hahn (2025) showed that people incorporate non-Euclidean aspects, such as swapping object features, into their nearest-neighbor judgments, quickly making psychological similarities impossible to embed in a low-dimensional Euclidean space.

His featural framework posits that two objects' common features increase their similarity, whereas each object's distinctive features (i.e., features present in one object but not the other) decrease similarity. Tversky (1977) formalized two main models in this framework: the contrast model, which computes a weighted difference between common and distinctive features, and the ratio model, which computes the proportion of common features relative to the total number of common and distinctive features.

These formulations naturally account for several key violations of geometric axioms.

For example, asymmetry arises because the distinctive features of each object can be weighted differently, making similarity directional. Specifically, by assuming that the mind weights distinctive features of the subject of comparison (i.e., I in "the similarity of I to J") more strongly than those of the object of comparison (i.e., J in the example above), the featural framework can explain why less salient items with fewer distinctive features are judged more similar to salient, distinctive items than vice versa. The trade-off between common and distinctive features can also account for violations of the nearest neighbor bound: A prototypical or superordinate object tends to have few distinctive features of its own while sharing many common features with its instances, allowing it to attain high similarity to a large number of instances simultaneously-unconstrained by the dimensionality limits that bound geometric models (Tversky & Hutchinson, 1986).

Furthermore, the common features effect is explained straightforwardly by the featural framework, in which similarity increases with the number of common features.

Tversky showed that this relationship can be predicted from the features people list, in particular when weighting features by their frequency of mention. People also tend to weight common features more when judging similarity and distinctive features more when judging difference, questioning the extent to which similarity and difference judgments are exact opposites. Moreover, Tversky (1977) also showed that feature weighting is context-dependent, shifting with the set of presented objects, making features become more or less diagnostic for a task. As a result, similarity is not fixed but can change with context and violate triangle inequality (Tversky & Gati, 1982).

The work of Tversky demonstrated that many empirical phenomena can be captured by adopting a different, feature-based representation, in which feature matching (i.e., identifying two objects' common and distinctive features) determines similarity. An alternative approach, however, is to retain geometric representations while extending them to accommodate the empirical limitations reviewed above. In the following sections, I review how geometric similarity models can address these limitations through psychologically plausible modifications to their formalization. These extensions help bridge the gap between theoretical assumptions and empirical findings, offering a refined framework for understanding psychological similarity.

Extending the Geometric Similarity Model

To overcome the empirical challenges of geometric similarity models, psychological research has proposed a number of extensions to its formalizations. These extensions aim not only to account for violations of the assumptions described above but also to be psychologically plausible and cognitively interpretable while remaining in a geometric framework. This review discusses four model extensions: attribute-specific weights, reflecting how attention is distributed across attributes when objects are compared; object-specific biases, capturing that some objects exert greater influence on similarity-based cognition, for instance due to their salience, frequency, or typicality; generalized similarity functions, which incorporate geometric aspects beyond distances or angles in similarity computation; and generalized psychological spaces, which represent objects in ways beyond points in a multidimensional map.

Attribute-Specific Weight Parameters

Some empirical challenges of geometric similarity models can be addressed by introducing a vector of attribute-specific parameters w = (w 1 , . . . , w N ) that weight the contribution of individual attributes to similarity. In distance-based similarities, the weights are multiplied with the attribute differences between objects (Eq. 1), yielding the weighted Minkowski distance (Carroll & Chang, 1970) where w n denotes the weight assigned to the difference | i n -j n | of attribute n (with0 ≤ w n ≤ 1 and n w n = 1).

Constraining the weights w to sum to 1 ensures model identifiability; without this constraint, w could trade off perfectly with the sensitivity parameter c in Eq. 2, such that different parametrizations of w and c would be observationally equivalent (see Seitz, 2023). An analogous weighted formulation can be defined for the angle-based cosine similarity and for the dot product (see Richie & Bhatia, 2021), showing that attribute weighting is an integral part of geometric similarities.

Weighting is not specific to geometric models: Tversky's (1977) featural framework likewise weights each feature by its salience and weights common against distinctive features. In fact, these weights are what allow the contrast model to produce asymmetries-similarity becomes directional only if the distinctive features of the subject and the object of comparison are weighted differently and differ in salience.

From a psychological perspective, these attribute weights are typically interpreted as reflecting how attention is distributed across attributes when objects are compared (Nosofsky, 2011; Shepard, 1964). Shepard (1964) argued that judged similarity depends on which attributes people attend to, and individual-differences scaling captures that different people weight the same attributes differently (Carroll & Chang, 1970). Geometrically, attention corresponds to stretching the psychological space along attended attributes and compressing it along unattended ones, which can change which objects are most similar (see Figure 5a). Moreover, the weights for the same objects can change with cognitive constraints such as time pressure (Lamberts & Brockdorff, 1997; Seitz et al., 2023; Wills et al., 2015) and with the task: Nosofsky (1986) found that categorization required different weights than the identification of the same stimuli, reflecting people's tendency to prioritize attributes that are diagnostic for the task at hand (Nosofsky, 1984; Tversky & Gati, 1982). For instance, Nosofsky (1989) found that when two categories are separated by a single attribute, people focus almost exclusively on that attribute and largely ignore the remaining ones, facilitating categorization near the category boundary (see Figure 5b; for eye-tracking evidence, see Rehder & Hoffman, 2005).

In contrast, when category membership depends on multiple attributes, attention is typically distributed more uniformly across them (but see Lee & Wetzels, 2010). Moreover, attention may trade off with variance, such that attributes with lower variance-where finer discrimination is required-receive greater weight than more variable attributes.

Attention weights play an analogous role in other domains: Exemplar models of multiple-cue judgment use the same weighted similarity to infer continuous criteria from the most similar previously encountered objects (Albrecht et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2016; Juslin et al., 2003), and in semantic domains, weighting the dimensions of word representations substantially improves predictions of similarity judgments between words (Richie & Bhatia, 2021). Moreover, attention weights can help explain violations of the assumptions underlying geometric models: In their standard form, attention weights preserve the metric properties of the space (see below); however, they provide a general mechanism for understanding the effect of common features and context on similarity.

Empirical implications.

Attention mechanisms can explain the similarity-increasing effect of common features (Seitz, 2023). Because the weights in Eq. 6 sum to 1, an additional attribute-common or not-draws attention away from the existing attributes and thus reduces the weight applied to their differences.

If the additional attribute is a common feature, the objects' distance decreases and their similarity increases. For example, two objects differing by one unit on a single attribute have a distance of d IJ = 1; once a common feature is added and attention is distributed uniformly, d IJ = .5 • 1 + .5 •0 = .5. The more attention the common feature attracts, the stronger the effect. This mechanism can account for the common features effects found by Gati and Tversky (1984) and in a similarity judgment study with geometric figures (Seitz, 2023), in both of which the common features gained psychological significance because they were unique to certain pairs of objects. Two implications follow. First, the account turns the sum-to-one constraint, introduced above for identifiability, into a substantive psychological assumption: Attention is a limited resource, so attending to a new attribute comes at the expense of the others. Second, unlike the contrast model, in which a common feature adds a constant amount of similarity, the account predicts that the common features effect depends on how much the objects otherwise differ-a so far untested way to distinguish the two accounts. Thus, while common features can enhance similarity directly, as Tversky argued, they can also do so indirectly by diverting attention away from differing attributes.

Attention mechanisms can also account for the extralist feature effect (Osth et al., 2023), a context effect in recognition memory previously considered incompatible with geometric similarity models (Johns & Mewhort, 2002; Mewhort & Johns, 2000 , 2005).

According to the extralist feature effect, an object is more easily recognized as new if it contains unique "extralist" features absent from all old objects. For instance, a green star is easily recognized as new when the old objects are a green cross and a green heart (i.e., the star shape becomes the extralist feature), but not when the old objects are a green cross and a blue star (where each feature of the green star matches one old object). In Mewhort and Johns (2000), participants were more accurate (93% vs. 82%) and faster (M = 1.61 s vs M = 2.33 s) at recognizing new geometric figures with two features when one feature matched two old objects and the other feature was extralist, compared to when each feature matched one old object. This effect is incompatible with standard geometric models, which predict identical similarity relations when the number of differing features remains constant (i.e., the green star differs from each old object in one feature). Osth et al. (2023), however, explained the effect using the attention-based mechanism of Eq. 6.

Their geometric similarity model dynamically shifted attention across trials to attributes with unexpected values in the context of the old objects, reducing the similarity between an object with extralist features and the old objects. As a result, objects with an extralist feature were more easily recognized as new. The model successfully predicted both accuracy rates and response times across multiple recognition experiments using various stimulus materials. Notably, the extralist feature effect emerged for separable attributes but not for integral attributes, likely because allocating attention to individual attributes is more challenging in the latter case.

Implications for metric structure. So far, attention weights have been assumed to change at most between contexts-for instance, across trials, as in the extralist feature effect-but to remain constant within a context, such that all comparisons within a trial use the same weights regardless of which objects are compared.

This raises a more general question: At what grain does attention vary? It may be a stable property of a person (Carroll & Chang, 1970), shift across trials or contexts (Osth et al., 2023), or even change from one comparison to the next within a trial. As long as attention is constant within a context, the weighted Minkowski distance satisfies all metric axioms: it is minimal for identical objects, symmetric, and satisfies triangle inequality. However, relaxing this assumption by allowing attention to vary across comparisons within a context has important implications for the model's metric structure, permitting violations of symmetry and triangle inequality (but not minimality; Micko & Fischer, 1970; Shepard, 1964). First, attention weights may vary across individual objects-that is, attributes are weighted differently depending on which object of a specific pair serves as the subject of comparison.

When comparing objects I and J, the salient attributes of either the subject or the object of comparison may receive greater weight depending on the direction of the comparison, causing s IJ ̸ = s JI and thus violating symmetry. An example for such a geometric similarity model with stimulus-specific weights is the general Euclidean model (F. W. Young, 1981).

Second, attention weights may vary across object pairs within a context-that is, the attributes weighted most heavily when comparing objects I and J may differ from those weighted most heavily when comparing J and K (Goldstone et al., 1997). This pair-specific attention can produce triangle inequality violations (Jäkel et al., 2008; Tversky & Gati, 1982): In Tversky's example, geographical attributes may dominate the comparison of Jamaica and Cuba and political attributes the comparison of Cuba and Russia, leaving Jamaica and Russia dissimilar. Likewise, if attention shifts toward the attributes that best discriminate among the options in a choice set, the model can capture the diagnosticity effect (Goldstone et al., 1997): Geographical latitude is diagnostic in the set {Hungary, Sweden, Norway}, making Austria most similar to Hungary, whereas political alignment becomes diagnostic in the set {Hungary, Sweden, Poland}, making Austria most similar to Sweden. However, this account remains informal, as it does not specify how attributes become diagnostic in a given context or how attention is reallocated as the choice set changes. One candidate is a mechanism analogous to the one proposed for the extralist feature effect (Osth et al., 2023): Attention may be drawn to attributes that most clearly divide the presented objects into groups (cf. the diagnosticity principle; Tversky, 1977), which would favor latitude in the first set and political alignment in the second.

Evidence for such fine-grained attention comes mainly from categorization, where attention was found to vary across regions of the attribute space (Aha & Goldstone, 1992; Matsuka & Corter, 2008; Nosofsky & Hu, 2023) and even across individual stimuli (Blair et al., 2009; Rodrigues & Murre, 2007; Sakamoto et al., 2004). For example, a geometric similarity model with stimulus-specific attention weights explained why participants classified transfer stimuli according to a learned unidimensional rule even when these were geometrically closest to exception items: The model attended to the rule attribute when comparing transfer stimuli with rule-consistent stimuli but to all attributes when comparing them with exception stimuli (Rodrigues & Murre, 2007).

Summary. In summary, attribute-specific attention parameters have a long-standing tradition in geometric similarity models and provide a unifying account for the common features effect (Seitz, 2023), contextual effects (e.g., Osth et al., 2023), and certain violations of metric axioms (Micko & Fischer, 1970). A key avenue for future research is to formalize how attention is shifted across and within trials, for instance by extending the mechanism that directs attention to extralist features (Osth et al., 2023) to common or diagnostic attributes. One challenge will be to determine which out of the potentially infinite number of attributes that are shared or that can distinguish between objects will receive attention (Pothos et al., 2013).

Finally, object biases can explain how an object becomes the nearest neighbor of more other objects than is geometrically possible in a low-dimensional space (Nosofsky, 1991; Tversky & Hutchinson, 1986). If nearest-neighbor judgments follow Eq. 7, the probability Pr(J|I) of stating that object J is the nearest neighbor of object I depends on both similarities and biases. Large biases can then outweigh geometric similarities: although object I may be closest to K in psychological space, its nearest neighbor may instead be an object J with a sufficiently large bias.

If J's bias is large enough, any number of objects can share J as their nearest neighbor, beyond the geometric bound. This mechanism helps explain why generic terms (e.g., fruit) often serve as nearest neighbors for many of their instances (e.g., apple, banana; see Tversky & Hutchinson, 1986).

Conceptually, generic terms can be seen as highly "attracting", large-bias points (analogous to strong magnets), located centrally in psychological space, allowing them to dominate nearest-neighbor relations despite geometric constraints (Nosofsky, 1991).

Like the similarity choice model, the distance-density model predicts minimality and symmetry violations (Krumhansl, 1978), and accounts for contextual effects such as Tversky's (1977) diagnosticity principle: including a distractor D similar to option J increases J's density, which in turn makes J feel less similar to the reference object I. Despite these parallels, Nosofsky (1991) identified an important theoretical divergence between the distance-density model and Tversky's (1977) contrast model regarding the joint predictions for asymmetric similarities and self-similarities. Assuming α > β to reflect more weight on the subject of the comparison, p IJ > p JI implies p JJ > p II for the contrast model (object J has more distinct, salient features and thus a higher self-similarity than I).

In Krumhansl's model, the opposite holds: p IJ > p JI implies p II > p JJ (object J lies in a denser region of psychological space, is harder to individuate and thus has a lower self-similarity than I). This divergence may be resolved if different object-specific biases underlie asymmetries in different paradigms: feature salience in similarity judgments, as in Tversky's model, but spatial density in similarity-based cognition such as same-different judgments and identification, as in Krumhansl's model (Nosofsky, 1987 (Nosofsky, , 1991)). Although the evidence for this dissociation remains limited, it suggests that the appropriate bias mechanism differs across paradigms (Corter, 1987).

Summary.

In sum, biases can capture psychological properties of individual objects (e.g., their frequency, typicality, salience, or density). Incorporating bias terms allows geometric similarity models to account for violations of minimality, symmetry, and nearest neighbor relations (Krumhansl, 1978; Nosofsky, 1991) and reveals strong conceptual links to featural approaches to similarity (cf. Generalized Similarity Functions

The extensions discussed so far keep the geometric similarity function and add parameters to it: Attention weights rescale the psychological space, and biases enter at the level of similarity-based cognition. A third extension modifies the similarity function itself, generalizing it to functions with weaker mathematical assumptions. Tversky's contrast model could also be counted among them, but as shown above, it can equally be expressed as a symmetric similarity plus object biases, and it departs from geometric models mainly in its representation (feature sets rather than points; see General Discussion). This section therefore focuses on functions directly derived from Euclidean distances, which remain among the most widely used similarity models in both psychology and machine learning.

One simple approach is to use the dot product between vectors, which links Euclidean distances and cosine similarity but allows for varying self-similarities: Because the dot product of a vector with itself equals its squared length, objects with different vector lengths can differ in self-similarity, predicting violations of minimality. For example, objects with large attribute values are predicted to exhibit higher self-similarity than more moderate objects, akin to increased salience. Taken as distances themselves, however, squared Euclidean distances do not always satisfy triangle inequality. For instance, the Euclidean distances d IJ = 3, d IK = 2, and d KJ = 2 satisfy triangle inequality, but their squares do not: d 2 IK + d 2 KJ = 8 <9 = d 2 IJ.

Because squaring penalizes large distances disproportionately, splitting a long path into two shorter segments reduces the total squared length-resulting in triangle inequality violations whenever K lies within the sphere that has I and J as endpoints of its diameter.

Squared Euclidean distances cannot, however, account for violations of corner inequality (see Figure 3b). If K forms a right angle with I and J, the squared distances via K add up exactly to d 2 IJ , whereas the squared distances via a point C on the line between I and J always add up to less. For the midpoint C, for example, the squares d 2 IC and d 2 CJ cover only half the area of d 2 IJ (Figure 3c). Hence, fundamentally, squaring is an increasing transformation of the Euclidean distance and thus leaves all ordinal relations between distances unchanged.

Because the ordinal corner inequality violations reported by Tversky and Gati (1982) are invariant to such transformations, they are incompatible with Euclidean and squared Euclidean distances alike. Accounting for them requires that differences on separable attributes combine in a subadditive way, such that two moderate differences outweigh one large difference-as, for example, in Minkowski distances with r < 1, in pair-specific attention weights, or in the generalized psychological spaces discussed below (Jäkel et al., 2008; Tversky & Gati, 1982).

Mahalanobis distance.

The squared Euclidean distance can be further generalized to the Mahalanobis distance, which is widely used in machine learning (e.g., Weinberger & Saul, 2009) and increasingly applied in psychological research (Annis et al., 2021; Battleday et al., 2020; Seitz et al., 2026). Unlike the squared Euclidean distance, the Mahalanobis distance incorporates variability information about the distribution from which the compared objects originate. Intuitively, it measures the number of standardized units by which one object differs from another within a given category distribution.

Σ J contains in its main diagonal the attribute variances and in its off-diagonal cells the pairwise attribute covariances (i.e., the unstandardized correlations) in category J . By using the inverse of the matrix, the Mahalanobis distance divides the attribute differences by the attribute variances and covariances. Variances and covariances affect the Mahalanobis distance in distinct ways (see Figure 7). Larger attribute variances lead to smaller distances, as the objects are considered fewer standardized units apart. Stronger correlations reduce the Mahalanobis distance when the differences between the objects align with the correlations but increase it when differences and correlations do not align. Furthermore, the Mahalanobis distance can, in principle, predict asymmetric similarities. Note that in Eq. 13, it is the variance-covariance matrix of the referent of the comparison (i.e., the category of J in "the similarity of I to J") that enters the computation. This contrasts with Tversky's (1977) contrast model and Krumhansl's (1978) distance-density model, in which the features or density of the subject of the comparison (i.e., I) typically receive more weight. When objects I ∈ I and J ∈ J belong to different categories, d IJ ̸ = d JI in general because the variance-covariance matrices Σ I and Σ J differ.

Specifically, if category J has larger attribute variances than category I, comparing I to J leads to the shorter distance, d IJ < d JI . Psychologically, this reflects that I appears relatively similar to J, as J stems from category J known to vary a lot, whereas J appears relatively dissimilar to I, as I stems from category I known to vary little. Correlations can also lead to asymmetries. If two categories differ in the strength of their attribute correlations, then the similarity between two objects, one from each category, is generally asymmetric. Even if two categories have attribute correlations of identical strength, asymmetric similarities result if the correlations differ in direction (i.e., a positive correlation in one category and a negative correlation in the other). If the same variance-covariance matrix applies to both objects, their similarity is always symmetric.

Admittedly, this account of asymmetry requires that the compared objects are evaluated against different reference distributions. This may be common, however, as objects are typically encountered as instances of broader categories whose variability can enter the comparison. A weight difference of 10 kg, for instance, seems unremarkable between two dogs but enormous between two cats, because dogs vary far more in size.

Accordingly, the account predicts that a wolf appears more similar to a dog-a referent from a highly variable category-than a dog appears to a wolf. Whether people indeed scale differences by the variability associated with the referent when judging similarity remains to be tested; so far, evidence for this mechanism comes mainly from categorization.

Note that the Mahalanobis distance is also closely related to general recognition theory, a leading categorization framework that classifies objects by means of decision boundaries rather than similarities (Ashby & Maddox, 1993). Human decision boundaries often closely align with the optimal boundaries that maximize categorization accuracy. When categories are represented as multivariate normal distributions, these optimal boundaries follow from comparing the likelihoods of an object under each category, which decrease with the object's Mahalanobis distance from the category centroids (Ashby & Maddox, 1992).

Summary.

In sum, generalized distance functions make less strong assumptions and can notably predict violations of the metric axioms (Jäkel et al., 2008) and contextual effects (e.g., Seitz et al., 2026). Future research could also consider further generalizations such as the Kullback-Leibler divergence, which computes distance as the difference between two probability distributions. The Kullback-Leibler divergence might model similarity relations when objects do not correspond to points in psychological space but to distributions, for instance due to the presence of perceptual noise (cf. Alfonso-Reese, 2001; & Lee, 1993; Ashby & Perrin, 1988; Seitz et al., 2025). Such perceptual noise may also explain differences in self-similarity, as more variable representations are less similar to themselves (Ennis et al., 1988).

Generalized Psychological Spaces

A final extension involves generalized geometric spaces, which move beyond the usual Euclidean representation. Instead of representing objects as points in a standard metric space, generalized spaces allow for more flexible representations. This flexibility can be especially useful for modeling psychological phenomena such as asymmetries, contextual effects, and nearest neighbor effects. One approach discussed in the literature is the use of Hilbert spaces (Jäkel et al., 2008), a geometric space that provides the basis for a quantum geometric model of similarity (Pothos et al., 2013).

Hilbert space.

A Hilbert space is a generalized space that extends the familiar Euclidean representation to infinite dimensions while preserving key geometric properties like inner products (angles), norms (lengths), and metrics (distances; Jäkel et al., 2007). Jäkel et al. (2008) showed that the similarity function of Eqs. 1 and 2 with p = r can define an inner product in a Hilbert space (i.e., the similarity between two objects is equivalent to the inner product of their representations in the Hilbert space). This property can be used to place the objects in Hilbert space based on their similarities in Euclidean space. In Hilbert space, all objects are normalized to have the same length, so they lie on the surface of a unit sphere (of potentially infinite dimensionality). Geometrically, the distance between two objects on the unit sphere can be interpreted as the chord uniting them (i.e., going through the sphere and not along its surface, see Figure 8). A psychologically interesting property of this distance metric is its upper bound at √ 2 when d IJ in Euclidean space approaches infinity. This reflects the idea that two very different objects cannot become much more different anymore psychologically-at some point, they are just totally different, represented by the bounded unit sphere of Hilbert space. One crucial property of Hilbert spaces is that they fulfill the metric axioms but not segmental additivity (d IJ = d IC + d CJ if C is located on the straight line connecting I and J in Euclidean space; Beals et al., 1968; Jäkel et al., 2008). Because all objects lie on a unit sphere, the distance between two objects is measured directly via the chord through the sphere, bypassing any third object C: Even if C lies on the straight line connecting I and J in Euclidean space, dIJ < dIC + dCJ (see Figure 8). The color circle illustrates this principle: Monochromatic lights, defined by a single dimension (wavelength), are mapped to a circle in two-dimensional space, and the distance between two colors is given by the chord joining them, bypassing any intermediate light (Beals et al., 1968; Shepard, 1980).

The lack of segmental additivity has direct implications for corner inequality: Interval-level violations ( dIC + dCJ > dIK + dKJ ) do not contradict the metric axioms in Hilbert space.

Because Eq. 14 is an increasing function of d IJ , however, ordinal corner inequality violations additionally require that attribute differences combine subadditively, as is the case for r < 1 (Jäkel et al., 2008). Jäkel et al. (2008) and Tversky and Gati (1982) describe instances of Minkowski distances with similar properties as Eq. 14: Specifically, Eq. 1 with r < 1 and p = r satisfies triangle inequality but not segmental additivity, whereas r < 1 and p = 1 satisfies segmental additivity but not triangle inequality.

Because p does not change the ordering of distances, both versions produce the same ordinal corner inequality violations-which can thus be attributed to a failure of either segmental additivity or triangle inequality.

Quantum model of geometric similarity.

In recent years, Hilbert spaces have been used to develop the quantum model of geometric similarity (Pothos et al., 2013). A key difference to the traditional geometric model is that objects are not interpreted as individual points in space but as subspaces of varying dimensionality. Each dimension of such a subspace denotes a set of features associated with the respective object.

Accordingly, the more one knows about an object, the greater the dimensionality of the subspace representing the object in Hilbert space. If different objects share some features, their subspaces in Hilbert space will overlap. At any moment in time, a person's thoughts can be represented by a unit-length state vector; the degree of correspondence between the state vector and a specific object I can then be measured by projecting the state vector onto the object's subspace. Geometrically, projection means that the perpendicular is dropped from the state vector onto the subspace. This corresponds to computing the part of the state vector contained in the subspace, which is mathematically achieved by multiplying the outer product II ⊤ of the subspace dimensions I with the state vector X (that is, II ⊤ X) 9 . The quantum similarity between two objects I and J is then based on the subsequent projection of the state vector X to the subspace of the first object I and from there to the subspace of the second object J. Specifically, the quantum similarity between I and J equals the squared length of the subsequent projections, JJ ⊤ II ⊤ X 2.

Note that this measure of similarity is a squared length: In the simplest case of one-dimensional subspaces, it equals the product of the squared cosines between the state vector and I and between I and J, and thus generalizes cosine similarity. It can be interpreted as the probability of thinking about one object from the perspective of another object (Pothos et al., 2013; Trueblood et al., 2014).

In addition to triangle and corner inequality violations, the model can predict asymmetric similarities. Specifically, it can predict Tversky's (1977) finding that objects about which people have little knowledge are more similar to objects about which people know a lot than vice versa. The quantum model represents the amount of knowledge about an object by the dimensionality of its subspace and predicts that objects with lower-dimensional subspaces are more similar to objects with higher-dimensional subspaces than vice versa, because projecting from a lower-to a higher-dimensional subspace retains more of the state vector's amplitude than vice versa (see Pothos et al., 2013, Fig. 2). One important prerequisite for this is that the two subspaces are neither identical nor orthogonal: Identical subspaces denote identical objects, for which asymmetric similarities make conceptually no sense; orthogonal subspaces, in turn, denote objects with opposite features and with a similarity of 0 in either comparison direction. Using the quantum approach, Pothos et al. (2013) discuss the famous example of Tversky (1977), according to which Korea is more similar to China than the other way round. Because people in general have a greater range of knowledge about China than about Korea, China will be represented by a subspace of larger dimensionality than Korea. Furthermore, the two subspaces will be related to each other and overlap without being completely identical (in other words, China and Korea have similar, but not identical features). The greater similarity from Korea to China than vice versa will now naturally emerge in the quantum model by the sequential projection of the state vector.

Finally, the quantum model can also predict contextual effects on similarity (Yearsley et al., 2022), including the diagnosticity effect (Pothos et al., 2013). To compute an option's similarity to Austria, the model projects the state vector onto the two other options (in both possible orders), then onto the option itself, and finally onto Austria, averaging over both orders. Through this mechanism, the quantum model predicts diagnosticity effects-the key factor is a direct projection between highly-overlapping subspaces of two choice options that makes the remaining, distinct choice option appear more similar to the reference stimulus. Other non-Euclidean geometry. In addition to Hilbert spaces, other non-Euclidean geometry can be used to create generalized psychological spaces. While Euclidean spaces are flat and lack curvature (resulting in planes such as a map when considering two dimensions), these non-Euclidean spaces have curvature. Positive curvature leads to elliptic surfaces (e.g., the sphere of the Earth); negative curvature results in hyperbolic surfaces (e.g., a saddle shape). Although such non-Euclidean spaces are frequently used in computer science to model non-Euclidean distances and similarity relations (Nickel & Kiela, 2017; Peng et al., 2021; Wilson et al., 2010), their application in psychology has only recently gained attention. Roads and Love (2024) discuss the Poincaré space, a hyperbolic space which expands exponentially as one moves away from its center.

For two dimensions, this hyperbolic space can be mapped onto the interior of a circle (the Poincaré disk), with the edges representing points infinitely far away. As one moves from the center of the disk toward its edges, the distance increases exponentially. Thus, objects close to the center have a small distance, while objects near the edges have a large distance. Because of this exponential growth, Poincaré space is particularly effective at representing hierarchical similarity relations (Klimovskaia et al., 2020; Nickel & Kiela, 2017): The generic term (e.g., fruit) is located at the origin, with the specific instances (e.g., apple) surrounding it. In Poincaré space, the number of nearest neighbors that can fit around the origin increases exponentially as one moves further from the origin, enabling a more flexible representation of hierarchical structures compared to Euclidean space. In this way, Poincaré and other hyperbolic spaces can accommodate tree-like structures (as in e.g., Carroll, 1976; Cunningham, 1978; Sattath & Tversky, 1977), accounting for why some objects are the nearest neighbor of more other objects than is possible in Euclidean geometry.

Summary.

In sum, generalized geometric spaces extend beyond traditional map-like mental representations. For example, Hilbert spaces, which form the basis of quantum approaches to geometric similarity, can predict violations of triangle inequality and symmetry as well as contextual effects (Pothos et al., 2013). Other spaces with curvature, such as hyperbolic spaces, can account for violations of nearest neighbor constraints and better represent hierarchical relationships (Roads & Love, 2024).

Violations are task-and material-dependent, not universal. The overview of empirical challenges showed that while people sometimes violate the assumptions of the geometric framework, these violations are not universal. For example, although minimality is occasionally violated in identification tasks (e.g., when people confuse one object with another), it is generally satisfied in similarity judgment tasks, which arguably capture similarity relations most directly. Similarly, Tversky and Hutchinson (1986) found that while nearest neighbor analyses occasionally exceeded geometric limits in conceptual data sets with generic terms, they generally fell within the admissible range for perceptual data lacking generic terms. As noted by Tversky and Gati (1982), even symmetry seems to hold in many situations, despite documented asymmetries in similarity judgments (e.g., Polk et al., 2002) and word associations (e.g., Griffiths et al., 2007) as well as asymmetric confusions in identification and same-different tasks (e.g., Tversky, 1977). Thus, violations of the assumptions of geometric similarities are task-and material-dependent rather than universal.

Geometric extensions are psychologically interpretable.

Even when people violate the geometric assumptions, these violations can often be addressed within the geometric framework through psychologically plausible extensions. Many extensions incorporate established psychological constructs, such as attention and biases toward certain response options, which are central to similarity-based cognition. Notably, some extensions were not originally proposed to address geometric violations but to model broader cognitive processes. For example, attribute-specific attention, prominently used to capture selective attention to diagnostic attributes in categorization tasks (see Fig. 5b), also addresses various violations of the assumptions of geometric similarity models.

The extensions also reflect a broader principle: Similarity is inherently context-sensitive (Goldstone et al., 1997). Attribute weights shift with the task, the set of presented objects, and the diagnosticity of individual attributes (Tversky, 1977), and object biases vary with properties such as salience, typicality, and frequency (Nosofsky, 1991). However, these extensions are not always necessary to model similarity per se:

Object-specific biases are most critical for identification and confusion data, where responses reflect not only similarity but also the salience of the response options (Corter, 1987; Nosofsky, 1991), and attribute-specific weights are particularly important for categorization tasks with differentially diagnostic attributes (Nosofsky, 1986). The extensions thus become necessary when the paradigm introduces additional factors that a purely geometric account cannot absorb.

The reviewed extensions also differ in how much flexibility they add to the model.

Attribute-specific attention and object-specific biases typically introduce free parameters that are estimated from the data. Generalized similarity functions and psychological spaces may also come with parameters, but some of these can be derived a priori rather than fitted, such as the attribute variances and correlations entering the Mahalanobis distance, which can be computed from the stimulus distributions. In either case, model comparison methods that account for model flexibility, such as cross-validation, allow testing whether an extension captures something psychologically meaningful or merely increases overfitting.

In sum, this review demonstrates that no single extension accounts for all violations.

Instead, a combination of the extensions-to be chosen based on the observed violations-can effectively address these challenges.

Geometric representations extend to naturalistic stimuli.

While this review has focused on the empirical challenges of geometric approaches to similarity, it has largely assumed that objects can be represented as points in a multidimensional space. However, this dimensional assumption itself can be questioned: Tversky (1977) argued that it suits simple perceptual stimuli such as colors, which vary continuously along a few dimensions; complex stimuli such as faces or personalities, by contrast, seem to consist of many qualitative features and cannot easily be represented as points in a psychological space. For example, faces can be described by features like shape or the presence of glasses-features that are nominal or binary rather than interval-scaled (see also Buchanan et al., 2019; McRae et al., 2005; Vinson & Vigliocco, 2008). If these objects cannot be represented in a geometric space, this would limit the applicability of geometric similarity models, which rely heavily on the dimensional assumption.

Yet, recent research suggests that the dimensional assumption extends far beyond simple, artificial stimuli: Even natural objects with initially unknown attributes can be robustly embedded in geometric spaces based on human similarity data, and these spaces are often interpretable and predict behavior across tasks (Hebart et al., 2020; Izydorczyk & Bröder, 2023 , 2026; Roads & Love, 2021; Sanders & Nosofsky, 2020). For instance, Hebart et al. (2020) modeled the psychological space of nearly 2,000 real-world objects using an odd-one-out triad task. The resulting 49 interpretable dimensions included perceptual (e.g., color, roundness, elongation) and conceptual aspects (e.g., artificiality, food-relatedness, value); objects were typically characterized by about ten of them, and higher values on a dimension indicated more typical instances of the corresponding concept. Roads and Love (2021) scaled this approach to 50,000 images, using a task in which participants selected the two out of eight images most similar to a reference image.

Psychological spaces can also be derived for semantic stimuli (De Deyne et al., 2019; McRae et al., 2005; Pennington et al., 2014), for instance from free associations, based on how often words are produced in response to each cue word. Such association-based spaces improve predictions of psychological variables (Hussain et al., 2024), and frequently outperform spaces derived from word co-occurrence in natural language corpora (Mikolov et al., 2017; Mikolov, Sutskever, et al., 2013; Pennington et al., 2014). For instance, association-based spaces paired with a weighted Manhattan distance best predicted similarity judgments between words of the same category (r = .77 out of sample), outperforming text-based spaces and unweighted distances, although different distance functions yielded strongly correlated predictions (Richie & Bhatia, 2021).

More generally, a growing body of work demonstrates that natural language processing and vision models can construct high-dimensional geometric representations of complex stimuli, which can then serve as psychological spaces for modeling similarity as well as judgment and decision-making more generally. Specifically, pre-trained neural networks produce embedding spaces in which geometric proximity corresponds to meaningful similarity, making standard functions such as cosine similarity and Euclidean distance directly applicable (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019; Roads & Love, 2024). In the domain of language, Bhatia (2024) applied large language models to risk-taking scenarios, constructing a high-dimensional embedding for each scenario and then predicting people's riskiness judgments of these scenarios. Scenarios with similar vector representations were also rated similarly by participants-although these judgments were not used to derive the embedding. In the visual domain, Battleday et al. (2020) extracted image embeddings from a convolutional neural network and used the resulting geometric representations as input to classical similarity-based models of human categorization. Radulescu et al. (2026) embedded images of objects using a convolutional neural network and used the resulting geometric representation as input for a computational model of visual search in immersive 3D environments. Thus, pre-trained neural networks can often produce psychologically valid geometric spaces, enabling the same representational framework to be applied to stimuli as varied as decision scenarios and naturalistic images.

Alternative Approaches to Modeling Psychological Similarity

On a larger scale, the computational processes underlying similarity are directly linked to the psychological space on which similarity operates-any similarity function presupposes some representational format, and conversely, any set of representations implies a corresponding comparison process (Roads & Love, 2024). So far, the present work has taken the geometric representation as given and sought to extend or modify the similarity function operating on it: adding bias or density terms to account for asymmetry (Krumhansl, 1978; Nosofsky, 1991), introducing dynamic attribute weighting to account for context effects (Goldstone et al., 1997; Kruschke, 1992), or replacing Euclidean distances and spaces with generalized geometry to accommodate violations of the metric axioms (Pothos et al., 2013; Sakamoto et al., 2008). An alternative strategy, however, is to adopt non-geometric psychological spaces whose associated comparison processes can predict these empirical limitations naturally, without requiring post-hoc modifications to the similarity function. I therefore now consider featural models (Tversky, 1977), probabilistic and topic-based models (Griffiths et al., 2007), neural network-based distributed representations (Roads & Love, 2024), and transformational and alignment-based models (Hahn et al., 2003; Markman & Gentner, 1993).

Featural models and set-theoretic representations.

The simplest departure from geometric space is the set-theoretic representation, in which objects are encoded as collections of discrete features rather than as points in a continuous space (Tversky, 1977).

As noted earlier, featural approaches that operate on such set-theoretic representations (e.g., Tversky, 1977) assume that similarity reflects a trade-off between common and distinctive features, thereby naturally accounting for many of the empirical violations discussed above (a summary is given at the end of the overview of empirical challenges).

The relationship between geometric and featural models is particularly interesting: although they differ in both representational and computational assumptions (Roads & Love, 2024), they are often mathematically translatable into one another (Nosofsky, 1991).

On the one hand, geometric models typically represent objects as vectors in a multi-dimensional continuous space, whereas featural models represent objects as sets of discrete features and compute similarity based on feature overlap (Lee & Navarro, 2002; Navarro & Lee, 2002a). On the other hand, this distinction is less rigid than it may appear: continuous dimensions can be discretized into features (e.g., by thresholding or binning), and conversely, sets of features can be embedded in continuous spaces. Moreover, the additive version of Tversky's contrast model and choice rules based on geometric similarity share a closely related formal structure (Gati & Tversky, 1982; Nosofsky, 1991), and several influential similarity-based models (e.g., the ALCOVE model of categorization) admit both geometric and featural instantiations (Lee and Navarro, 2002; for a discussion, see Kruschke, 2008).

Some research has explored combining geometric and featural models into hybrid similarity models (Carroll, 1976; Navarro & Lee, 2002a; Nosofsky & Zaki, 2003). Nosofsky and Zaki (2003) proposed a hybrid model that computes psychological similarity as the product of geometric similarity (Eq. 2) and two parameters, C (with C > 1) and D (with 0 < D < 1), representing the influence of common and distinctive features, respectively.

This model fit old-new recognition data significantly better than a purely geometric model, explaining over 98% of the variance. Similarly, Navarro and Lee (2002a) modeled similarity as the difference between the number of common features and the Minkowski distance between objects. Expanding on this idea, Navarro and Lee (2004) demonstrated that some attributes contribute more to similarity when they differ, while others matter more when they match, highlighting the interplay between geometric differences and feature matches.

Topic models and distributed probabilistic representations. Griffiths et al. (2007) proposed a probabilistic account of semantic association, in which each word is represented as a probability distribution over latent topics that capture patterns of co-occurrence across language, and the association between two words is given by the conditional probability of one word given the other. Because conditional probabilities are directional and need not satisfy metric constraints, the topic model naturally accommodates the key violations observed in free-association data: asymmetries (e.g., "text" elicits "book" more often than the reverse, because "text" shares its topics with "book", while "book" also occurs in topics beyond those of "text"), triangle inequality violations (e.g., "bank" shares financial topics with "money" and geographical topics with "river", while "money" and "river" share few topics), and violations of the nearest neighbor bound (e.g., a general word like "animal" occurs broadly across many topics and is thus probable given many specific instances). In this respect, topic models resemble a probabilistic variant of Tversky's (1977) featural framework, in which topics play the role of features and similarity reflects the degree to which two words overlap in topics.

Network models and graph-based representations.

A more substantive departure from geometric space is the graph-based representation, in which objects are encoded as nodes and their relations as directed or weighted edges. Here the representational format is inherently non-metric: there is no assumption of continuity or dimensionality in the underlying space, and similarity is measured through processes such as spreading activation, shortest-path, or graph edit distance. In semantic networks derived from free associations, for instance, the proximity of two concepts can be defined via the shortest path between them (Kumar et al., 2020) or via random walks that integrate over all paths between them, discounting longer ones (De Deyne et al., 2016). The latter better predict similarity judgments, mainly because they also capture indirect paths that shortest-path measures overlook, and the same computation was also found to predict inductive generalization between concepts (Rubino & Piazza, 2026).

A further development of this idea is constituted by (neural) networks, which can be viewed as weighted graphs. In the previous section, pre-trained networks served only as a tool to obtain geometric psychological spaces; here, the network itself is treated as a model of how similarity is represented and computed. Unlike graph models, which explicitly encode relations, networks learn distributed representations implicitly from data, encoding objects as high-dimensional activation patterns whose structure emerges from training (Hinton, 1989). Similarity is then measured in the resulting embedding space-via cosine similarity, dot products, or learned metric functions. The contrast to the geometric approach is thus not one of geometric versus non-geometric representations: whereas geometric models combine a fixed space with a separately specified similarity function, networks learn the representation and the similarity function jointly (Roads & Love, 2024).

Neural network approaches, while powerful, face a challenge of opacity: the learned representations are difficult to interpret, making it hard to determine whether a good fit reflects genuine psychological insight or simply the flexibility of a high-dimensional model (Roads & Love, 2024).

Graph-based and distributed representations can naturally accommodate for some empirical challenges such as asymmetric similarity judgments. Spreading activation models (Anderson, 1983; Collins & Loftus, 1975) produce asymmetries because activation spreads from a cue node along weighted edges, so the similarity from I to J depends on the outgoing path structure from I, which differs from that of J-a mechanism that explains classic priming asymmetries like "canary" priming "bird" faster than vice versa. In connectionist accounts, Smolensky (1986) and Hinton (1989) showed that asymmetric similarity can emerge directly from network weight structure as a consequence of the dynamics of settling or retrieval. Similarly, distributed memory models (Hintzman, 1984 (Hintzman, , 1988) treat retrieval as an inherently asymmetric process-a probe activates a memory echo in a directed fashion-naturally accommodating asymmetric judgments without requiring an asymmetric representational space. This line of work has since been extended to demonstrate that distributed representations-including those of neural networks-can predict behavioral asymmetries without explicitly encoding them (Polk et al., 2002).

Alignment and transformational models and structure among features.

While geometric models treat features as unstructured and independent of one another, many features have spatial or temporal relationships (e.g., one feature is to the left of another) or hierarchical relationships (e.g., one feature is part of another). To account for such structure, alignment and transformational models of similarity have been developed.

Alignment models align the parts of objects based on their roles within each object.

For example, when comparing two stimuli consisting of three circles of varying sizes each, the smallest circle in one object is aligned with the smallest circle in the other, even if in absolute size it more closely matches another circle (Markman & Gentner, 1993).

Psychological similarity is influenced more by feature matches or differences between aligned parts than between unaligned parts (Goldstone, 1994; Markman & Gentner, 1996).

In this way, a feature match or difference is considered in the context of the objects' overall structure, such that a matching feature on unaligned parts may even reduce similarity (Goldstone, 1996). Transformational models, on the other hand, compute similarity based on the number of steps required to transform one object into the other (Hahn et al., 2003).

Proposed transformations include geometric changes, such as rotations (Ullman, 1996), and pattern changes, such as reversals (Imai, 1977). Hodgetts et al. (2009) identified three key transformations: creating a new feature, applying it to one or both objects, and swapping features within an object. For instance, transforming the object "square-circle" (where "square" is to the left of "circle") into "circle-triangle" requires creating the feature "triangle," replacing "square" with "triangle," and swapping the positions of the features-a total of three transformation steps. Importantly, Hodgetts and Hahn (2025) showed that such transformations can produce similarity relations incompatible with Euclidean spaces, as they inherently account for structural relationships among features that geometric approaches neglect.

Summary.

Taken together, this comparison across frameworks suggests that no single approach account for perceived similarity optimally across all stimulus domains and task paradigms. Featural models excel for discrete feature sets but may be less suited to continuous perceptual domains. Probabilistic topic models handle semantic violations elegantly but are largely restricted to language. Network models are flexible but often opaque. The geometric framework offers a unified and interpretable account across perceptual and conceptual domains, but its adequacy depends on appropriate extensions to address the violations observed in a given task and stimulus set.

Conclusion

Geometric similarity models are probably the most influential yet also the most controversially discussed framework of psychological similarity. They are widely used in theories of higher-order cognition (e.g., identification and categorization, language, and memory) and in multivariate statistics and machine learning (e.g., multidimensional scaling and clustering), but they also face a range of empirical challenges, as humans can violate the assumptions they rely on. This review demonstrated that many of these challenges can be addressed within the geometric framework through psychologically compelling model extensions. Although not the only viable account of psychological similarity, the geometric framework, when appropriately extended, remains a principled, interpretable, and competitive account-and its empirical limitations are now well-characterized enough to guide the selection of extensions on a case-by-case basis. The diversity of psychological similarity reflects the complexity of human cognition, underscoring the need for flexible formal frameworks that adapt to the nuanced ways cognitive systems perceive similarity.

Theoretical Implications: Bridging Geometric and Psychological Similarities

This review has two interrelated aims. The first is descriptive: to systematically map which empirical violations of geometric assumptions can be addressed by which model extensions, and under what conditions. The second is normative: to argue that the geometric framework, when appropriately extended, remains a viable and competitive account of psychological similarity-not merely as a default assumption inherited from psychophysics, but as a theoretically principled choice with distinct advantages. Geometric models constitute arguably the most prominent framework to investigate similarity: For instance, multidimensional scaling infers an embedding in which geometric distances correspond as closely as possible to human similarity ratings (Shepard, 1962a (Shepard, , 1962b)), and it provided the basis for many similarity-based models of higher-order cognition (e.g., Nosofsky, 1986)-illustrating the deep connection between geometric similarities, cognitive models, and machine learning algorithms. Given the violations reviewed above (see Table 1), however, the question remains whether geometric similarities can truly capture human similarity relations. I see three principal reasons to adopt a geometric representation as the starting point, which I discuss in turn below. First, violations of the geometric assumptions are task-and material-dependent rather than universal, and geometric representations are particularly well-suited to paradigms in which stimuli vary continuously along identifiable dimensions-colors, tones, sizes, orientations. Second, the geometric framework offers strong psychological interpretability: similarities such as dot products are easy to compute, distances and angles are intuitive, and the extensions reviewed here map cleanly onto established psychological constructs.
