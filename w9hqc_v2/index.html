To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 30 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The human sensorimotor system can represent actions in the absence of movement, termed motor simulation. These motor simulations can be perceptually (externally) driven through observation of an actor or cognitively (internally) driven through imagining movement. Although there is evidence humans can represent multiple actions simultaneously, whether different motor simulations can concurrently be represented is unclear. We investigated the capacity of the sensorimotor system to simultaneously represent observed (action observation; AO) and imagined actions (motor imagery; MI). Two competing hypotheses were tested concerning whether AO and MI could co-exist or not, The Dual Action Simulation Hypothesis (DASH) and Visual Guidance Hypothesis (VGH), respectively. Fifteen participants completed a functional magnetic resonance imagining (fMRI) study, requiring them to complete four distinct conditions involving imagination or observation of a rhythmical hand tapping action. Conditions involved: AO, AO with a counting task (i.e., with cognitive load), MI, or simultaneous AO and MI of different actions (AOMI). Through representational similarity analysis, AO and MI motor simulations were evidenced in the right ventral premotor cortex, supporting DASH over VGH.

Multidimensional scaling of representational similarity data indicated a unique representational pattern for AOMI, differing from AO and MI across different dimensions. Whole-brain analyses were conducted, showing descriptive support for greater engagement of sensorimotor regions during AOMI vs. the summation of independent AO and MI conditions occurred, complementing findings from our representational similarity analysis. While dual motor simulations appear possible, our data suggests that the representational pattern of AOMI is distinct, producing superadditive sensorimotor activity. Neither DASH nor VGH adequately explain these data.

Introduction

The human brain has the propensity to represent actions in the absence of movement across cortical and subcortical sensorimotor regions 1 , a phenomenon termed motor simulation 2 . Motor simulation can be external or internal in origin. Externally driven motor simulation is most typically cued perceptually through observing the actions of another (action observation; AO). In contrast, the imagination of movement (termed motor imagery; MI) is an internal sensory experience, in the absence of an immediate corresponding sensory input 3 . MI involves imagining the visual and kinesthetic properties of a movement 3, 4 , resulting in an internal simulation of that action 2 . These two forms of motor simulation (i.e., AO and MI) show overlap in the neural networks recruited for actual movement, forming the basis for the long-standing proposal of their 'functional equivalence' with movement execution 2 . Nuances in the topographical patterns of activation associated with each form (see 1 for meta-analysis) however, suggest that each form is associated with its own unique sensorimotor representation.

Research investigating social interactions provides evidence of the capacity of the sensorimotor system to simultaneously represent multiple actions. This has been shown through the observation of multiple, different actions 5 and through neurophysiological evidence showing distinct representations of actions from auditory or seen actions of others 6, 7 . The fronto-parietal network underpinning our propensity for representing multiple actions is broadly known as the action observation network 8 , with seminal research related to the mirror neuron system 9 . With this associative network as the basis, humans can simultaneously represent their own actions and those of others, affording the navigation and seamless coordination of our behavior with others 6.

While movement execution and AO-related representations can co-exist within the sensorimotor system 7 , it is yet to be determined whether this is true for multiple covert actions involving observation and MI-driven motor simulations.

A somewhat consistent finding is that synchronizing AO and MI experiences of the same actions in parallel (termed 'Congruent AOMI') results in enhanced behavioral outcomes compared to its independent components [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] . At the cortical level, there is greater, or at least comparable, activations across sensorimotor-related brain regions for congruent AOMI than that of independent AO or MI [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] . Although the effects of this neurophysiological augmentation on behaviour have received considerable study, here we focus on what underpins these augmented activations. At the heart of this question is whether the sensorimotor system is simultaneously representing independent motor simulations through AO and MI. There are theories relevant to this question and the nature of these representations, which can be categorized into those supporting dual motor simulations and those that do not.

Perhaps the most prominent explanation for greater activity across sensorimotor-related brain regions during AOMI compared to independent AO or MI is the Dual Action Simulation Hypothesis (DASH; 20, 21 ). According to this hypothesis, during AOMI there is a simultaneous representation of actions -generated through independent AO and MI components -existing as two separate sensorimotor streams which can merge or compete depending on whether the same or different actions are represented in each simulation modality 21 . The process of "merging" is thought to explain the greater sensorimotor activation reported during congruent AOMI of the same actions, compared to its independent components 15, 17, 18 . A second explanation that contrasts to DASH is the Visual Guidance Hypothesis (VGH; 22 ). This proposes that MI alone is the driver of motor-related activity during AOMI, with the AO component constrained to a sensory representation, only guiding the sensorimotor MI representation. There is conflicting evidence concerning the validity of either hypothesis (e.g., 23,22; 24,25 ) and to date, studies have been limited to transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) investigations of corticospinal excitability. TMS is unable to distinguish across these hypotheses as it does not capture activity across the fronto-parietal network, nor allow examination of the neurophysiological overlap of these independent and combined covert states. We tested predictions of DASH and VGH by examining representational patterns of AOMI-related brain activation (via Representational Similarity Analysis, RSA, a variant of multivoxel pattern analysis 26 ) obtained using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). In RSA, independent voxel intensities from specified regions of interest (ROI) are correlated across different conditions, providing an index of representational similarity between conditions. To discern the contributions of the two independent processes we instructed simultaneous AO and MI of distinguishable actions (i.e., AO of vertical hand tapping and MI of lateral hand tapping).

This instructional method for different actions has been termed 'Coordinative AOMI' 20 , but hereon we will simply refer to this method as AOMI, permitting identification of each representation within the sensorimotor system. To estimate the independent components of AOMI, as predicted by the different theories (i.e., DASH and VGH), representational patterns for independent AO and MI (i.e., 'control') conditions were established (see Figure 1). A third control condition -AOcount, where participants engaged in a secondary counting task, was also included to reduce the likelihood of spontaneous MI during AO. As this condition also involved a small cognitive load, it was included to better match the cognitive recruitment of AOMI conditions.

We isolated the bilateral ventral premotor cortex (PMv) and inferior parietal lobule (IPL) as ROIs, due to evidence of consistent recruitment of these regions during independent AO and MI 1 . If sensorimotor representations of observed and imagined actions can co-exist (i.e., both AO and MI simultaneously present in AOMI, as proposed by DASH), these independent conditions when combined would be representationally 'similar' to AOMI. The alternative, where the AO component serves merely as a guide for the sensorimotor MI representation (as proposed by VGH), would be evidenced by AOcount when combined with MI being representationally 'similar' to AOMI. In addition to ROI-based analyses, we also conducted whole brain analyses to address specific predictions of the two hypotheses (Figure 1) and better inform on broader AOMI-related activations. We expected that AOMI would result in a similar distribution and magnitude of activations across sensorimotor-related regions when compared to the independent conditions, as proposed by DASH. Results

All 15 participants were included in the final analyses. Across all scanning sessions, the mean total signal to noise ratio was 55.66 (SD = 4.51) and mean framewise displacement was .13mm (SD = .056). Information pertaining to voxel data used for RSA analysis is given in Table S3.

Regions of interest and representational similarity analysis

To test general predictions related to each AOMI hypothesis, contrasts were conducted comparing the differential activations of each instruction (calculated relative to rest) within regions of interest and comparing the representational similarity of each independent condition when correlated with AOMI. We tested the capacity of the human sensorimotor system to simultaneously represent different covert forms of action. An approach was used that permitted AO and MI content to be simultaneously experienced yet representationally distinguishable, through the assignment of different action kinematics for each form. A type of multi-voxel pattern analysis (i.e., RSA) was applied to blood oxygen level-dependent activity data obtained via fMRI to decode AOMI content. In the absence of actual movement, we provide evidence that the sensorimotor system can represent two independent and unique sensorimotor representations. RSA analyses indicated that both AO and MI representations were present during AOMI -evidenced by high similarity with AOMI -but evidence for either hypothesis (DASH or VGH) could be claimed within the right PMv. Here, there was no difference between the similarities of AOMI-AO and AOMI-MI in this premotor region, AOcount did have significantly less similarity to AOMI. Therefore, AO activations in AOMI are motor-related and not purely sensory.

Multidimensional scaling of the representational similarity data showed that AOMI had unique dimensions relative to the independent conditions, indicating that the content of AOMI was not simply that of AO plus MI representations. Although the whole-brain analyses lacked sufficient power to withstand multiple contrast corrections, the whole brain data also provided support that AOMI was unique. Greater sensorimotor activity was observed during AOMI than the summative activity of the AO and MI conditions. Collectively, these data contest the proposal that MI alone is the driver of sensorimotor-related activity during AOMI, directly refuting the key premise of the VGH 22 . These data instead support the conclusion that the brain can simultaneously simulate AO and MI actions, supporting DASH, with the caveat that AOMI is not simply the summation of these dual motor simulations. In what follows we discuss these data within the context of past work, in reference to theories that might explain the "super-additive" AOMI activations, and in consideration of these data for behaviour and learning.

Greater similarities within the right PMv for both AO and MI representations with AOMI, and no difference between them, provide support that both actions were represented and distinguishable; supporting dual motor simulations and previous TMS research 23 . This critical comparison was further tested against the auditory cortex (control ROI), confirming this difference to not occur due to similar vascular architectures or potential global activations. The PMv is a structure activated by both AO and MI independently 1 , with these activations relating more to the content of actions (i.e., kinematic information) rather than action goals 29 . While the representational similarity for MI with AOMI (r = .49) was numerically greater than for AO with AOMI (r = .39), they were not statistically different. Although this absence of difference in similarities between AO and MI supports dual action representation, aligning with research suggesting that the sensorimotor system can support multiple observed actions 5 , we could only confirm true equivalence between these conditions with a liberal .3 equivalence boundary. A greater similarity for MI with AOMI may contributed to this lack of equivalence at more conservative boundaries and could be inherent to AOMI of differing actions. Requirements to monitor and attend to self-generated MI content may naturally draw attention (e.g., gaze) away from the observed action 30 , limiting the processing of this visual information (e.g., 23 ). Indeed, there is evidence that MI influences gaze patterns and that gaze contributes to the sensorimotor representation of observed actions 23.

An unanswered question is why differences were identified in the right PMv but absent in the left PMv. An explanation could be that effector-related content was lateralized to the left (i.e., contralateral hemisphere), as with physically performed unilateral actions. There were descriptively greater similarities in the left PMv for AOMI-AO and AOMI-AOcount (.49 and .46, respectively), than in the right PMv. While movement execution predominantly activates the contralateral hemisphere, evidence concerning lateralization of AO and MI representations is ambiguous 1 . TMS research provides support for effector specific activations in the contralateral hemisphere for AO, MI and AOMI 18, 23 ; however, previous fMRI research provides evidence for bilateral activation across the hemispheres 31, 32 . The representational similarities in the left PMv between AO and MI (r = .38) can be taken as a liberal index of shared effector-related information between these conditions, given that some of this similarity may also be due to shared processes beyond effector-related information. As our representational similarities for AOMI-AO and AOMI-MI exceeded .38 (i.e., .49 and .52, respectively), this would support the proposal that genuine AO and MI related activations were captured within AOMI, beyond shared effector-related content. In the right PMv, however, similarities were reduced, more so for AO conditions. While speculative, these activations within the right hemisphere may provide more genuine condition-level activity independent of effector-related content. In such a case, this would explain the sensitivity to detect similarity differences between AOMI-MI and AOMI-AOcount.

An implicit assumption accompanying this prior explanation regarding the asymmetry across the left and right PMv is that the AOcount condition did what was intended (i.e., reduce motor engagement). The counting manipulation was included in order to occupy working memory -similar to the requirements of MI 33 and presumably AOMI -and prevent spontaneous MI. Both AO conditions activated the bilateral PMv compared to rest, yet resulted in different similarities to AOMI (in the right PMv). This creates two possibilities; 1) that the counting manipulation did not work, still resulting in motor simulation, or 2) that the AO component of AOMI is not sensory. Our data provides evidence for both, though favouring the latter. AOcount activated motor-related regions, but this activity differed from AO in representational content and its similarity to AOMI. Therefore, while the counting manipulation did not abolish motor engagement as intended, it altered AO at the voxel-level. Accordingly, our data supports distinct motor representations within AOMI at the level of the PMv.

Comparing activation patterns in the IPL across the AO conditions provides further insight into 'what' sensorimotor information is represented during AOMI. The IPL is considered to encode the goal-related information of action 34 , play a key role in sensory integration 35 , and as it pertains to MI, is involved in the manipulation and comparison of MI content with sensory input 36 . A lack of difference between AO and MI conditions, in terms of their representational similarity with AOMI, does not strictly allow the rejection of any hypothesis in these ROIs which are upstream of the PMv. One explanation for the lack of difference between these conditions is that goal-related information during AO and MI is similarly processed within the IPL. Thus, representational differences for these conditions may only become apparent when projected downstream to the PMv, where action content is most pertinent. This asymmetry between regions could be due to instructional differences for the AO conditions and the manner participants attended to action kinematics. Due to the additional demands of the secondary counting task in the AOcount condition, participants' attention may have been biased to the endpoint (i.e., the counted component of the action), whereas only the AO condition may have afforded consistent monitoring of the action kinematics. Such reasoning would explain the progressive divergence of representational similarities as information was projected further downstream, resulting in the slight asymmetries between the IPL and PMv similarities, and specifically between the AOMI-AO and AOMI-AOcount similarities detected in the right PMv.

The IPL in general has been implicated in sense of agency, with activations increasing with reduced sense of motor control 37 . When imagined and observed actions differ, there is distinction and distancing of the imagined and observed content 30 , while still interacting through the temporal information (i.e., the timing of tapping). Theoretically, by this distinction of AO and MI actions, actions could be represented as two separate representations and processed independently contributing to IPL activity. This is different to actually performing an action and receiving veridical visual feedback, or during AOMI of the same actions, where visual and imagined action content have spatiotemporal correlations. In these latter instances, there would be opportunity for visuo-motor integration and unification of represented content (e.g., 38, 30 ).

Although not a classical "mirror" region, we isolated left and right V1 to help infer whether the AO component of AOMI was mostly sensory in nature. In left V1, a difference was found in the extent to which AOcount activated V1 compared to AOMI, while the comparison between AO and AOMI trended toward significance. Surprisingly, while the ROI analysis revealed suppressed activity for MI relative to baseline and AO conditions, MI had the greatest representational similarity to AOMI in this region. One collective explanation for these findings is that engaging in MI requires, or at least is enhanced by, a visual representation of the action, resulting in different activation of V1 39 . Indeed, visual imagery has been reported to downregulate the representation of irrelevant perceptual content 40, 41 . Here, imagery was performed with similar visual displays during both MI and AOMI conditions and therefore requirements for visual imagery in these two conditions were similar 42 , potentially explaining the suppressive MI effect and similarities between MI and AOMI.

Eye movement recordings during imagery show that the eyes respond in a similar manner during imagery as they do when watching or acting 43 . As such, it might have been useful to have collected gaze data (i.e., via eye traking) in the scanner to get some additional evidence (beyond our post-scanner surveys) that motor imagery was being performed. During AOMI, gaze has also been shown to alternate between the observed and imagined acts 23 . Saccadic eye movements specifically can influence cortical activity, notably in V1 44 . In our study, .saccades and consequential foveal information would have been most similar for MI and AOMI, than for AO and AOMI. With evidence that optimizing foveal coverage results in greater corticospinal responses during observation 45, 46 , the similarity we report for AOMI-AO in the PMv and IPL may be reduced relative to what could be present in AOMI of the same actions, where gaze is primarily drawn to the observed effector 23 . Therefore, while we report evidence for the distinguishable AO and MI representations within the sensorimotor system during AOMI, this is likely a conservative estimate for the AO representation and there is potential that it could be stronger during typical uses of congruent AOMI, when AO and MI of the same task are performed synchronously.

Inspection of the multidimensionality of the representational similarities provides a new perspective on AOMI. While we provide evidence that both AO and MI representations could be decoded from AOMI within the right PMv, multidimensional scaling gives evidence that AOMI is a unique representation, containing additional information not accounted for by independent AO and MI. As observed across all ROIs, AOMI consistently occupied a unique multidimensional space, with significantly different spatial coordinates to the independent conditions across at least two dimensions in each ROI. This suggests that AOMI is not consistently overlapping with AO and MI, nor situated between them. Therefore, the sensorimotor content of AOMI may not be as simple as predicted by DASH and VGH, where additive activations suggest additional processes are required when the two action states are different, yet performed simultaneously.

Although not significant at the population-level, clusters of activation in the precentral gyrus during AOMI were observed relative to either of the independent AOcount and MI conditions. This was not the case when compared to AO. The absence of difference across the precentral gyrus (comprising pre-and primary motor cortices) between AOMI and AO could indeed be explained by the presence of spontaneous MI during AO 20, 47 , which has been reported to occur quite frequently during AO 48, 49 .

In sum, this data provides support that AOMI provided unique conditions affording increased sensorimotor activity when compared to conditions where MI was isolated or when attempts were made to control for spontaneous MI during AO (i.e., AOcount).

The whole-brain data serves to descriptively help in characterizing sensorimotor mechanisms of covert actions. Data from these analyses support previous work showing enhanced corticospinal excitability during AOMI 22, 23, 24, 25 and greater BOLD responses 31, 32 compared to independent AO or MI. Our data indicates bilateral activation of the precentral gyrus during AOMI as well as consistent recruitment of the right superior parietal lobe (at the betweensubject level). Evidence that both AO and MI representations are distinguishable within AOMI provides support for DASH whilst allowing the rejection of VGH. According to specific predictions of DASH however, we also expected equivalence between AOMI and AO+MI. While this held true at the between-subject level, there were unthresholded clusters which suggested greater activations for AOMI. These data, coupled with the significant differences in multidimensional properties for AOMI show that it is more than just the sum of its parts, which is not captured by either DASH or VGH.

An alternative explanation for AOMI-related activations and specifically additive activations, is the Sensory Conflict Hypothesis 30 . While also predicated on dual sensorimotor representations, the Sensory Conflict Hypothesis proposes additional activations and learning during AOMI to be driven by recruitment of the forward model and prediction errors, generated through discrepancies in imagined and observed kinematic information. Whole brain analyses revealed AOMI to activate the precentral gyrus and superior parietal lobe (SPL). The right SPL has been implicated in sensory predictions during MI 50 , the processing of perceptually-driven prediction errors 51 , and representational gating of self-driven and observed sensorimotor representation 52 . Although the right SPL is involved in many other functions (e.g., 53, 54 ), its unique activation during AOMI may be a result of the temporally interacting AO and MI content, concurrent with differences in the spatial content. There is evidence that the SPL selectively represents simultaneous performed and observed actions, such that the observed action will only be simultaneously represented when relevant to the performed action 52 . In the context of our data, this gating mechanism would explain the additional activation of the SPL for AOMI than AO+MI and AOcount+MI, due to the association of AO and MI content.

Evidence that observed and imagined actions can coexist may inform on processes which contribute to observational learning. MI has been proposed to be akin to, or to at least involve forward model predictions associated with anticipation of sensory effects 55, 56 . Forward model predictions are also thought to be elicited by AO 57, 58, 59 , or what may be MI during AO. Taking the perspective that MI is akin to the forward model and conditionally active during AO, with the capacity to represent observed and imagined actions simultaneously, there is opportunity for their respective kinematic information to interact and update the forward model 30.

This study is the first to give multivoxel-based evidence regarding the capacity of the sensorimotor system to simultaneously represent observed and imagined actions in parallel (even when these experiences are distinct). Although we provide evidence for dual simulations, and thus refuted the VGH regarding the combined effects of AOMI, research is still required to further separate the manner in which simultaneous actions are represented. For example, investigations comparing DASH and SCH accounts and their differences in how and where AO and MI compete (i.e., DASH) or generate prediction errors (i.e., SCH), would be informative. The wider adoption of representational similarity and connectivity analyses approaches, focusing on representational similarities across different sensorimotor regions and their related changes, would help, as well as isolating areas deemed critical for error detection.

In terms of other limitation, we acknowledge that order effects may have influenced the conditions. The delivery of AO conditions before performing isolated MI or AOMI may have impacted what is imagined and there is increased potential for spontaneous MI during AO conditions, when AO is completed following AOMI or MI. We were also unable to truly discern condition-level pattern similarities (and differences) while controlling for action content. Because we created a distinguishable MI representation with a lateral action, from the AO and AOcount vertical actions, it is possible that any activations covaried with action differences. Although there is no reason to think that these different actions should influence the sensori-motor areas of the brain we were primarily interested in, in future research, it would be important to counterbalance these observed and imagined actions to allow separation. Finally, although we demonstrated variation in representational similarity across conditions, values were consistently positive. This may be due to the overlapping content across conditions (i.e., right hand actions and visual stimuli). In future research, greater scaling of similarity/dissimilarity across conditions, such as comparing different effectors or a control condition involving no motor content, would aid in distinguishing across conditions.

Method details

Participants completed task familiarization to the covert practice conditions outside of the scanner and a questionnaire to measure AOMI ability before completing the in-scanner tasks.

Familiarization consisted of participants performing lateral hand taps, providing MI content for the in-scanner task. At this stage participants were not informed that they would later imagine this task, to reduce the likelihood of spontaneous MI during AO conditions (e.g., 46, 47 ). AOMI ability was assessed through the completion of the combined action observation and motor imagery ability questionnaire (AOMI-AQ) 70 . The AOMI-AQ is a Likert-type rating scale consisting of 8 items, measuring self-reported AOMI ability. The AOMI-AQ was completed after the scanning session so not to unintentionally cue the use of AOMI during AO conditions. Although AOMI may be naturally used by some as a default observational process, not introducing the concept of AOMI until necessary, ensured the measurement of each participant's naturalistic observational process. MI ability was assessed using an adapted version of the MIQ-3 71 . This adapted MIQ-3 required participants to perform the same actions as in the AOMI-AQ before imagining performing the actions visually (first-person perspective) and kinaesthetically. Following this they rated their ability to imagine the action.

Data acquisition

Participants entered a 3T MRI scanner (Phillips 3.0 T whole body MRI scanner) mounted with an 8-channel sensitivity encoding head coil (SENSE factor = 2.4) and parallel imaging.

Participants wore over-ear noise-cancelling headphones to receive task-based instructions and to reduce distractions. The scanning component begun with a structural scan (High-resolution T1 scan: TR = 1900 ms, FOV = 256 mm, 160 slices, 1 mm thickness, 3.2 min), followed by four functional scans with a single shot EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 240 mm, 105 slices, 2.5 mm thickness, 6 min/scan).

As illustrated in Figure S1, participants completed four functional task runs (using a block design), one for each of the following conditions: action observation, AOcount, MI, and AOMI. Instructions and experimental stimuli were delivered using PsychoPy Experiment Builder 60 , ensuring time synched delivery within functional runs. Within each functional run participants completed 5 task blocks lasting 30 seconds, interspersed by 30 second rest blocks (5 min runs). Conditions were delivered in a partially counterbalanced order across participants. For each participant, observation conditions preceded imagery conditions to reduce the likelihood of spontaneous or intentional imagery during AO. The two observation conditions were counterbalanced across participants. The final two runs completed were MI and AOMI, completed in a counterbalanced manner across participants to control for order effects (see 71, 72 for similar counterbalancing procedures).

Action Observation (AO)

As illustrated in Figure 1, participants observed a video of an action performed by a male (vertical hand tapping) from the first-person perspective for 30 seconds. This action was performed rhythmically at a pace of 55 beats per minute (bpm). The aim of this condition was to capture activations based on how individuals would most naturally observe movement.

Action Observation with counting (AOcount)

Participants observed the same action, but they were additionally instructed to count the number of times the hand tapped whilst observing. To ensure adherence, participants were told that the number of taps may differ across videos and that they would be asked about this after the run. The aim of this condition was to reduce the likelihood of MI during observation by instructing a secondary cognitive task, potentially decreasing cortical motor activations.

Motor Imagery (MI)

Participants saw an image of the environment in which the AO video was recorded, except no biological motion (i.e., the hand) was shown. Participants were required to imagine, from a first-person perspective, that they were performing a lateral hand tapping task between the blue dots (as experienced during task familiarization), focusing on the kinesthetic sensations. As imagery conditions were completed after AO conditions, participants were asked to imagine their horizontal hand tapping at the same pace which they previously observed (55 bpm). Participants were asked to report how well they were able to imagine the task on completion of the run 73.

Reports were ascertained using a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (like actually moving).

Combined Action Observation and Motor Imagery (AOMI)

Participants were exposed to the same videos used in the AO conditions. Simultaneously, participants were instructed to imagine themselves performing the action as described above in the MI condition (i.e., lateral hand tapping at 55 bpm). Instructions encouraged participants to imagine the feeling of performing the horizontal action (kinesthetic imagery) in synchrony with the observed vertical action. Using the same Likert-type scale as for the MI condition, participants were asked to report how well they were able to imagine the task on completion of the run.

To inform on the magnitude of activations within pre-specified regions and infer on the potential contribution of independent conditions during AOMI, a region of interest (ROI) analysis was conducted comparing conditions within the bilateral PMv, IPL and in V1. ROIs were created using 7mm spheres using FSL with MNI coordinates informed by previous literature 1 . Two independent spheres were generated bilaterally for the PMv (left MNI coordinates; x = -54, y = 6, z = 34: right; x = 54, y = 8, z = 34), the IPL (left MNI coordinates; x = -54, y = 36, z = 24: right; x = 54, y = 36, z = 24) and V1 (left MNI coordinates; x = -10, y = -100, z = 6: right; x = 10, y = -100, z = 6).

ROIs activations were extracted from standard space for each participant's first-level activation map for each condition, with smoothing applied. The extracted activation was averaged across voxels within each ROI. All ROI related analyses were conducted using R statistical software 64 . Comparisons were conducted using repeated-measure linear regression for each ROI with treatment coded conditions, comparing all conditions to AOMI (reference level).

To assess representational similarities of the independent conditions with AOMI, RSA was conducted on voxel-by-voxel data within each ROI. For RSA, data remained unsmoothed to better capture independent voxel intensities 78 . ROI masks were reverse normalized from MNI standard space into each participants' native space prior to extraction of each participant's voxel intensities. For each participant, a correlation matrix was calculated in R, by correlating voxel activity patterns across conditions within each ROI. All correlations were z transformed to normalize prior to statistical analyses. Once participant-level data were extracted and transformed, these data were analysed at the group-level using linear mixed-effects models to determine which representations were present during AOMI. For this analysis, conditions were treatment coded comparing the similarity index of MI with AOMI (AOMI-MI) to each of the similarities for the AO conditions with AOMI (AOMI-AO and AOMI-AOcount).

The representational similarity matrices for participants were converted into representational dissimilarity matrices (1 -r). Multidimensional scaling (MDS) was performed on the group-average RDM using the stats package function cmdscale to obtain a lowdimensional (3D) representation of the representational space. Subject-specific RDMs were constructed with MDS applied to each. Individual data were then aligned to the group-average configuration using Procrustes analysis implemented using the vegan function Procrustes to remove differences in rotation, translation, and scale. Each dimension was analyzed using independent repeated-measures linear regression models with treatment coding applied to conditions, comparing all independent conditions to the AOMI condition. As our analyses isolated comparisons of two or more conditions to a single condition, adjustments for multiple comparisons were applied using the Dunnett test 79 , optimizing statistical power for the design.

Control ROI analysis.

A control ROI (primary auditory cortex; MNI coordinates x = -42, y = -24, z = 10) was also analyzed, to ensure that any resultant ordering did not arise due to shared vascular architecture or tissue composition. We also tested whether critical ordering could be explained solely by a uniform spatial contribution. For any critical ROIs that resulted, we tested the interaction against A1 using 10,000 within-participant whole-map condition-label permutations. Complete maps were relabeled without rearranging their voxels. This procedure retained each map's spatial autocorrelation, value distribution, stable voxel-wise structure, participant-specific ROI geometry, and complete set of pairwise similarities.

Whole-brain analysis

A whole-brain univariate analysis was conducted to localize activations for AO, AOcount, MI and AOMI conditions. To assess activations extending beyond sensorimotor regions, contrasts were conducted at the condition-level in standard space, averaging across each participant's activation map.
