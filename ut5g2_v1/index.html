To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Full text

Abstract

Despite established appetite-stimulating effects of cannabis use, binge eating is seldom assessed in these samples. Its prevalence, clinical importance, and demographic reach among people with cannabis-involved drug use disorder are largely unknown. We analyzed 12,591 adults from the Collaborative Psychiatric Epidemiology Surveys who completed the eating disorder and substance use modules. Using design-weighted estimation, we estimated prevalence of binge eating among adults with a cannabis-involved drug use disorder and examined whether binge eating marks greater burden and disability and whether it differs by demographic group. Recurrent binge eating affected 11.2% of adults with a cannabis-involved drug use disorder (95% CI [8.4, 14.7]). Separately, 5.5% met criteria for DSM-IV binge eating disorder. Prevalence rose across levels of cannabis involvement (adjusted OR per level = 2.11, 95% CI [1.75, 2.55]). Adults with both conditions had the highest prevalence of suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, major depression, and generalized anxiety disorder. At least one lifetime suicide attempt was reported by 27.8% of this group and by only 3.3% of adults with neither condition. Binge eating alone was associated with a level of functional disability comparable to cannabis-involved drug use disorder alone, while adults with both conditions had the highest WHODAS time out of role and role impairment scores. Among adults with cannabis-involved drug use disorder, binge eating also marked a more damaging drug course. Among adults with a cannabis-involved drug use disorder, recurrent binge eating was more prevalent among women (17.4%) than men (8.6%). Men nevertheless made up an estimated 54.1% of affected adults. Prevalence ranged from 9.7% to 12.3% across racial and ethnic groups. Findings indicate that binge eating is common and linked with adverse outcomes among those with cannabis-involved drug use disorder, thus warranting targeted interventions.

Introduction

Cannabis use disorder (CUD) is a common and largely untreated condition in the United States. In a national survey conducted in 2012 and 2013, 6.3% of adults met lifetime criteria for DSM-5 CUD and 2.5% met criteria in the past year (Hasin et al., 2016). CUD was associated with psychiatric comorbidity and disability and was rarely treated (Hasin et al., 2016). Both cannabis use more broadly and CUD increased substantially between 2001 to 2013 (Hasin et al., 2015). Wider legalization and more potent products may raise prevalence even further (Connor et al., 2021). Psychosocial treatments can reduce cannabis use and related problems although enduring abstinence remains uncommon (Connor et al., 2021).

Binge eating is also common and clinically important. Binge eating disorder (BED) involves recurrent episodes of eating unusually large amounts of food with a sense of loss-ofcontrol over one's eating in the moment (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Surveys that used the World Health Organization Composite International Diagnostic Interview found BED to be more prevalent than bulimia nervosa (BN) in the United States (Hudson et al., 2007) and across 14 countries (Kessler et al., 2013), however exact estimates depend on the criteria applied. A study of 36,306 U.S. adults that used DSM-5 criteria estimated lifetime BED prevalence at 0.85% (Udo & Grilo, 2018). BED is associated with other psychiatric disorders, suicide risk, role impairment, and severe obesity, but fewer than half of affected adults receive treatment (Hudson et al., 2007; Kessler et al., 2013). For example, in a national sample, 22.9% of adults with lifetime BED reported at least one prior suicide attempt (adjusted OR = 4.83, 95% CI [3.54, 6.60]; Udo et al., 2019).

Substance use disorders are a documented comorbidity of eating disorders, and vice versa (Bahji et al., 2019). Cannabis may be especially relevant to binge eating due to its influence on appetite and craving (Elran-Barak et al., 2025). Among young adults, past-year cannabis use was associated with binge eating in women but not in men (Elran-Barak et al., 2025). Among 165 treatment-seeking adults with at least weekly binge eating, more than 23% reported cannabis use in the previous three months (Wilkinson et al., 2024). Wilkinson et al. noted that only two earlier studies, both more than 20 years old, had tested whether cannabis use relates to clinical severity in samples with binge eating. These studies began with young adults or with people seeking treatment for binge eating. To our knowledge, none has estimated the prevalence of binge eating among adults with CUD in a nationally representative sample. It also remains unclear whether binge eating marks a more severe clinical presentation among people with CUD.

Diagnostic definitions add further complication to this already limited literature. BN is a diagnostic rule-out for BED (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Analyses restricted to BED therefore exclude people who binge and also purge. They also exclude people who report recurrent yet sub-clinical binge eating without meeting full criteria for any eating disorder. Binge eating must therefore be examined along a graded continuum ranging from a behavioral phenotype (binge episodes at least twice weekly for at least three months) through DSM-IV BED and BN.

Whether binge eating deserves attention in CUD treatment also depends on who is affected. Eating disorder research has historically centered young, White women from relatively affluent backgrounds (Alegria et al., 2007). Based on these samples, there is no solid grounding for generalization of binge eating relevance to the more diverse populations typically included in substance use treatment and research. However, findings from the Collaborative Psychiatric Epidemiology Surveys (CPES) argue for generalization in part. For example, BED prevalence was similar across ethnic groups, and any binge eating was more common in ethnic minority groups than among non-Latino White adults (Marques et al., 2011). Whether the same holds among adults with CUD is unknown.

We leveraged the CPES, which pools three nationally representative surveys of U.S. adults that share a common diagnostic interview (Heeringa et al., 2004; Kessler & Üstün, 2004; Pennell et al., 2004), to examine prevalence and correlates of CUD co-occurring with binge eating across the diagnostic spectrum. We had three aims: First, we estimated the prevalence of binge eating among adults with a CUD and tested whether binge eating prevalence increases with level of cannabis involvement. Second, we compared adults with both conditions, either condition alone, or neither condition on psychiatric burden, drug use course, and functional impairment. Third, we tested whether prevalence and the association between CUD and binge eating differ by sex, race, and ethnicity.

Methods

Data Source and Sample

The Collaborative Psychiatric Epidemiology Surveys (CPES) pooled three nationally representative surveys of U.S. adults conducted between 2001 and 2003: the National Comorbidity Survey Replication (NCS-R), the National Survey of American Life, and the National Latino and Asian American Study (Heeringa et al., 2004). These surveys used a common sampling framework and the World Mental Health version of the Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI; Heeringa et al., 2004; Kessler & Üstün, 2004; Pennell et al., 2004). Public-use data were obtained from the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (study 20240). Because CPES codes diagnostic variables as not endorsed when respondents were not administered a given module, we restricted analyses to the 12,591 respondents who were administered both the eating disorder and substance use modules and had valid long-form sampling weights.

Analytic Groups

For analyses of psychiatric burden and functional disability, the analytic sample was classified into four mutually exclusive groups based on lifetime recurrent binge eating and cannabis-involved drug use disorder: neither condition (n = 11,219), binge eating only (n = 632), cannabis-involved drug use disorder only (n = 641), and both conditions (n = 99). For prevalence analyses, adults were classified by level of cannabis involvement: no lifetime drug use (n = 7,728), cannabis use without a drug use disorder (n = 3,835), and cannabis-involved drug use disorder (n = 740). Adults who used other drugs but did not have a cannabis-involved drug use disorder (n = 288) were retained in the sample and are reported in the supplementary materials.

Measures

Cannabis Involvement. The CIDI screens individual drug classes separately but assesses DSM-IV drug abuse and dependence criteria across drugs in aggregate. We defined three levels of cannabis involvement: no lifetime drug use, cannabis use without a drug use disorder, and cannabis-involved drug use disorder, defined as lifetime drug abuse or dependence among respondents with lifetime cannabis use (n = 740). Among respondents in the cannabis-involved drug use disorder group, 128 reported cannabis as their only drug and therefore met criteria for a cannabis use disorder. The remaining 612 also reported lifetime use of other drugs, so their drug use disorder could not be attributed specifically to cannabis.

Binge Eating.

Binge eating was examined along a graded continuum, consistent with prior analyses of these surveys (Hudson et al., 2007). Measures included recurrent binge eating, defined as eating binges at least twice weekly for several months based on the CIDI screening item; binge eating with loss of control; and DSM-IV binge eating disorder (BED) and bulimia nervosa (BN), based on CPES-supplied diagnostic algorithms. BED was defined using the DSM-IV bulimia nervosa diagnostic hierarchy. Measures referring to the past 12 months were used in sensitivity analyses. In the NCS-R, the eating disorder module was administered to a random half of Part II respondents (Hudson et al., 2007).

Clinical Burden and Drug Use Course. Clinical outcomes included lifetime suicidal ideation, suicide plans, and suicide attempts; lifetime major depression and generalized anxiety disorder; fair or poor self-rated mental and physical health; obesity, defined as a body mass index of 30 or higher; and current unemployment. Drug use course indicators included giving up important activities because of drug use and continuing to use drugs despite physical or emotional problems. Functional Disability. Functional disability was assessed using World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS) domain scores, which range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater disability. We also examined the number of days in the past 30 days that health problems limited usual activities and self-rated performance on days worked during the past 30 days. Self-rated work performance ranged from 0 to 10 and was administered only in the NCS-R. Days out of role were assessed in the NCS-R and NLAAS, and the WHODAS role impairment domain in the NCS-R and NSAL; the remaining WHODAS domains were assessed in all three surveys.

Covariates. The primary pooled models included sex, age modeled with linear and quadratic terms, education, marital status, race and ethnicity, and source survey. Demographicstratified gradient models used the same covariates except the stratifying variable and any covariate constant within that stratum: sex-stratified models retained race and ethnicity, and race-stratified models retained sex. Both interaction models included the full covariate set.

Work-performance models omitted the constant source-survey indicator.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses accounted for the CPES survey design using stratum, cluster, and longform sampling weights with Taylor series linearization. The analytic sample was treated as a subpopulation of the full survey sample. Weighted prevalence estimates used logit-based 95% confidence intervals. All adjusted models included the covariates listed above and accounted for the survey design. Binary outcomes were analyzed using logistic regression and reported as odds ratios, continuous outcomes were analyzed using linear models and reported as adjusted mean differences, and count outcomes were analyzed using quasi-Poisson models and reported as rate ratios. Analyses were conducted in R using the survey package (Lumley, 2004).

Prevalence of Binge Eating Across Cannabis Involvement.

We estimated the weighted prevalence of each binge eating phenotype overall, at each level of cannabis involvement, and within the cannabis-involved drug use disorder group. We tested the cannabis involvement gradient using logistic models with involvement level entered as an ordered variable, reporting the adjusted odds ratio for each one-level increase. We also fit models using the full cannabis involvement group classification. Additional analyses examined the reverse direction, with binge eating predicting cannabis outcomes, and described the order of onset among adults with both conditions. Two sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, gradient models were additionally adjusted for lifetime alcohol use disorder. Second, we repeated analyses using binge eating measures referring to the past 12 months and cannabis use frequency during the past 12 months.

Psychiatric Burden and Drug Use Course. We classified adults into four mutually exclusive groups: neither condition, binge eating only, cannabis-involved drug use disorder only, or both conditions. We compared the weighted prevalence of nine psychiatric and functional outcomes across groups and estimated adjusted odds ratios using the neither-condition group as the reference. Additive interaction was assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction, with confidence intervals estimated using the delta method. Within the cannabisinvolved drug use disorder group, we compared adults with and without recurrent binge eating on drug use course and treatment outcomes. These analyses used logistic regression for binary outcomes and a quasi-Poisson model for years of drug problems.

Functional Disability.

Across the same four groups, we estimated weighted mean WHODAS domain scores and adjusted mean differences using the neither-condition group as the reference. Days out of role were modeled using quasi-Poisson rate ratios. To estimate the annual excess days out of role associated with co-occurring binge eating, we calculated the unadjusted difference in weighted 30-day means between adults with both conditions and those with cannabis-involved drug use disorder only, multiplied this difference by 12, and estimated a confidence interval using the delta method. We also estimated adjusted differences in work performance among NCS-R respondents. Within the cannabis-involved drug use disorder group, additional models estimated the association of recurrent binge eating with disability outcomes. As a sensitivity analysis, disability models were additionally adjusted for lifetime major depression and generalized anxiety disorder.

Demographic Patterning.

Within the cannabis-involved drug use disorder group, we estimated the weighted prevalence of recurrent binge eating by sex and by race and ethnicity, as well as each demographic group's weighted share of all adults with recurrent binge eating.

We then estimated the cannabis involvement gradient separately within each demographic group and tested moderation using interaction terms between cannabis involvement level and sex and between cannabis involvement level and race and ethnicity. Interaction terms were evaluated using design-adjusted Wald tests.

Results

Prevalence of Binge Eating Across Cannabis Involvement

Table 1 presents the weighted prevalence of each binge eating phenotype by level of cannabis involvement (see also Supplementary Figure S1). Recurrent binge eating affected 5.0% of the total sample (95% CI [4.4, 5.7]) and 3.1% of adults who reported no drug use (95% CI [2.6, 3.7]). Prevalence increased to 6.9% among adults who used cannabis without a drug use disorder (95% CI [5.6, 8.5]) and was highest among adults with a cannabis-involved drug use disorder. Within this group, prevalence was 11.2% overall (95% CI [8.4, 14.7]), 15.0% among those who reported cannabis as their only drug (95% CI [6.7, 30.3]), and 10.6% among those who also used other drugs (95% CI [7.9, 14.1]). Within the cannabis-involved drug use disorder group, 5.5% met criteria for DSM-IV BED (95% CI [3.9, 7.8]) and 7.2% met criteria for BED or BN (95% CI [5.3, 9.8]). In adjusted models, the odds of each binge eating phenotype approximately doubled with each increase in cannabis involvement level (adjusted ORs = 2.11 to 2.32, all p < .001). The gradient remained significant after additional adjustment for lifetime alcohol use disorder (adjusted OR for recurrent binge eating = 1.96, 95% CI [1.51, 2.54]; Supplementary Tables S2 and S3).

The association was also present when modeled in the reverse direction and when measures referring to the past 12 months were examined. Recurrent binge eating was associated with higher odds of cannabis-involved drug use disorder (adjusted OR = 2.56, 95% CI [1.73, 3.80]) and cannabis-only drug use disorder (adjusted OR = 3.31, 95% CI [1.47, 7.49]; Supplementary Table S3). Among adults reporting cannabis use in the past 12 months, recurrent binge eating was reported by 5.1% of those who used cannabis less than weekly and 4.8% of those who used cannabis weekly or more often, compared with 1.9% of those who reported no cannabis use during the past 12 months. The adjusted odds increased across levels of cannabis use frequency (adjusted OR per level = 1.76, 95% CI [1.34, 2.31]; Supplementary Table S4). Among the 58 adults with both conditions whose ages of onset could be determined, binge eating preceded the first drug problem in 48.3% of cases and followed it in 41.4%.

Psychiatric Burden and Drug Use Course

Table 2 (Panel A) and Figure 1 present psychiatric outcomes across the four condition groups. Adults with both conditions had the highest prevalence of all suicidality outcomes and of mood and anxiety disorders. A lifetime suicide attempt was reported by 27.8% of adults with both conditions, 17.3% of those with cannabis-involved drug use disorder only, 10.5% of those with binge eating only, and 3.3% of those with neither condition. Compared with adults with neither condition, those with both conditions had substantially higher adjusted odds of a lifetime suicide attempt (adjusted OR = 7.94, 95% CI [3.85, 16.38]). Adjusted odds were also elevated for suicidal ideation, suicide plans, major depression, generalized anxiety disorder, and fair or poor mental and physical health (Table 2; full contrasts in Supplementary Table S5). Obesity and current unemployment did not differ significantly across groups. Tests of additive interaction were not significant for any outcome (Supplementary Table S6).

Among adults with cannabis-involved drug use disorder, those with recurrent binge eating were more likely to report giving up important activities because of drug use (60.2% versus 35.9%; adjusted OR = 2.61, 95% CI [1.43, 4.79]) and continuing to use drugs despite physical or emotional problems (56.8% versus 37.1%; adjusted OR = 2.12, 95% CI [1.30, 3.45]).

Other indicators of drug use course, treatment, and years of drug problems did not differ significantly (Supplementary Table S7 and Figure S4).

Functional Disability

Figure 2 and Table 2 (Panel B) present functional disability across the four groups.

Compared with adults with neither condition, adjusted WHODAS time out of role scores were 11.5 points higher among those with binge eating only (95% CI [6.8, 16.2]), 8.2 points higher among those with cannabis-involved drug use disorder only (95% CI [5.0, 11.3]), and 15.1 points higher among those with both conditions (95% CI [6.2, 24.0]). Role impairment showed the same pattern, with adjusted differences of 3.7, 3.1, and 9.4 points, respectively (all p < .05).

Across all domains, binge eating alone was associated with at least as much disability as cannabis-involved drug use disorder alone. This pattern remained after additional adjustment for lifetime major depression and generalized anxiety disorder (Supplementary Table S8).

Adults with both conditions reported an average of 4.6 days out of role in the past 30 days, compared with 2.3 days among those with neither condition (rate ratio = 2.00, 95% CI [1.27, 3.13]). Compared with adults with cannabis-involved drug use disorder only, those with both conditions had an estimated 8.7 additional days out of role per year, although the confidence interval was wide and included zero (95% CI [-15.2, 32.6]; Supplementary Table S9). Self-rated work performance on days worked was significantly lower only among adults with both conditions (adjusted difference = -0.60 on a 0 to 10 scale, 95% CI [-1.17, -0.04]). Within the cannabis-involved drug use disorder group, recurrent binge eating was associated with higher scores for time out of role (+5.9 points) and role impairment (+6.5 points), although these estimates were imprecise (Supplementary Table S8 and Figure S3).

Demographic Patterning

Table 3 presents the prevalence of recurrent binge eating within the cannabis-involved drug use disorder group by sex and by race and ethnicity. Recurrent binge eating was about twice as prevalent among women (17.4%, 95% CI [11.4, 25.6]) as among men (8.6%, 95% CI [5.5, 13.1]). However, men accounted for 54.1% of adults with both conditions. Prevalence did not differ significantly across racial and ethnic groups and ranged from 9.7% to 12.3%.

The cannabis involvement gradient was positive in every demographic subgroup, with adjusted ORs ranging from 1.58 to 2.49 (see Supplementary Figure S2). The gradient was statistically significant in all groups except the Asian and other group. Tests of interaction were not significant for sex (p = .060) or race and ethnicity (p = .194).

Discussion

This study provides national estimates of binge eating co-occurrence among adults with cannabis-involved drug use disorder. About one in nine of these adults reported recurrent binge eating and about one in eighteen met DSM-IV criteria for BED. These rates are roughly two to four times those in the general population and among adults reporting no drug use (populationwide rates of recurrent binge eating and BED were 5.0% and 2.4%; rates among adults reporting no drug use were 3.1% and 1.3%). Prevalence of binge eating also rose with level of cannabis involvement. In terms of clinical burden, adults with both cannabis-involved drug use disorder and binge eating carried the highest prevalence of suicidality and of mood and anxiety disorders. Binge eating alone was associated with disability comparable to that of a cannabisinvolved drug use disorder alone. Adults with both conditions had the highest role impairment.

The added contribution of binge eating within the disorder group was directionally consistent but imprecise.

Prevalence of Co-occurring CUD and Binge Eating

Restricting attention to BED would have understated the prevalence of recurrent binge eating among those with cannabis-involved drug use disorder by roughly half (5.5% versus 11.2%). The overlap is also visible from the opposite direction. Wilkinson et al. (2024) found that more than 23% of treatment-seeking adults with binge eating reported recent cannabis use.

Recurrent binge eating was associated with higher odds of cannabis-involved drug use disorder.

The association was not an artifact of lifetime recall. For example, past-year binge eating was more common among past-year cannabis users (5.1% and 4.8%) than among nonusers (1.9%).

Finally, among the 58 adults with both conditions and dateable onsets, binge eating preceded the first drug problem in 48% of cases and followed the first drug problem in 41% of cases. This pattern is compatible with a bidirectional relationship.

Clinical Significance

At least one lifetime suicide attempt was reported by 27.8% of adults with both cannabisinvolved drug use disorder and binge eating, while prior suicide attempts were reported by only 17.3% of those with cannabis-involved drug use disorder alone, 10.5% with binge eating alone, and 3.3% with neither (i.e., those with both conditions had 7.94 times higher adjusted odds of suicide attempt than those with neither (95% CI [3.85, 16.38])). Among those with cannabisinvolved drug use disorder, recurrent binge eating was also associated with having given up important activities because of drug use and with continued use despite physical or emotional problems. Clinically, this suggests that binge eating identifies a subgroup of adults with cannabis-involved drug use disorder who are at higher risk. These adults may benefit from suicide risk assessment and from integrated treatment. Future research should test in prospective cohorts whether binge eating predicts poorer response to cannabis treatment. Trials should then evaluate whether adding binge eating treatment improves both cannabis and eating outcomes. Replication in samples collected after DSM-5 and after cannabis legalization is also needed.

Functional Disability

Binge eating is sometimes treated as a secondary concern in substance use research.

Our results do not support that view. For example, binge eating alone was associated with a +11.5 point increase in time out of role. The corresponding increase for cannabis-involved drug use disorder alone was +8.2 points. These differences were not explained by co-occurring major depression or generalized anxiety disorder, indicating that binge eating confers disability beyond the internalizing conditions that commonly accompany it. Adults with both conditions scored highest on role impairment (13.9 versus 8.2 among adults with binge eating alone, 7.0 among adults with cannabis-involved drug use disorder alone, and 4.5 among adults with neither condition). They reported an average of 4.6 days out of role in the past 30 days versus 2.3 days among adults with neither condition (3.8 days among adults with cannabis-involved drug use disorder alone and 4.5 days among adults with binge eating alone). The cost was not limited to missed days. Adults with both conditions also performed worse on the days they did work, a pattern not seen with either condition alone. Among adults with cannabis-involved drug use disorder, binge eating was associated with a directionally higher but imprecise adjusted difference in role impairment (+6.5 points). The excess in role impairment exceeded the sum of the two single-condition excesses. This pattern would indicate that the two conditions compound one another's impairment beyond a simple sum, although formal tests of additive interaction did not reach statistical significance.

Stability of Patterns Across Sex, Race, and Ethnicity

Women with a cannabis-involved drug use disorder were about twice as likely as men to report recurrent binge eating (17.4% versus 8.6%), a difference that is consistent with the wider eating disorder literature (Hudson et al., 2007; Kessler et al., 2013). However, men are far more likely to have CUD than women (Hasin et al., 2016). As a result, men made up about half of adults with both conditions in the CPES sample. Elran-Barak et al. (2025) reported an association between cannabis use and binge eating among young women but not young men.

In our data the cannabis gradient was positive in both sexes, though the association was stronger among women (adjusted OR = 2.44 versus 1.78 in men). The test for a sex difference was not statistically significant (p = .060).

Prevalence of co-occurring cannabis-involved drug use disorder and binge eating did not differ significantly by race or ethnicity (9.7% to 12.3% across four groups), and the binge eating -cannabis involvement gradient was positive in every group (imprecise for Asian and other adults: adjusted OR = 1.58, 95% CI [0.93, 2.68]). This agrees with evidence that BED prevalence is similar across ethnic groups in the CPES (Marques et al., 2011). While a lack of significant differences is not proof of equivalence, there is no empirical basis for presuming binge eating to be irrelevant to racially minoritized patients with cannabis-involved drug use disorder.

Clinical Implications

Based on the above estimates in our sample, about one in nine adults with cannabisinvolved drug use disorder would screen positive for recurrent binge eating. BED often goes undetected and untreated (Giel et al., 2022; Kessler et al., 2013). Brief screening in substance use treatment settings, applied regardless of sex or race, is therefore well supported.

Psychosocial treatments can reduce cannabis use but enduring abstinence is uncommon (Connor et al., 2021). Whether, and to what extent, untreated binge eating contributes to this outcome is a priority for future CUD treatment studies.

Limitations

There are several limitations that must be considered when interpreting our results. First, the design of CPES is cross-sectional and relies on retrospective reports from lay-administered interviews. Second, the interview used assesses drug use disorder criteria in aggregate across drug classes, and a CUD-specific diagnosis was possible only for respondents who reported cannabis as their only drug (n = 128). The remaining 612 of the 740 adults with a cannabisinvolved drug use disorder also met criteria involving other drugs. The co-occurring group was small (n = 99) and some subgroup cells were modest (for example, 66 adults with the disorder in the Asian and other racial groups). Interaction tests and within-group comparisons therefore had limited power. Third, the CPES surveys were conducted between 2001 and 2003, predating DSM-5 diagnostic classification of BED and the later rise in CUD (Hasin et al., 2015). They also predate recent legalization and changes in potency (Connor et al., 2021). The estimate of 922,000 affected adults refers to the population of that period. DSM-IV criteria for BED require binge episodes at least twice weekly for six months. The behavioral phenotype required episodes at least twice weekly for at least three months. The diagnostic group is therefore nested within the phenotype group by design. DSM-5 lowered the BED threshold to once weekly for three months (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Because the CPES eating disorder module screened for binge eating at least twice weekly, respondents who met only the lower DSM-5 frequency threshold were not assessed further. DSM-5 criteria therefore could not be applied retrospectively. All prevalence estimates reported here are based on DSM-IV criteria and are likely conservative relative to estimates using current DSM-5 criteria. Finally, the eating disorder module was administered to a random half of NCS-R Part II respondents, and the supplied CPES composite weights do not incorporate an adjustment for this module-level subsampling. We therefore report weighted prevalences and associations rather than absolute national counts.

Conclusion

Binge eating is common, clinically important, and demographically widespread among U.S. adults with CUD. Adults with both conditions carry a particularly high burden of suicidality and role impairment. Cannabis research and treatment should assess binge eating routinely and across demographic groups.
