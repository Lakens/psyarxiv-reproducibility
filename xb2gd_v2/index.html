Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Creative engagement may shape not only the ideas people generate but also their willingness to take calculated risks in subsequent decisions, yet this possibility remains underexplored. Across three studies, we examined this relationship and whether general self-efficacy or affective state accounts for the relationship. Study 1 (N = 228) used a cross-sectional survey and found that everyday creative ideation behavior was positively associated with overall and domain-specific risk-taking propensity. General self-efficacy did not mediate this association. Study 2 (N = 83) experimentally compared calculated risk taking after creative drawing versus standard coloring using a binary lottery choice. Creative drawing increased affective dominance but did not significantly influence calculated risk taking. Study 3 (N = 94) compared calculated risk taking after creative writing versus standard typing using a six-level investmentchoice task. Creative writing increased both calculated risk taking and affective dominance, but the change in dominance did not mediate the effect on calculated risk taking. These findings suggest that creative engagement can influence subsequent calculated risk taking, although the effect may depend on the creative task and the sensitivity of the risk measure. Neither general self-efficacy nor affective dominance explained this relationship, highlighting the need to investigate alternative psychological mechanisms.

1 Introduction

When developing a novel idea, composing a novel story, or drawing a novel image, the creators must move beyond safe and conventional responses and pursue untested possibilities that may fail. Creative activity, the process of generating ideas or products that are both novel and useful or appropriate (Runco & Jaeger, 2012), therefore involves an element of risk taking, broadly defined as engaging in behaviors or making choices under uncertainty that may produce beneficial or adverse consequences (Duell & Steinberg, 2020; Trimpop, 1994). Consistent with this overlap, creativity and risk taking have long been regarded as closely related (Bonetto et al., 2020; Crepaldi et al., 2024; Graciano et al., 2023). Yet existing research has largely examined this relationship through stable individual differences in creative personality, creative ability, and risk-taking propensity. It therefore remains unclear whether creative engagement itself influences subsequent risk takin, particularly calculated risk taking involving deliberate consideration of potential outcomes (Zhang & Smith, 2025). If it does, creative engagement may influence later innovation and decisions beyond the creative task, suggesting that it is not merely a source of creative output but also an experience that shapes subsequent decision makings. The present research primarily addresses this question by examining whether brief creative engagement can influence subsequent calculated risk taking.

1.1 Creativity and risk taking

Research has examined the relationship between creativity and risk taking in both directions. One line of research has examined risk taking as an antecedent or facilitating condition for creativity (Beghetto et al., 2021; Dewett, 2006; Graciano et al., 2023; Harada, 2020; Shen et al., 2018). Within entrepreneurial and educational contexts, risk taking has been conceptualized as a facilitating condition that helps translate creativity into innovation (Graciano et al., 2023). Park et al. (2024) found that employees generated more ideas on days when their felt responsibility for change and willingness to take risks were both high, and daily idea generation statistically mediated the association between this motivational congruence and creative performance. Beghetto et al. (2021) further reported that intellectual risk taking strengthened the association between creative confidence and creative behavior. In addition, research also probed the relationships between risk taking, divergent thinking and convergent thinking performance. While Shen et al. (2018) found that lower risk taking was only associated with better convergent thinking performance, Harada (2020) found that risk taking was only associated with better divergent thinking performance. A recent finding indicated that willingness to take creative risks was positively related to originality but negatively related to usefulness, with creative metacognition strengthening the former association and attenuating the latter (Woo & Lee, 2024). Overall, these findings generally suggest that a willingness to take risks can facilitate creativity, although its benefits may vary across creative outcomes.

Another line of research has considered the reverse direction, investigating whether creativity is associated with risk taking (Bonetto et al., 2020; Crepaldi et al., 2024; Tyagi et al., 2017). Charyton et al. (2013) found that creative attributes (unconventional, insightful, confident, inventive, reflective, and broad interests) were associated with greater willingness to express unconventional ideas despite possible criticism or reputational costs. Tyagi et al. (2017) found that several measures of creativity were most consistently related to social risk taking, whereas their associations with financial, ethical, health, and recreational risks were weaker or inconsistent. Bonetto et al. (2020) further observed such positive associations between measures of creativity and social risk taking. A recent review also suggest an overall positive relationship between creativity and risk taking, suggesting people with lower creativity tend to avoid risky situations and decisions, while highly-creative individuals tend not to flinch from challenging situations (Crepaldi et al., 2024). These findings indicate that creativity-related traits and abilities are also closely associated with individuals' willingness to take risks.

Taken together, existing research has mostly either treated risk taking as an antecedent of creativity or examined cross-sectional associations between risk taking and relatively stable creative traits and abilities. However, these associations may partly reflect shared personality dispositions, particularly openness to experience. Research has shown that openness to experience is not only positively associated with divergent thinking (Grajzel et al., 2023), predicts everyday creative activity (Jauk et al., 2014), and is the Big Five trait most strongly associated with risk propensity (Highhouse et al., 2022). These shared dispositional correlates limit the interpretation of existing findings.

In addition, recent research distinguishes calculated from spontaneous risk taking: the former involves deliberate and planned risk taking after careful consideration of outcomes, whereas the latter is impulsive, emotion-driven, and thrill seeking, with little deliberation (Zhang & Smith, 2025). Previous creativity research has rarely differentiated these two styles, leaving their respective relationships with creativity unclear. Calculated risk taking is particularly relevant because creative work often requires intentionally pursuing uncertain but potentially valuable ideas after weighing their possible costs and benefits. Therefore, to enrich understanding of the relationship between creativity and risk taking, experimental research is needed to determine whether creative engagement itself influences subsequent calculated risk taking.

Although direct evidence remains limited, the psychological consequences of creative engagement provide a basis for expecting such an effect on subsequent calculated risk taking.

1.2 Psychological consequences of creative engagement

Evidence has already shown that creative engagement changes how people feel and experience themselves during and after the creative process. Creative ideation increased participants' feeling of autonomy, although it also heightened concern about how their ideas would be evaluated (Kim et al., 2023). A multigroup randomized study similarly found that creative tasks increased positive emotion partly through enhancing autonomous self-expression (Bujacz et al., 2016). Even brief creative activities have been associated with improved mood. For instance, creating a mandala reduced negative affect relative to a control activity (Babouchkina & Robbins, 2015), and a single session of open-studio art making was followed by higher positive affect and lower negative affect (Kaimal & Ray, 2017). Besides, positive and negative affect also change over the course of a creative task rather than only at its end (Montag-Smit & Keith, 2023). Moreover, daily creative activity is also associated with changes in affective state. Results showed that daily creativity also predicts higher activated positive affect and flourishing on the following day (Conner et al., 2018; Conner & Silvia, 2015; Smith et al., 2022). More recent experience-sampling evidence showed that everyday creative engagement was accompanied by greater autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which in turn were associated with higher positive affect (Putney et al., 2024), and people were more likely to engage in everyday creative activity on days when they felt happy and energetic (Silvia et al., 2014). Population research found that people who participated more frequently in creative and cultural activities reported higher well-being. (Cuypers et al., 2012; Fancourt & Finn, 2019; Finn et al., 2025).

These findings indicate that both brief and sustained creative engagement can shape individuals' affective experiences.

Beyond its effects on affective experience, creative engagement may also influence subsequent decision making. Krause et al. (2024) found brief creative ideation increased subsequent reward seeking and indulgent choices, partly through activation of the behavioral approach system. Xu et al. (2021) found that engaging in a creative activity enhanced individuals' feeling of autonomy, thus inducing a positive affective state, which in turn led to higher donation behaviors. Charness and Grieco (2022) recently found that participants who completed creative rather than routine tasks exhibited greater ambiguity tolerance during a monetary lottery. Creative engagement even induced a preference for horror entertainment through higher self-efficacy (Yang & Liu, 2024). These experiments indicate that creative engagement can carry over to subsequent decisions through affective and cognitive-motivational processes.

Overall, research on the affective and decision-related consequences of creative engagement provides converging grounds for expecting it to influence subsequent calculated risk-taking behavior. Yet, much less is known about whether creative engagement influences subsequent calculated risk-taking behavior and, if so, through what candidate psychological pathways.

1.3 Creative engagement may affect risk taking through general self-efficacy

General self-efficacy, the broad belief that one can manage challenges and reach a goal (Bandura, 1977; Luszczynska et al., 2005; Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995), may serve as a plausible pathway linking creative engagement to calculated risk taking.

According to social cognitive theory, successful experiences contribute to efficacy beliefs, which subsequently influence people's choice of activities, effort, and persistence when they encounter obstacles (Bandura, 1977; Judge et al., 2007).

Research showed that participants reported higher general self-efficacy after a session of free art making (Forgeard et al., 2021; Kaimal & Ray, 2017). Experimentally induced perceptions of competence increased opportunity recognition and risk taking, but the effect did not generalize to a similar task (Krueger & Dickson, 1994). Self-efficacy has also been shown to mediate the effect of creative engagement on subsequent preference for horror entertainment, although the outcome did not involve a conventional risktaking decision (Yang & Liu, 2024). These studies suggest that creative engagement may strengthen general self-efficacy, which could increase willingness to pursue uncertain or challenging options. However, whether general self-efficacy explains the effect of creative engagement on calculated risk-taking decisions remains unknown.

1.4 Creative engagement may affect risk taking through affective state

Changes in affective state may provide another pathway through which creative engagement influences subsequent calculated risk taking. Within the Pleasure-Arousal-Dominance Framework, affective state is characterized along three dimensions: pleasure, arousal, and dominance (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974; Russell & Mehrabian, 1977). Pleasure reflects hedonic valence, arousal reflects the level of activation, and dominance reflects the subjective experience of being in control rather than controlled in a given situation (Bakker et al., 2014; Mehrabian, 1996). Previous research suggests that engaging in creative activities can enhance positive affect and strengthen individuals' sense of autonomy (Kim et al., 2023; Montag-Smit & Keith, 2023; Putney et al., 2024). Because open-ended creative tasks permit self-directed decisions about how ideas are developed, the autonomy afforded by creative engagement may also temporarily heighten felt control or affective dominance.

Moreover, research has also shown that affective responses can influence subsequent decision making. The Risk-as-Feelings Perspective proposes that immediate affective responses can influence risk taking independently of deliberate evaluations of probabilities and outcomes (Loewenstein et al., 2001). The Appraisal-Tendency Framework further suggests that affective states shape subsequent judgments not only through their pleasure but also through the situational appraisals they carry, including certainty, responsibility, and control (Han et al., 2007; Lerner & Keltner, 2001; Lerner et al., 2015). Consistent with this account, fear and anger, despite sharing negative valence, exerted opposite effects on risk perception and choice partly because they involve different appraisals of certainty and personal control (Lerner & Keltner, 2001). Experimentally increasing the sense that one's actions cause and control outcomes has been associated with greater perceived ability to control risks and greater willingness to take them (Damen, 2019). More recent research similarly found that greater action-outcome predictability simultaneously increased sense of agency and risk taking, although the study did not observe that agency itself caused the change in risk taking (Zhu et al., 2025). Together, these accounts suggest that affective states influence risk taking not only through valence but also through appraisals of certainty and control, suggesting affective state, especially affective dominance, as a theoretically candidate pathway linking creative engagement to subsequent calculated risk taking. This study also aimed to explore whether changes in affective states mediated the effect of creative engagement on calculated risk-taking decisions.

1.5 The present research

The present research examined whether creative engagement can influence subsequent risk-taking behavior through three studies. We further investigated general self-efficacy and affective state as two potential psychological pathways underlying this relationship. As a preliminary study, Study 1 used a cross-sectional survey to examine whether everyday creative engagement was positively associated with general risktaking propensity and the potential mediating role of general self-efficacy. Studies 2 and 3 narrowed the investigation to calculated risk taking by experimentally testing whether brief creative engagement influenced subsequent financial risk decisions, in which potential gains and losses were explicitly presented. They also examined whether changes in affective state accounted for this effect. In Study 2, participants completed either a creative drawing task or a standard coloring task before making a binary lottery choice with real monetary consequences. Study 3 extended the experimental investigation by comparing creative writing with standard typing and using an investment-choice task comprising six ordered levels of risk and expected return.

Together, these studies examined the relationship between creative engagement and calculated risk taking, as well as the potential underlying psychological pathways, across different research designs, creative modalities, and measures of risk taking. The specific hypotheses are presented in the corresponding study sections. The study protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of XXX University (masked for peer review; ID: 2025060) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2 Study 1

Study 1 used data from a cross-sectional survey to examine whether everyday creative engagement is associated with risk-taking propensity and to explore the potential role of general self-efficacy in this association. We proposed the following hypotheses: (H1) creative engagement would be positively associated with risk-taking propensity; and (H2) general self-efficacy would statistically mediate this association.

2.1 Method

2.1.1 Participants

A priori power analysis (two-tailed Pearson product-moment correlation, power = 0.80, α = 0.05, ρ = 0.30) was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007). The analysis indicated that a minimum sample size of 84 participants was required. This study recruited a total of 247 university students online. Nineteen participants were excluded from the final sample for failing to follow the task instructions. The final sample therefore consisted of 228 participants (158 females), with a mean age of 19.89 years (SD = 0.55). All participants provided online informed consent prior to the survey and received course credits for their participation.

2.1.2 Measures

Creative ideation behavior. We used everyday creative ideation behavior to index everyday creative engagement, which was assessed with the 23-item Runco Ideational Behavior Scale (Runco et al., 2001), which measures everyday ideational behaviors on a 1 (never) to 5 (just about every day) scale (e.g., 'I come up with a lot of ideas or solutions to problems'). Internal consistency was acceptable in the present sample (Cronbach's α = 0.89).

General self-efficacy.

General self-efficacy was assessed with 10-item General Self-Efficacy Scale (Wang et al., 2001), which measures individuals' generalized, dispositional belief in their ability to cope with difficulties, solve problems, and achieve goals on a 1 (completely incorrect) to 4 (completely correct) scale (e.g., 'I can always manage to solve difficult problems if I try hard enough'). Higher total scores indicate greater general confidence. Internal consistency was acceptable in the present sample (Cronbach's α = 0.84).

Risk-taking propensity.

Risk-taking propensity was assessed with 40-item Domain-Specific Risk-Taking Scale (Weber et al., 2002), covering ethical, investment, gambling, health/safety, recreation, and social domains. Items were rated from 1 (very unwilling) to 5 (very willing). Scores were summed within each domain, with higher scores indicating greater propensity to take risks. Cronbach's α values were .76, .78, .83, .67, .78, and .57 for the six domains, respectively, and .90 for the overall scale in this study.

2.1.3 Procedure

The survey was administered online via Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/). After reading the study information and providing informed consent, participants completed demographic questions, followed by measures of creative ideation behavior, general self-efficacy, and risk-taking propensity.

2.1.4 Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations were first computed for all variables. To test Hypothesis I, a hierarchical linear regression was conducted on total risk-taking propensity. Gender and general self-efficacy were entered in Step 1, followed by creative ideation behavior in Step 2 to assess its incremental contribution to explaining risk-taking propensity. Similar regression models were repeated for each risk domain as supplementary analyses, with p-values corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

To test Hypothesis II, a mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS Model 4 to examine whether general self-efficacy mediated the association between creative ideation behavior and risk-taking propensity. Indirect effects were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap samples and considered statistically significant when their 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (CI) excluded zero (Hayes, 2013).

2.3 Interim discussion

Study 1 found that creative ideation behavior was positively associated with overall risk-taking propensity and explained additional variance beyond gender and general self-efficacy. It also positively predicted all six risk domains in the adjusted regression models. Hypothesis 1 was supported. Although creative ideation behavior was positively related to general self-efficacy, self-efficacy did not predict overall risktaking propensity, and the indirect effect was not significant. Hypothesis 2 was not supported. Given that Study 1 provided no evidence that general self-efficacy mediated the association between creative ideation behavior and risk-taking propensity, general self-efficacy was included only as a covariate in the relevant analyses in Studies 2 and 3.

Moreover, because Study 1 did not assess openness to experience, it could not rule out the possibility that this shared dispositional factor contributed to the observed association (Highhouse et al., 2022). Moreover, its risk measure did not distinguish calculated from spontaneous risk taking. Studies 2 and 3 therefore used randomized controlled experiments and financial decision tasks with explicit potential gains and losses to examine whether brief creative engagement influences subsequent calculated risk taking. Study 2 extended Study 1 by experimentally examining whether engaging in a brief creative activity influences subsequent calculated risk taking. Given that monetary paradigms allow probabilities and potential outcomes to be explicitly specified and provide a direct behavioral measure of calculated risk taking, a monetary choice task was used in this study. Participants were randomly assigned to complete either a creative drawing task or a standard coloring control task before making a binary lottery choice with real monetary consequences. Affective states were measured before and after the activity to examine whether the creative activity altered participants' affective state and whether change in affective state mediated the effect of creative activity on calculated risk taking. We proposed the following hypotheses: (H3) creative ideation behavior would increase calculated risk taking; (H4) creative ideation behavior would induce higher level of affective valence, arousal, and dominance; and (H5) affective state would mediate the association between creative activity and calculated risk taking.

3.1 Method

3.1.1 Participants

A priori power analysis (Pearson's chi-square test for a 2 × 2 contingency table, power = 0.80, α = 0.05, Cohen's w = 0.30, df = 1) was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007). The analysis indicated that a minimum total sample size of 88 participants was required. This study recruited a total of 90 native Mandarin Chinese speakers. Seven participants were excluded from the final sample for failing to follow the task instructions. The final sample consisted of 83 participants (M = 19.88, SD = 0.43; 69 females), randomly assigned to either the creative activity group (n = 40) or the control group (n = 43). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and provided written informed consent prior to the experiment. Participants received participation gifts, course credit, and ¥3 for their participation.

3.1.2 Measures

Risk-taking decision task. Calculated risk-taking behavior was assessed using a lottery task with real monetary consequences. Participants received a ¥3 endowment and chose between keeping it as a guaranteed payment or exchanging it for a 10% chance of receiving ¥10 and a 90% chance of receiving nothing. Before making their choice, participants estimated the proportion of the wheel occupied by a red area and their subjective probability of winning, both on a scale from 0‰ to 1000‰. Lottery entry was coded as 1 and non-entry as 0. Participants who entered the lottery proceeded to a computerized roulette wheel, with outcomes generated independently for each participant at a fixed 10% winning probability. Course credit and the participation gift were unaffected by lottery outcomes.

Affective states. Affective states were assessed using the Self-Assessment Manikin (Bradley & Lang, 1994), a nonverbal pictorial self-report measure of pleasure, arousal, and dominance. Each dimension was rated on a 9-point scale. Pleasure ranged from very unpleasant (1) to very pleasant (9), arousal from very calm (1) to very excited (9), and dominance from feeling controlled (1) to feeling in control (9).

Subjective creative experience.

Subjective creative experience was assessed with a single item: "While coloring the elephants, I tried to be as creative as possible."

Responses were made on a 7-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater perceived creativity during the task.

General self-efficacy.

General self-efficacy was also assessed using the same 10item General Self-Efficacy Scale used in Study 1. Internal consistency was .86 in the present sample.

3.1.3 Experimental design and procedure

This study employed a one-factor, two-level between-subjects design, with participants randomly assigned to either a creative activity group or a control group.

Following the drawing paradigm of Xu et al. (2021), participants in the creative activity group completed a 6-min creative drawing task, whereas those in the control group completed a 6-min standard coloring task.

The experiment was conducted individually in a quiet laboratory. Upon arrival, participants were seated at a table and provided written informed consent. They first completed the demographic measures and the pretest of affective states. Next, participants in the creative activity group used multicolored crayons to color and decorate two elephant outlines, whereas those in the control group used only a gray crayon to completely fill in both outlines. Paper size, outline dimensions and placement, and the experimental setting were held constant across participants.

Participants in the creative activity group received the following instructions: You now have a box of crayons in different colors and two blank elephant pictures. You may use any number and any color of crayons to color and decorate the elephants. Be as creative as possible and create two unique elephants. Do not worry about whether your drawings are conventional or realistic. Participants in the control group received the following instructions: You now have a gray crayon and two blank elephant pictures.

Please use the gray crayon to color the elephants and completely fill in both pictures.

During the task, the experimenter provided no feedback regarding the aesthetic quality, completeness, or creativity of the drawings.

After the drawing task, participants rated their subjective creative experience and posttest of affective states, followed by measures of general self-efficacy. Finally, participants completed the risk-taking decision task, which comprised a visual areaestimation task, a subjective winning-probability judgment, and a lottery choice. 3.3 Interim discussion

Study 2 found that the creative drawing task produced a stronger subjective creative experience than the standard coloring task. However, the creative activity group did not exhibit significantly greater calculated risk taking than the control group, yielding no evidence for Hypothesis 3. The creative activity increased affective dominance, but change in dominance did not predict calculated risk taking, and the mediating effect was not significant. Therefore, Hypothesis 4 was partially supported, but Hypothesis 5 was not supported.

With respect to the null finding of group difference in calculated risk taking, the features of the creative task and binary monetary choice task may offer some interpretations. Creativity comprises both domain-general and domain-specific components, suggesting that visual and verbal creative activities may engage partly different processes (Acar et al., 2024; Cubillos-Pinilla et al., 2025). This study employed a visual creative activity, and the effects of creative engagement may vary across task modalities. In addition, experimentally elicited risk preferences are sensitive to the format of the decision task (Holzmeister & Stefan, 2021). The relatively small stakes and single binary choice used in Study 2 may have limited the salience of the decision and provided insufficient differentiation among participants.

4 Study 3

Study 3 examined whether the null behavioral result in Study 2 might depend on the modality of creative task or the sensitivity of the calculated monetary risk-taking measure. Study 3 therefore examined the robustness of the effect using a verbal creative writing task and a more differentiated calculated risk-taking measure comprising six ordered risk levels. Participants completed either a generative creative writing task or a standard typing control task before choosing among six monetary investment options with progressively higher levels of risk and expected return. Given the increase in affective dominance observed in Study 2, Study 3 again assessed affective states to determine whether change in affective dominance mediated the effect of creative activity on subsequent calculated risk taking. Hypotheses 3-5 were again examined in Study 3.

4.1 Method

4.1.1 Participants

A priori power analysis (independent-samples t-test, power = 0.80, α = 0.05, d = 0.50, one-tailed) was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007). The analysis indicated that a minimum total sample size of 102 participants was required. The study recruited a total of 103 college students. All participants provided written informed consent before the experiment. Nine participants were excluded from the final sample for failing to follow the task instructions. The final sample therefore consisted of 94 participants (ages: M = 19.86, SD = 1.22; 76 females), with 49 assigned to the creative activity group and 45 to the control group. All participants had normal or corrected-tonormal vision and received ¥10 for their participation.

4.1.2 Measures

Risk-taking decision task.

Calculated risk-taking was assessed using an investment-choice task adapted from Bechler et al. (2024). Participants received a ¥10 cash endowment and selected one of six investment options. They were informed that potential gains or losses resulting from their choice would affect the amount they ultimately received. They could retain the full endowment without investing or select investment A, B, C, D, or E. These investments offered average returns of 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25%, with possible returns ranging from -15% to 25%, -30% to 50%, -45% to 75%, -60% to 100%, and -75% to 125%, respectively. Choices were coded from 0 (no investment) to 5 (investment E), with higher scores indicating a selection of riskier investment options. Although participants believed that their choices had real monetary consequences, all ultimately retained the ¥10 endowment after the experiment, regardless of their choice. The participants were fully debriefed after the experiment.

Baseline risk-taking propensity and general self-efficacy were assessed using the same measures as in Study 1. Cronbach's α was .83 for general self-efficacy and .88 for the overall risk-taking scale. The corresponding values for the ethical, investment, gambling, health/safety, recreational, and social subscales were .82, .68, .79, .77, .60, and .67, respectively. Affective states were assessed using the same SAM scales as in Study 2. Subjective creative experience was assessed using a single item: "While completing the task, I tried to be as creative as possible." Creative performance. Following Prabhakaran et al. (2014), each story was rated on five dimensions using 5-point scales (1 = low,5 = high): overall creativity (the extent to which the participant told a unique story that "came alive"), descriptiveness (the extent to which the participant added details), semantic flexibility (the range and manner in which the three keywords were used), humor (the inclusion of clever, witty, or amusing elements), and emotiveness (the expression of emotion and shifts in emotion). Four trained raters, blind to the study hypotheses, independently evaluated the stories. Interrater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) across the four raters. The ICCs were .85 for overall creativity, .77 for descriptiveness, .64 for semantic flexibility, .89 for humor, and .80 for emotiveness.

Ratings for each dimension were averaged across the four raters and used in all analyses.

4.1.3 Experimental design and procedure

This study employed a one-factor, two-level between-subjects design, with participants randomly assigned to either a creative activity group or a control group.

Following the paradigm of Yang and Liu (2024), participants were randomly assigned to a creative activity group, which completed a 7-min creative writing task session, or to a control group, which completed a 7-min standard typing task session.

The experiment was conducted individually in a quiet laboratory. Upon arrival, participants were seated at a table and provided written informed consent. They first completed the demographic measures and the pretest of affective states and baseline risk-taking propensity (Figure 3). Next, participants in the creative activity condition completed a 7-min creative writing session comprising two 3.5-min tasks, whereas those in the control condition completed a 7-min standard typing session comprising two 3.5-min tasks. Keyword presentation, text-box dimensions and position, and all other experimental settings were held constant across groups.

In both groups, the same set of keywords (i.e. Task A: moon, tree shadows, and song; Task B: campus, basketball, pencil case) was displayed at the top of the computer screen. Participants in the creative activity group wrote a short story incorporating all the keywords and were instructed to imagine vivid scenes, be as creative as possible, and not be constrained by realism. Participants in the control group transcribed a provided paragraph containing the same keywords into the text box without revising, elaborating, or adding original content. After the tasks, participants rated their subjective creative experience and affective states, then completed the general selfefficacy scale. They subsequently received ¥10 in cash and signed a receipt acknowledging payment before choosing whether to retain the money or select one of five investment options (A-E). After completing the task, participants were fully debriefed and informed that their investment choice would not affect their payment. to the creative activity group (n = 49) or the control group (n = 45). The pretest measured demographics, affective states, and baseline risk-taking propensity. The posttest included affective states, subjective creative experience, and the general selfefficacy.

4.1.4 Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.6.0. Independent-samples t tests compared the two groups on baseline risk-taking propensity and subjective creative experience, with the latter serving as a manipulation check.

To test Hypothesis III, the calculated risk taking was firstly submitted to an independent-samples t test with GROUP as the between-subject factor. Next, an ordinal logistic regression model with a cumulative logit link was fitted to the calculated risk taking, with GROUP as the predictor, controlling for gender and general self-efficacy.

The proportional odds assumption was assessed using a likelihood-ratio test of parallel lines.

To test Hypothesis IV, separate 2 (TIME: pretest vs. posttest) × 2 (GROUP: creative activity vs. control) mixed-design ANOVAs were conducted to assess changes in affective pleasure, arousal, and dominance, followed by simple-effects analyses where appropriate.

To test Hypothesis V, a mediation analysis was conducted to examine whether the change in affective dominance mediated the effect of GROUP on the calculated risk taking, adjusting for gender and general self-efficacy. Counterfactual mediation analysis was conducted in R 4.6.0 using linear regression for the mediator and ordered logistic regression for the outcome (Imai et al., 2010). The average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), and total effect were expressed as differences in the model-implied expected calculated risk taking (coded 0-5), with percentile 95% CI obtained from 5,000 nonparametric bootstrap samples.

Additionally, an exploratory analysis, Spearman's rank-order correlation was conducted to examine whether creative performance was related to calculated risk taking, controlling for gender and general self-efficacy.

4.2 Results

4.2.1 Creative activity induced stronger subjective creative experience

The independent-samples t test showed no significant group difference in baseline risk-taking propensity, t(92) = 1.21, p = .228, and general self-efficacy, t(92) = 1.85, p = .068.

The independent-samples t test on subjective creative experience showed that participants in the creative activity group reported stronger subjective creative experience (M = 4.98, SD = 1.16) than those in the control group (M = 2.71, SD = 1.58), t(92) = 7.98, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.65 (Figure 4A). This is consistent with a successful manipulation of the instructed task set, although the check rests on a single self-report item.

4.2.2 Creative activity significantly increased affective dominance

TIME × GROUP mixed-design ANOVAs were conducted on affective pleasure, arousal, and dominance. Results showed that the main effect of TIME was not significant on pleasure, F(1, 92) = 1.46, p = .231, ηp² = .02, but was significant for arousal, F(1, 92) = 35.96, p < .001, ηp² = .28. Post hoc tests showed that arousal increased from pretest (M = 4.64, SD = 1.76) to posttest (M = 5.78, SD = 1.67).

Results also demonstrated a significant TIME × GROUP interaction for dominance, F(1, 92) = 10.84, p = .001, ηp² = .11 (Figure 4B). Simple effect analysis showed that dominance increased from pretest (M = 5.71, SD = 1.66) to posttest (M = 6.61, SD = 1.51) in the creative activity group, whereas no significant difference between pretest and posttest was observed in the control group (p = 1.000, ηp² = .00).

4.2.3 Creative activity significantly increased calculated risk taking

The independent-samples t test showed that the calculated risk taking of creative activity group (M = 3.10, SD = 1.28) was significantly higher than that of control group (M = 2.31, SD = 1.44), t(92) = 2.82, p = .006, Cohen's d = 0.58 (Figure 4C).

An ordinal logistic regression examined the association between GROUP and calculated risk taking, controlling for gender and general self-efficacy. Participants in the creative activity group had higher odds of selecting higher-risk options than those in the control group, B = 0.96, SE = 0.39, p = .013, OR = 2.61, 95% CI [1.22, 5.57].

4.2.4 Creative performance was not associated with calculated risk taking

Partial Spearman rank-order correlations, controlling for gender and general selfefficacy, showed that none of the five dimensions of creative performance was significantly related to calculated risk taking, partial ρs = .02 to .19, ps ≥ .211. 4.2.5 Affective dominance did not mediate the relationship between creative activity and calculated risk taking

A mediation analysis was conducted using R 4.6.0, with GROUP as the predictor, change in affective dominance as the mediator, and calculated risk taking as an ordered outcome, controlling for gender and general self-efficacy. The creative activity positively predicted affective dominance, a = 0.93, SE = 0.28, t(90) = 3.30, p = .001, 95% CI [0.42, 1.45], but the change in dominance did not predict the calculated risk taking, b = 0.03, SE = 0.15, p = .866, 95% CI [0.76, 1.37]. 4.3 Interim discussion

Study 3 found that the creative writing task induced a stronger subjective creative experience, and led participants to select investment options with higher risk (for higher returns) than the standard typing task. Hypothesis 3 was supported in Study 3. This group difference remained significant in the ordinal logistic regression after adjusting for gender and general self-efficacy. Moreover, creative performance was not significantly associated with calculated risk taking, suggesting that the observed increase in calculated risk taking was associated with creative engagement rather than the quality of participants' creative output. Affective dominance also increased selectively in the creative activity group, but it did not predict calculated risk taking or mediate the association between creative activity and calculated risk taking. Therefore, Hypothesis 4 was partially supported, but Hypothesis 5 was not supported.

5 General Discussion

5.1 Main findings

The present research examined whether creative engagement is associated with and can influence subsequent calculated risk taking, as well as whether general selfefficacy and affective state account for this relationship. Study 1 showed that everyday creative engagement was positively associated with overall risk-taking propensity and with domain-specific forms of risk taking, even after controlling for gender and general self-efficacy. General self-efficacy, however, did not mediate this association. Hypothesis 1 was supported, but Hypothesis 2 was not supported. Study 2 showed that a brief creative drawing activity increased affective dominance but did not significantly affect calculated risk taking in a binary monetary lottery. Study 3 extended the experimental investigation using a verbal creative writing task and a more differentiated investment-choice measure. Participants who completed the creative writing task selected higher-risk, higher-return investment options than those who completed the standard typing task. Creative writing also increased affective dominance, but this change did not mediate the effect on calculated risk taking. Collectively, Hypothesis 3 and 4 was partially supported, but Hypothesis 5 was not supported.

5.2 Creative engagement was associated with subsequent calculated risk taking

Results showed that everyday creative engagement was positively associated with overall risk-taking propensity and a brief creative writing activity could increase subsequent calculated risk taking. The results extend previous research by examining creative engagement as a possible antecedent of subsequent risk taking (Bonetto et al., 2020; Crepaldi et al., 2024). The findings are consistent with an emerging body of research showing that a creative experience can carry over to subsequent choices.

Creative ideation has been found to increase reward seeking and indulgent behavior (Krause et al., 2024). Solving problems with an Aha! experience increased subsequent preference for risky monetary rewards (Yu et al., 2024). Charness and Grieco (2022) similarly found that completing a creative task increased investment in a lottery with unknown probabilities. The present research extends these studies by examining whether visual and verbal creative activities affect subsequent calculated risk taking using both a binary lottery choice and a graded investment choice. The results provide initial evidence that, under some task conditions, even risk taking involving explicit evaluation of potential outcomes can be shaped by prior creative engagement, thereby refining our understanding of the relationship between creative engagement and risk taking.

Study 1 also showed that everyday creative engagement was positively associated with risk-taking propensity across six specific domains: ethical, investment, gambling, health/safety, recreational, and social risk taking. This broader pattern extends the findings of Tyagi et al. (2017), who observed the most consistent associations between creativity and social risk taking, with weaker or less consistent associations in the financial, ethical, health/safety, and recreational domains. This finding raises the possibility that creative engagement may influence multiple forms of risk taking beyond calculated financial decisions. Future experimental research should test this possibility across different risk domains.

Study 3 further showed that creative performance was not significantly correlated with calculated risk taking. Although this null correlation should be interpreted cautiously, it may imply that engaging in the creative activity matters more than the quality of the creative outcomes. A similar distinction appears in research showing that subsequent behavior was influenced by actual participation in a creative activity, but not by simply priming the concept of creativity (Xu et al., 2021).

The nonsignificant group difference in calculated risk taking in Study 2, may reflect features of both the creative activity and the risk-taking measure matters.

Because creativity involves both domain-general and domain-specific processes, visual and verbal creative activities may have different downstream effects (Acar et al., 2024; Cubillos-Pinilla et al., 2025). Moreover, risk preferences are sensitive to elicitation format, and the relatively small stakes and single binary choice may have limited the sensitivity of the measure (Holzmeister & Stefan, 2021). Future studies could investigate the potential effect of creativity task modality and decision measure sensitivity on the relationship between creative engagement and calculated risk taking.

5.3 Neither proposed mediating pathways explained the relationship

The mediation results in Study 1 did not support general self-efficacy as an explanation for the relationship between creative engagement and risk-taking propensity. Creative ideation behavior was positively associated with general selfefficacy, but general self-efficacy did not predict overall risk-taking propensity after the other variables were considered. This finding differs from Yang and Liu (2024), who found that self-efficacy mediated the effect of creative engagement on preference for horror entertainment. They further showed that the overall effect emerged when participants received positive feedback or completed a relatively easy creative task, but disappeared following negative feedback or a difficult task. This pattern suggests that self-efficacy may be more likely to increase when creative engagement provides a clear experience of competence.

Creative engagement also altered affective dominance but not the other affective dimensions consistently. This finding accords with recent evidence that creative ideation enhanced feeling of autonomy by allowing participants to explore multiple idea domains and cross idea boundaries (Kim et al., 2023). Everyday creative activity has likewise been associated with greater autonomy and competence (Putney et al., 2024). Creative tasks may therefore temporarily increase the subjective experience of being in control. However, this finding was not in line with previous evidence that everyday creative engagement could enhance positive affect (Conner et al., 2018; Conner & Silvia, 2015; Smith et al., 2022), which may suggest that the affective effects of creative engagement may also depend on the duration or frequency of the creative activity.

Nevertheless, dominance did not predict subsequent calculated risk taking, and its indirect effect was not significant. H5 was therefore not supported. This pattern resembles previous findings that found that action-outcome predictability increased both sense of agency and risk taking but found no evidence that agency itself caused the behavioral change (Zhu et al., 2025). Affective dominance may consequently be an outcome of creative engagement without being the mechanism responsible for greater calculated risk taking. Future research could examine more specific processes, such as reward sensitivity and behavioral activation (Krause et al., 2024), tolerance of ambiguity (Charness & Grieco, 2022), and perceived control over decision outcomes (Damen, 2019; Langer, 1975; Moore, 2016).

5.4 Theoretical and practical implication

Theoretically, the central contribution of the present research is to recast creative engagement from an outcome to be explained into an experience that may shape subsequent decision making. Previous research has repeatedly reported positive correlation between risk taking and creativity, while their associations may partly reflect shared characteristics such as openness to experience. The present research provides evidence that this relationship is not limited to stable individual differences.

Under some task conditions, engaging in a creative activity can influence subsequent calculated risk taking. The findings therefore extend creativity research by identifying a potential behavioral spillover from creative engagement to subsequent choices involving explicit calculated risk-return trade-offs, complementing previously documented effects on ambiguity tolerance, indulgent choices, charitable giving, and entertainment preferences (Charness & Grieco, 2022; Krause et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2021; Yang & Liu, 2024).

Practically, in education, open-ended creative activities may help students become more comfortable considering uncertain alternatives, although their long-term effects on calculated risk taking require longitudinal evidence. In scientific, entrepreneurial, organizational, and governmental settings, creative engagement may similarly encourage experimentation when excessive caution impedes innovation. Conversely, sustained involvement in highly routine activities might contribute to more conservative decision patterns, but this possibility remains to be tested. Individuals and organizations should therefore recognize creative engagement as a possible influence on subsequent choices and retain explicit evaluation criteria when the stakes are high.

5.5 Limitations and future directions

Several questions remain for future research. First, although Study 1 indicated that everyday creative engagement was associated with risk-taking propensity across multiple domains, the experiments focused exclusively on calculated monetary risk taking. Experimental research is therefore needed to determine whether the effect extends to other types of calculated risk taking. Second, although random assignment in Studies 2 and 3 should have reduced systematic between-condition differences in openness to experience, this trait was not measured. Future research should assess openness to verify baseline equivalence and examine whether it moderates the effect of creative engagement. Third, the experimental studies focused on calculated risk taking through financial decisions involving explicit potential outcomes. Whether creative engagement also influences spontaneous risk taking remains to be examined (Zhang & Smith, 2025). Fourth, the experiments also captured only the immediate consequences of brief creative engagement, leaving open whether sustained creative engagement shapes consequential real-world behavior over time. Fifth, the psychological mechanism that underlies the creative engagement effect also remains unresolved.

Research with more sensitive process measures and multimodal methods, including psychophysiological measures, electroencephalography, functional near-infrared spectroscopy, and functional magnetic resonance imaging (Lu & Hao, 2026; Strouthos et al., 2025), may help clarify the neuro-cognitive processes linking creative engagement to subsequent calculated risk taking.
