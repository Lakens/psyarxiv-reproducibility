2 A typical finding in motion perception tasks like ours is that people generally overestimate motion they observe, identifying more pronounced frames as the last seen image in the motion sequence than was really shown, across stimulus domains (e.g., facial expressions, Yoshikawa & Sato, 2008; Prigent et al., 2018; but see Thornton, 2014); abstract stimuli, Freyd & Finke, 1984; for a review, see Hubbard, 2005; actions, Currie et al., 2025; Hudson, Nicholson, Ellis, & Bach, 2016; Hudson, Nicholson, Simpson et al., 2016). These biases are taken as evidence for the predictive nature of motion perception, where expectations about a motion’s next steps become integrated with its perceptual representation (Hogendoorn, 2022; White, 2026). Experiments 2a and 2b test for the first time whether, in face emotion perception, these overestimations are shaped by participants’ prior expectations about the person’s emotional state. If so, then participants should not only identify more expressive probe faces as identical to the last seen expression, but these overestimations should increase when the facial expressions (e.g., a smile) were expected from the prior emotion statement (e.g., “I feel so happy!” ; congruent) than when it was not expected (“I feel so sad!” ; incongruent). Such findings would provide the first evidence that prior information does not only change how people rate emotional expressions (as in Experiments 1a and 1b) but also how they perceptually represent them. Across experiments, we varied the duration between the final frame of the morph sequence and the onset of the probe (Experiment 2a, 1000 ms; Experiment 2b, 333 ms). Studies outside of emotion perception suggest that the largest predictive influences on motion perception are at about 300 ms after stimulus presentation (e.g., Hubbard, 2005; Zucchini et al., 2023). A recent study suggests that integration of higher-order expectations and sensory information happens at a later stage, when recurrent processing between levels in the hierarchy has taken place (Ntikas et al., 2025, also see Hogendoorn, 2022). Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Full text

Abstract

Context is known to change how people evaluate facial expressions. However, predictive processing accounts claim that context should also shape face perceptual representation, causing objectively identical expressions to appear different perceptually. We tested this in five experiments with English-speaking adults in the UK and Ireland, recruited through a university participant pool and online platforms (total N = 413; 18-49 years; data collected 2023-2026).

Participants heard how a person was feeling, watched that person's ambiguous expression change towards a congruent or incongruent emotion, and judged the expression's final seen emotional state. Expressions were rated as happier after happy statements and sadder after sad statements (Experiment 1a), replicating prior work. However, they were also rated as more intense when congruent with prior statements than when incongruent (Experiment 1b), ruling out biases from statement-to-response mappings. When participants perceptually compared final expressions with slightly more or less expressive probe faces, they generally overestimated face expressiveness (Experiments 2a, 2b). Crucially, this overestimation was larger for expressions congruent with prior expectations, but only when the probe appeared after a longer delay (1000 vs 333 ms). The same pattern emerged when the emotion was not explicitly stated but inferred from a variety of news that participants delivered to the person (Experiment 3). Prior knowledge of another's emotional state therefore shapes not only the evaluation of the person's facial expressions but also their perceptual representation, consistent with predictive accounts. Generalization to naturalistic expressions, other emotions, and cultures remains to be tested.

Introduction

When meeting people, it often feels like we can just effortlessly "read" their emotions from their faces. The ability to identify other people's emotions is crucial for smooth social interactions, and is linked to quality of life (e.g., Fulford et al., 2014), empathy (e.g., Besel & Yuille, 2010), and accuracy in judging the personality of other people (e.g., Jaksic & Schlegel, 2020). Similarly, better prediction of how other people's emotions change is associated with more successful friendships (Zhao et al., 2022), lower levels of loneliness (Zhao et al., 2022; Barrick et al., 2024), and larger social networks (Barrick et al., 2024). Yet, despite the importance of emotion understanding for everyday social cognition, the mechanisms that underpin people's understanding of others' facial expressions are unclear.

Dominant proposals (e.g., basic emotion theory; Ekman & Cordaro, 2011; Izard, 2011; Levenson, 2011; Panksepp & Watt, 2011; for an overview, see Tracy & Randles, 2011) have long conceptualized emotion perception as a primarily stimulus-driven bottom-up process.

These accounts rest on the assumption that there are basic emotions (e.g., anger, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, and happiness) that people universally experience, and that these emotions are expressed through distinct (innate) facial muscle configurations and other bodily signals. If so, perceivers could simply "read out" the emotion in another's face by identifying (even subtle) emotion-specific facial signals. Consistent with such views, the proposed basic emotions are recognized from their facial displays across different cultures (e.g., Ekman & Heider, 1988; for meta-analysis, see Elfenbein & Ambady, 2002), and distinct facial muscles seem to drive the recognition of certain emotions (e.g., mouth region for happiness recognition; Beaudry et al., 2014; Smith et al., 2005).

More recent studies have challenged basic emotion views. Research has indicated that the emotions people experience in daily life are rarely accompanied by the proposed stereotypical emotion displays, and that typical emotion displays are much more ambiguous than assumed by basic emotion theories (Barrett et al., 2019; Durán & Fernández-Dols, 2021).

Moreover, even when asked to judge only the facial expression, observers draw upon a variety of contextual information beyond the face itself (for a meta-analysis, see Steward et al., 2025). This includes not only other expressor-specific cues, like body language and vocalizations (e.g., Aviezer et al., 2012; de Gelder & Vroomen, 2000; Lecker et al., 2020), which could be part of evolved emotion displays, but also expressor-external cues, like the emotional context the person is placed in (e.g., Abramson et al., 2021; Carroll & Russell, 1996; Hess et al., 2020; Pullinen et al., 2026) and even the perceiver's own mood (Bouhuys et al., 1995; Lawrie et al., 2019) and attitudes (Korb et al., 2022). Sometimes, expressor-external contextual information fully accounts for people's emotion inferences, with little added contributions from facial expressions (Goel et al., 2024). Together, these findings challenge the core assumptions of basic emotion theories: that there are clear one-to-one mappings between facial expressions and emotions, and that these links are the primary driver of emotion inferences (but see Keltner, Sauter, et al., 2019 for attempts to integrate these findings within basic emotion theories).

Recent approaches therefore argue that face emotion perception is better conceptualized as a top-down predictive process (e.g., Barrett, 2017). Building on general predictive frameworks for perception and action (e.g., Bach & Schenke, 2017; Clark, 2013; Hohwy, 2013; Otten et al., 2017), these approaches assume that observers use prior information to form an internal model of another's emotional state (e.g., "She is happy"), with associated top-down predictions about how their facial expression will evolve if this internal model were correct (e.g., pulled lip corners, raised cheeks). By testing these predictions against the sensory input, the internal model can be confirmed, and ambiguities in the sensory signals can be "filled in" by prior assumptions (e.g., Roelfsema & de Lange, 2016), so that even ambiguous expressions acquire coherent meaning. In contrast, apparent mismatches (e.g., raised inner eyebrows and depressed lip corners associated with sadness) elicit prediction-error signals that are fed upwards along the hierarchy, triggering revisions of one's prior assumptions until sensory input and prior assumptions align (e.g., "She's not happy after all").

Through this reciprocal information exchange between higher-order information and lower-order sensory information, predictive models essentially implement an (approximate) Bayesian updating process (Clark, 2013; Hohwy, 2013). Such a mechanism would allow all relevant information -expressor-internal and external, irrespective of whether it was received before, during or after an expression change -to shape emotion perception, in a close-tooptimal manner that integrates all sources of information into a coherent percept. As a consequence, lower-order facial expression signals would not only drive higher-order emotion inferences, but higher-order information about someone's emotional state would also shape how their facial expressions are perceptually represented, potentially explaining why observers sometimes perceive identical (neutral) expressions differently when seeing them in different contexts (i.e., the Kuleshov effect, e.g., Barratt et al., 2016).

The hypothesis that prior information affects both how people interpret and perceptually represent emotion expressions has not been previously tested. Prior research has investigated whether prior information is combined with information from the face when people are asked to explicitly reason about the emotion that another person experiences (e.g., Goel et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2015). These studies do not address whether prior information changes how people represent the facial expression itself. Other research did investigate whether contextual information affects which emotions people derive from a face (e.g., Aviezer et al., 2012; Carroll & Russell, 1996; de Gelder & Vroomen, 2000; for review and metaanalysis, see Steward et al., 2025). However, these studies only asked people to evaluate the apparent emotions they recognize in a face (e.g., categorize emotion, rate emotion valence), without testing whether the perceptual representation of the face itself has changed. Indeed, virtually none of these studies rule out simple response biases (Firestone & Scholl, 2016), where people's face emotion judgements might reflect the evaluation of the contextual information itself, instead of perceived changes in the expression (e.g., rating a face as happier not because the face appears happier but because the context is happier). The existing research therefore says nothing about whether prior information about others affects how their facial expressions are perceptually represented.

Across five experiments, we systematically test for the first time whether prior information shapes not only how people evaluate emotional expressions they see in others, but also how they perceptually represent them. We use the following research strategy.

Experiments 1a and 1b first establish a novel task to confirm whether simple prior information about someone's emotional state (e.g., are they happy or sad) affects emotion ratings of their facial expressions, like in prior research. We also test whether this holds even when response biases are ruled out, by asking participants not to rate emotions themselves but instead to rate the intensity of facial expressions (irrespective of the emotion shown). Experiments 2a and 2b then translate this design into a psychophysical task, where participants perceptually compare the last seen facial expression with a (more or less expressive) "probe" expression presented afterwards, to test whether -and under what circumstances -prior information does not only affect ratings of emotional facial expressions, but their perceptual representation itself. Finally, the preregistered Experiment 3 extends this design into a task that more closely mirrors faceto-face interactions. Here, participants derived expectations about others' emotional state not from experimentally given information, but from a wide variety of emotionally relevant news that they decide to give to the other person themselves. We show that prior information shapes emotional expression's representation in this more complex ecological task, just as it does in simpler designs.

Experiment 1a and 1b

Experiments 1a and 1b developed a novel task to test whether prior information about another's emotional state affects the evaluation of their facial expressions. In each trial, participants saw a person's face and heard how they were feeling (e.g., "I feel so happy!" vs "I feel so sad!"). The face then either morphed towards a congruent (in 2/3 of trials) or incongruent facial expression (1/3 of trials), before disappearing at different stages mid-movement. If prior information shapes face emotion perception, emotion ratings should not only reflect the objective amount of happiness and sadness in the facial expression (i.e., how much it has progressed through the motion sequence), but also prior information about the person's emotional state. Happy expressions should therefore appear happier when expecting a happy compared to a sad emotional state, and vice versa for sad expressions.

In Experiment 1a, participants rated the emotion of the last seen expression in terms of given emotion labels (i.e., how happy/sad the facial expression was), like in prior research (see Steward et al., 2025). A problem is that the ratings made from pre-given emotion labels are susceptible to response bias (e.g., Firestone & Scholl, 2016; Russell, 1993), making it unclear whether the emotional context truly affects the perception of the facial expression or only the response that participants gave. Here, and in prior research, higher "happy" ratings might therefore be given not because the expression truly seems happier, but because the context itself drove the higher happiness ratings, without affecting emotion perception per se. Indeed, it is well known that pre-given emotion labels can inflate emotion recognition (Barrett et al., 2019; Nelson & Russell, 2013; Russell, 1994) and that available labels can bias ratings of identical facial expressions (Russell, 1993). Experiment 1b therefore did not ask participants to rate how happy/sad the last seen expression was, but to merely rate the intensity of the emotion in the expression, irrespective of which emotion was shown. This eliminates the direct mapping between emotion ratings and context and directly measures perceived emotional intensity of the emotional expression. If prior information shapes emotion evaluation, then we should again find that intensity ratings increase the more the expressions have progressed, and that congruent statement-face pairings are rated as more intense than incongruent ones.

Method

Participants

Participants (Experiment 1a, N = 71; Experiment 1b, N = 85) were recruited through the Psychology Research Participation Scheme at the University of Aberdeen (Experiment 1a, used as the "ambiguous" initial facial expression. The dynamic morph sequence was created by stepping upwards or downwards through the morph sequence from the initial expression in steps of two. The resulting sequences of four (e.g., face 20-22-24-26), five (20-22-24-26-28), and six frames (e.g., 20-22-24-26-28-30) showed an ambiguous facial expression changing towards happy or sad, without reaching the peak expression intensity (i.e., face 40 or face 0).

The predictive emotion cues were auditory emotion statements "I feel so happy!" and "I feel so sad!", recorded by two male and two female individuals, with "I feel so happy!" statements pronounced with a happy intonation and "I feel so sad!" pronounced with a sad intonation. The different identities of voices were then matched with a unique face identity.

The experiment was programmed using Inquisit 6.6.3 software by Millisecond (2022) and presented using Inquisit Player. Participants who completed the study online accessed the experiment remotely on their device and were required to use a computer with a keyboard and mouse. Mobile devices were not allowed.

Procedure

Main task

Each trial of the main task (Figure 1A) started with a fixation cross for 1000 ms, followed by an initial facial expression (50% happy, 50% sad blend). The auditory emotion statement ("I feel so happy/sad!") was played after a random interval of 300 ms to 1000 ms after the onset of the initial facial expression. These cues were explained to participants to reflect what the person on the screen was feeling. 500 ms after the statement ended, the face started changing expression towards happy or sad in a four, five or six frame morph sequence, with each frame presented for 50 ms, including the initial facial expression (note that because of this, the initial face was presented for 550 ms in total after audio offset). After the face disappeared, the screen went blank for 333 ms and the rating scale was presented. In Experiment 1a, participants were asked to rate the final frame of the morph sequence by indicating "How sad or happy was the last seen expression?" on a 'Sad' to 'Happy' scale with a 'Neutral' response in the middle. In Experiment 1b, participants rated "How emotionally intense is this facial expression?" with a scale labelled as 'Not intense at all, 0%' to 'Extremely intense, 100%'. Both scales had a range of 0 to 100 with increments of 10. Participants could use the full scale by using a slider, with an unlimited time to respond.

Participants first completed ten practice trials, which consisted of six-frame morph sequences and included at least six congruent face-statement trials, with the opportunity to repeat the practice block if necessary. The experiment consisted of 180 experimental trials across five blocks, with the number of trials for the factorial combination of auditory emotion statements, face emotion, and sequence lengths, chosen such that in 2/3rds of trials the face change was congruent with the emotion statement and otherwise incongruent. This ensured that the emotion statements were at least somewhat predictive of the upcoming expression, and do not undermine contingencies from everyday life. Blocks were separated by 30 s breaks, which participants could extend as needed. Twenty-two additional catch trials were interleaved with the experimental trials, with at least four per block, and at least two additional catch trials in the Practice block. These trials appeared after participants made their response, asking them to report whether they heard the statement "I feel so happy!" or "I feel so sad!" in the trial. Catch trials were not analyzed but served as an attention check to identify participants who had not followed instructions and turned their audio off or ignored the statements.

Static face rating task

In Experiment 1b, before participants proceeded to the main task, they completed 48 trials of face ratings of static expressions (24 expressions presented twice). Each trial started with a fixation cross for 1000 ms. Then a static facial expression was shown, selected from the same four identities, emotions (Happy, Sad), and final morph stages of the main task (e.g., face 26 from a 4-frame sequence; face 28 -5 frames; face 30 -6 frames). While the face was on screen, participants were asked to rate "How emotionally intense is this facial expression?" with a slider from "Not intense at all, 0%" to "Extremely intense, 100%" (0-100 scale).

Participants had an unlimited time to rate the faces. Comparing static and dynamic face ratings allowed us to explore whether dynamic faces were overestimated, as demonstrated in Yoshikawa and Sato (2008; see Supplementary Material).

Exclusion Criteria

Individual responses were excluded when they were faster than 200 ms or slower than 10,000 ms (1.67%, Experiment 1a; 1.19%, Experiment 1b).

Individual participants were excluded when they produced incomplete datasets (Experiment 1a, N = 3; Experiment 1b, N = 1), took part in the experiment a second time (second attempt excluded; Experiment 1a, N = 1; Experiment 1b N = 3), had fewer than 90 valid trials after individual trial exclusions (N = 0), or identified less than 70% of the audio statements correctly in the catch trials (Experiment 1a, N = 6; Experiment 1b, N = 9). Correlation measures were used to exclude participants who did not distinguish between the different morph intensities. For Experiment 1a, we calculated the correlation coefficient between participants' emotion ratings for morph sequence lengths across emotion statements (i.e., 6 frames sad face -5 frames sad face -4 frames sad face -4 frames happy face -5 frames happy face -6 frames happy face). A positive correlation indicates that participants rated longer morph sequences as more emotional, in line with the emotion portrayed in the face. For Experiment 1b, we calculated the correlation coefficient between the mean intensity ratings for different frame lengths across face and emotion statement pairings (4, 5, 6 frames). A positive correlation shows that participants accurately reported facial expressions in longer morph sequences as more intense. In each experiment, we excluded participants with correlation coefficient of r < .6. In Experiment 1a, one additional participant was excluded and in Experiment 1b, 14 additional participants were excluded.

Upon checking the data, we also additionally excluded one participant from each experiment whose context effect was very large (z-score of 6.163, Experiment 1a; 5.927, Experiment 1b) in the predicted direction. This was not determined a priori but makes the analyses more conservative and leaves all effects unchanged.

Experiment 1a

To test whether expectations from emotional statements impact the emotion ratings of dynamic facial expressions, we used a within-subjects ANOVA with factors Face Emotion (Happy, Sad) and Emotion Statement (Happy, Sad). Experiment 1b

Experiment 1b tested whether the influence of higher-order emotion information also affects face perception when measured with emotion intensity ratings, which are less susceptible to response biases. We analyzed face emotion intensity ratings with a 2 x 2 repeated measures ANOVA, with the factors Face Emotion (Happy, Sad) and Emotion Statement (Happy, Sad). Experiment 2a and 2b

Experiments 1a and 1b showed that prior information about another's emotional state shapes how their facial expression is evaluated. Importantly, this was found not only with emotion judgements, as used in prior research (Steward et al., 2025), but also with intensity judgments that eliminated the direct mapping between statement emotion and emotion rating.

The results, therefore, provide first evidence that prior information about how a person is feeling affects how their facial expression is represented, while ruling out response biases arising from an overlap between context and the rating scale. However, they leave open whether prior information only affects the higher-order evaluation of facial expressions, or whether it shapes the perceptual representation of the facial expression itself, in other words, whether the same face "appears" differently with different prior information.

Experiments 2a and 2b used a psychophysical task to directly test whether prior information affects the perceptual representation of observed expressions, as predicted by the tight coupling of higher-order stimulus inferences and their perceptual representation in predictive processing frameworks (e.g., Clark, 2013). As before, people received prior (auditory) information about how a person was feeling, before watching their face change towards a congruent or incongruent expression. Now, after the face disappeared, participants did not rate the last seen expression; instead, they compared it to a probe face image that showed the same face with a slightly more or less expressive expression (e.g., further or not as far in the morph sequence). They pressed a button if they perceived both faces as identical and another button if they appeared different.

A typical finding in motion perception tasks like ours is that people generally overestimate motion they observe, identifying more pronounced frames as the last seen image in the motion sequence than was really shown, across stimulus domains (e.g., facial expressions, Yoshikawa & Sato, 2008; Prigent et al., 2018; but see Thornton, 2014); abstract stimuli, Freyd & Finke, 1984; for a review, see Hubbard, 2005; actions, Currie et al., 2025; Hudson, Nicholson, Ellis, & Bach, 2016; Hudson, Nicholson, Simpson et al., 2016). These biases are taken as evidence for the predictive nature of motion perception, where expectations about a motion's next steps become integrated with its perceptual representation (Hogendoorn, 2022; White, 2026). Experiments 2a and 2b test for the first time whether, in face emotion perception, these overestimations are shaped by participants' prior expectations about the person's emotional state. If so, then participants should not only identify more expressive probe faces as identical to the last seen expression, but these overestimations should increase when the facial expressions (e.g., a smile) were expected from the prior emotion statement (e.g., "I feel so happy!" ; congruent) than when it was not expected ("I feel so sad!" ; incongruent). Such findings would provide the first evidence that prior information does not only change how people rate emotional expressions (as in Experiments 1a and 1b) but also how they perceptually represent them. Across experiments, we varied the duration between the final frame of the morph sequence and the onset of the probe (Experiment 2a, 1000 ms; Experiment 2b, 333 ms). Studies outside of emotion perception suggest that the largest predictive influences on motion perception are at about 300 ms after stimulus presentation (e.g., Hubbard, 2005; Zucchini et al., 2023). However, these timings usually reflect influences of lower-order expectations derived directly from the seen motion itself (e.g., a simple extrapolation of prior trajectories). A recent study suggests that integration of higher-order expectations and sensory information happens at a later stage, when recurrent processing between levels in the hierarchy has taken place (Ntikas et al., 2025, also see Hogendoorn, 2022). Therefore, varying the timing between to-be-judged stimulus and reference image allows us to test whether any predictive influence on emotion perception is immediate, or occurs only after recurrent feedback processes were able to affect the representation of the seen stimulus. The sample size was partly determined by a pilot study (unpublished) using a probe response task, which yielded an effect size of ηp² = .08 (Cohen's d = .29) for the crucial Face Emotion and Emotion Statement interaction (65 participants after exclusions). We aimed for a larger number of participants (N = 100) to allow for smaller effect sizes to be found. Sensitivity analysis, using G*Power 3.1.9.6 (Faul et al., 2009), showed that 100 participants allow us to detect an effect size of at least d = .28 (ηp² = .075), with .80 power and an alpha value of .05.

Method

Stimuli, Apparatus, and Procedure

Stimuli and apparatus were identical to Experiment 1a and 1b.

The procedure was identical to Experiment 1a and 1b, with the exception of the fixation cross duration (750 ms, Experiment 2a; 1000 ms, Experiment 2b) and the response itself (see Figure 1A). After participants heard the statement and watched the face change expression and disappear, a probe face was presented 1000 ms (Experiment 2a) or 333 ms (Experiment 2b) later. This probe was the final frame of the morph sequence that was displaced either forwards into the morph sequence (+1, +3, +5 frames, i.e., more expressive than the final frame) or backwards (-1, -3, -5 frames, i.e., less expressive than the final frame), resulting in more or less expressive probes (see Figure 1B). Participants then judged whether the probe was the "same"

or "different" as the final expression in the morph sequence by using the keyboard ("s" key for same, "d" key for different). The final probe expression was presented until response, and responses were allowed to be made 200 ms after the probe appeared in Experiment 2b.

Participants first completed ten practice trials, consisting of morph sequences of six frames, including at least six congruent face-statement trials and two incongruent trials, with the opportunity to repeat them if necessary. There were 216 experimental trials across six blocks, separated by a 30s break in Experiment 2a and 20s break in Experiment 2b, which participants could extend as needed. Twenty-seven catch trials were included in the task to check that participants were paying attention to the audio (see Experiments 1a and 1b), with at least 4 per block, and at least 2 additional catch trials were included in the practice block.

Exclusion Criteria

Individual responses were excluded when they were faster than 200 ms or slower than 3000 ms (Experiment 2a, 6% of trials; Experiment 2b, 7.02% of trials).

Participants were excluded when they had incomplete datasets due to technical issues (Experiment 2b, N = 1), took part a second time (second attempt excluded; Experiment 2b, N = 1), had less than 90 trials after trial exclusions (N = 1, Experiment 2b), or did not identify 70% of the audio correctly in the catch trials (N = 10, Experiment 2a; N = 18, Experiment 2b).

As in Experiments 1a and 1b, we only included participants who adequately performed the task and distinguished between the last morph frame and probe frames. We calculated the correlation between the proportion of "different" responses (i.e., judgement that the probe is different from the final frame of the morph sequence) and the absolute probe distance to the final morph frame for each participant (-+1, -+3, -+5 probe). A positive correlation indicates that the proportion of "different" responses increases as the probe frame is more different to the real final frame. We excluded participants with correlation coefficients r < .6 (N = 11, Experiment 2a; N = 28, Experiment 2b).

Finally, piloting suggested that participants may misunderstand the task and report whether the final expression matched the emotion of the statement rather than comparing the probe to the final expression of the morph sequence. To exclude these participants, we subtracted the average proportion of "different" responses across all probes for congruent Face Emotion and Emotion Statement conditions from the proportion of "different" responses for incongruent Face Emotion and Emotion Statement conditions. High positive values, therefore, indicate that participants judged congruent Face Emotion and Emotion Statement combinations as "same" more than incongruent pairings, regardless of the dissimilarity of the probe. We excluded participants whose difference between incongruent and congruent conditions was above .3 (N = 1, Experiment 2a).

Transparency and Openness

Transparency and openness and R packages used for data preprocessing/analysis were identical to Experiment 1a and 1b. The study design and its analyses were not preregistered.

Data was collected in 2024 for Experiment 2a and 2023 to 2024 for Experiment 2b.

Experiment 3

Experiments 2a and 2b confirmed that perceptual representations of dynamic facial expressions are biased away from what was objectively presented, and towards expectations.

Facial expressions were generally overestimated, so that participants identified a more expressive probe face to be identical to the last seen expression. This is in line with the proposal that perception of dynamic facial expression is shaped by expectations of how the expressions will develop further (e.g., Yoshikawa & Sato, 2008). Importantly, it showed for the first time that prior information about how the person is feeling determines this overestimation. We found stronger overestimation when participants expected the current facial expression based on the information that they had received about the person's emotional state, compared to when they expected a different facial expression. Interestingly, this was the case only when there was longer time (1000ms, Experiment 2a) between the final face that participants saw and the probe face they judged it against, and not for shorter intervals (333ms, Experiment 2b).

One limitation of Experiments 2a and 2b is that they relied upon a highly artificial experimental situation to demonstrate the influence of prior information, using prototypical and repetitive auditory statements that explicitly stated how a person is feeling ("I feel so happy/sad") and were pronounced in the corresponding emotion. The preregistered Experiment 3 therefore tested whether the effects replicate with more complex cues, in an experimental setting that more closely reflects everyday interactions. Before the initial face changed expression, participants were presented with two possible written news statements that could evoke happiness or sadness, one of which they could decide to "give" to the other person. The face then started changing facial expression and, after it disappeared mid-movement, was followed by a probe face (1000 ms gap), which was more or less expressive than the final morph face (as in Experiment 2a). Participants judged whether the probe face was "same"/"different" to the final expression they had seen. If the influence of prior information generalizes to a more ecological experimental situation, facial expressions should again be overestimated, and this overestimation should depend on whether the participants expected this emotional response or a different one. The use of written, rather than auditory statements, also allows us to disentangle whether any such effects are driven by the emotional vocalization or, as hypothesized, by expectations about the person's emotional state and the facial expressions through which this state should manifest itself.

Method

Participants

One hundred and twenty-five participants, with normal or corrected-to-normal vision, recruited through Prolific, took part in a preregistered online study. Participants recruited through Prolific were aged 18 to 30 years, resided in the UK or Ireland, and their first language was English. After exclusions (see Exclusion Criteria), 93 participants were included (18 to 30 years, Mage = 24.59, SDage = 3.7; 45 males, 1 non-binary; 10 left-handed, 4 ambidextrous).

Participants provided informed consent prior to participating in the study. The study was approved by the School of Psychology ethics board of the University of Aberdeen. This experiment used preregistered sequential analyses (Lakens, 2014 (Lakens, , 2022)). Power analysis was based on the effect size of the crucial Face Emotion and News Statement interaction in Supplementary Experiment 2 (one-sided padjusted = .058, d = .15), which employed a similar experimental paradigm as in Experiment 3. As we aimed to increase the effect size by adapting the experimental paradigm of Supplementary Experiment 2 (see Stimulus and Apparatus, Procedure), we targeted an effect size of d = .2. Given a one-sided test with alpha of .05, power of 80%, four looks, and the O'Brien-Fleming type alpha and beta spending functions, maximally 181 participants would be needed, after exclusions. The four preregistered looks were at 40% (N = 73), 50% (N = 91), 75% (N = 136), and 100% of the data (N = 181). Alpha boundaries for these looks were p = .002 (look 1), p = .005 (look 2), p = .022 (look 3), and p = .043 (look 4), allowing to stop data collection early when the p-value is smaller than the boundary. Beta boundaries were p = .511 (look 1), p = .374 (look 2), and p = .129 (look 3), allowing to stop for futility when the p-value is larger than these boundaries. Due to online data collection, we recruited two more participants for look 2 than intended (93 instead of 91) and adjusted the design parameters accordingly. Data collection was stopped at this look.

Stimuli and Apparatus

The face stimuli were of the same identities and face emotions as in Experiment 1a and 1b. They were morphed in 45 frames using Psychomorph (Tiddeman & Perrett, 2001 , 2002) with the middle expression (morph 23) used as the initial ambiguous face. Morph sequences of four, five, and six frames, including the initial face, were created by stepping 3 frames. The presentation time of each frame was increased to 83 ms to make the task easier, to reduce the number of exclusions.

Twenty happy and twenty sad statements were initially created to reflect news that could be given to a person that is likely to evoke the corresponding emotion in them and were used in Supplementary Experiment 2. Ten happy statements and ten sad statements, which were rated as the most emotionally intense in a separate experiment (participants were asked, "What intensity of emotion would this news evoke if it was given to a person?" on a 0-100 scale from "Not intense at all" to "Extremely intense"), were chosen for this experiment. Example statements were "It seems like your phone call probably saved his life" (happy) or "I got a call from the vet. Charlie is not doing well" (sad).

Apparatus was identical to Experiment 1a and 1b.

Procedure

The procedure was similar to Experiment 2a (see Figure 3.1). On each trial, after the fixation cross (1000 ms), an initial facial expression appeared in the middle of the screen, which was followed 49 frames later (e.g., for 60hz refresh rate display, approximately 817 ms) by one happy and one sad statement presented on the left and right side of the face, position randomized. The experiment was set up so that participants gave an equal number of happy and sad news over its course (108 each). Participants could freely choose the statement they wanted to give, but after each break, they were encouraged to focus on giving more happy or sad news to ensure each was chosen roughly equally towards the end of the experiment. When participants reached the maximum of statements of one emotion category, in the subsequent trials they were still presented with happy and sad news but were only able to select the less chosen category. After picking the news, by clicking on the chosen statement, 500 ms later the face started changing expression in 3, 4, or 5 frames, with each frame presented for 83 ms.

After the face disappeared, a probe face was presented 1000 ms later, as in Experiment 2a, and participants decided with a press of a button ("s" vs "d") whether it was the same as or different to the last expression in the sequence.

Participants completed 10 practice trials and 216 experimental trials across six blocks.

Blocks were separated by a 30s break, which participants were able to extend as needed. We included 22 additional catch trials across the experiment (additional 1 catch trial in practice blocks). Catch trials looked like a regular trial where participants gave news, however, after choosing the news, participants were presented with the chosen statement and a corresponding "incorrect" statement and had to select which one they had chosen (e.g., "I got a call from your mom. Charlotte is not doing well" vs "I got a message from your mom. Charlotte is not feeling okay")foot_0 . Positions of these statements were randomized and no feedback was provided.

Exclusion criteria were preregistered and similar to Experiment 2a, with the exception of the catch trials threshold (60%) and an addition of individual trial exclusion where the news statement was picked faster than 500 ms. A total of 14.6% of individual trials were excluded (7.93% for statement picking; 7.02% for response). One participant was excluded due to an incomplete dataset, one due to completing experiment twice (second attempt excluded), six due to a low number of trials after individual trial exclusions, and one due to catch trial performance. Transparency and openness declaration was identical to Experiment 1a and 1b .

As in Experiments 2a and 2b, we first tested whether dynamic facial expressions were generally overestimated, reflected in a higher proportion of more expressive probes being accepted as the same as the final morph expression compared to less expressive probes. General Discussion

The current series of experiments tested for the first time a crucial hypothesis of predictive processing accounts of (social) cognition (e.g., Bach & Schenke, 2017; Barrett, 2017; Clark, 2013; Hohwy, 2013; Otten et al., 2017): that prior information about someone's emotional state should not only affect how their emotional expressions are interpreted, but also how the expressions themselves are perceptually represented.

The results consistently revealed the predicted shaping of face emotion perception by prior information, across tasks and measurement methodologies. Experiments 1a and 1b showed that prior information about someone's emotional state affected explicit ratings of their facial expression, showing increased ratings of happiness and sadness (Experiment 1a), as well as increased perceived intensity (Experiment 1b) of faces that were congruent with the prior emotion information about the other person.

Thus, the same happy expression was rated as happier (and more intense) when perceivers heard that the person is happy and as sadder (and less intense) when the auditory cue indicated sadness. This shows, in line with predictive processing proposals (e.g., Clark, 2013; Otten et al., 2017), that face emotion perception integrates prior higher-order information about someone's emotional state with the actual sensory information from the facial expressions.

Experiments 2a and 2b used a psychophysical task to test for the first time whether prior emotion information does not only affect the evaluation of emotional expressions, like in Experiments 1a and 1b, but also their perceptual representation. Participants judged whether a (more or less expressive) probe face, presented directly after the dynamic expression, was identical to the final expression in the morph sequence. We found, first, that dynamic expressions were consistently overestimated, with the final expression judged as more expressive than it actually was. This finding is in line with prior work suggesting that the perceptual representation of dynamic stimuli is not veridical but biased towards their expected next steps, both within (e.g., Yoshikawa & Sato, 2008) and outside face emotion perception (e.g., Currie et al., 2025; Freyd & Finke, 1984; Hudson, Nicholson, Ellis, & Bach, 2016; Hudson, Nicholson, Simpson et al. 2016; for a review, see Hogendoorn, 2022). Importantly, we found that prior information about how a person is feeling determined these overestimations. With a sufficient delay (1000 ms) between the final face and probe face in Experiment 2a (but not with a shorter delay in Experiment 2b), participants overestimated facial expressions more when they were preceded by an auditory emotion statement that was congruent with the facial expression change than when it was incongruent, revealing an influence of prior information on the perceptual expectations that shape how other people's emotional expressions are represented.

Finally, Experiment 3 demonstrated that the perceptual shaping through prior information generalizes to a more ecological task, in which participants themselves decided which piece of news to give to another person, and therefore, which emotional state to induce.

Moreover, instead of the stereotypical "I feel so happy/sad" statements of the previous experiments, the statements never mentioned any emotion directly, were provided as written statements without emotional intonation, and captured a wide range of emotional information encountered in everyday life. Despite these changes, we found similar overestimation of facial expressions, which was again larger when the facial expression change matched the person's expected emotional state. Greater overestimation of congruent face and news statements, therefore, likely reflects an inferential process where perceivers derive how a situation would make a person feel, which then shapes the perception of the upcoming facial reaction.

Together, the current results show for the first time that prior expectations do not impact only face emotion evaluations but can also shape their perceptual representation, shifting the typical overestimation of dynamic facial expression towards the emotion expected from the person's emotion or news statements. They challenge basic emotion accounts (e.g., Ekman & Cordaro, 2011), according to which emotions are primarily inferred from facial expressions or other expressor-related perceptual cues, like vocalizations and body language (Keltner, Sauter, et al., 2019; Keltner, Tracy, et al., 2019). In such accounts, prior information only affects the final interpretation of an emotional expression but would not affect the perceptual encoding we observed here. Instead, our findings support predictive processing accounts (e.g., Barrett, 2017; Clark, 2013) that conceptualize emotion perception, and social perception more generally, as a process of "hypothesis" testing and updating where the final percept reflects an approximate Bayesian integration of top-down expectations and bottom-up sensory input. In these frameworks, any higher-order interpretational change of facial emotion directly feeds into the perceptual representation of the expression itself, shifting it subtly from what was perceived towards what was expected. The result is a coherent percept that integrates all available sources of information, so that what one perceives shapes one's higher-order evaluations, and higherorder information, in turn, shapes how expressions are perceptually represented. Moreover, like we found here, this should happen irrespective of whether the prior expectation comes from the expressor themselves (Experiment 1a to 2b) or from expressor-external information about the emotional situation they are in (Experiment 3).

The current studies provide a substantial methodological advance over prior work. A problem with previous literature on context effects in face emotion perception is that most studies asked participants to categorize or rate the specific emotions in the facial expressions relative to pre-given emotion labels. This made it difficult to rule out potential response biases (e.g., Firestone & Scholl, 2016), as it has long been known that available response options directly affect how emotions are categorized (Russell, 1993). The results may therefore reflect an influence on the rating (or categorization) response itself, biasing them towards sadness or happiness without affecting face emotion evaluation per se. Experiment 1b for the first time eliminated the mapping between the emotional context and the response options by removing any emotion labels from the ratings, merely asking participants to rate the intensity of the expression, irrespective of the emotion it displayed. The replication of the influence of prior information on face evaluation with similar effect sizes (emotion ratings, d = .457; intensity ratings, d = .473) substantially limits the potential influences of such response biases.

Experiments 2a, 2b, and 3 further confirmed this. Here, participants were never asked to judge the emotion in the expression, merely to perceptually compare whether one expression was identical to a probe face presented after. Nevertheless, a similar shaping of face perception through prior emotion information was observed. Thus, next to identifying an influence of prior information on the perceptual encoding of facial expressions, these studies provide additional evidence against a role of response biases in producing the resulting changes in either evaluation or subjective perceptual judgements of facial expressions.

The pattern of results across Experiments 2a, 2b, and 3 provides novel insights into the stage of processing in which the integration of prior and perceptual information occurs during face emotion perception. We found that the general overestimation of facial expressionshappy expressions seen as happier than they really were, sad expressions as sadder -was observed both with short (333 ms, Experiment 2b) and longer (1000 ms, Experiment 2a, Experiment 3) delays between face encoding and probe judgment. This suggests that lowerorder visual expectations about how a facial expression will develop are integrated rapidly into dynamic face perception. In contrast, the shaping of these overestimations towards the person's (higher-order) emotional state by predictive cues was restricted to longer delays. These findings challenge proposals that top-down information may affect even the earliest stages of perceptual processing when retinal input reaches the visual cortex (e.g., Rauss et al., 2011). Instead, it is consistent with recent proposals, which suggest that when perceiving dynamic stimuli, the brain integrates sensory information with prior information from other sources only in a later "editing" stage. Here, initial stimulus representations are refined and updated, producing a coherent percept that serves as basis for (conscious) perception, decision-making, and action (Hogendoorn, 2022; Schmitt & de Lange, 2025; White, 2026).

Recent studies outside emotion perception are consistent with this proposal, showing that changes to early sensory processing are primarily found for expectations from within the perceptual system itself, especially when scaffolded by extensive stimulus experience (e.g., Alilović et al., 2019; Walsh et al., 2024), like the expectation that dynamic expressions will develop further into a full expression observed here. In contrast, effects of higher-order expectations (i.e., ice will melt, Hafri et al., 2022; some body postures are difficult to maintain, Han et al., 2024) are typically observed with longer delays. Moreover, EEG evidence (Ntikas et al., 2025) indicates that the resulting changes in perceptual judgments are better predicted by later EEG components (e.g., P2 and P3b, 200 to 500 ms after stimulus onset) than components related to early sensory processing (e.g., P1, N1; 50 ms to 200 ms). Together, the data suggest that emotion perception emerges from a two-stage integration process (see White, 2026), which initially extrapolates the currently seen expressions towards the full emotional display and then refines it towards prior information about what emotion this person is likely experiencing, producing a final percept that integrates both sources of information.

An interesting observation was that, in Experiments 2a and 2b, happy facial expressions were overestimated more than sad facial expressions. While not predicted, this finding is fully in line with the idea that the observed overestimations reflect the expectations that people have about how the observed motion will develop further. If so, one would expect reduced overestimation for expressions that already seem to have reached their ultimate state than those further away from it (for an example outside emotion perception, see Hubbard & Motes, 2005).

Consistent with this hypothesis, Experiment 1b shows that happy facial expressions were indeed rated as less intense than their sad counterparts, as is the case more generally for happy faces in the IASLab face set. Moreover, the happy facial expressions we used had a closed mouth, which may be perceived as less intense than expressions showing an open-mouth smile.

The increased overestimation for happy expression here is, therefore, fully in line with predictive proposals, in which people expect already intense sad expressions to not develop much further, compared to less intense happy expressions.

The current findings have important implications for our understanding of emotion perception in everyday life. The ability to predict emotional reactions, such as how people's emotional states transition, is associated with social benefits for the perceiver, such as lower reports of loneliness and larger social networks (Barrick et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2022). Since these studies focus on reasoning about other people's hypothetical emotional states, rather than visual perception of changing facial expressions, a promising avenue would be to investigate whether these forms of predictions are related and whether greater overestimation of facial expressions found in our tasks has similar links to social functioning outcomes. Similarly, our findings that face emotion perception is not veridical and driven by expectations may explain misunderstandings of other people's emotions (e.g., Aviezer et al., 2012), cultural differences in emotion interpretations (e.g., Yuki et al., 2007; Saito et al., 2023), as well as biases in emotion perception in social anxiety (e.g., Heuer et al., 2010) and depression (e.g., Liu et al., 2012), with the latter already being considered within the predictive processing framework (e.g., Kube et al., 2020). They highlight the importance of including contextual cues in experimental paradigms to provide a fuller picture of how emotions are inferred and perceived from the face, as well as in computational models designed to capture human emotion perception in the field of computer vision and machine learning.

Constraints on generality

The present study provides a systematic investigation of how expectations impact dynamic emotion perception, showing that both emotion evaluation and perception can be affected by prior information about someone's emotion state, as measured across different response tasks and different types of cues. However, there are some important limitations and constraints on generality to consider when interpreting these findings.

First, to increase the experimental control of the facial movement, we relied upon dynamic facial morphs, which have been widely used to study dynamic emotion perception (Dobs et al., 2018). However, naturalistic spontaneous dynamic expressions may be more ambiguous than the linearly morphed expressions relied upon here, as the former may better capture important temporal features of muscle movements, such as the movement onset, acceleration, and offset, increasing the variability in sensory evidence. Interestingly, predictive processing proposals assume that contextual influences should be, if anything, enhanced for such ambiguous expressions, as less precise perceptual representations provide fewer constraints on top-down influences and, therefore, are weighted less in the integration process (e.g., Yon & Frith, 2021; Pullinen et al., 2026). This may also be achieved by expanding the number of facial emotions used within this task, including those that are often confusable and perceptually similar (e.g., anger and disgust; Susskind et al., 2007). In both cases, larger effects of prior emotion information than those observed here would be expected.

Second, our studies focused on understanding how prior information is relied upon in face emotion perception in young and middle-aged adults (range across experiments was 18-49 years old). Research comparing context reliance between younger and older adults in face emotion judgements does suggest age-related differences, with older people relying more on context compared to younger adults (e.g., Noh & Isaacowitz, 2013; Lawrie et al., 2025; in these studies, older adults were 59/60+, younger were 17-29). Our experimental paradigm could, therefore, be used in future research to investigate whether the effects found in the present studies become even more pronounced with age. Furthermore, while parts of our studies relied mostly on the student population (e.g., Experiment 1a), experiments that relied solely or partially on data collection through online participation platforms (e.g., Experiment 1b, 3) potentially have recruited a more diverse group of participants, although no individual differences information (e.g., ethnicity) has been collected to confirm this. The present studies have, however, recruited participants residing in English-speaking countries, to ensure spontaneous processing of the emotion cues, with a large part of the sample including participants from the UK. Therefore, this limits our understanding as to what type of information is important when making face emotion judgements and how people's expectations of the likely emotion may differ across different cultures, especially in the context of Experiment 3 that included statements that required a situation-to-emotion inference.

When judging how a person is feeling from their facial expressions, perceivers use different sources of information to inform their judgements, as reflected in findings of context effects in face evaluation. Predictive processing accounts propose that these effects reflect participants' expectations shaping not only face emotion evaluation but also the perceptual representation of facial expressions. We tested this across five experiments and found for the first time that these expectations shape not only explicit face evaluation but also the perceptual representation of dynamic facial expressions at a later stage of face emotion processing.
