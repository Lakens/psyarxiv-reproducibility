Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

For many years, the problem of preemption has been discussed in second language acquisition research, i.e., how a learner 'unlearns' an initially transferred representation when the input does not provide contradictory evidence. In this study we examine how Yang's (2002) Variational Learning model can account for cases of preemption. To do so, we address the expungement of Modal Existential Constructions (MECs) from the L2 English Interlanguage grammar of L1 Spanish and French learners. Using an acceptability judgement task, we analyze participants' retreat from non-facilitative transfer considering two implicational universals associated with MECs and frequency effects. We find that the more marked an element is on an implicational hierarchy, the easier it is to remove. However, there is also evidence that frequency interacts with markedness to affect the rate of restructuring. These findings suggest a need to more seriously consider how domainspecific (language universals) and domain-general learning mechanisms (frequency) interact during second language acquisition and grammar restructuring.

1. Introduction

To successfully acquire a second language (L2), one must not only learn new sounds, vocabulary, and grammatical regularities, but also unlearn a great deal of linguistic baggage from one's native language (e.g., Trahey & White, 1993; White, 2003). Indeed, this preemption problem may seem particularly acute when one's native language (L1) provides two options, while the target language allows one but not the other. This is because there is no direct negative evidence in the primary linguistic data of the new language that directly indicates that only one of the options available in one's native language is well-formed in the new language.

For example, in contexts of circumstantial possibility, Spanish and French (among many other languages) allow the use of both an indefinite pronoun and a Modal Existential Construction (MEC), while English allows only the former. Therefore, because L1 Spanish and L1 French learners of L2 English may initially transfer the availability of both options, in acquiring English they will need to preempt the availability of the MEC. This unlearning presents a learnability issue, however, due to their low frequency of occurrence and the lack of negative evidence in the input.

This study investigates the extent to which Yang's (2002) Variational Learning model (VL) can provide insights into the preemption of the MEC by L1 Spanish and L1 French learners of English. While there is no (positive) evidence in the input that shows that the MEC is ungrammatical in English, VL raises the possibility that overcoming the preemption problem may occur through competition between multiple grammars. Furthermore, the interaction of frequency effects with an implicational universal (presumably, a part of Universal Grammar) may shed light on both the transfer of MECs from the L1 and the path toward their expungement in L2 English. We implement an acceptability judgement task, testing acceptance of MECs along two implicational hierarchies: MEC-embedding verbs and wh-words that may occur in MECs.

2. Literature review

Linguists have long been fascinated by what Baker and McCarthy (1981) termed "the logical problem of language acquisition": learners must project an infinite range of grammatical expressions despite exposure to only a finite set of primary linguistic data. That is, the task of language acquisition is underdetermined by the data available to the learner, which would pose challenges for the acquirer if their only resource were the language input (Hornstein & Lightfoot, 1981; White, 2003). As such, (some) researchers have postulated various types of constraints on the acquirer's available hypothesis space (in the tradition of Chomsky, 1965). We will not review the various approaches to such constraints here. Instead, we will focus on one model of language acquisition: VL provides a formal means of integrating the quantitative properties of the input with constraints on the possible grammars an acquirer will postulate (Yang, 2002; Legate & Yang, 2007).

Under VL, the learner posits a population of competing grammars which is constrained by the innate hypothesis space given by UG. Within this hypothesis space, grammars are probabilistically selected to parse the input, and the success (or failure) of the chosen grammar then affects the probability that it will be selected again in the future. The algorithm in (1) illustrates this process (Yang, 2002, pp. 26-27).

(1) Upon the presentation of an input datum s, the child a. selects a grammar Gi with probability Pi b. analyzes s with Gi c. if successful, reward Gi by increasing Pi otherwise, punish Gi by decreasing Pi Thus, convergence on the target grammar is determined by the interaction of language universals (provided by UG), which constrain the hypothesis space, and input frequency (quantitative properties of the input), which provides evidence of the relative strengths of each posited grammar in the target language. While VL was developed for L1 acquisition, it can be extended to L2 acquisition as well, with some key differences. While the initial state of L1 acquisition is essentially just the constraints placed on the available hypothesis space, we assume the initial state of L2 acquisition to be the steady state of the L1 system (Schwartz & Sprouse, 1996). Under a VL account, the L2 initial state is therefore the L1 probabilities that particular grammars are chosen. If the L2 input is not consistent with the transferred system, the probabilities of particular grammars being chosen must be adjusted. Some researchers also suggest the L1 grammar may retain a privileged or default role (Slabakova, 2008; Yang & Montrul, 2017). Additionally, the speed of restructuring from previous non-facilitative transfer (i.e., convergence on the target grammar) is dependent on the target language input, including frequency of the kind of input necessary for prompting changes to the current state of probabilities. Of course, frequency of input in the L2 is often qualitatively and quantitatively different from that of the L1, and many additional factors not relevant in L1 acquisition play a role in the learner's convergence on the target grammar, such as variability in learner-directed speech, settings in which input is received, type of input (written vs. oral), and individual differences in language learning aptitude. 1Given our goal in this paper of applying VL to L2 acquisition research, we now discuss findings in L2 regarding its two components: frequency (quantitative properties) and implicational universals (constraints on the hypothesis space). First, researchers across all traditions generally agree that input is vital for L2 acquisition (Rankin & Unsworth, 2016; Slabakova et al., 2014). However, while usage-based accounts (e.g., Wulff & Ellis, 2018; Tomasello, 2003) attempt to demonstrate that (second) language acquisition can be accomplished solely through the use of domain-general cognition and data-driven learning, generative accounts attribute an additional important role to domain-specific cognitive principles and/or mechanisms which constrain the acquisition process (Schwartz & Sprouse, 2013 , 2026; White, 2003). Therefore, VL is more aligned with these latter accounts.

Researchers in the generative tradition typically point to two findings in discussing the role of frequency in L2 acquisition: non-acquisition despite frequent input and acquisition despite a poverty of the stimulus. On the first point, consider suppliance of obligatory morphology, such as third person singular present tense -s in English. While such morphology is exorbitantly frequent in the input, no matter how frequency is calculated, and taught/corrected extensively in class, even highly proficient learners of English whose L1s exhibit subject-verb agreement morphology may still not supply correct morphology in production (e.g., VanPatten et al., 2012; VanPatten & Williams, 2014). On the second point, even when the learner's L1 does not provide facilitative transfer, the input provides no evidence for a particular phenomenon, and the phenomenon is not taught in the classroom (i.e., the poverty of the stimulus situation), learners can acquire target-like knowledge of that phenomenon (for a recent overview, see Schwartz & Sprouse, 2026). So, frequency is neither sufficient nor (in some cases) necessary for successful L2 acquisition, but it is certainly a primary driver of grammar restructuring.

The role of implicational universals was addressed early in the discussion of L2 acquisition. 2 This research stemmed from two different traditions, typological (e.g., Eckman, 1985) and generative (e.g., White, 1989b). Whereas the former was based on comparisons of the world's languages and implicational universals (Greenberg 1963), and the latter was based on cognitive constraints built into the language learner's individual cognition, both make the same predictions in several cases (White, 1989a). For example, both approaches would argue that if there is an implicational universal like 'If P then Q', learners should never posit a grammar containing P and not Q (e.g., Hawkins, 1987). Subsequent research has suggested that other universal mechanisms remain at play in L2 acquisition (see, e.g., Schwartz & Sprouse, 1996), so one would expect implicational universals to still constrain the available hypothesis space as well.

So far, only a few studies have applied VL to L2 acquisition research (Rankin, 2014 (Rankin, , 2023;; Morales Reyes & Montrul, 2020). Rankin (2014) examined the interpretation of wh-questions by L1 German/L2 English learners. The study found that even highly proficienct learners displayed non-target-like interpretations of these questions due to transfer from the L1 (i.e., V2). In examining the frequency of different types of wh-questions in the input (i.e., simple tense, periphrastic tense, and copula), Rankin found that the vast majority of input sentences can be parsed by the L1 grammar and can result in the target interpretation, so the transferred grammar would be frequently rewarded. This continuous rewarding would result in serious delay in restructuring the L2 system to exclude non-target grammars. In a later study, Rankin (2023) further examined the acquisition/loss of V2 by L1 German/L2 English learners, finding that the frequency of sentences that could be analyzed as V2 in English with lexical vs. non-lexical (copula/auxiliary) verbs and different types of clause-initial elements mirrors to a large extent findings in the experimental literature for the conditions where V2 is more likely to be produced in L2 speech; that is, the more frequently sentences that cannot be analyzed as instances of V2 occur in the input, the less likely learners are to produce V2 in those contexts. Finally, Morales Reyes and Montrul (2020) examined comprehension of verbal agreement morphology by L1 English/L2 Spanish and L1 Spanish/L2 English children. In comparison to English, Spanish displays much more abundant evidence for subject agreement, and under VL, one would predict a faster rate of acquisition of the target property when evidence is more frequent. As predicted, the L2 Spanish acquirers displayed higher accuracy in comprehending agreement than the L2 English acquirers.

So, to date, research applying VL to L2 acquisition has been promising, but it has focused only on its first component, frequency. Of course, many studies have addressed constraints on the hypothesis space with L2 (i.e., universals), but separately from the framework of VL. Therefore, L2 VL studies have not yet addressed the role of implicational universals, or universals generally, in interaction with the role of frequency. Finally, VL has not been tested on the acquisition of properties at the lexicon-syntax interface in L2. The goal of this paper is to undertake such an investigation.

2.1 Modal existential constructions

In numerous languages, there exists a phenomenon called the modal existential (wh-)construction (MEC; Grosu, 2004; Šimík, 2011), illustrated in (2)-(3) below. MECs exhibit three core components. First, they belong to the class of wh-constructions, such as wh-questions and free relatives. That is, MECs obligatorily contain a wh-word that must be fronted, so only languages that have overt wh-movement may exhibit MECs. However, even in languages with overt wh-movement, MECs may not exist; for example, Romance languages display MECs (2-3), but (most) Germanic languages do not (4).

(2) Il n'a pas où mourir.

(French) he neg-have neg where die.INF 'He doesn't have anywhere to die.' (Hirschbühler, 1978, p. 218) (3) ¿Tienes con qué escribir? (Spanish) have.2sg with what write.INF 'Do you have something to write with?' (Šimík, 2011, p. 6) (4) *Do you have what to write with?

Second, MECs belong to the class of existential constructions, such as there-existential sentences in English. That is, MECs always assert the existence of something, and they are typically embedded under existential predicates (e.g., be or have). Finally, they belong to the class of modal constructions, such as infinitives embedded under modal verbs or infinitival relative clauses. Specifically, MECs always display a circumstantial possibility reading, never carrying deontic/epistemic flavors or necessity force.

Šimík (2011) identifies four implicational universals associated with MECs, alongside four absolute universals and five tendencies. Here, we focus on two of the implicational universals. The first of these involves the set of verbs that embed MECs (5). The least marked verbs are those that are typically used in existential constructions cross-linguistically, such as be and have (have-type). More marked are dynamic predicates, such as find and seek (find-type), give and bring (give-type), and build and buy (build-type). Thus, the verbs that can embed MECs are those whose lexical meaning supports existential quantification of an indefinite internal argument (Szabolcsi, 1986; Grosu, 2004). Notably, however, verbs such as want and need (want-type) cannot embed MECs.

(5) Hierarchy of MEC-embedding verbs {have-type} > {find-type} > {give-, build-type}; {want-type} is universally unavailable Šimík explains some aspects of the verb hierarchy on the basis of lexical decomposition: verbs that embed MECs contain BE in some capacity (cf. Izvorski, 1998).

For the have-type verbs, be and have (where have is a complex predicate composed of BE + AT), BE is part of the lexical material (e.g., Freeze, 1992; Kayne, 1993; Harley, 2004). The dynamic verbs that embed MECs (nearly) all express a change of state. Additionally, these verbs encode a result state that corresponds to the existence/availability of something (e.g., Beck & Johnson, 2004; Dowty, 1972; Larson, 1988; von Wright, 1963). It is in the result state that an event extension BE argument can be selected. Thus, the reason want-type verbs do not embed MECs, despite having related semantics to other MEC-embedding verbs, is because they do not encode result states. Šimík does not discuss the further division between the dynamic verbs in embedding MECs, where find-type verbs are less marked than give-and build-type verbs; however, this division can be observed elsewhere in the grammatical system. Specifically, all of the individual verbs within each of the possible MEC-embedding verb-types have been argued to be members of the inventory of intensional transitive verbs (Moltmann, 1997; Schwarz, 2020), though not all intensional transitive verbs may embed MECs (e.g., want and need). One of the main approaches to these verbs, propositionalism, argues that intensional transitive verbs do not relate two noun phrases to one another; rather, they contain hidden clausal structure (McCawley, 1974; den Dikken et al., 2018). This argument comes from the observation that sentences like (6a) can be paraphrased as (6b). ( 6)a. John wants a dog. b. John wants to have a dog.

The clearest, and most discussed, cases of hidden clauses come from volitive or volitional verbs which are argued to embed a non-finite complement containing have. Notably, this hidden verb corresponds to the least marked verb-type on the hierarchy. Den Dikken et al. (2018)also argue that verbs of searching contain a hidden clause of a different type, namely one containing find. This argument is exemplified by the fact that seek can often be paraphrased as 'try to find' or 'seek to find' ( 7).

(7) a. John is seeking his mother. b. John is trying to find his mother. c. John is seeking to find his mother.

Again, the verb in the hidden clause corresponds to one of the verb-types that embed MECs, but it is one of the dynamic verbs. For the remaining verb-types there are no arguments, as far as we are aware, that they occur as the verbs of hidden complements for any class of intensional transitive verbs. Therefore, based on this propositionalist-style analysis, find-type verbs may be more basic (or contain more basic elements) than other dynamic verbs that allow MECs, producing the observed hierarchy. 3The second hierarchy involves the set of wh-words that participate in MECs (8). Šimík (2011) observes that all languages that exhibit MECs do so with what, who, and where, but fewer languages also do so with when and how, and finally even fewer languages allow MECs with why (8). Additionally, while wh-words may participate in MECs, multi-word wh-phrases never do. For example, Spanish is shown to allow all possible wh-words in MECs, but French is noted to be more restrictive than Spanish (Thomas, 2008). Specifically, Thomas (2008) argues that French is among the most restrictive languages by only allowing the use of the least marked wh-words (i.e., what, who, and where). However, our data from native speakers suggest that younger French speakers accept MECs with all wh-words except why.

(8) Wh-word hierarchy {what, who, where} > {when, how} > {why}; wh-phrases are universally unavailable Šimík does not provide an explanation of the observed hierarchy; however, this hierarchy is compatible with otherwise observed wh-hierarchies in the acquisition literature, where children begin to produce the wh-words in the order represented in (8; e.g., Roeper & de Villiers, 2011).

Additionally, this hierarchy is relevant for locality phenomena in that the lower a wh-word is on the hierarchy in (8), the less likely it is that it can be extracted from an embedded structure (Rizzi, 1990). As with the hierarchy for the predicates that can embed MECs in (5), the logic of our investigation relies on the accuracy of the hierarchy of wh-words in (8), not its ultimate explanation.

2.2 Research questions

This study investigates the transfer of MECs by L1 Spanish and L1 French learners to L2 English, as well as the subsequent restructuring of the L2 Interlanguage grammar (retreat from transfer), through the lens of VL. Participants completed an acceptability judgement task that tested for the presence of MECs in their English Interlanguage system with a focus on the two implicational hierarchies presented above: (1) verb-type, and (2) question word.

The research questions underlying this study are as follows: 1.

Do learners transfer MECs? Guided by Schwartz and Sprouse's (1996) assumptions of full transfer of the L1 system at the onset of L2 acquisition, we first sought to verify whether MECs transfer from the L1 to the L2. However, the granularity of said transfer is still unclear. That is, will learners transfer the licensing of MECs generally, or will precisely the lexical items (verbs and/or wh-words) that occur with MECs in the L1 be the only lexical items that occur with MECs in the L2? Sprouse's (2006) Relexification Hypothesis predicts the latter.

2. Do the implicational universals associated with MECs determine the path in which learners retreat from transfer? Following VL, the implicational universals will determine the path in which learners retreat from transfer. Specifically, if the implicational universals play a role, learners should retreat from the more marked grammar either before, or simultaneously with, the less marked grammar.

3. How does input frequency interact with any effect found for the role of implicational universals? While it would be fully consistent with VL that input frequency interacts with implicational universals in the case of MECs, VL does not necessarily require this.

3. Method 3.1

Participants Data were collected from 25 L1 English, 33 L1 Spanish/L2 English, and 32 L1 French/L2 English participants. Participants were recruited via Prolific and provided with $10 compensation for their time. At the time of testing, participants were living in the United States (L1 English), Spain (L1 Spanish), or France (L1 French). Participants were required to be at least 18 years old. Additionally, the L2 English groups were required to have acquired English as their L2 sensu stricto, i.e., as the first language after their L1, but they were not restricted regarding further sequentially acquired languages. Simultaneous bilinguals (multiple L1s) were not included. The L1 English group was not restricted regarding languages acquired sequentially after the L1 (i.e., L2+), but as with the L2 groups, simultaneous bilinguals were excluded.

Of the recruited participants, 8 were later excluded from the analysis. Because the study was conducted remotely and due to our recruitment method, more conservative attention to the data was adopted. Participants were excluded for one or more of the following reasons: • Simultaneous bilinguals and/or English not the L2 (n = 3); • Clear lack of effort and/or low scores on the filler items (n = 3); • Incomplete data sets (n = 4).

5. Discussion

In this study we examined the preemption of MECs by L1 Spanish and L1 French learners of L2 English. We first examined the learners' behavior in their L1s, finding that while the L1 Spanish learners behaved as expected, based on the extant literature, the L1 French learners did not. That is, the L1 Spanish learners accepted MECs with all (licit) embedding verbs and with all wh-words. The L1 French learners, however, accepted MECs with all (licit) embedding verbs (as expected), but only rejected MECs with why, accepting all other wh-words (contra Hirschbühler, 1978). We also found that the L1 English speakers performed largely as expected in English: generally rejecting MECs regardless of the wh-word or the embedding verb. However, there was some increased acceptance of find-type verbs. We suggest that this behavior is due to many of the items containing these verbs being grammatical strings, albeit not appropriate in the provided context.

Regarding the L2 English Interlanguage grammar, we found residual evidence of transfer, though our participants were sufficiently advanced that they were approaching target-like behavior. Further evidence of transfer was found in the effects of proficiency on participants' ratings (i.e., ratings were higher at lower proficiencies). Additionally, both groups tended to rate MECs with find-type verbs higher than other verb-types. Again, this behavior may be linked to a lack of integration of the context when making the acceptability judgement. Furthermore, for the L1 Spanish learners, who were generally more accepting of MECs, there was a trend for have-type verbs to be rated higher than other verb types. This trend suggests that preempting less marked options in an implicational universal is more difficult than preempting more marked options. Furthermore, we found that, among the wh-words, why (the most marked wh-word) was typically rated lower than the others. Again, this behavior points to markedness within an implicational hierarchy playing a role in the ease of preemption. Finally, where tended to be rated higher than the other wh-words by both groups. We address this finding in the following section.

5.1 Effects of frequency

To get a sense of the occurrence of each wh-word in MECs cross-linguistically, we searched the Spanish Web 2023 (esTenTen) corpus, which is a corpus of collected internet texts from European and American Spanish, using SketchEngine (Kilgarriff & Renau, 2013). We employed a CQL search consisting of the lemma tener ('to have'), an optional preposition, a wh-word, and an infinitive verb, and the returned tokens were manually checked by a native Spanish speaker to remove sentences that were not MECs. We found that about 60 percent of all the identified MECs contained 'what' and 'who', but only about 15 percent contained 'where'. The remaining 25 percent of MECs were distributed among the other wh-words. These findings correspond to Šimík's (2008) corpus search of Czech MECs, where 'what' and 'who' accounted for 44 and 34 percent of all MECs found, respectively; 22 percent contained 'when', 'how', and 'why' combined. However, Šimík did not identify any instances of 'where' in his search. The alignment of these two corpus searches suggests that 'where'-MECs occur less frequently than the other wh-words at the same level of the implicational hierarchy in languages that have MECs. Next, to get a sense of the type and amount of (relevant) input the L2 learners would be exposed to in English, we searched for contexts where MECs would be licit in the L1. We used simple string searches consisting of the verb have (lemma), some-/any-indefinites, and to (e.g., 'have something to'). The returned tokens were further manually checked to remove any tokens that would not allow MECs in Spanish or French. For example, 'I can't come, I have something do' would be removed because it has the incorrect modal flavor (i.e., deontic) and force (i.e., necessity). We only conducted the search for the indefinite pronouns corresponding to 'what', 'who', and 'where' because the other wh-words do not have indefinite pronouns of the same type (e.g., *somewhen). However, based on the findings from our search of the L1 Spanish corpus and Šimík's (2008) Czech corpus search, we expect that the contexts that would allow MECs with 'when', 'how', and 'why' would occur less frequently in the English input as well.

We further searched four English corpora: the British National Corpus (BNC; BNC Consortium, 2007), the Corpus of English as a Foreign Language (COREFL; Lozano et al., 2020), the Arab College Corpus: EFL Learners' Corpus (ArabCC; Abboud et al., 2025), and the Flensburg English Classroom Corpus (FLECC; Jäkel, 2010). We examined the BNC because it provides an estimate of what L1 English speakers would be exposed to. While it is likely not a good estimate of L2 input (e.g., Rankin & Wagner, 2025), it is a larger corpus, and size can be an issue for L2 corpora. COREFL was chosen because it provides both written and spoken English data from L1 Spanish and German speakers, and it is relatively large by L2 standards. ArabCC was chosen because it contains written English essays from L1 Arabic college students, and while Arabic is not one of our tested languages, it does have MECs (Caponigro, 2003; Grosu, 2004). Finally, FLECC was chosen because it contains transcriptions of English language lessons in German schools from 3 rd to 10 th grade and contains speech from both the students and teachers, so it might provide more information on the input to students than the other two L2 corpora. Table 5 summarizes the findings of this search by corpus. We can see from Table 5 that contexts where MECs would be licit in the L1s are not particularly frequent in English, especially in the L2 input. While there are a few examples of MEC-licit contexts in two of the L2 corpora, there are none in the third. Additionally, their occurrence per million words is not particularly high even in the BNC. Still, a pattern starts to emerge among the three searched indefinites: contexts where 'what'-MECs would be licit occur most frequently, followed by 'who'-MECs, and 'where'-MECs are least frequent. In fact, across all the L2 corpora, no instances of contexts where a 'where'-MEC would be licit in the L1 were found.

We assume that the choice between the MEC and the indefinite pronoun is in competition under VL. As the learner continues to encounter input that can be successfully parsed with the indefinite pronoun, but never encounters input that rewards the MEC grammar, the probability of selecting the grammar with the indefinite pronoun increases. Because the combined probability of selecting either grammar can never exceed 1.0, the probability of selecting the grammar with the MEC correspondingly decreases with each increase for the indefinite pronoun grammar. This process continues until the probability of selecting the MEC grammar is at such a negligible level that it is never retrieved or difficult to access. Functionally, at this point the MEC grammar has been expunged/preempted from the interlanguage system. We would therefore expect that MECs with wh-words where the corresponding combination of an MEC embedding verb and the indefinite pronoun (e.g., have what vs. have something) are less frequent in the input will be expunged more slowly because the rewards for the indefinite pronoun grammar are accruing at a slower rate. Note that this argumentation assumes that the granularity of competing grammars is at the lexical level and not simply broad constructions. Therefore, based on the corpus findings above, MECs occurring with 'where', 'how', 'when', and 'why' should be expunged more slowly than MECs occurring with 'what' or 'who'.

However, it is at this point that the implicational universals play a role. Because the whwords other than 'where' are lower (more marked) in the hierarchy, they must be expunged when something higher (less marked) on the hierarchy is expunged. So, as the high frequency of occurrence for 'what' and 'who' MECs leads to their preemption, it also causes the preemption of 'when', 'how', and 'why' MECs. However, because 'where' is at the same level of the implicational hierarchy as 'what' and 'who', its preemption is not caused by their expungement. This is exactly the pattern observed for the L1 Spanish learners.

To wrap up our discussion, we return to our three research questions. RQ 1: Do learners transfer MECs? Yes, but we were unable to delve into Sprouse's (2006) fine-grained Relexification Hypothesis as planned, because our L1 French data do not match the claimed difference in acceptability of MECs with different wh-words.

RQ 2: Do the implicational universals associated with MECs determine the path in which learners retreat from transfer? Yes, it seems that wh-words on the more marked end of the hierarchy are easier to expunge from the Interlanguage grammar than those at the less marked end of the hierarchy.

RQ 3: How does input frequency interact with any effect found for the role of implicational universals? We were able to observe this effect only for the interaction with the implicational universal regarding wh-words, but not for the implicational universal regarding embedding predicates. In this case, the preemption of less marked (but more frequently occurring) wh-words caused the preemption of more marked (but less frequent) wh-words. However, the preemption of the more frequent wh-words did not cause the preemption of less frequent wh-words at the same level of markedness.

6. Conclusion

This study focused on one of the under-investigated puzzles of nonnative language acquisition, viz., how it is possible for a developing Interlanguage grammar to retreat from a morphosyntactic possibility transferred from the L1 when there is no direct negative evidence in the TL input that the structure in question is ungrammatical in the L2. In the case at hand, the poverty of the stimulus for the retraction is particularly acute for (at least) three reasons: (1) The transferred structure is associated with a subtle semantic effect (circumstantial possibility, not deontic or epistemic modality). (2) The unacceptability of the transferred structure is not mentioned in language instruction. (3) Discourse contexts in which either the ungrammatical L1 structure or its grammatical L2 paraphrase might occur appear to be strikingly infrequent. Yang's (2002) Variational Learning approach, nevertheless, offers a way to model the observable differences between L1 Spanish vs. L1 French L2 learners of English. First, there is clear evidence in their acceptability ratings that learners in both groups have transferred the possibility of MECs from their L1, given that there is nothing in the L2 input to suggest that MECs are licit in English. Furthermore, the L1 Spanish learners display an overall higher rate of acceptance of MECs in English than the L1 French learners do, aligning with the fact that MECs are generally rated lower in L1 French than in L1 Spanish. Assuming that in contexts of circumstantial possibility there is a competition between an MEC and an indefinite pronoun, the contexts in which an MEC are headed by 'where' appear to be less frequent, and thus MECs with 'where' are slower to be expunged than MECs with 'what' and 'who.' This confirms the relevance of frequency in the sense of VL.

This study is limited specifically because it examines the retraction from a transferred structure for which there are very infrequent discourse contexts for competition, and the level of granularity of our results cannot fully match those found in the syntax-semantics literature based on introspective data from L1 speakers of languages that sanction MECs. Furthermore, it would be impractical to conduct longitudinal studies tracking the retreat from MECs in the English Interlanguage grammars of individual learners. Thus, we have had to rely on relatively crude measures such as self-reported language dominance. Nevertheless, an overall picture has emerged that is consistent with expectations derived from Yang's VL approach. Additionally, our experimental results for French do not fully match what is reported in the syntax-semantics literature (e.g., Hirschbühler 1978; Šimik 2011); thus, we could not test the granularity of transfer at the level of individual lexical items (in the sense of Sprouse 2006).

Finally, this study considered L2 learners whose L1s differ in the set of wh-words that may be used in MECs. Future research could investigate L2 learners whose L1s differ in the set of verbs that can embed MECs or that differ in both respects. One might also seek participants with lower L2 English proficiency than the participants recruited for the present study. This would allow for a more nuanced investigation of the granularity of transfer. On a broader scale, further research could consider other cases of potential interaction of frequency with implicational universals.
