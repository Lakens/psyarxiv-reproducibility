Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Therapeutic relationships between service users and staff on inpatient mental health wards are central to the delivery of high-quality care and have been prioritised by policy makers. Yet these relationships are frequently found to be poor across inpatient settings, and more evidence is needed on how to foster good therapeutic relationships. In collaboration with lived experience researchers, we conducted a large systematic review, which was requested and carried out in partnership with NHS England, to identify facilitators and barriers to effective therapeutic relationships on inpatient wards.

Methods: We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, HMIC (between January 1999-September 2025) for quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods primary research studies.

Study quality was assessed using the Mixed Method Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Data were synthesised using an iterative analytic process, informed by framework analysis and narrative synthesis, across five interconnected levels: 1) individual (service user and staff experiences), 2) microsystem (daily interactions), 3) mesosystem (ward environment), 4) macrosystem (the wider organisation), 5) chronosystem (time and change across levels).

Results: 184 studies with 19,984 participants (n=16,138 service users, n=3,846 staff) were included. Across study designs, four main categories emerged: 1) reducing power imbalances helped facilitate positive relationships and, conversely, restrictive practices damaged them; 2) staff relational skills including empathy, non-judgemental approaches and treating service users as individuals, rather than simply 'patients', helped build positive relationships; 3) staff continuity and time for staff to connect with service users was critical to building positive relationships; ward contexts that prioritised task completion and/or were risk-focused weakened relationships; 4) ward environments that created opportunities to build interpersonal connections between staff and service users (e.g. shared spaces) strengthened relationships whereas those that provided limited opportunities for interaction weakened relationships.

We identified four key factors that can help or hinder therapeutic relationships on mental health inpatient wards: power dynamics between staff and service users; restrictive practices; relational skills of staff; staff availability and consistency, and ward environments that facilitated interpersonal connections. We found evidence gaps particularly for minoritised ethnic communities and organisational influences. We suggest strategies which could help relationship building, including support with repairing relational ruptures, greater emphasis on relationship building in staff roles, and ward environments that support connection.

Registration: The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251047929).

Introduction

A therapeutic relationship refers to the relationships between staff and the people they care for, which can have a positive, neutral or negative effect. There is no universally agreed definition of therapeutic relationships, and most research on this topic has been conducted within psychotherapy or counselling contexts. We, therefore, developed a working definition of therapeutic relationships to support this study (see below), and our related work has summarised definitions used across the academic landscape [1].

Therapeutic relationships between service users and staff are widely recognised as central to the delivery of safe, effective, high-quality mental healthcare. Reviews of people receiving care from inpatient and outpatient mental health services have found that positive therapeutic relationships are associated with improved outcomes [2], including better quality of life, recovery and functioning, and fewer hospitalisations [2] [3] [4]. Yet, therapeutic relationships between staff and service users are often reported to be poor on mental health inpatient wards [5]. In the UK in 2025 the Health Services Safety Investigations Body (HSSIB) investigated learnings from deaths in inpatient mental health services [6]. The recommendations in the report prioritised the importance of therapeutic relationships [6].

It is important to understand what helps establish and sustain therapeutic relationships, as well as what undermines them, on inpatient mental health wards. Two narrative reviews [7], both including 48 papers, have summarised the literature on therapeutic relationships in the context of psychiatric nursing care and various acute psychiatric settings [8, 9]. It remains unclear, therefore, what barriers and facilitators to positive therapeutic relationships exist across staff clinical disciplines and across the breadth of psychiatric inpatient settings. In addition, the narrative review research teams did not involve people with lived experience of inpatient care in the design of the review aims or objectives nor in the synthesis of data. The two narrative reviews identified barriers to therapeutic relationships at the individual-level (e.g. among staff and service users) and organisational-level. Barriers included the use of restrictive practices (practices which limit a service user's freedom and are considered necessary to keep service users safe, including involuntary admission), lack of staff time and availability, lack of staff job satisfaction, staff treating service users as 'patients' instead of individuals, and organisational rules and codes of conduct which can limit engagement [8, 9].

Facilitators were less frequently reported and included good staff communication skills and understanding and meeting the individual needs of service users [8, 9].

There is a need to examine barriers and facilitators to therapeutic relationships for groups that may be overrepresented in mental health inpatient care, including people from ethnic minority backgrounds and migrants. For example, a systematic review of international research found people from Black Caribbean backgrounds were 2.5 times more likely to be involuntarily admitted to psychiatric hospital than White people while those from south Asian backgrounds were 1.3 times more likely to be admitted involuntarily [10]. Migrants were also 1.5 times more likely than non-migrants to be involuntarily admitted [10]. To our knowledge, no review on therapeutic relationships has specifically examined the evidence for underserved groups.

Given the limitations of prior international reviews, prioritisation of therapeutic relationships by the Health Services Safety Investigations Body (HSSIB), our research group, the NIHR Policy Research Unit in Mental Health (MHPRU) was requested by NHS England to conduct a systematic review examining the barriers and facilitators to therapeutic relationships on mental health inpatient wards. The review was carried out in partnership with NHS England and will complement their official response to the HSSIB Report. We addressed the following research questions: i) What are the facilitators that promote therapeutic relationships between staff and service users in mental health inpatient settings, including for underserved groups?

ii) What are the barriers to forming therapeutic relationships between staff and service users in mental health inpatient settings, including for underserved groups?

METHODS

Lived experience researcher involvement and project working group

A project working group consisting of researchers, clinicians, Lived Experience Researchers (LERs), and NHS England policy makers met monthly throughout the project lifecycle. LERs had experience of using mental health services, including receiving care in inpatient wards or supporting those with mental health difficulties. The LERs contributed extensively to this review, including collaborating on protocol development, study selection, undertaking data extraction and synthesis, reviewing the manuscript and writing the lived experience commentary.

Definition of therapeutic relationships and facilitators and barriers

For the purposes of the review, we reached a consensus definition of 'therapeutic relationships' and 'facilitators and barriers' through reviewing definitions from previous literature, and discussion with the project working group which included people with experience of mental health inpatient care as clinicians or service users.

Therapeutic relationship: Therapeutic relationship is difficult to define. Existing research has largely focused on psychotherapy or counselling contexts, defining the relationship between therapist and client. This has limited relevance to inpatient settings and does not account for the impact of restrictive practices on the relationship. Our working group also felt it was important to include recognition that the relationship may also have dysfunctional patterns.

We agreed on the following definition: 'The therapeutic relationship refers to the relationship between staff and service users in inpatient care which could have a positive, neutral or negative effect. The relationship could be characterised by empathy, trust, care, respect, communication, connection and collaboration, but could also have dysfunctional patterns and an imbalance of power. Staff should maintain professional and ethical boundaries throughout the relationship.' Facilitators and Barriers: Facilitators and Barriers may not be formally labelled as such in studies; we therefore developed the following definition: 'Facilitators are factors that support, promote, or enable the development and/or maintenance of therapeutic relationships between staff and service users. Barriers are factors that prevent, hinder or obstruct the development and/or maintenance of therapeutic relationships between staff and service users.' Underserved groups: Underserved groups refer to people who may experience disadvantage, discrimination, reduced power or exclusion. These experiences contribute to inequalities in mental health inpatient care experiences and therapeutic relationships. These groups may include people detained under mental health legislation, people from ethnic minority backgrounds, people accessing care via the criminal justice system and others who face structural or social disadvantage.

Research Group

Study design and protocol

We conducted a systematic mixed methods review following guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [11], Cochrane guidance for the rapid synthesis of qualitative data in reviews [12], and narrative synthesis for systematic reviews [13]. The review is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (see Additional File 1: Appendix 1 for the PRISMA checklist) [7]. The protocol for this review is published on Prospero (ref: CRD420251047929) [14] (see additional file 1: appendix 2 for protocol amendments).

Eligibility criteria

We included studies which explored facilitators or barriers to any form of therapeutic relationship in mental health inpatient care (see definition of inpatient care in Table 1 below) between service users and staff. Eligibility criteria are summarised in Table 1 and in full in the protocol [14].

Information sources and search strategy

We combined terms for therapeutic relationship, mental health conditions, inpatient care and facilitators or barriers using Boolean operators (AND, OR). We searched for published studies in the following databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, and CINAHL. We searched for grey literature in the following databases: HMIC (Health Management Information Consortium), Google and Google Scholar, limiting screening to the first 10 pages (see Additional File 1: Appendix 3 for full search strategy). Searches were run from the prior 25 years (from 1 st January 1999). We chose this time frame to: i) ensure the research was relevant to current practice, and ii) 1999 was when the National Service Framework for mental health was published in the UK which introduced a set of standard models for mental health care delivery [15]. There were no language restrictions. For papers not in English, a fluent speaker or AI or neural machine translation [16] was used to screen title and abstracts of papers for inclusion. This approach has been recommended as a way of reducing language bias in reviews, and previous research has shown satisfactory accuracy [17]. A fluent speaker was required to screen the full texts and extract data from papers.

Selection process

Reviewers screened titles and abstracts (RC, AD, WL, JB, JL, FE, SL), and full texts (RC, AD, JB, JL, FE, SL, LL, DC). To ensure consistent application of eligibility criteria, all reviewers initially independently screened the same titles, abstracts and full texts of 10 papers before coming back together as a group to discuss decisions around eligibility. The remaining titles and abstracts and full texts were shared out for screening across researchers. Reviewers independently double screened 30% of titles and abstracts with good (88%) agreement [18].

Reviewers independently double screened 100% of full texts, with 78% agreement. All disagreements were resolved through discussion within the review team.

Data extraction

Data extraction was completed in Microsoft Excel by reviewers (RC, SL, JB, WL, DC, LL, FE, JL, AD). The data extraction sheet was piloted by two senior members of the team and revised accordingly. Detailed instruction guides were provided to all team members conducting data extraction. The team met weekly to discuss and resolve data extraction queries. The extracted data included basic information about the study (e.g. aim, methods, country, lived experience involvement, participant number and characteristics) and quantitative or qualitative data which addressed our research questions. Data were identified as representing a facilitator and/or barrier during extraction.

Study risk of bias assessment

Quality of included studies was independently assessed by reviewers (RC, AD, MT, JT, JL, KT, DC, SO) using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). A proportion of papers (20%) were double rated, with discrepancies resolved through discussion; an agreement rate of over 90% was achieved during double rating. Each study was evaluated according to the two screening questions and the five criteria based on the study design which were all rated as "yes", "no", or "can't tell". Studies were rated out of a total of 5, with 1 given for 'yes' and 0 given for 'no' or 'can't tell'.

Synthesis methods

We synthesised data using a convergent synthesis design, whereby qualitative and quantitative data were analysed and first presented separately and then integrated [19].

Qualitative synthesis:

Qualitative data were synthesised in Microsoft Excel by the review team (LL, UF, RC, JB, AD, WL, DC, MC, KH), which included lived experience researchers. We developed an analytic framework informed by Bronfenbrenner's socio-ecological model [20] to structure the synthesis and capture the complexity of factors shaping the therapeutic relationship. The model comprised five interconnected levels: i) The individual level, encompassing the characteristics and experiences that service users and staff bring with them ii) The microsystem, referring to day-to-day interactions between staff and service users iii) The mesosystem, reflecting the ward environment and staff team dynamics iv) The macrosystem, capturing wider organisational and societal influences v) The chronosystem, reflecting the role of time and change across these interacting levels Together, these interconnected levels provided a framework for understanding the research questions across individual, interpersonal, environmental, and wider systemic contexts, recognising that therapeutic relationships are shaped by a web of interconnected systems.

To categorise and synthesise data within this framework, we undertook an iterative analytic process [21], informed by principles of framework analysis [12, 21, 22]:

We extracted data from each paper and identified the data as a facilitator or a barrier.

ii) We developed inductive codes to address the research questions.

iii) The codes were mapped to one of the 5 system levels (individual, microsystem, mesosystem, macrosystem, or chronosystem).

iv) Each code, alongside a brief description of how it influenced the therapeutic relationship and whether it represented a facilitator or barrier, was entered into the framework. The framework was refined through data familiarisation and coding.

We developed Microsoft Excel spreadsheets to group codes into themes and subthemes within each system-level. Codes from each paper were reviewed and grouped according to shared patterns relating to their influence on the therapeutic relationship, while also distinguishing whether they represented facilitators or barriers. A theme or subtheme consisted of data from two or more studies. Finally, a summary table and accompanying narrative description were developed for each theme, within each level.

Quantitative synthesis:

Quantitative data were synthesised in Microsoft Excel by the review team (SL, RC, AD, WL, MC, KH, JL). Data were synthesised into a framework using the 5 interconnected levels that were used for the qualitative synthesis: 1) Individual Service User and Staff, 2) Microsystem, 3) Mesosystem, 4) Macrosystem, 5) Chronosystem.

To categorise the data into the 5 levels we developed spreadsheets in Microsoft Excel and carried out the following: i) Read over the extracted data for each paper. iv) Synthesis of data within each of the 5 levels. Within each level, data were reviewed and grouped into categories according to shared patterns relating to their influence on the therapeutic relationship, while also distinguishing whether they represented a facilitator, barrier or null relationship.

v) A summary table and narrative descriptions for each category, under each level was developed. A category was included if it had two or more studies for each level.

Integration: Quantitative and qualitative results were tabulated side-by-side within each level. Similar qualitative themes and quantitative categories were grouped together to give overall categories which summarised findings across both types of data.

Results of qualitative synthesis

The qualitative synthesis included 141 studies, including 9 mixed-methods studies [23] [24] [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] [30] [31]. Underserved groups: Across all levels, most themes included the perspectives of service users from underserved groups or staff working with people from underserved groups (e.g. working on forensic wards). For both staff and service users these were dual diagnosis (co-occurring substance use and mental health condition), forensic inpatient settings, involuntarily admitted to wards, including being detained under mental health legislation, received restrictive interventions including being restrained. For staff, this also included older adults, women with postpartum psychosis, asylum seekers and people from diverse cultural and racial backgrounds, including black service users.

Level 1a: Individual service user (characteristics and experiences of service users that influenced the therapeutic relationship). Facilitators were from staff and service user perspectives, barriers were only from the service user perspective.

Facilitators: It was important for staff to have good relational skills, including listening and involving service users in their treatment [24, 30, [40] [41] [42] [43] [44] [45] [32] [33] [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] [39] and recognising and treating service users as individuals instead of 'patients' which helped service users feel valued [39, 40, [46] [47] [48] [49] [50] [51] [52]. The therapeutic relationship was also improved when service users felt they had more agency [33, 36] and shared more of their experience and story [53].

Barriers: Relationships with staff were hindered by inadequate staff relational skills, including not listening to service users [30, 36, 37, 49, 54, 55], and mental health challenges for service users, for example feeling distressed could make it difficult to engage with staff and could make it harder for staff to communicate effectively [30, 46, [64] [65] [66] [67] [68] [69] [70] [56] [57] [58] [59] [60] [61] [62] [63]. Other key barriers were when service users felt they were seen only as 'patients' instead of unique individuals [39, 44, 50, 71] and experiences of restrictive practices (practices which limit a service user's freedom and are considered necessary to keep service users safe, including involuntary admission) [24, 27, 72, 73] which could lead to fearfulness and mistrust of staff, damaging the therapeutic relationship.

Level 1b: Individual staff (characteristics and experiences of staff that influenced the therapeutic relationship). Facilitators and barriers were from staff and service user perspectives.

Facilitators: The therapeutic relationship was strengthened when staff had a variety of relational skills including active listening, being empathetic, and being authentic [30, 31, 66, 68, 69, 73-79, 33, 80-89, 35, 90-99, 37, 47, 48, 53, 56, 63] and had non-judgemental attitudes characterised by openness [24, 29, 100-107, 38, 49, 54, 56, 62, 63, 66, 88]. These attitudes were necessary for staff to approach relationships positively, creating an environment that facilitated trust [42, 43, 106, 108-112, 51, 54, 60, 67, 78, 82, 93, 98]. Staff who focused on person-centred care [23, 29, 113, 114, 46, 48, 56, 66, 92, 95, 101, 111] and initiated contact with service users [63, 98, 99, 113, 115, 116], including non-mental health-related small talk, and suggesting activities, could build trust with service users, strengthening the therapeutic relationship. Staff who were self-aware, understanding the impact of their work on their own emotions, for example through self-reflective practice and maintaining appropriate boundaries, could better empathise with service users [75, 106, 108, [117] [118] [119].

Barriers: key barriers were a negative staff approach to the relationship with service users, especially approaches that reinforced power imbalances and were punitive [37, 39, 110, 117, 120, 121, 43, 58, 67, 72, 77, 78, 97, 102]. Linked to this, other barriers were an absence of adequate relational skills, such as staff who lacked empathy or did not listen [30, 54, 58, 72, 91, 92, 94, 101, 122, 123] and negative attitudes from staff, including judgemental and stigmatising attitudes [39, 58, 120, 124, 125, 71, 80, 82, 85, 87, 103, 107, 119]. Another barrier was the emotional strain and moral distress for staff that could come from working in an inpatient setting, including burn-out due to the demands and volatile nature of their work and distress from enforcing treatment interventions that conflicted with their caring values [26, 48, 120-122, 126-128, 56, 58, 62, 81, 85, 101, 109, 118]. Staff who were inexperienced, or perceived to be inexperienced, in the inpatient care setting had more difficulty connecting with service users and could be more likely to use coercive strategies, further eroding the relationship [58, 77, 108, 120, 121].

Level 2: Microsystem (interactions between staff and service users). Facilitators and barriers were from staff and service user perspectives.

Facilitators:

A key facilitator was power sharing. Therapeutic relationships were strengthened when staff moved beyond traditional hierarchical roles toward more equal ways of relating, such as through structured collaborative practices that gave service users a voice in their care [24, 28, 59, 63-65, 67, 68, 70, 78, 81, 85, 33, 86, 94-97, 99-101, 105, 106, 34, 113, 114, 119, 120, 123, 124, 127, 129-131, 38, 132-141, 40, 41, 44, 48, 54]. Staff presence/availability was key; therapeutic relationships were strengthened when staff were physically present, accessible and proactively engaged with service users [24, 28, 100, 101, 110, 123, 127, 129, 132, 142-144, 34, 145-148, 35, 43, 45, 70, 72, 80, 95]. It was important for staff to have good relational skills, including communicating clearly and honestly, listening attentively and responding with empathy [27, 32, 104, 106, 108, 145, 148-151, 37, 51, 79, 82, 85, 93, 95, 102]. Consistency and continuity of mental health care, when service users experienced regular contact with the same staff over time, this built trust. When staff persisted through difficulty and rebuilt trust after conflict, this signalled that service users mattered regardless of ruptures to the relationship [35, 46, 52, 59, 83, 100, 110, 122, 131, 138]. Barriers: Poor staff relational skills could erode trust and cause service users to disengage from the therapeutic relationship, this included poor non-verbal engagement, failure to explain decisions and when communication was perceived as infantilising, dismissive, or absent [30, 32, 37, 39, 88, 120, 124, 148, 149, 152]. Staff emotional distancing and unavailability, were barriers, including retreating physically to offices or emotionally behind a clinical role, or failing to follow through on commitments, could lead service users to feel abandoned and burdensome, undermining the therapeutic relationship [26, 50, 62, 71, 114, 115, 123, 125, 130]. Coercive or restrictive practices, including restraint, compulsory treatment and the absence of shared decision-making could damage or destroy therapeutic relationships. Fear of coercion could lead service users to withhold information, weakening therapeutic engagement [45, 54, 106, 108, 114, 123, 127, 153-155]. Lack of consistency and continuity of care, including unfamiliar staff and short stays for service users undermined therapeutic relationships [52, 71, 77, 80, 100, 152]. Conflicting views and realities between staff and service users was a barrier, including disagreements about treatment and cultural differences [44, 89, 102, 149, 156].

Level 3: Mesosystem (ward environment and staff team dynamics). Facilitators and barriers were from staff and service user perspectives.

Facilitators: Ward structures and processes which support staff to prioritise spending time to connect with service users [28, 51, 158, 63, 70, 88, 95, 115, 133, 148, 157] and which enable consistent staffing were identified as key facilitators [33, 43, 59, 60, 100, 111, 123]. Ward environments which were safe places to connect, enabled by safe physical environments and/or predictable routines and rules, also helped develop the therapeutic relationship [27, 90, 134, 138]. Staff development and support in turn supported therapeutic relationships, this included informal learning from colleagues, training, supervision, reflective spaces and debriefs [62, 77, 100, 102, 138]. Effective teamwork facilitated therapeutic relationships through the support it provided to staff and due to the benefits of consistency between team members which helped service users form relationships across the team [23, 28, 41, 48, 59, 100, 120, 134, 138].

Barriers: A lack of staff time and availability [30, 37, 96, 97, 100, 101, 104, 107-109, 127, 149, 44, 159, 46, 52, 68, 70, 79, 85, 87], for example due to high administrative responsibilities and staff spending time in offices, and also staff shortages [57, 58, 70, 88, 159] reduced opportunities for staff to spend time with service users, reducing the conditions for the development of a relationship. Inconsistent and changing staff teams linked to high staff turnover could lead to service users being assigned multiple members of staff, making it difficult to develop relationships [45-47, 102, 130]. Ward and team cultures could be a barrier when they undermined staff wanting to invest in relationships e.g. dominant narratives about the risk of becoming 'over-involved'. Hierarchical and rigid team structures constrained staff advocacy, and service users noticed when teams were not working well together [46, 58, 93, 126]. When service users felt they lacked involvement in decisions, they felt less valued and less inclined to trust staff which weakened the therapeutic relationship [35, 39, 60, 114, 155].

Restrictive and disempowering practices were important barriers, these included restricted freedoms (locked doors, frequent observations) [30, 42, 55, 80, 88, 160] alongside restraint and other coercive practices [47, 49, 97, 102, 141, 50, 56, 70, 73, 85, 93, 95, 96]. When the ward prioritised risk-focused routines and practices this could limit engagement and prevent staff connecting to service users as people [37, 59, 86, 124, 154, 161]. Rigid rules and routines were also a barrier, for example when staff were seen to be hiding behind the rules to avoid genuine connection this communicated mistrust in service users [23, 34, 50, 56, 82, 85, 114, 115, 121].

Level 4: Macrosystem (wider organisational and societal influences). Facilitators and barriers

were from staff and service user perspectives.

Facilitators: supportive organisational structures and policies emerged as key facilitators which could embed relational collaboration into practice, providing a foundation for therapeutic relationships to develop. This included a balance between experienced and inexperienced staff on shift to reduce the use of coercive practices and structured routines to enable service user involvement in decisions, such as attending treatment meetings [94, 106, 108]. Ward environments that supported engagement, including quiet spaces, welcoming social atmospheres and access to outdoor areas could support opportunities for therapeutic engagement between staff and service users [25, 100].

Barriers:

A key barrier was staffing pressures and resource constraints, which reduced staff time and capacity for one-to-one contact with service users, undermining therapeutic relationships [28, 30, 41, 46, 77, 94, 111, 120, 133, 143]. Ward environments that weakened engagement were also barriers. Locked doors, noise, visible restraints and an absence of quiet or welcoming spaces constrained therapeutic contact [67, 70, 115, 120, 130, 162]. Legal frameworks and power structures including The Mental Health Act, courtmandated treatments and police involvement in admitting service users involuntarily, could complicate service user trust and agency within therapeutic relationships [27, 35, 67, 70, 161].

Linked to this, power imbalances and coercive practices, including the use of clinical authority, seclusion and coercion damaged service users' trust and agency within therapeutic relationships [52, 55, 145]. Stigma, discrimination and lack of cultural competence were identified as barriers in which unaddressed structural racism, cultural insensitivity and entrenched ward cultures left service users, particularly those from underserved communities, experiencing relationships with staff as unsafe [42, 67, 104, 120]. Task-focused and transactional organisational cultures, when organisational cultures prioritised efficiency, risk management and task completion over relational care, this limited opportunities for therapeutic engagement [95, 104, 163]. Staff with insufficient training in communication or therapeutic skills could default to coercive or task-based approaches that undermined therapeutic relationships [72, 108, 148]. Fragmented and limited mental health systems and little access to external support systems (family and social support) created conditions in which consistent therapeutic relationships were difficult to maintain [46, 94, 111].

Results of quantitative synthesis

The quantitative synthesis included 42 studies, including 2 mixed-methods studies [164, 165].

A summary of results is in Table 4. There was no quantitative evidence at the Macrosystem level, and only one study [166] examined the role of time at the Chronosystem level by evaluating the trajectory of therapeutic relationship at early, middle, and later stage of a course of psychological intervention. Eight RCTs [167] [168] [169] [170] [171] [172] [173] [174] and five non-randomised trials [164, [175] [176] [177] [178] assessed the effects of psychosocial, hormonal or organisational interventions in improving therapeutic relationships.

The remaining studies (n=31) were cross-sectional/observational studies which examined the association between a determinant and the measured therapeutic relationship using t-test, correlation, regression, structural equation modelling, or moderation analysis. These analyses mostly estimate the strength and direction of an association at a single time point and, therefore, cannot support any causal inferences.

Underserved groups: Across all levels few quantitative studies examined determinants specifically within underserved groups, and these were concentrated at the individual service user and mesosystem levels. The groups represented were staff and service users in forensic or secure settings, including being detained under mental health legislation, and being involuntarily admitted.

Level 1a: Individual service user (characteristics and experiences of service users)

For service users' demographic characteristics, mixed evidence was found for age where one study [179] found older service users reported a stronger relationship while another study [180] found no significant association between service users' age and their perceived therapeutic relationships. Only one study compared therapeutic relationship between male and female service users and found no significant differences across gender [180].

Service users' attachment and relational styles were assessed across three studies [180] [181] [182], with service users' tendency to perceive interactions as hostile associated with a weaker alliance [180] and no significant associations with anxious or avoidant attachment styles [182].

One study examined attachment styles by service users' gender and distinguished same-sex and opposite-sex styles [181]. For female service users, the study found service users with secure or preoccupied same-sex attachment style reporting strong affective bond with staff compared to those with dismissing style [181]. For male service users, those with secure or fearful same-sex attachment styles were rated higher on working alliance by case managers compared to those with dismissing style [181]. There was no significant difference across the four opposite-sex attachment styles (secure, fearful, preoccupied, dismissing) of male or female service users rated by service users or case managers [181]. For power, choice and control, two studies found that perceived/reported readiness to change facilitated the relationship: service users at the contemplation stage (considering change but perceived to be ambivalent) reported a stronger emotional bond and agreement on collaboration [183], and treatment-ready service users reported a stronger working alliance [184]. Being in the action, maintenance or precontemplation stages was not associated with the alliance [183].

For service users' mental health diagnoses or symptom severity, more severe psychotic or depressive symptoms [185], or the presentation of psychiatric symptoms as paranoid disturbance [180] were associated with weaker therapeutic relationships, though no significant association was found for therapist-rated impairment [186]. One study [187] compared service users with or without traits of three personality disorders (borderline, narcissistic, antisocial) and found service users with borderline personality disorder traits with weaker working alliance. There was no between-group difference between service users with narcissistic or antisocial personality disorder traits and those without [187]. Therapeutic relationship did not differ significantly across different diagnoses, including depressive, anxiety, somatoform, stress, eating, personality disorders [188]. For service users' psychological or emotional distress, service users who felt more trapped by their external environment reported a weaker affective bond with staff [189], although this was not significant for total working alliance, while feelings of defeat, internal entrapment, and suicidality were not associated with the alliance [189]. Two studies [186, 190] found no association between service users' psychological distress and therapeutic relationship. There was mixed evidence for service users' interpersonal distress, where one study found it to be negatively associated with therapeutic relationships [190] but another study found the association not significant [186]. For treatment response and outcomes, two studies found service users with greater symptom reduction post treatment had a better therapeutic relationship [188, 191], however in one study this relationship did not hold when the therapeutic relationship was rated by staff [191]. Improvement in wellbeing was also found to be associated with higher quality of therapeutic alliance [192].

Level 1b: Individual staff (characteristics and experiences of staff)

For staff experience, mixed evidence was found for the number of years staff spent working in mental health care, with positive association with staff-rated alliance in one study [193] but negative association with staff-rated alliance in another study [189]. For staff expertise and training, staff with PhD or Master's-level qualifications reported a higher total alliance than those qualified to Bachelor's level [193], while no significant differences were found between staff specialised in mental health nursing and non-specialised staff [193]. For staff relational skills, one study assessed four dimensions of staff's empathic approach [193] and found that a greater tendency to feel compassion (perspective taking) and concern (empathic concern) towards service users was associated with a stronger total alliance, the tendency to react with personal distress to service users' suffering (personal distress) was associated with a weaker alliance, while staff's capacity to imagine the situation and feelings of service users (fantasy) was not significantly associated with any dimension of the working alliance.

For staff demographic and personal characteristics, no significant association was found between staff's attachment styles (secure or preoccupied) and therapeutic alliance [190]. Mixed evidence was found for staff gender across three studies, with one study that finding female staff reporting a higher total alliance than male staff [189], and no significant associations with gender in two other studies [190, 193].

Level 2: Microsystem (day-to-day interactions between staff and service users)

For staff presence and continuity, a single study produced divergent findings where the length of current admission and the length of staff-service user relationship was positively associated with service user-reported working alliance, but negative association was found for staff-rated alliance [189]. Staff contract type (full-time or other) was not associated with any dimension of the alliance [193].

Level 3: Mesosystem (ward environment and staff team dynamics)

For psychosocial interventions, two RCTs [167, 175] found positive effects of interventions on therapeutic relationship, including acceptance and commitment therapy compared to TAU [175], and animal-assisted therapy compared to traditional counselling [167]. Mixed evidence was found in an RCT comparing a single-session group songwriting music therapy to a singlesession group psychoeducation [168] with positive effect found in staff-rated alliance but no significant effect in service user-rated alliance. Three further RCTs found no significant effect of interventions in improving therapeutic relationships compared to TAU, including a motivational-interviewing-based manual for inpatient anorexia nervosa [169], an ultra-brief cognitive behavioural therapy intervention [170], and peer-worker-supported psychiatric advance directives [171].

For power, choice and control, service users' belief that the admission process was fair and justified was positively associated with working alliance [194] and staff and service users rated a better therapeutic alliance when recovery oriented practices were used (e.g. service user involvement and choice in their care) [164]. Mixed evidence was found for involuntary admission where stronger alliance was found in service users admitted voluntarily compared to those admitted involuntary [194], but the opposite was found in another study where involuntarily admitted service users rated higher alliance [180]. Three studies assessed the association between perceived coercion and therapeutic relationship and generated mixed findings of positive association [180], negative association [195], and no significant association [194].

For ward environment, positive ward social climate with greater freedom to express feelings, stronger mutual support, and clearer goals and rules [179], service user satisfaction with the forensic ward [196], and mental health service meeting the attachment needs of service users, including provision of a 'secure base' [182]. No significant difference in therapeutic hold (service users' perceived support towards their therapeutic needs) was found across closed, recently opened, or open ward types [197].

For training and organisational interventions, three organisational interventions were shown to have positive effect on therapeutic relationship, including staff reflective practice consultation compared to a programme to encourage staff-patient contact [177], shared decision-making training for staff and service user compared to TAU [173], and staff communication training [176]. Two organisational interventions were found to have no significant effect, including a shared decision-making training for service user compared to non-specific cognitive group training [172], and a staff workshop on challenging behaviours [164]. Staff evidence-based practice training [178] was found with mixed evidence, with positive effect (pre-vs post-training on total alliance and intervention vs TAU on total alliance), but with no effect when compared to TAU on improving affective therapeutic bonds.

Together, quantitative and qualitative data both underpinned numerous potential facilitators and barriers to the therapeutic relationships, mainly at the individual and microsystem levels.

These were the role of power and control versus agency and choice, staff relational skills, their perception of their work, emotional strain in the role, level of experience and training and presence and availability on the wards. At the inpatient unit or organisational levels, both qualitative and quantitative data underpinned the need for ward environments that support connection, effective teamwork and support for staff.

Discussion

We conducted a mixed methods systematic review looking at the facilitators and barriers to effective therapeutic relationships in mental health inpatient care. The review was requested and carried out in partnership with NHS England. To our knowledge this is the largest systematic review conducted on this topic to date. We included 184 studies (the majority (72%) were qualitative studies) which represented over 16,000 service users and over 3,500 staff participants.

For both the qualitative and quantitative synthesis, more studies focused on the individual level, the experiences and characteristics of service users and staff, such as staff relational skills, the microsystem level, the interactions between staff and service users and the mesosystem, ward environment and staff team dynamics, than on how these factors could be supported by the wider organisation and society (macrosystem). Most categories included the views of underserved groups although there were significant evidence gaps, with these groups mainly comprising of people treated in forensic units, people treated involuntarily or who had received restrictive practices.

Key findings

The quantitative and qualitative data suggest four prominent facilitators or barriers to the therapeutic relationship: i) The role of power and control versus agency and choice, where reducing power imbalances and increasing service user agency over their treatment could help build relationships but restrictive practices, limited freedoms for service users and rigid rules and routines on wards could damage or destroy these relationships.

ii) Staff relational skills and approaches that improved the therapeutic relationship included listening, compassion, empathy, a non-judgemental approach and treating service users as individuals instead of only as 'patients'. Conversely, skills or approaches that weakened or damaged the relationship included staff ignoring service users and having stigmatising attitudes.

iii) Linked to this, staff availability, time to connect and continuity were important for relationship building. Regular contact over time with the same staff member and staff having protected time to connect with service users were key to developing a relationship along with repairing connections following a rupture. In contrast, relationships were weakened when the ward culture was task-and risk focused and staff spent more time in offices doing administrative tasks than engaging with service users.

iv) Ward environments and organisational cultures were important. Therapeutic relationships were strengthened when there were spaces on the ward that supported connection such as access to outdoor spaces, protected time to engage with service users and when wards felt safe, including predictable routines and rules and service users felt they had freedom to be open and express their feelings. Therapeutic relationships also benefitted from shared-decision making training for staff and service users and communication training and reflective practice for staff. However, relationships were weakened when wards had locked doors, inconsistent policies and task and risk-focused routines and practices.

Prior reviews

Our results are in line with and have expanded on the findings of two narrative reviews which both included a smaller number of papers (48 studies) than the current review and did not closely adhere to systematic review methodology [8, 9]. These reviews mainly focused on barriers, finding these to be from the individual to the organisational level including restrictive practices, lack of staff time and ward rules which limited engagement. Facilitators included good staff communication skills and meeting the individual needs of service users.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this review are the large number of included studies. We included almost 200 studies which were qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods with over 19,500 participants, making this the largest review to date to examine this topic. Given this and that our review involved lived experience researchers, our findings have clear implications and will be of use to a range of audiences including service users, academics, clinicians and policy makers.

Limitations to the review included, in the qualitative synthesis views were from the perspective of staff (n=2293) more than service users (n=1443). This could reflect the greater challenges of collecting data with service users (including more complex ethical processes).

This could also reflect power imbalances extending to research, with staff viewed as having more responsibility over the therapeutic relationship than patients. However, given the large numbers of participants in both groups, both perspectives were represented across a substantial number of papers. For the quantitative synthesis, the evidence base was substantially smaller than the qualitative research and heterogeneous, with most determinants examined in only one or two studies, using a range of alliance measures and largely cross-sectional, correlational designs. Findings can therefore not be interpreted as causal, only as the direction and consistency of associations. Following the guidance on how to best evaluate results of the MMAT [198] , no qualitative conclusion about the quality of papers can be drawn. Instead, we offer a descriptive breakdown of how many papers met a certain percentage of quality criteria. It should be noted that mixed methods studies cannot be scored higher than their lowest constituent component score; thus, overall scores for these papers will not account for the quality ratings of individual items on the MMAT questionnaire, which may demonstrate a higher quality criteria percentage. Furthermore, MMAT does not offer a distinct quality criteria concerning sample size and selection approaches for qualitative studies, meaning that there is nowhere to account for qualitative studies that provide little to no sample information.

Evidence gaps and future research

There is a gap in research for people from underserved groups. There were only 2 studies which explored issues for people from minoritised ethnic groups, and both were with staff [67, 156]. Given that some groups are over-represented in inpatient settings and may have poorer experiences in these settings, such as black ethnic groups, it is important that further research is conducted into experiences of therapeutic relationships on wards for people from minoritised ethnic groups from the individual staff and service user level to looking at organisational factors which could affect these relationships. Linked to this most studies did not report ethnicity or race for service users and staff. It is important for future research to report this, so that we can identify representativeness of the sample and whether there are any disparities in factors effecting the therapeutic relationship for certain groups of people. A further gap we identified was the limited number of studies with children; it would be important for further research to be conducted with child and adolescent samples.

Fewer studies researched facilitators and barriers at the organisational and societal levels and only one study looked at the role of time and change across levels (Chronosystem). This could reflect the increased complexity of research exploring organisations and contexts. This means there is a gap in the evidence to support the development of policy, service structures and support processes which facilitate therapeutic relationships. It risks placing all the responsibility, and blame with individual staff and service users, rather than considering the context and structures needed to enable relational practice. For example, increased expectations of productivity and documentation for staff may result in staff having limited time to engage with service users and build relationships. Future research could explore how organisations and structures can support staff to work in a way that facilitates therapeutic relationships.

Implications for practice

We identified the following key implications for practice:

Power imbalances

Power imbalances between staff and service users were a barrier to building therapeutic relationships. Staff relating to service users in more equal ways could be beneficial whilst also maintaining personal and professional boundaries. For example, through trying to have more informal conversations with service users, getting to know people not only as 'patients' but as equal, whole people with complex lives, and going for walks with service users outside wards (if possible). Service users could be encouraged to make advanced directives about their care which could help people to feel they have some agency even when they are at their most unwell. Measures designed to help staff and patients to get to know each other as people have been shown to be beneficial [199].

Coercion and restrictive practice

Coercive and restrictive practices could damage or destroy the therapeutic relationship.

Repairing the damaged relationship after the use of these practices is important, for example, guidance for repairing the relationship with service users following ruptures could be developed [200, 201]. Services could consider strategies to try to reduce the use of restrictive practices. For example, both SafeWards and Six Core Strategies provide interventions which aim to reduce conflict and restrictive practices on wards [199, 202].

Staff relational skills and effective staff-patient communication

Staff relational skills and effective staff-patient communication are foundational for successful therapeutic relationships. Potential approaches could include strategies to improve service user involvement in their treatment through shared decision making [203]. Understanding different cultures and cultural beliefs could also strengthen relationships and reduce potential for disengagement.

Ward structures and environments that encourage service user engagement

Ward structures and environments that encourage engagement could be materialised through having access to therapeutic activities and outdoor spaces on wards, protected time to engage with service users [204], and initiatives to reduce staff turnover so that staff are familiar to service users. It could be helpful for routines and rules to be consistent but with some flexibility and not entirely focused on risk management [205] . Changes in organisational cultures could promote relationship building, such as making this an explicit goal and part of staff's job description. Reflective practice and supervision could help staff to evaluate their relational engagement with service users.

Implications for policy

Policies can address organisational barriers that hinder Therapeutic Relationships, such as aiming to reduce the use of restrictive and coercive practices and direct resources towards interventions that foster Therapeutic Relationships, such as shared decision making [203].

Examples of such policies include NHS England's Culture of Care Standards (NHSE 2025), their 'Enhanced Therapeutic Observations and Care Programme' which aims to support NHS Trusts to improve their observation practices [206] and resources to reduce the use of restrictive practices on mental health inpatient wards [207].

National policies, investment priorities and research funding could include a focus on identifying evidence that aids staff recruitment, education, training and ongoing support, supervision and rewards that best facilitate the employment and retention of staff with the attributes and skills to develop and sustain therapeutic relationships in the unique and challenging environments of inpatient mental health wards.

Conclusion

We conducted the largest review to date on barriers and facilitators to effective therapeutic relationships on mental health inpatient wards, including almost 200 studies, with over 19,500 participants. We found four main factors that could help or hinder the relationship.

Imbalances in power, working towards more equal relationships, restrictive practices and staff relational skills were important barriers or facilitators to the relationship. Staff availability and consistency was also important for the relationship along with ward environments that supported connection. Our review identified significant evidence gaps, highlighting the need for further research, particularly for people from minoritised ethnic groups and into organisational level factors which could help or hinder the relationship. We suggest strategies that could help to improve relationships onwards, including use of advance directives, guidance for staff to repair relationships with service users after ruptures, such as use of restrictive practices, protected time for staff to engage with service users and relationship building becoming a more explicit part of job descriptions for staff.

Authors: Jude Beng and T.K.

As Black African commentators -one female and one male -currently living in England, our shared heritage and lived experiences naturally shapes the observations, thoughts, and reflections presented throughout this commentary.

This paper finds that effective, individualised staff-patient communication, and daily interaction serve as the vital foundation of successful therapeutic relationships. It actively strengthens clinical connections, multidisciplinary teamwork, community resilience, and overall patient outcomes.

We do not just experience mental health policy on paper; as inpatients, we actively live these structural frameworks every single second. At the individual and/or microsystem level, we see, hear, and feel everything on the ward. when our caring staff are untrained, unsupported, stressed, understaffed, or severely overworked, it compromises our environment and we directly feel unsafe [207].

When internal ward structures and team dynamics malfunction, at the mesosystem level, our personal recovery is significantly delayed. Because the physical ward environment directly impacts patient peace of mind, anxiety, and depression levels, we strongly recommend holistically involving patients in healthcare decisions according to their cognitive ability, thereby fostering genuine agency and empowerment [207].

Furthermore, at the macrosystem level, our inpatient experiences are heavily shaped by distinct cultural, ethnic, racial, and religious factors; Black and White patients often navigate care differently due to differing backgrounds, languages, and beliefs. As Black migrants, outside realities -including historical trauma, housing or socioeconomic anxieties, and systemic inequalities -stalks us into the hospital. Unfortunately, the health system routinely treats these wards as isolated islands, ignoring these critical external factors in treatment plans.

At the chronosystem level, inpatient mental health wards cannot Solely fix a broken society, but they must not replicate its systemic failures. True inclusive leadership and management require looking far beyond the ward doors. We implore policymakers to design actionable, funded policies that treat us as whole, complex individuals -physically, socially, and culturallyensuring admission serves as a brief, supportive intervention rather than a penalty for societal gaps.

Ultimately, we spotlight these critical gaps to radically improve therapeutic relationships and ensure the psychiatric ward becomes a safe space where inpatients can genuinely recover and heal.
