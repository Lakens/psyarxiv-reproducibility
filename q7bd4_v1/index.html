How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

College students encounter academic hurdles such as failing exams or missing goals. The Motivational Theory of Life-Span Development outlines goal engagement, goal adjustment, and self-protection control strategies for regulating behavior after setbacks. We examined profiles of intended control strategy use after midterms in difficult courses, and their predictors and outcomes. We identified four student profiles: Low, Medium, High, and Engaged Strategy. Lower midterm performance predicted membership in the High and Engaged Strategy profiles. Engaged Strategy students (high goal engagement, low goal adjustment and self-protection) outperformed High Strategy students and, in most quarters, Medium Strategy students in final course grades. High Strategy students (high engagement, goal adjustment, and self-protection) had the lowest final course grades. Both High and Engaged Strategy students reported more positive feelings than others, but High Strategy students also reported more negative feelings than Low Strategy students. Findings suggest that focusing on goal engagement after a setback is more adaptive than combining it with goal adjustment and self-protection, and that students need support in identifying adaptive strategies.

1 Introduction

College students face a multitude of academic challenges as they become primary agents of their own education. Compared with high school, college offers far greater freedom for students to structure their own learning activities and environments. Against this background, the ability to self-regulate goals and behaviors becomes particularly critical when students encounter difficulties in challenging courses. Successfully navigating such obstacles is essential not only for academic success, but also for students' psychological well-being (Adlaf et al., 2001; Hussain et al., 2013).

The Motivational Theory of Life-Span Development (MTD; Heckhausen et al., 2010 Heckhausen et al., , 2019;; Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995) provides a framework for understanding how individuals respond to such challenges. The theory outlines a range of control strategies that individuals use to engage with, adjust, or disengage from their goals when they face obstacles. When college students receive a disappointing midterm exam grade in a difficult course, for instance, they may persist through goal engagement strategies (e.g., increasing effort or seeking help), lower their aspirations through goal adjustment strategies, or protect their selfesteem through self-protection strategies (e.g., reminding themselves that others are struggling too).

In the present study, we examined how students combine these strategies, and whether some combinations of control strategies are better suited than others to help students succeed in challenging courses while maintaining their well-being. We applied latent profile analysis (LPA) to data from 1,338 undergraduate students in their most difficult course across three consecutive academic quarters. We identified profiles of students' intended control strategy use after midterm exams, examined how midterm performance and course-specific motivational beliefs predicted profile membership, and tested whether the profiles differed in final course grades and psychological well-being. MTD posits that individuals use a repertoire of control strategies to adapt to opportunities and constraints in their environment. Although the theory was originally developed in the context of broader developmental goals across adulthood (e.g., career and family goals), it has been extended to educational goals to understand how learners respond to challenges in learning environments (e.g., Hamm et al., 2019; von Keyserlingk et al., 2022).

In the context of a challenging college course, students can respond to setbacks with four types of strategies. Goal engagement strategies involve actively pursuing the goal, for example by increasing study time and effort, seeking help from peers or counselors when one's own skills or resources are insufficient, or sustaining one's motivation by anticipating success or avoiding distractions. Goal adjustment strategies involve revising the goal itself, such as lowering one's grade aspirations for the course to a more attainable level. Selfprotection strategies involve buffering the threat that setbacks pose to one's self-esteem and confidence, for example by attributing failure to external factors or by reminding oneself that others are struggling as well. Whereas goal adjustment changes the goal, self-protection leaves it intact and protects the motivational resources needed for further goal pursuit. Goal disengagement strategies involve abandoning the goal altogether (e.g., withdrawing from the course).

Central to the theory is the congruence principle: goal engagement is adaptive when goals remain attainable despite setbacks, whereas goal adjustment and disengagement become adaptive when goals are unattainable or excessively costly (Heckhausen et al., 2010 (Heckhausen et al., , 2019)).

Accordingly, no single strategy is adaptive by itself; its value depends on the situation and on the other strategies a student uses alongside it.

1.2 Why Do College Students Select Some Control Strategies Over Others?

According to MTD, control strategy use depends on two characteristics of a goal: its desirability and its attainability (Heckhausen et al., 2010). Individuals tend to engage with goals that are desirable and attainable and to adjust or disengage from goals that are not.

Situated Expectancy-Value Theory (SEVT; Eccles et al., 1983; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) offers a way to capture these goal characteristics in a college course. Expectancies for success, i.e., students' beliefs about how well they will perform in an upcoming task, reflect the perceived attainability of doing well in a course. Subjective task values, which comprise the attainment, intrinsic, and utility value as well as the perceived cost of a task, reflect the desirability of doing well in a course. These motivational beliefs are key drivers of students' engagement and performance: students with higher expectancies and values show greater course engagement (e.g., Trautwein et al., 2012; Wolters et al., 2013; Wu & Fan, 2017) and better course performance (e.g., Fan & Dempsey, 2017; Wang & Liou, 2018; Wu et al., 2020).

Von Keyserlingk et al. (2022) examined how these beliefs and students' midterm exam experiences relate to their intended control strategy use after a midterm exam in a difficult college course. Students with higher subjective task values intended to use more goal engagement, goal adjustment, and self-protection strategies, which aligns with the MTD's assumption that individuals commit to desired and valued goals. The authors interpreted the higher use of goal adjustment among these students as adaptive recalibration of overambitious goals. Students with higher ability beliefs intended to use fewer goal adjustment strategies, which aligns with the MTD's assumption that individuals keep their goals when they are generally attainable. Students who perceived their midterm exam as more difficult than expected intended to invest more effort and to adjust their grade aspirations. These associations held mainly for students with low or average subjective task values, whereas students with high values planned high levels of effort and adjustment regardless of their exam experience. These findings suggest that students do not rely exclusively on either goal engagement or adjustment, particularly after a setback, but may combine them in different ways.

Whereas expectancies reflect perceived attainability, performance feedback provides objective information about it and may also shape students' control strategy use. Hall (2008) found that poorer test grades predicted a shift in students' relative emphasis toward positively reinterpreting their negative experiences, a strategy conceptually related to self-protection.

Taken together, prior research suggests that both motivational beliefs and performance feedback are associated with students' control strategy use. However, this evidence is limited in two ways. First, objective performance feedback has not been examined alongside motivational beliefs. Von Keyserlingk et al. (2022) assessed exam difficulty relative to students' prior expectations, which is not equivalent to poor performance: a student with low expectations who performs poorly may still rate the exam "as expected." Hall (2008) examined grades but not motivational beliefs as predictors of control strategy use. Second, both studies examined strategies separately and thus cannot show which combinations of strategies students adopt. Examining how midterm performance and motivational beliefs predict profiles of control strategy use can address these gaps.

1.3 How Do College Students Benefit From Different Control Strategies?

Von Keyserlingk et al.'s (2022) findings suggest that students may combine control strategies in different ways, but whether some combinations are more adaptive than others remains unclear. According to the congruence principle, the adaptiveness of a strategy depends on the situation and on the other strategies used alongside it (Heckhausen et al., 2010). Von Keyserlingk et al. (2022) therefore called for research linking control strategies to subsequent course outcomes. In the following, we review evidence on how control strategies relate to academic performance and psychological well-being.

Goal engagement strategies generally predict academic success. Students who planned to invest more time and effort in their studies obtained higher course grades, beyond their prior achievement (Hamm et al., 2013). Goal engagement strategies that sustain motivation, such as reminding oneself why one's education matters, anticipating success, or avoiding distractions, did not directly predict grades but predicted greater effort investment months later, which in turn predicted higher grades (Hamm et al., 2013). These motivation-sustaining strategies also appear to protect well-being: among students with low high school grades, they predicted fewer depressive symptoms, indirectly through greater effort investment and more favorable emotions about their course performance (Hamm et al., 2015). An online intervention that taught these motivation-sustaining strategies to students with both low high school grades and low optimism improved their performance by a full letter grade on three subsequent class tests and increased their odds of course completion (Hamm et al., 2019).

Person-centered research further suggests that investing time and effort may be particularly important. Han et al. (2018) identified profiles of first-year students based on three learning strategies: time management, self-regulated learning, and goal engagement (i.e., investing time and effort in one's academic goals). Students high in goal engagement but only moderate in the other two strategies earned writing course grades and credits comparable to those of students high in all three strategies, although their first-semester GPA was lower.

Evidence on self-protection strategies is more mixed. In one study, positively reinterpreting academic experiences, a strategy conceptually related to self-protection, negatively predicted final course grades and GPA after accounting for prior achievement and other motivational factors (Daniels et al., 2014). In another study, the benefits of selfprotection strategies depended on students' initial performance (Hall, Perry, et al., 2006).

Among students who had performed poorly on their first course test, those who reported more positive reinterpretation and comforting social comparisons felt happier and prouder about their course performance. Those who combined these strategies with a strong sense of control also achieved higher cumulative GPAs, withdrew from fewer courses, and reported less general stress and regret than those who relied on a sense of control alone. Among students who performed well, these self-protection strategies showed no such benefits. Positive reinterpretation was also associated with lower perceived stress among female but not male students (Hall, Chipperfield, et al., 2006).

To our knowledge, evidence on goal adjustment and academic performance or wellbeing in college remains scarce. Taken together, prior research suggests that the benefits of control strategies depend on students' initial standing and on how strategies are combined, with self-protection appearing most beneficial for struggling students with a strong sense of control. However, these studies examined at most two strategies at a time and did not include goal adjustment. Therefore, it is unclear whether self-protection is similarly beneficial when combined with goal engagement or goal adjustment, and more generally, whether profiles of goal engagement, goal adjustment, and self-protection strategies differ in their associations with achievement and psychological well-being.

2 The Current Study

According to MTD, control strategies are a central process through which individuals regulate their motivation and behavior during goal pursuit (Heckhausen et al., 2010). Prior research has linked control strategies to college students' academic performance and psychological well-being, but the findings are mixed. The benefits of a given strategy appear to depend on students' initial performance and on the other strategies they use (Hall, Perry, et al., 2006). Students also seem to use several strategies simultaneously, for example investing more effort while adjusting their grade aspirations after a setback (von Keyserlingk et al., 2022). However, prior studies examined strategies separately or in pairs, using variablecentered analyses that cannot show which strategies students combine.

With the present study, we aim to improve our understanding of adaptive combinations of control strategy use in relevant and naturalistic contexts. We used longitudinal data from 1,338 undergraduate students across three academic quarters. Students reported their intended control strategy use after receiving their midterm grades in the course they considered most difficult. Because very few students withdrew from their most difficult course, we focused on goal engagement, goal adjustment, and self-protection and did not examine goal disengagement. We applied latent profile analysis (LPA), a person-centered approach that identifies subgroups of students with similar patterns of use (Spurk et al., 2020), to address three research questions.

RQ1: What profiles of intended control strategy use can be identified among undergraduate students?

Given the exploratory nature of LPA, we did not formulate hypotheses about the number or characteristics of the profiles (for similar approaches, see Han et al., 2018; Hong et al., 2020; Linnenbrink-Garcia et al., 2018). Instead, our goal was to identify common profiles of control strategy combinations in challenging college courses that could, for example, be characterized by a strong focus on goal engagement, a strong focus on goal adjustment, or combinations of all types of strategies.

RQ2: Do course-specific motivational beliefs and midterm exam grades predict profile membership?

Based on the theoretical assumptions of MTD (Heckhausen et al., 2010) and empirical findings from von Keyserlingk et al. (2022), we expected students with higher ability beliefs to be less likely to belong to profiles high in goal adjustment, and students with higher subjective task values to be more likely to belong to profiles with higher overall strategy endorsement. Setbacks have been associated with intentions both to invest more effort and to adjust one's goals (von Keyserlingk et al., 2022), as well as with greater reliance on positive reinterpretation (Hall, 2008). We therefore expected students with lower midterm grades to be more likely to belong to profiles that combine goal engagement with goal adjustment or selfprotection. Because gender, first-generation status, ethnic-minority status, and high school GPA have been linked to students' strategy use and academic success (Han et al., 2018; Jury et al., 2017; Richardson et al., 2012), we included them as control variables.

RQ3: Do students in the identified profiles differ in final course grades and psychological well-being?

Because goal engagement strategies predict better course performance (Hamm et al., 2013; Han et al., 2018), we expected students in profiles high in goal engagement to attain higher final course grades. Based on the mixed nature of prior empirical findings, we did not formulate specific hypotheses for psychological well-being. Goal engagement strategies have been linked to fewer depressive symptoms among students with low high school grades (Hamm et al., 2015). Self-protection strategies combined with a strong sense of control have been linked to lower stress among students who performed poorly (Hall, Perry, et al., 2006).

However, how combinations of goal engagement, goal adjustment, and self-protection relate to well-being has not been examined.

3.3.1 Control Strategy Items

Intended Control Strategy Use. Ten items assessed how students planned to use control strategies to improve their course performance (Table 1). All items started with the prompt "Thinking about the next exam in your difficult course [course name], how likely is it that you will…?" Six items assessed goal engagement strategies (e.g., "increase your effort and time invested in this course," "get help from classmates or friends," "try to stay away from anything that could distract you from your course work"), two items assessed goal adjustment strategies (e.g., "adjust your grade aspiration for this course"), and two items assessed self-protection strategies (e.g., "remind yourself that other students are struggling too in this course"). Students responded on a 7-point scale from 1 (not at all likely) to 7 (extremely likely). The research team developed the items based on the MTD literature (Heckhausen et al., 2010 (Heckhausen et al., , 2019;; Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995). Because individual items were used as profile indicators, scale reliability was not calculated.

3.3.2 Course-Specific Predictors

Midterm Exam Grades.

Students' self-reported midterm exam grades were converted to a numerical scale from 0 (F) to 12 (A+).

Course-Specific Ability Beliefs.

Three items were used to assess how students perceived their performance in their most difficult course, as an operationalization of expectancies for success. One example item is "Over the last few weeks, how good have you been in learning new things in your most difficult course [course name]?" Students responded to these items on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all good) to 7 (extremely good). These items had a high internal consistency (fall: α = .91; winter: α = .92; spring: α = .92).

Subjective Task Values. Six items in total were used to assess students' subjective task values in their most difficult course, with two items each devoted to utility, intrinsic, and attainment value of the course. All items started with the prompt "Based on your experiences in this course, how much is your most difficult course [course name]…?" Example items are "interesting to you" (intrinsic value), "useful in terms of your long-term goals" (utility value), and "important to your academic identity" (attainment value). Students responded to these items on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely). The research team developed the ability belief and subjective task value items based on the SEVT literature (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). The research team adjusted the items to reflect students' motivational beliefs in specific college courses regardless of course domain and adopted construct-specific anchors instead of agree-disagree statements to circumvent response biases (Gehlbach, 2015; Saris et al., 2010). The items had a high internal consistency (fall: α = .91; winter: α = .91; spring: α = .91). The control strategy and motivational belief items have been used in previous studies with data from the same project (Gaspard et al., 2025; Rubach et al., 2022; von Keyserlingk et al., 2022).

3.3.3 Control Variables

Gender (1 = male,0 = female; students who did not report a binary gender, ≤ 1.5% per quarter, were treated as missing because the group was too small for separate analysis), underrepresented minority (URM) status (1 = Black, Hispanic, Pacific Islander, or American Indian/Alaska Native;0 = other), first-generation college student status (1 = neither parent holds a college-equivalent degree;0 = continuing-generation), and high school GPA (weighted 5.0 scale) were obtained from administrative records.

3.3.4 Academic and Psychological Outcomes

Final Course Grades. Students' official final course grades were converted to a numerical scale from 0 (F) to 12 (A+).

Positive and Negative Feelings (PF & NF).

Sixteen items assessed students' general psychological well-being during the quarter: positive feelings (nine items) and negative feelings (seven items). All items started with the prompt "How often in the last term have you felt…?" Two example items for positive feelings are "happy with your academic activities" and "well supported emotionally," and two example items for negative feelings are "anxious about your academic performance" and "worried about making friends." Students responded to these items on a slider scale from 0 (never) to 100 (all of the time). The research team developed the items. Both the positive scale (fall: α = .89; winter: α = .89; spring: α = .89) and the negative scale had high internal consistencies (fall: α = .81; winter: α = .80; spring: α = .82).

3.4 Data Analysis

We prepared the data in IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., 2022) and conducted LPA in Mplus 8.6 (Muthén & Muthén, 2021). Because the specification of the variance-covariance matrix can affect the number of profiles identified, we tested models with two to five profiles under all six possible variance-covariance structures in each quarter (Spurk et al., 2020). Only the model with equal variances across profiles and no within-profile covariances, which corresponds to the Mplus default, converged in all quarters; we therefore used this specification in all subsequent analyses.

Each model was estimated with 500 initial-stage random starts, 50 initial-stage iterations, and 50 final-stage optimizations and rerun with twice the random starts to replicate the best log-likelihood. We selected the final model based on statistical indicators (Collins & Lanza, 2010) and conceptual interpretability (Sterba & Bauer, 2010), considering information criteria (AIC, BIC, SABIC), likelihood ratio tests (VLMRT, aVLMRT, BLRT; Geiser, 2013; Morin & Wang, 2016), entropy, average classification probabilities, and profile sizes (each > 5% of the sample; Merz & Roesch, 2011). We ran LPAs separately for the fall, winter, and spring quarters to examine whether common combinations of control strategy use replicated in the three subsequent courses of the first college year.

To address RQ2, we related the predictors and control variables to profile membership using the bias-corrected three-step approach (R3STEP) in Mplus (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014), a standard method for covariate analysis in mixture models (Bakk & Kuha, 2021). To address RQ3, we compared outcome means across profiles using the Bolck-Croon-Hagenaars (BCH) method, which performs best with continuous distal outcomes (Bakk & Vermunt, 2016). Both approaches account for uncertainty in profile assignment.

3.4.1 Handling Missing Data

Because profiles were estimated separately for each quarter, each quarter's analytic sample included all students who completed the control strategy survey in that quarter. Of the students in the fall sample, 27% ( 2020-21 cohort), 39% (2021-22), and 31% (2022-23)also participated in both winter and spring. Missing a later quarter (winter or spring) was largely unrelated to demographic characteristics, midterm grades, motivational beliefs, control strategy items, and outcomes: only 5 of 60 correlations were significant, and all were small (|r| ≤ .20). Within each quarter's analytic sample, missingness on individual variables was low for control strategy items (1%-2%), motivational beliefs (0%-1%), demographic variables (1%-6%), and midterm grades (2%-5%), and higher for high school GPA (13%-15%), final course grades (8%-12%), and positive and negative feelings (12%-43%).

Missingness in positive feelings and negative feelings was highest in spring (43% vs. 18% in fall and 12% in winter), possibly because students were less motivated to respond to surveys at the beginning of the summer break. Missingness indicators were largely unrelated to the control strategy items (Online Resource 1, Tables S1-S3); only missingness in positive feelings and negative feelings correlated weakly with getting counselor help in winter and spring. Therefore, we assumed data were missing at random (MAR; Enders, 2010) and used full information maximum likelihood (FIML; Enders, 2010) for the outcome analysis and multiple imputation (Royston, 2005) for the covariate analysis.

In this study, we examined profiles of college students' intended control strategy use after a midterm exam in their most difficult course, their predictors, and their associations with final course grades and psychological well-being. We identified four profiles: High, Medium, Low, and Engaged Strategy. Students with lower midterm grades and higher subjective task values were more likely to be in the High or Engaged Strategy profile, i.e., to intend to use all strategies or goal engagement strategies at high levels. Despite lower midterm grades, students in the Engaged Strategy profile attained higher final course grades than students in the High Strategy profile and, in two of three quarters, the Medium Strategy profile, whereas students in the High Strategy profile obtained the lowest grades and reported both more positive and more negative feelings.

5.1 Profiles of Intended Control Strategy Use After Midterm

The Engaged Strategy profile aligns with the theoretical proposition that goal engagement strategies tend to function together, such that individuals mobilize several means to reach a goal, for example by increasing their effort and avoiding distractions (Heckhausen et al., 2010). The High Strategy profile, in contrast, combined high goal engagement with goal adjustment and self-protection. This pattern is consistent with the finding that students intend to invest more effort and adjust their aspirations at the same time after a setback (von Keyserlingk et al., 2022). In our sample, no profile combined goal adjustment or selfprotection with low goal engagement. A possible explanation is that students at the studied competitive research university may be reluctant to lower their aspirations without also working harder.

Notably, students in all profiles were less likely to endorse seeking help from counselors or academic resource centers and attributing their struggles externally than other strategies. In a high-achieving environment, acknowledging difficulties and seeking help may be stigmatized, and students may feel pressured to solve problems on their own. Indeed, students are more likely to seek help in supportive, mastery-oriented than in performanceoriented learning environments (Karabenick & Gonida, 2017).

5.2 Predictors Associated With Profile Membership

Students with higher midterm grades were more likely to be in the Low or Medium Strategy profile, suggesting they saw little need to change their approach (i.e., to increase effort or adjust goals) because they were already performing well. Students with lower midterm grades were more likely to be in the High or Engaged Strategy profile and thus intended to use all strategies, or goal engagement strategies specifically, at high levels. This aligns with MTD that individuals use control strategies to mobilize effort or adjust their goals in the face of obstacles and setbacks (Heckhausen et al., 2010). It also echoes the finding that students who perceived the midterm exam as more difficult than expected intended to invest more effort and adjust their aspirations (von Keyserlingk et al., 2022) and extends it by showing that students' intended strategy use responds to objective performance feedback.

Students with higher subjective task values were more likely to be in the High and Engaged Strategy profiles, and students with lower values were more likely to be in the Low Strategy profile. This is consistent with SEVT's proposition that students who find a course interesting, useful, or important invest more in it (Eccles et al., 1983; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020): students who valued the course more intended to increase their effort after a setback and stay committed to the course. The fact that higher values also predicted membership in the High rather than the Engaged Strategy profile in two quarters is consistent with the finding that students who value a course also report more goal adjustment, which may reflect the recalibration of overly ambitious goals (von Keyserlingk et al., 2022).

Contrary to our hypothesis, ability beliefs were largely unrelated to profile membership; the only effect, in fall, indicated that students with higher ability beliefs were more likely to be in the High than in the Low or Medium Strategy profile. In the present study, ability beliefs were assessed as students' perceived performance over the past few weeks rather than their expectations for future exams. Given their substantial correlation with midterm grades (rs = .58 to .62), midterm grades, a more objective indicator of performance, may have overshadowed ability beliefs as a predictor of intended control strategy use.

Among the control variables, female and underrepresented minority students were somewhat more likely to be in the High Strategy profile, but these effects were inconsistent across quarters and should be interpreted with caution.

5.3 Outcomes Associated With Profile Membership

The Engaged Strategy profile was associated with higher final course grades than the High Strategy profile in all quarters and than the Medium Strategy profile in two quarters, consistent with findings that goal engagement strategies predict academic success (Hamm et al., 2013; Han et al., 2018). Students in both the Engaged and High Strategy profiles reported more positive feelings than students in the Low and, in most quarters, the Medium Strategy profile, but students in the High Strategy profile also reported more negative feelings than those in the Low Strategy profile. The Engaged Strategy profile thus appears to be the most adaptive: despite lower midterm grades than students in the Low and Medium Strategy profiles in most quarters, these students attained the most favorable academic and psychological outcomes overall at the end of the term. This is consistent with MTD-based research showing that goal engagement strategies particularly benefit students who face academic barriers (Hamm et al., 2015 (Hamm et al., , 2019)).

Students in the Low and Medium Strategy profiles, who had performed better on the midterm, did not outperform students in the Engaged Strategy profile in final course grades.

Although these students had rated the course as their most difficult, they may have been performing as well as or better than they expected. Because students need to distribute limited time and resources across all their courses, maintaining their current approach in the difficult course may reflect efficient distribution of their resources.

5.4 Theoretical Implications

Our findings support and extend MTD. According to the congruence principle, control strategies are adaptive when they match the opportunities and constraints of a situation (Heckhausen et al., 2010 (Heckhausen et al., , 2019)). Goal engagement is adaptive when goals remain attainable despite setbacks. Although we did not measure goals or their attainability directly, the favorable outcomes of the Engaged Strategy profile suggest that these students perceived their goals as still attainable and chose congruent strategies. Students in this profile may have had the potential to succeed but intensified their effort only after receiving a relatively low midterm grade.

The High Strategy profile is more difficult to interpret. Combining strategies is not maladaptive per se: goal adjustment can reflect the recalibration of overambitious goals (von Keyserlingk et al., 2022), and self-protection can sustain motivation for continued striving, particularly among struggling students (Hall, Perry, et al., 2006). Yet students who combined high goal engagement with goal adjustment and self-protection obtained the lowest final course grades and reported more negative feelings, despite also reporting more positive feelings. One explanation is that these students were uncertain whether their goals were still attainable. Intending to work harder while lowering one's aspirations and normalizing one's struggles may reflect this uncertainty, and dividing limited resources across strategies may be less effective than investing them in goal engagement. Moreover, although students in this profile endorsed help-seeking more than students in other profiles, it was among their least endorsed strategies. These students might have benefited most from support in selecting effective learning strategies, rather than simply investing more time and effort, which can lead to exhaustion without the desired results. Finally, because students in the High Strategy profile also had the lowest midterm grades, their lower final grades may reflect their ongoing academic struggles.

5.5 Practical Implications

Our findings have three implications for educational practice. First, it is important to identify struggling students and provide targeted learning support. About one third of the students in our sample belonged to the High Strategy profile (24%-42% per quarter), which was associated with the lowest final course grades. These students may be highly motivated but uncertain about how to respond effectively to academic challenges, illustrating that more effort does not always translate into better outcomes. Studies using digital trace data similarly show that many students struggle with self-regulated learning and exhibit engagement patterns associated with low performance (Nguyen et al., 2020; Rodriguez et al., 2021; von Keyserlingk et al., 2025). Interventions such as the digital "Science of Learning to Learn" training and early warning systems can help identify these students and improve their learning skills and grades (Bernacki, Chavez, et al., 2020; Bernacki, Vosicka, et al., 2020).

Second, institutions should promote campus learning resources and encourage helpseeking. Students in our sample rarely intended to seek help from academic counselors or the learning assistance center, which may be common in competitive college environments.

Because adaptive help-seeking supports academic persistence and success, instructors and administrators should create contexts that encourage help-seeking (Johnson et al., 2022; Karabenick & Gonida, 2017) and examine potential barriers, such as stigma or a lack of awareness of available resources.

Finally, support should help students evaluate whether their goals remain attainable and select strategies accordingly. No profile in our sample combined goal adjustment with low goal engagement, suggesting that students in high-achieving environments rarely consider stepping back from their grade goals, possibly because of institutional or cultural pressures. Students in the High Strategy profile intended to engage and adjust simultaneously, a combination associated with poorer outcomes. Interventions should help students reassess goal attainability, select strategies that match their circumstances, and, when necessary, pivot to more attainable goals, which has been linked to higher well-being (Wrosch et al., 2003), rather than simply encouraging all students to work harder.

6 Limitations and Future Directions

One strength of our study is that we used longitudinal data from three student cohorts to investigate profiles, profile predictors, and profile outcomes in up to three difficult courses per student. We replicated these profiles across three consecutive quarters, demonstrating their robustness.

However, our study has some limitations. First, our sample consisted of students from a competitive research university, which may explain why no profile combined goal adjustment with low goal engagement. Future research should examine whether different institutional contexts, such as community colleges or less selective institutions, produce different profile structures.

Second, we measured students' intentions or likelihood of strategy use, rather than implementation, which may not fully capture students' regulatory behavior. Future studies could employ experience sampling methods or behavioral trace data to assess strategy use in real time. Moreover, latent transition analysis could reveal whether students shift between profiles as they encounter new setbacks or consistently adopt particular regulatory patterns. In our sample, too few students participated in all three quarters to conduct such an analysis. Third, we used the final course grade as the sole academic outcome, without accounting for students' grade expectations, which are key to understanding how students adjust their goals. Future studies should assess grade expectations alongside performance metrics to provide a more complete picture of profile adaptivity.

Fourth, our psychological well-being survey was a generic, retrospective measure of positive and negative feelings across the quarter. Although experiences in a very difficult course likely affect overall well-being during a quarter, they do not represent students' entire college experience. Future studies should therefore assess course-specific emotions at multiple time points to examine how they change in response to performance feedback and control strategy use.

7 Conclusion

We investigated college students' profiles of intended control strategy use after midterm exams in their most difficult course, as well as the predictors and outcomes of these profiles. Of the four profiles identified, the Engaged Strategy profile appeared to be the most adaptive response to a setback, as it was associated with higher final course grades and more positive feelings, despite relatively low midterm exam performance. Combining high goal engagement with goal adjustment and self-protection, in contrast, was associated with the lowest grades and more negative feelings. These findings are consistent with MTD's proposition that adaptive self-regulation requires strategies that are congruent with the situation. Educators and advisors should help students assess whether their goals remain attainable, point them to support structures that can improve their learning behaviors and strategies, and pay particular attention to highly motivated students who struggle to choose effective strategies. Further research should examine these profiles, their predictors, and their adaptivity across institutional contexts and populations.
