Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

We investigated whether mice can discriminate static dot arrays differing in relative numerosity and whether stimulus magnitude influences side choice. In Experiment 1, mice were trained in a touchscreen-equipped operant-conditioning chamber to select the larger of two dot arrays (8 vs. 4) and then completed reinforced test blocks that introduced untrained numerosity pairs and stimulus manipulations of continuous physical variables. Mice showed evidence of transfer to novel comparisons, and accuracy varied systematically with the numerical ratio, consistent with Weber-like scaling. Accuracy remained above chance with open-circle displays, but performance declined when cumulative surface area was equated and when discrete item structure was disrupted by phase scrambling. These findings indicate that mice can learn and generalize relative numerosity discriminations, although their performance was also influenced by correlated continuous visual properties. Thus, the results do not establish stimulus control by numerosity independently of these properties. In Experiment 2, we examined whether stimulus magnitude influenced side choice under noncontingent probe conditions, in which identical dot arrays were presented simultaneously on the left and right sides of the screen. Under these conditions, the probability of choosing the left side increased with numerosity at the group level. Together, these results demonstrate that mice can learn and generalize discriminations between static dot arrays and provide limited evidence of a magnitude-related bias in side choice.

The ability to discriminate magnitudes, including discrete numerosities, allows animals to make adaptive decisions in contexts such as foraging, predator avoidance, mate choice, and social interaction (Macchinizzi et al., 2025). In many such situations, individuals compare the number of items or agents present using visual information available at a single moment. Sensitivity to numerosity and related magnitude dimensions has been documented across a wide range of taxa, including birds, primates, fish, amphibians, and invertebrates (Feigenson et al., 2004; Agrillo & Bisazza, 2014), suggesting that magnitude discrimination is a broadly conserved cognitive capacity. Recent reviews of avian numerical cognition further emphasize the value of comparative approaches for understanding the evolution and mechanisms of numerical abilities (Regolin et al., 2025). Despite their central role in behavioral neuroscience, laboratory mice (Mus musculus) remain underrepresented in studies examining numerosity judgments based on purely visual, static displays.

Existing evidence indicates that rodents can process numerosity across a variety of experimental paradigms, but much of the mouse literature has relied on sequential events or response-based counting rather than on simultaneous visual arrays (Meck & Church, 1983; Berkay et al., 2016; Çavdaroğlu & Balcı, 2016; Gür et al., 2021; Light et al., 2019). In sequential paradigms, numerosity is inferred from the accumulation of actions or events over time, which limits direct comparison with studies of visual numerosity discrimination using static displays. By contrast, studies in rats have demonstrated ratio-sensitive performance in twochoice discrimination tasks using simultaneous visual stimuli (Cox & Montrose, 2016), and striped field mice (Apodemus agrarius) can discriminate relative numerosity from dot arrays presented at spatially distinct locations within an arena (Reznikova et al., 2019). It remains unclear how readily laboratory mice acquire relative numerosity discriminations from arrays with manipulations of continuous physical properties and whether their performance shows Weber-type 2 GOTO & KIMURA ratio dependence.

Establishing reliable visual numerosity discrimination is a prerequisite for addressing broader questions about how numerical magnitude is represented and used by animals. Beyond discrimination itself, a major open question is whether numerical magnitude systematically influences spatial responding, as predicted by accounts that posit a mental number line. In humans, the Spatial-Numerical Association of Response Codes (SNARC) effect describes systematic links between numerical magnitude and spatial responses (Dehaene et al., 1993; Toomarian & Hubbard, 2018). Evidence for analogous magnitude-space associations in nonhuman animals is mixed and appears to depend on species and task structure (Rugani & Rosa-Salva, 2021). Robust group-level spatial-numerical biases have been reported in chicks and honeybees under some procedures (Rugani et al., 2015 (Rugani et al., , 2020;; Giurfa et al., 2022), whereas other studies have found individual-level associations that vary in direction across subjects, as observed in pigeons and other birds (Lazareva et al., 2020). Evidence from primates and fish has also been mixed, with some studies reporting no reliable directional biases (Triki & Bshary, 2018; Beran et al., 2019), whereas others have demonstrated magnitude-related spatial associations in rhesus macaques (Rugani et al., 2024). Interpretation of magnitude-space effects in nonhuman animals therefore remains debated, with concerns about task dependence and about inferring a unitary mental number line from spatial response biases alone (Fragaszy, 2019; Eccher et al., 2026). Crucially, any test of spatial-numerical associations presupposes that animals extract numerical magnitude from the visual stimuli themselves; this requirement has not been sufficiently validated in laboratory mice using static arrays, where performance may instead be supported by nonnumerical visual properties that covary with numerosity, such as cumulative surface area, density, element size, and spatial configuration.

A prominent candidate mechanism for visual numerosity judgments is the approximate number system, which supports analog magnitude representations and is behaviorally characterized by Weber's law. Under Weber scaling, discriminability depends on numerical ratio rather than absolute difference, so comparisons with coarser ratios are easier than those with finer ratios (Piazza et al., 2004; Nieder & Dehaene, 2009). Demonstrating ratio dependence therefore provides a principled benchmark for assessing whether animals extract numerical magnitude from visual arrays and for validating stimulus conditions before testing spatialnumerical associations. Static dot arrays, however, intrinsically covary numerosity with continuous visual dimensions such as total area, density, and spatial configuration. A convincing assessment in rodents therefore requires testing untrained numerosity pairs, analyzing Weber-like scaling, and applying stimulus manipulations designed to reduce or disrupt nonnumerical cues.

In the present study, numerosity refers to the number of discrete items in a display, quantity is used more broadly to refer to discrete and continuous stimulus dimensions together, and magnitude is reserved for broader representational or spatial-mapping interpretations. In static dot-array tasks, numerosity covaries with continuous visual dimensions such as total area and spatial configuration. Accordingly, we first examined whether mice could discriminate relative numerosity in static dot arrays and whether performance showed ratio dependence while remaining sensitive to correlated nonnumerical visual cues. We then examined whether stimulus magnitude was associated with side choice under noncontingent probe conditions.

General Methods Subjects

Six male C57BL/6N mice (Japan SLC, Japan) were used. The animals arrived at 8 weeks of age and were group housed in a standard plastic cage (182 × 260 × 128 mm) with paper bedding (Alpha-Dri, Shepherd Specialty Papers) under a 12:12-hr light-dark cycle (lights on from 06:00 to 18:00). Behavioral testing began after a 2-week acclimation period. Mice were maintained on a food-restricted regimen throughout the study. Mean body weight decreased from 23.9 g (range = 22.0-25.0 g) to 21.6 g (range = 19.5-23.0 g) following food restriction and was subsequently maintained by providing daily food supplements (typically 1.75 g per mouse) in addition to the food reinforcers earned during task sessions. Water was available ad libitum in the home cages. All testing was conducted during the light phase.

The sample size was comparable to sample sizes used in previous touchscreen studies of visual discrimination in mice. No a priori power analysis was conducted.

Apparatus

Testing was conducted in an operant conditioning chamber (Med Associates, ENV-307W) housed within a soundattenuating enclosure (ENV-022V). The chamber measured 21.6 × 18.0 × 17.8 cm (length × width × height) and had a metal frame, transparent Perspex walls, and a stainlesssteel grid floor. A pellet dispenser (Bio-Serv F0071-J50) located outside the chamber delivered 20-mg food pellets to a centrally positioned magazine on the rear wall. The magazine was equipped with a light-emitting diode and a photocell detector (ENV-303HDW) to monitor head entries. A houselight (1.0 A) and a tone generator (ENV-324W) were mounted on the rear panel. The front wall was fitted with a 10.4-in. touchscreen monitor (1,024 × 768 pixels; K-Limbic Systems) with an infrared touch frame. A black acrylic mask covered the monitor, leaving two square response windows (7.5 × 7.5 cm) visible, each positioned 2 cm above the floor. A horizontal bar fence below the screen restricted access to these response areas. All components, including the lights, magazine, tone generator, pellet dispenser, and detec-tors, were controlled via a digital input/output board (Velleman K8055) connected to a Lenovo ThinkPad T440p running custom software written in Visual Basic 6 (Microsoft).

Stimuli

Representative stimuli for each condition are shown in Figure 1. Stimuli were presented within two square response windows on the touchscreen. Each stimulus consisted of a white-on-black dot array. The elements were white circles (4.3 mm in diameter) displayed against a black background. Mice viewed the screen from approximately 2.5-10 cm during choice responses (Figure 2). At these distances, each 4.3-mm dot subtended approximately 9.8° to 2.5° of visual angle. Behavioral measurements indicate that C57BL/6 mice have a spatial visual acuity of approximately 0.5 cycles/degree (Prusky et al., 2000), suggesting that the highcontrast dots used here were sufficiently large to be visually resolved even at the longest estimated viewing distance. Because viewing distance was not measured directly, these values should be regarded as approximate estimates. Dot centers were constrained to an invisible 4 × 4 grid, yielding 16 possible locations. For each numerosity, four arrangements were pseudorandomly generated in advance by assigning dots to nonoverlapping positions on the 4 × 4 grid. These fixed arrangements were combined into four predetermined 8-versus-4 stimulus pairs and used throughout training. Each stimulus pair was presented in both left-right assignments across the two response windows, with the rewarded numerosity appearing equally often in the left and right windows.

During the test phases, untrained numerosity pairs and stimulus manipulations were introduced in reinforced test blocks to assess performance beyond the trained 8 versus 4 comparison. In one manipulation, the arrays were rendered as open-circle displays to alter element appearance while preserving numerosity. In another, cumulative surface area was equated between the two arrays on each trial by scaling element size, thereby reducing total area as a correlated cue. In a third manipulation, phase-scrambled versions of the dot arrays were generated by randomizing the phase spectrum while preserving the amplitude spectrum, thus preserving global luminance and spatial-frequency content while disrupting discrete item structure.

Experiment 1: Discrimination of relative numerosity

This experiment examined whether mice could learn to select the larger of two dot arrays and generalize this discrimination across novel numerosity pairs and visual manipulations, and whether their performance followed Weber's law.

Methods

Mice were first habituated to the testing chamber and trained to retrieve 20-mg food pellets from the central mag-azine (magazine training). They were then shaped to make a nose-touch response to a white circle (2.0 cm in diameter) presented on the touchscreen. Once this response was performed reliably, the stimulus location was randomized across trials. A self-start procedure was then introduced. Each trial began with a blinking magazine light (250-ms interval), and a head entry into the magazine triggered the simultaneous presentation of two stimuli, one in each response window. These pretraining steps, including magazine training, autoshaping, shaping of screen-directed responses, and the self-start procedure, followed the methods described in detail by Goto and Hataji (2020) and Goto and Watanabe (2021).

After completion of pretraining, mice began the 8 versus 4 discrimination training. This choice was motivated in part by previous touchscreen-based quantity-discrimination work showing faster acquisition of pick-large than pick-small contingencies and an initial bias toward larger quantities in birds (Lazareva et al., 2020). The 8-versus-4 comparison was selected because its .50 numerical ratio was expected to provide a readily discriminable starting condition for establishing the touchscreen procedure and because it allowed subsequent transfer tests to include numerosities both below and above the trained values. The comparison also spans the conventional boundary between small and large numerosities discussed in accounts of non-symbolic numerical cognition, although the present study was not designed to distinguish the object-tracking and approximate-number systems (Feigenson et al., 2004). A schematic illustration of the sequence of events within a trial is provided in Figure 2. Each trial was initiated by a head entry into the central magazine, which triggered the simultaneous presentation of two dot arrays, one in each response window. Responses were registered when a mouse touched either response window. Correct choices, defined as selection of the array with the larger numerosity, were reinforced with a food pellet, a 1-s chime, and 5-s illumination of the magazine. Incorrect choices resulted in a 5-s blackout and no reinforcement. Following an incorrect response, the same numerosity pair was presented again in a correction trial until the correct response was made; correction trials were excluded from all analyses. Each daily session consisted of 32 trials, excluding correction trials, and the rewarded numerosity was presented equally often on the left and right. The intertrial interval was 20 s.

Training was conducted 5 days per week for a minimum of 20 sessions (640 trials). Acquisition was operationally defined as a mean accuracy of at least 75% across five consecutive sessions, corresponding to at least 120 correct choices out of 160 noncorrection trials. Five mice met this criterion within the initial 20 sessions. Tests with novel stimuli

After acquiring the 8 versus 4 discrimination, the mice completed six test blocks. The six test blocks were administered in the same fixed order (Tests 1-6) for all mice. Each test block consisted of 20 sessions (32 trials per session) and was preceded by 10 baseline recovery sessions using the original 8 versus 4 stimuli. In all test blocks, the same choose-larger contingency used during acquisition was maintained: choices of the larger numerosity (S+) were reinforced, whereas choices of the smaller numerosity (S-) were not reinforced. This reinforced testing procedure was used to maintain stable responding and motivation across multiple transfer and non-numerical control tests. As in acquisition, correction trials were administered following incorrect choices to discourage the development of side bias. Because reinforced testing could allow within-test learning, we additionally analyzed the first session of each test block as an early-test measure of transfer before extended exposure to the test contingencies.

In Test 1, S+ was held constant at 8, whereas S-var-ied across 7, 6, 5, and 4 to evaluate whether the mice had adopted an absolute "choose 8" strategy. In Test 2, S-was held constant at 4, whereas S+ varied across 5, 6, 7, and 8 to evaluate whether the mice had adopted an absolute "avoid 4" strategy. In Test 3, the mice were tested on novel numerosity pairs with a fixed numerical ratio of .50 (16 vs. 8, 8 vs. 4, 4 vs. 2, and 2 vs. 1). In Test 4, the same numerosity pairs used in Test 3 were presented as open-circle displays.

In Test 5, the same numerosity pairs were tested with cumulative element area equated across the two arrays on each trial by scaling element size. In Test 6, the same numerosity pairs were tested using phase-scrambled versions of the stimuli to assess the contribution of discrete item structure.

Discussion Mice learned to select the larger of two dot arrays and showed evidence of transfer to several novel numerosity pairs. After acquiring the 8-versus-4 discrimination, they performed above chance on several untrained comparisons involving both familiar and unfamiliar numerosities, although performance on the most difficult comparison, 8 versus 7, did not exceed chance. This pattern of transfer is consistent with previous demonstrations of approximate numerosity discrimination in nonhuman animals using two-choice paradigms, including rats tested with visual arrays (Cox & Montrose, 2016) and striped field mice (Reznikova et al., 2019). Because the test blocks were reinforced and first-session transfer was incomplete for some comparisons, performance across the full test blocks may reflect both initial transfer of the trained relation and subsequent learning within the test blocks.

Performance varied systematically as a function of numerical ratio, and accuracy decreased as the numerical ratio approached 1.0, a pattern consistent with Weber-like scaling. The relation between numerical ratio and discrimination accuracy was similar across Tests 1 and 2, indicating that ratio-dependent performance generalized across both test formats. Ratio-dependent performance of this kind has been reported across a wide range of nonhuman species, including primates (Cantlon & Brannon, 2006), birds (Ditz & Nieder, 2016) and fish (Agrillo et al., 2009; Piffer et al., 2013), and is widely interpreted as a behavioral signature of approximate magnitude representations. The present transfer pattern is consistent with the use of a relative numerical strategy, although the design was not intended to distinguish this strategy definitively from other stimulus-control pos- sibilities. Recent comparative work has emphasized that animals may vary in their reliance on relative versus absolute numerical strategies during numerosity discrimination (Brosche et al., 2025).

The control manipulations provided further insight into the perceptual basis of this discrimination. When filled dots were replaced with open circles (Test 4), performance remained above chance across all numerical pairs, although it was weakest for the largest numerosity pair. When dot size was adjusted to equate cumulative surface area between arrays (Test 5), performance fell to chance or below-chance levels for most comparisons, suggesting that cumulative area likely served as a supporting cue in the earlier tests. Disrupting discrete item structure through phase scrambling (Test 6) also impaired performance. Mice did not perform above chance for the larger pairs, indicating that the global luminance and spatial-frequency information preserved by phase scrambling was not sufficient to support those discriminations. By contrast, performance remained above chance for 4 versus 2 and 2 versus 1, suggesting that some residual nonnumerical information preserved in the scrambled displays was sufficient to guide choice for these smaller pairs. Thus, Test 6 should not be interpreted as showing preserved numerosity-based discrimination for the smaller pairs, but rather as evidence that performance was sensitive to discrete item structure and to additional visual information that remained available after scrambling. Although the phase-scrambled condition was included primarily as a control for discrete item structure, it is also theoretically relevant because the two cerebral hemispheres have been proposed to differ in their sensitivity to spatial-frequency information, which may contribute to spatial-numerical associations (Felisatti et al., 2020).

Overall, these findings suggest that mice did not rely on numerosity in isolation but that performance was also influenced by nonnumerical visual dimensions, including cumulative surface area, item structure, and spatial-frequency content. Such cue integration has also been documented in numerosity discrimination tasks in other nonhuman animals (Agrillo et al., 2011). The findings from Experiment 1 indicate that mice can discriminate static dot arrays differing in relative numerosity and that their performance shows behavioral properties commonly associated with approximate magnitude processing. However, the present results do not establish stimulus control by numerosity independently of correlated continuous visual properties.

Experiment 2: Magnitude-related bias in side choice

after relative numerosity training Numerosity has been proposed to interact systematically with spatial responding, as described by the Spatial-Numerical Association of Response Codes (SNARC) effect in humans (Dehaene et al., 1993; Toomarian & Hubbard, 2018). Evidence for analogous magnitude-space associations has also been reported in some nonhuman species, including chicks and honeybees, using procedures in which animals are trained on numerical discriminations and then tested with equal-equal probe displays that reveal lateralized choice biases as a function of numerosity (Rugani et al., 2015; Giurfa et al., 2022; see Felisatti, Giurfa, & Rugani, 2026 for a comparative review). At the same time, other work has emphasized substantial context dependence and individual variability in the presence, direction, and strength of spatial-magnitude associations in animals, including apes, bears, pigeons, blue jays, and rhesus macaques (Gazes et al., 2017; Johnson-Ulrich & Vonk, 2018; Lazareva et al., 2020; Jackson et al., 2026). Against this background, and building directly on Experiment 1, the present experiment examined whether side choice varied systematically with stimulus magnitude under probe conditions in which iden-tical visual arrays were presented simultaneously on the left and right sides of the touchscreen.

Methods

Procedure

The same six mice from Experiment 1 were tested in Experiment 2. No additional performance criterion was imposed between the Experiment 1 test blocks or before the start of Experiment 2. Mice were tested in the same touchscreen-equipped operant-conditioning chamber and under the same trial structure used in Experiment 1. Each trial began with a head entry into the illuminated magazine, after which two stimuli were presented simultaneously in the left and right response windows.

Each daily session consisted of 32 trials: 24 baseline trials and 8 test trials. Baseline and test trials were randomly interleaved, and each test numerosity occurred equally often within a session. In baseline trials, the 8 versus 4 discrimination from Experiment 1 was maintained, with the larger numerosity designated as the correct choice. Correct responses were reinforced with a food pellet, and incorrect responses triggered a 5-s timeout followed by a correction trial in which the same numerosity pair was repeated until a correct response was made.

In test trials, two identical arrays were presented (2 vs. 2, 4 vs. 4, 8 vs. 8, or 16 vs. 16). Each numerosity occurred twice per session (two trials each, for a total of eight test trials). Reinforcement was delivered regardless of whether the mouse responded to the left or right array, and no correction trials were administered on test trials. The primary dependent measure was side choice on test trials, coded as selection of the left-side array versus the right-side array and analyzed as a function of numerosity. Each mouse completed 20 daily sessions, yielding a total of 160 test trials per subject (40 per numerosity).

Statistical analyses

Analyses focused on probe trials (2 vs. 2, 4 vs. 4, 8 vs. 8, and 16 vs. 16). Baseline 8 versus 4 trials from the same sessions were analyzed separately using an intercept-only binomial GLMM to test for an overall left-right side bias. For the main probe analysis, trial-level side choice was analyzed using binomial GLMMs with a logit link and subject as a random intercept. Left choices were coded as 1 and right choices as 0. Numerosity was entered both as a centered continuous predictor and as a categorical factor. Three nested models were fitted: an intercept-only model, a lineartrend model, and a categorical model. Likelihood-ratio tests were used to compare these models and to evaluate whether side choice varied as a function of numerosity and whether the categorical model improved fit beyond the linear-trend model. Discussion

Experiment 2 examined whether side choice varied systematically with probe numerosity under equal-equal conditions in which reinforcement was independent of left-right responding. The results showed a positive linear relation between probe numerosity and the probability of choosing the left side, suggesting a magnitude-related spatial bias under the present task conditions. However, this pattern was stronger at the level of the overall linear trend than at the level of individual probe numerosities or individual subjects and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Baseline 8 versus 4 trials also did not show a significant overall side bias, suggesting that the probe pattern did not simply reflect a preexisting left-right preference. This finding is broadly consistent with previous reports that numerical magnitude can influence spatial biases in nonhuman animals, although the direction and strength of such effects vary across species and procedures (Gazes et al., 2017; Johnson-Ulrich & Vonk, 2018; Lazareva et al., 2020; Jackson et al., 2026).

A strength of the present design is that reinforcement on probe trials was noncontingent with respect to side choice, reducing the likelihood that the observed pattern reflected direct reinforcement of left or right responses during the probes. However, because the same mice were tested in Experiment 2 after completing Experiment 1, the present design cannot determine whether the observed magnituderelated spatial bias reflects an innate predisposition, an association acquired through prior experience, or an interaction between the two. Recent evidence in newborn chicks indicates that experience with numerical sequences can modify spatial response tendencies (Felisatti, Macchinizzi, et al., 2026). Importantly, the choose-larger training contingency did not systematically favor either spatial location and therefore does not, by itself, explain the direction of the observed bias. Thus, although prior learning may have influenced spatial responding, its contribution remains unknown.

General Discussion

The present study addressed two related questions about magnitude processing in mice using static visual dot arrays. First, Experiment 1 examined whether mice could extract behaviorally relevant magnitude information from such displays, as indicated by transfer to novel comparisons, ratiodependent performance, and sensitivity to non-numerical control manipulations. The results showed that mice learned a relative numerosity discrimination and generalized it beyond the training pair, but also that performance was influenced by visual properties correlated with numerosity, particularly under more demanding stimulus conditions. Experiment 2 then tested whether this information influenced side choice when identical dot arrays were presented simultaneously on the left and right and neither side was designated as correct. Under those probe conditions, the probability of left-side choice increased with numerosity. Taken together, these findings suggest that mice can extract behaviorally relevant magnitude information from static dot arrays and that this information can, under the present task conditions, bias lateralized choice. The results of Experiment 1 extend previous work on numerosity processing in mice by demonstrating relative numerosity discrimination from simultaneously presented static arrays. Previous work has established that mice can process numerosity-like information with scalar variability, but those findings have come predominantly from sequential or response-based paradigms (Berkay et al., 2016; Çavdaroğlu & Balcı, 2016; Gür et al., 2021; Light et al., 2019) rather than simultaneous visual comparisons. The mice also generalized their performance to comparisons involving novel numerosities, including both smaller values (2 vs. 1) and larger values (16 vs. 8), a pattern more consistent with flexible relative numerosity discrimination than with reliance on memorized stimulus configurations. Performance across numerosity pairs in the generalization tests was also consistent with approximate magnitude representations contributing to discrimination. Accuracy was highest for comparisons with coarser numerical ratios and declined as ratios approached unity, most clearly in the 8 versus 7 comparison. Across Tests 1 and 2, accuracy decreased as the numerical ratio approached unity, and this relation did not differ significantly between the two test formats. Although the present design was not intended to estimate Weber fractions precisely, the observed ratio dependence is consistent with prior evidence from rodent response-based paradigms and with broader comparative findings showing scalar variability in magnitude discrimination (Berkay et al., 2016; Çavdaroğlu & Balcı, 2016; Cantlon & Brannon, 2006; Agrillo et al., 2010).

Control manipulations highlighted how continuous visual dimensions contributed to discrimination. Replacing filled dots with open circles left performance above chance across all numerosity pairs, although it was weakest for the largest numerosity pair. Equating cumulative surface area or disrupting discrete item structure through phase scrambling generally reduced accuracy, although the effects differed across numerosity pairs. Taken together, these findings indicate that mice did not rely on numerosity in isolation. Rather, their performance was influenced by visual properties correlated with numerosity, such as total area and spatial structure. A similar pattern has been reported in wolves and dogs tested with simultaneously presented dot arrays in a touchscreen paradigm: although performance followed Weber's law, control analyses suggested that choices were also supported by continuous nonnumerical cues, including stimulus size and shape (Rivas-Blanco et al., 2020). This convergence suggests that ratio-dependent performance and reliance on correlated visual information are not mutually exclusive in comparative numerosity tasks.

This point is important for interpreting Experiment 2. The spatial bias was observed after the mice had completed discrimination training in which numerosity was behaviorally relevant but in which continuous visual dimensions also contributed to performance. Accordingly, the probe result is best interpreted as evidence that magnitude-related information, rather than necessarily numerosity alone, biased side choice. Under these conditions, larger magnitudes were associated with a higher probability of left-side choice. This direction differs from the canonical SNARC pattern typically reported in humans (Dehaene et al., 1993; Toomarian & Hubbard, 2018), but directional consistency should not be assumed in nonhuman animals. Comparative findings have been mixed, with left-right numerical biases reported under some procedures in chicks and honeybees (Rugani et al., 2015 (Rugani et al., , 2020;; Giurfa et al., 2022), but with substantial variability across species, tasks, and individuals in studies of apes, bears, pigeons, blue jays, and rhesus macaques (Gazes et al., 2017; Johnson-Ulrich & Vonk, 2018; Lazareva et al., 2020; Felisatti, Giurfa, & Rugani, 2026; Jackson et al., 2026). The present result therefore supports a taskdependent magnitude-related spatial bias in mice rather than a straightforward animal analogue of the human SNARC effect.

An additional consideration is that the present probe procedure differs from anchor-based paradigms in which animals are explicitly trained on a reference numerosity and spatial bias on equal-equal trials is interpreted relative to that reference (Rugani et al., 2015 (Rugani et al., , 2020)). Here, the mice were trained on a relative numerosity rule rather than on a single reference value, and spatial bias was assessed as a function of absolute numerosity. This design was well suited to detecting monotonic, magnitude-dependent modulation of side choice under conditions with minimal explicit spatial contingencies, but it does not test the referencedependent reversals that are central to some anchor-based accounts. An additional probe reported in the Supplementary Materials showed a directionally similar trend when dot positions were randomized (Figure S2), providing preliminary support for the main effect. However, because the supplementary effect was weaker and equal-equal probe procedures have yielded mixed results across species (Beran et al., 2019; Triki & Bshary, 2018), further replication will be needed to establish the reliability and boundary conditions of this pattern.

Several limitations constrain the generality of the present findings. All mice were trained to choose the larger numerosity. Accordingly, the present findings show that mice can acquire and generalize a choose-larger discrimination, but they do not establish equivalent learning under a choosesmaller rule. Because previous research suggests that numerosity discrimination can be asymmetric across reward contingencies, whether the same pattern would emerge in the present touchscreen task remains an open question (Inman et al., 2015; Lazareva et al., 2020). Because all mice had extensive prior discrimination training, the present study cannot determine whether the magnitude-related sidechoice bias was present before training or emerged through experience. Although stimulus control was improved relative to many earlier rodent studies, numerosity and continuous visual dimensions were not fully decorrelated across all conditions, limiting direct comparison with human SNARC effects. Moreover, the small sample size and substantial individual variability, including opposite trends in two of the six mice, limit the generalizability of the group-level effect.

Despite these limitations, the present findings have broader significance. At the behavioral level, they show that mice can learn and generalize relative numerosity discrimination from static visual arrays and exhibit ratio-dependent performance in a simultaneous visual comparison task. They also show that, following training on relative numerosity discrimination, side choice can vary systematically with stimulus magnitude under noncontingent probe conditions. Methodologically, these findings support the use of mice as a promising model for investigating how magnitude-related information extracted from visual displays guides choice. Because mice are well suited to neural recording and targeted manipulation, this paradigm provides a foundation for identifying the neural mechanisms that support relative numerosity discrimination and magnitude-dependent spatial bias.
