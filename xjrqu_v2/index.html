Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background Digital platforms provide contexts for both normative adolescent sexual exploration and potential victimization. We lack understanding of the diversity of experiences adolescents encounter, and importantly, and how they subjectively appraise them. How a young person subjectively perceives an event may impact associations with health.

Methods This preregistered study used population-based self-report data from 3,675 adolescents aged 12-16 years to examine the prevalence of different online sexual experiences and violations, ranging from sharing sexual content to having nude images spread on social media. Additionally, the study probes several measures of subjective appraisals, including how distressing experiences were, regret and assessments of the extent to which one felt one was forced or tricked into the experience. Generalized linear models were used to examine associations between cumulative exposure to online sexual experiences and violations and mental health symptoms (Hopkins Symptom Checklist), somatic symptoms (Children's Somatic Symptoms Inventory), and current and peak subjective distress related to these experiences. Regret was examined as a potential moderator. Logistic regression examined the history of offline sexual abuse by an adult as a risk factor.

2 Results By age 16, 49% of adolescents reported at least one online sexual experience or violation. Greater cumulative exposure was associated with more mental and somatic symptoms and higher current and peak subjective distress. Associations with symptoms remained even when accounting for symptoms from two years prior, family affluence, removing most severe items, and excluding participants reporting being coerced or tricked into the experience. Regret did not moderate exposure -subjective distress associations.

Offline sexual abuse by an adult substantially increased the odds of having online sexual experiences or violations. Girls reported more coercion and subjective distress related to online sexual experiences, although boys were less likely to provide data on these measures.

Conclusions Digital contexts should be recognized as important developmental arenas in which adolescents navigate sexuality and intimacy, and where normative exploratory processes and potential risks co-occur. Future research should examine the socio-contextual factors that may render some online sexual experiences detrimental to health, informing efforts to create safer digital spaces that support adolescents' exploration while protecting them from harm.

Introduction

Digital technologies have become embedded in the everyday lives of children and adolescents (Ofcom, 2025), shaping how young people form identities, explore sexuality, and build relationships (Maes et al., 2022; Nesi et al., 2020). Developmental psychologists increasingly argue that online environments should therefore be treated as central developmental contexts in which key social and psychological processes unfold (Nesi et al., 2020). Like traditional physical contexts in which adolescents interact with peers, such as schools, leisure activities, and other offline social settings, digital environments can provide opportunities for connection, self-expression, help-seeking, and exploration. At the same time, digital environments have unique affordances, including permanence, public visibility and disseminability. The content of online interactions can also be rapidly shared with large numbers of other people, thereby making harmful experiences more difficult to escape or contain. Quantifiable feedback and constant connectivity may also introduce risks that undermine well-being and mental health (Keles et al., 2020; Nesi et al., 2018; Orben et al., 2024).

Mental and somatic health in the digital age

Studying online experiences in relation to mental and somatic health is particularly important in adolescence. This developmental period is marked by substantial physical and psychological changes, as well as social reorientation toward peers (Nelson et al., 2005).

Major developmental changes include heightened sensitivity to peer evaluation and rejection (Sebastian et al., 2010; Sommerville, 2013), increased need for social belonging (Blakemore & Mills, 2014), and peer influence on behavior, including risk-taking (Simons-Morton et al., 2011 , 2019). These developmentally normative aspects of adolescence likely interact with the online contexts that adolescents navigate, shaping both the kinds of experiences they encounter and how they respond to them. For example, just as adolescents may be prone to risk-taking in offline peer settings (Simons-Morton et al., 2011), similar processes may occur in online environments, but in ways that are shaped by the specific affordances of digital platforms. Given that many mental health problems emerge in adolescence, and that prevention is particularly critical around the time of onset (Solmi et al., 2022), it is important to understand how adverse experiences that arise in online contexts may shape adolescent mental health.

Although changing cultural attitudes toward mental health, including reduced stigma and greater willingness to report symptoms, may influence prevalences of mental health symptoms, available evidence suggests that they cannot fully account for the observed decline in adolescent mental health over the past decade (McElroy et al., 2023). This has sparked debate about whether increased engagement with digital platforms among young people may contribute causally to poorer adolescent mental health (Coyne et al., 2020; Twenge et al., 2018). However, despite the intuitive appeal of this idea, empirical support remains mixed, in part because of methodological shortcomings, including a lack of specificity. Much research has, for example, relied on broad measures such as time spent online (Riehm et al., 2019; Twenge et al., 2018), which may obscure the heterogeneity of experiences young people have, as well as the heterogeneity of responses these experiences may evoke. Just as decades of research have explored nuances in traditional offline developmental contexts, developmental psychology must now also examine the nuances of the online developmental contexts that young people inhabit today.

Turning attention to sexual experiences adolescents may encounter in online development contexts, these can vary from consensual normative experiences to sexual abuse (Döring, 2014; Wolak et al., 2008). Although emerging research suggests that online sexual abuse is associated with health outcomes comparable to those linked to offline sexual abuse (Jonsson et al., 2019), abuse occurring in online contexts is still sometimes minimized or underestimated by professionals (Hamilton-Giachritsis et al., 2017), highlighting the need for further research on its associations with mental and somatic health outcomes.

The Need for Greater Specificity in Studying Adolescents' Experiences in Online Settings

Studying links between broad measures such as time spend on digital platforms and health outcomes (e.g. Riehm et al., 2019) yield thought-provoking findings, but may not provide insights into the specific types of experiences young individuals encounter and importantly, how they subjectively experience them.

Not all experiences in digital settings are inherently negative. Specific affordances of digital platforms, such as anonymity and accessibility, may allow adolescents to explore sexuality and intimacy and to seek information and support (Suto et al., 2025). As such, by creating opportunities for connection, exploration, and support, digital platforms may facilitate normative developmental processes. However, these same affordances may also increase the risk of adverse experiences, including sexual harassment, solicitation, and abuse.

The online context can lower young people's inhibitions and create a false sense of safety, while accessibility and affordability may extend perpetrators' reach both geographically, by expanding the pool of potential victims beyond immediate offline environments, and temporally, by enabling contact late into the night in ways that may deepen influence and preserve secrecy (Hamilton-Giachritsis et al., 2017). Permanence, whereby sexual content may be difficult to remove from the internet, and the ease and speed of potential dissemination are additional features that may influence how harmful an online sexual experience can become (Page et al., 2025; Salter et al., 2023). Online context can thus promote developmental processes that unfold in adolescence but also pose numerous risks. A parallel distinction has characterized research on adolescent sexual development more broadly: a longstanding emphasis on sexual risks and adverse outcomes has been complemented by research examining sexuality as a normative developmental process with positive qualities and consequences. Tolman and McClelland (2011) called for integrating these perspectives rather than treating positive sexuality and risk management as opposing domains. From this perspective, adolescent sexual development involves learning both how to pursue wanted, positive sexual experiences and how to recognize, negotiate, and manage potential risks. This integrated perspective should also inform research on sexual experiences in online developmental contexts, where opportunities for exploration and connection coexist with potential risks. Additionally, it is increasingly recognized that, in order to understand how adverse events affect children's development, research must also consider children's subjective appraisals (Pollak & Smith, 2021). Seemingly similar events may be experienced as minimally or highly distressing depending on the child's individual characteristics and contextual factors, and subjective experience of the seemingly adverse event is a predictor of health outcomes (Ferschmann et al., 2026). It is thus important not only to examine correlates of event-based characteristics (what happened) but also inquire into how they were experienced by the young person (was it distressing for the person).

The Aims of the Study

The present study aims to address these knowledge gaps by moving beyond broad indicators of digital engagement by examining adolescents' experiences with range of online sexual experiences, alongside their subjective appraisals of those experiences. In addition to subjective distress related to how sexual experiences in online settings are perceived by adolescents, this study will also examine regret. Examining regret is not intended to place responsibility on adolescents for negative outcomes or harms that may arise following an interaction. Rather, regret may be informative in understanding the gray zone between consensual and non-consensual sexual experiences by capturing how individuals interpret an experience after it has taken place (Akre et al., 2013). This may be particularly relevant in online settings, where an initially voluntary interaction can later take on a different meaning, for example if private sexual content is shared beyond the intended recipient. Greater insight into adolescents' subjective appraisals as they navigate sexual experiences in online contexts is therefore critical for understanding associations with mental and somatic health.

Using cross-sectional data from a large population-based sample of Norwegian adolescents aged 12-16 years, the present preregistered study had several aims. First, we aimed to map the range of sexual experiences that adolescents encounter in online settings, including both voluntary behaviors (e.g., sending sexual content) and non-consensual acts (e.g., receiving unwanted sexual content or image-based abuse). We included this range of experiences as, particularly among older adolescents, the boundaries between developmentally normative voluntary exploration and abuse can be blurry. Second, we will explore associations between these sexual experiences and violations in online settings and mental and somatic symptoms. Third, we aimed to investigate subjective appraisals of these online experiences, including perceived distress and subsequent regret. If regret reflects a retrospective appraisal of the adverse experience, including the perception that one's own actions may have contributed to what occurred (van Dijk & Zeelenberg, 2002), then higher levels regret may also intensify retrospective subjective distress related to what happened.

Finally, we examined whether prior exposure to offline sexual abuse by an adult is associated with increased odds of reporting sexual experiences and violations in online settings. This expectation is consistent with the polyvictimization framework, which suggests that children exposed to one form of victimization are at increased risk of experiencing other forms of victimization across contexts (Finkelhor et al., 2007).

Our preregistered hypotheses were formulated as follows: H1: Higher cumulative exposure to sexual experiences and violations in online settings will be associated with poorer mental and somatic health. H2: Higher cumulative exposure to sexual experiences and violations in online settings will be associated with higher levels of subjective distress related to these experiences. H3: the association between sexual experiences and violations in online settings and subjective distress will be stronger in individuals with higher levels of regret. H4: exposure to sexual abuse in offline settings committed by an adult will increase the odds of having sexual experiences or being exposed to sexual violations in online settings.

Methods

This study, including its hypotheses and analytical plan, was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/mqy78). Any deviations from the preregistration are reported and justified below.

Sample

This study leveraged data from the prospective Norwegian population-based study, UEVO (Hafstad et al., 2020). At baseline in 2019, we conducted a stratified probability sampling of schools based on school region, school size and whether the schools have above or below regional average of ethnic minority children. The aim of this recruitment procedure was to recruit youth from a representative sample of Norwegian schools. The initial sample size at baseline consisted of 9,240 adolescents aged 12-16 years. Several data collection waves had been conducted since then, using a combination of repeated, adapted, and newly introduced measures across time points. These follow-up data collections included an extraordinary wave related to the Covid19-pandemic in 2020, one in 2021 (Hafstad et al., 2022) and a recent wave in 2026 that has not yet been published. The preregistered analyses for the present study were performed on the cross-sectional data collected in 2021 as this data wave included the most comprehensive assessment of sexual experiences and violations in online settings. One sensitivity analysis included data on somatic and mental health symptoms both from baseline and 2021, so that pre-existing differences on the outcome measures could be accounted for. The demographic information in the sample used in the present study are presented in Table 1. Note. Family affluence was assessed with one item about the family's ability to afford needed goods, rated from 0 (applies very well) to 3 (applies very poorly); low family affluence indicates responses of 2 or 3.

Three-hundred-and-thirty-four individuals displayed some form of missingness on the items inquiring into sexual experiences and violations in online settings -82% of these individuals were missing data on two or more items. Missingness showed substantial overlap across measures: among the 235 participants missing all five exposure items, 97.9% also lacked HSCL data and 98.3% lacked CSSI data. Therefore, we only included individuals with complete data on these five items, resulting in a sample size of 3,341. Attrition analyses described in greater detail below indicated that girls and individuals from more affluent families were more likely to have complete data.

Ethic Statement

The study protocol was approved by the Regional Committee for Ethics in Medical and Health research in the Southeastern region of Norway.

A unique feature of this study is that participation did not require parental consent, enabled by the independent consent regulation introduced in 2017 (Health Research Act § 17), which permits such consent from the age of 12 under specific conditions. The regulation is intended for exceptional circumstances and includes, among other conditions, situations in which parental consent could compromise participation or disclosure because the research concerns information that parents may have an interest in concealing (e.g., violence, neglect, or abuse), requirements that the societal value of the research clearly outweigh potential burdens to participants, and obligations for researchers to have procedures in place for responding if participation reveals a need for health care or other support. Additionally, data collection took place during school hours. Both consent procedures and the context in which data are collected (e.g., at school versus at home) may influence participation and disclosure rates among children (Meinck et al., 2025).

Consequently, these steps helped to mitigate selective dropout, thereby enhancing the overall representativeness of the dataset. From the early stages of the project through to the dissemination of results, user involvement was prioritized through a youth expert panel composed of adolescents aged 14-15 years (Hafstad et al., 2020).

Measures

Sexual experiences and violations in online settings

Exposure was calculated as the sum of scores on items based on questions partly adapted from Jernbro and Janson (2017). The wording of these items was further refined following a pilot study conducted at a local high school in the Oslo region. In a sequence of questions related to experiences related to online experiences, the youth were asked the following questions: 1) Has it ever happened that someone sent you images of their private parts (genitals, breasts, or buttocks)? 2) Have you ever sent or posted sexual content (text, video, or images) on the internet? 3) Has anyone made you perform sexual acts on yourself while they were watching? 4) Have you ever received sexual content (verbal, images, or online settings was also operationalized as a binary variable, indicating the presence or absence of such experiences on any of the items.

Subjective Distress

Adolescents who reported exposure to sexual experiences and violations in online settings were asked follow-up questions regarding the subjective appraisal of those experiences. The first question assessed current subjective distress: 'On a scale from 1 to 10, how much does this bother you right now?' The second question measured peak subjective distress: 'On a scale from 1 to 10, how much has this bothered you at its worst?' For both questions, 1 indicated 'not at all,' and 10 indicated 'extremely'. These two constructs are related but distinct (Ferschmann et al., 2026).

Offline sexual abuse committed by an adult

Exposure to sexual abuse committed by an adult in offline context was conceptualized as experience with at least one of these experiences: Has it ever happened 1) that an adult showed you their private parts (genitals, breasts, or buttocks)? 2) that an adult made you show your private parts to them? 3) that an adult made you touch their private parts? 4) that an adult touched your private parts? 5) an adult engaged in or attempted to engage in sexual acts with you [digital penetration (i.e. using fingers), oral sex, or intercourse]? Each item was rated in terms of frequency as 0: never, 1: once, 2: a couple of times, 3: many times. Exposure to physical sexual abuse committed by an adult was also operationalized as a binary variable, indicating the presence or absence of such experiences on any of the items.

Mental and Somatic symptoms

The Hopkins Symptom Checklist (HSCL-10), a widely used self-report screening tool for symptoms of depression and anxiety, also in youth (Finbråten et al., 2021; Schmalbach et al., 2021). Each item describes a specific symptom. The study participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they had been bothered by this symptom during the past two weeks on a scale 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). Cronbach's alpha was 0.93.

The 8-item Children's Somatic Symptoms Inventory (CSSI-8), was used to assess selfreported somatic symptoms (Stone et al., 2019 (Stone et al., , 2024;; Walker et al., 2009). The instrument includes items describing stomach pain, headache, faintness or dizziness, arm or leg pain, a fast heartbeat, nausea, and weakness. Respondents were asked to indicate frequency of different somatic symptoms in the past two weeks with response options ranging from 0 (never) to 3 (very often). The Cronbach's alpha was .85.

Regret

Study participants who reported at least one exposure to sexual experience or violation in online settings also received a follow-up question pertaining to regret: Have you ever regretted doing this? This item was rated as0 = no,1 = yes, a bit, and2 = yes, very much. In analyses, regret was used as factor with two levels: yes and no.

Coercion and deception

Study participants who reported at least one exposure to a sexual experience or violations were furthermore asked about the extent of coercion and deception in the experience: Did you feel tricked, threatened or forced to do this? The item was coded as0 = no,1 = yes, a bit and2 = yes, very much. In analyses, this variable was used as a factor with two levels: yes and no.

Mental and somatic symptoms from two years prior

Pre-existing symptoms were measured two years prior using the HSCL-10 and CSSI-8, already described under outcomes. Cronbach's alpha were .91 and .79, respectively.

Note that the measure used in data collection in 2019 included only 9 items as item 'Feeling everything is an effort' was inadvertently omitted during the construction of the web survey.

Gender

Study participants were asked to indicate their gender by choosing either boy, girl, or 'none of the categories fit me'. Boys were coded as 0, girls as 1 and the third category was coded as 2. In our analyses, we use all three categories, however, we report only for boys and girls as the third category included only 56 individuals.

Family affluence

Family affluence was assessed with a single item asking respondents to indicate how well the following statement applied to their situation: 'In my family, we can usually afford to buy the things that we need.' Response options were:0 = applies very well,1 = applies quite well,2 = applies quite poorly, and3 = applies very poorly.

Age at assessment

Age was measured as self-report in years.

Birthplace

Birthplace was assessed as self-reported and rates either as0 = born in Norway or1 = born outside of Norway.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R (R Core Team, 2026) version 4.2.3 in Rstudio (Posit Team, 2023).

Missing data

As described in the Sample section, data was complete for the main predictor, online sexual experiences and violations. We examined patterns and extent of missingness across all other variables included in the preregistered analyses. Missingness was low for demographic variables, including gender (<1%), birthplace (<1%), and family affluence (<1%). Similarly, low to moderate levels of missing data were observed for exposure to physical sexual abuse by an adult (1%), current mental health symptoms (3%), and somatic health symptoms (6%).

Of note, regarding missingness on item levels for the measures of mental and somatic symptoms measures by HSCL and CSSI, respectively. For CSSI, complete data were required to calculate a total sum score. For HSCL, in line with existing recommendations (Strand et al., 2003), a total sum score was calculated for individuals who were missing data on up to one HSCL item. In these cases, the missing item was replaced with the mean of the remaining items for that individual.

We did not preregister imputation of missing data and therefore conducted primary analyses using complete cases. However, to assess the robustness of results to missing data assumptions, we conducted sensitivity analyses in which missing values on these variables were imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE). Substantial missingness (>50%) was observed for certain variables, including mental health symptoms assessed two years prior. This was expected due to the study design, as these measures were unavailable for participants recruited later into the cohort. In addition, some follow-up questions (such as feelings of regret) were administered only to participants who reported exposure to online sexual experiences and violations, resulting in structurally missing data for unexposed participants. Analyses involving these variables were therefore restricted to individuals with complete data.

Attrition analyses

We compared youth without complete data on online sexual experiences and violations to those with complete data. First, logistic regression analyses were conducted to calculate the odds of having complete data based on the young person's self-reported gender, family affluence and birthplace (i.e. being born in Norway or not). Gender, family affluence and country of birth were entered simultaneously as predictors of having complete data. Next, we examined whether individuals with incomplete data differed from those with complete data on key variables, including mental and somatic symptoms collected in 2019 and 2021. To compare group means, we began by performing Levene's test (Levene, 1960) to test whether variances in the groups that were being compared were equal, and then performed independent samples t-tests. Since mental and somatic health variables highly skewed, we opted for bootstrap t-test (Efron & Tibshirani, 1994; Hutcheson & Brown, 2024) by means of the boot.t.test function in the R package MKinfer (Kohl, 2019). The number of bootstraps was set to 10,000. The attrition analyses indicated that girls had higher odds of having complete data on online sexual experiences and violations compared to boys (OR = 1.68, 95% CI [1.19, 2.40], p = .004). In addition, lower family affluence was associated with lower odds of complete data (OR = 0.73 per unit increase, 95% CI [0.56, 0.99], p = .033). Bootstrap t-tests showed no significant differences between participants with complete and incomplete data on any of measures of mental and somatic health outcomes, either those measured in 2021-or two-years prior.

Preregistered analyses

Hypotheses 1-3 were tested using Generalized linear models (GLM; Nelder & Wedderburn, 1972), a flexible approach suitable for modelling positively skewed and heteroscedastic data commonly observed in psychological science (Ng & Cribbie, 2017; Smith et al., 2022). These analyses were ran using already build-in functions in R as well as package MASS (Venables & Ripley, 2002). Two GLMs were fitted to examine associations between exposure to online sexual experiences and violations and poorer mental and somatic health (hypothesis 1). The total cumulative score of online sexual experiences and violations served as the predictor (Figure 1), while HSCL and CSSI scores represented mental and somatic health outcomes, respectively. Gender and age at the time of assessment were included as covariates. Given that the two outcome variables were integers with abundance of zero values, we proceeded as follows; Models were initially specified with a Poisson distribution and assessed for over-dispersion (Zuur et al., 2009). If the over-dispersion parameter exceeded 1, we fitted and compared new GLMs. One quasi-Poisson GLM with corrected standard errors and one negative binomial GLM, both using log and square root link functions. Model selection was guided using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Decrease in these model indices indicated a better model fit. The model fit selection was also follow-up with inspection of the residual plots, because quasi-poison models do not produce AIC and BIC values (Zuur et al., 2009). We specified two GLM models with gaussian distribution with robust standard errors to examine association between cumulative exposure to online sexual experiences and violations and currently experienced subjective distress and peak subjective distress as outcome variables, respectively (Hypothesis 2).

The same model was extended with an interaction term consisting of exposure to online sexual experiences and violations and level of self-reported regret, to test whether associations between exposure to online sexual experiences and violations and subjective distress would be stronger in in individuals with higher levels of regret (hypothesis 3).

Finally, a logistic regression with offline sexual abuse committed by an adult as predictor, exposure to online sexual experiences and violations (coded as exposed vs. not exposed) as outcome, and gender and age at assessment as covariates was performed to examine whether offline sexual abuse was associated with increased odds of exposure to online sexual experiences and violations.

Sensitivity analyses

As preregistered, significant associations identified in the models testing hypothesis 1 were followed up by re-running the analyses with mental and/or somatic health scores assessed two years earlier included as covariates.

Deviation from preregistration

We performed chi-square tests of independence to examine associations between gender and different online sexual experiences and violations, as well as regret and coercion.

For each analysis, participants with a non-missing response on the respective item were included. Analyses of regret and coercion were further restricted to participants endorsing at least one online sexual experience or violation. Gender differences in peak and subjective distress were also explored using independent T-tests.

In our preregistration, we stated that we would conduct sensitivity analyses including age at first exposure to online sexual experiences and violations, as well as duration of such experiences as additional covariates -as recent research indicates that timing and duration represent related but distinct features of childhood adversity, differentially associated with mental and somatic health outcomes across adolescence (Hafstad et al., 2026). However, given the low number of available responses, we did not perform these analyses. Among individuals who reported on the five items measuring exposure to online sexual experiences and violations, only 2.8% reported on age at first exposure and 6.7% reported on duration.

Finally, only gender and age at assessment were preregistered as covariates in models testing our hypotheses. We repeated the same analyses with an additional covariate (family affluence) to examine the robustness of our findings.

Because the experiences captured by the cumulative score vary in voluntariness and severity, we also conducted sensitivity analyses to see whether the associations identified when testing H1 remained after removing experiences with the clearest indicators of severity or unwillingness. Specifically, we first recalculated the cumulative exposure score after excluding the two most severe items: 'Has anyone made you perform sexual acts on yourself while they were watching?' and 'Has anyone spread nude images or videos of you on social media?'. While the former directly involves coercions, the latter item is a form of nonconsensual dissemination of sexual content, which has previously been linked to internalizing problems in youth (Sciacca et al., 2023). For comprehensive review of associations between image-based sexual abuse and mental health, see Hellevik et al. (2025).

We then re-ran the analyses testing associations with mental and somatic symptoms. Finally, we re-ran the analyses after excluding participants who reported being tricked, threatened or forced, and re-ran the analyses.

Discussion

This study aimed to move beyond broad indicators of young people's digital engagement, such as time spent online, by examining specific online sexual experiences and violations and their associations with somatic and mental health symptoms. Importantly, the study sought to increase specificity further by moving beyond mere event descriptions to include measures of subjective appraisals of these experiences. Greater exposure to online sexual experiences and violations was associated with more mental and somatic health symptoms. Greater exposure was also linked to more negative subjective appraisals regarding these experiences. This latter association was not moderated by regret. Having previously experienced sexual abuse by an adult in offline settings was linked to substantial increased odds of reporting sexual experiences and violations in online settings. With nearly half of the population-based sample reporting some form of sexual experiences or violations in online settings by age 16, the present findings highlight that digital platforms constitute important developmental contexts where adolescents navigate sexuality, intimacy, vulnerability and potential harm.

Online Sexual Experiences and Violations Relate to Mental and Somatic Health

Hypothesis 1 was supported: the association between sexual experiences and violations in online settings and higher levels of mental and somatic symptoms. The association with mental symptoms is consistent with studies showing that adolescents whose sexual images have been shared without their consent report higher levels of depression, lower self-esteem, suicidality, and other mental health difficulties (Pampati et al., 2020; Schmidt et al., 2024; Sciacca et al., 2023). The association with somatic symptoms is also in line with the broader literature on sexual victimization. Prospective and longitudinal studies have found that sexual abuse and assault during childhood or adolescence are associated with subsequent headaches, abdominal pain, nausea, and other functional somatic symptoms (Bonvanie et al., 2015; Casanovas et al., 2022). Importantly, such associations do not appear to be limited to abuse perpetrated by adults. Sexual harassment by peers, including physical sexual harassment and sexual name-calling, has also been prospectively associated with somatic and depressive symptoms among adolescents (Dahlqvist et al., 2016). Although the experiences included in the present cumulative score varied in their nature and severity, several represented clear violations of sexual autonomy, privacy, and consent, such as being made to perform sexual acts while being watched or having nude images or videos distributed on social media. Such violations may evoke shame, fear of further dissemination, loss of control, and difficulties in interpersonal relationships, which may contribute to sustained distress and potentially manifest in both psychological and somatic symptoms (Hellevik et al., 2025).

More notable, however, was that the associations with both mental and somatic symptoms remained significant even after i) removing the most clearly abusive or coercive items (being made to perform sexual acts while being watched or having one's nude images or videos being spread on social media), and ii) excluding adolescents who specifically reported being tricked, threatened or forced into any of the measured sexual experiences in online context. This suggests that the association is not only driven by the most overtly abusive or coercive experiences.

This finding may be considered in light of research on normative sexual development, which suggests that the implications of sexual experiences for health and well-being depend not only on the nature of the experience itself, but also on how it is perceived and evaluated within its developmental and socio-cultural context. Youth sexual activity has been associated with both poorer mental health and greater well-being, with associations varying according to factors such as peer norms (for example whether an experience is age-appropriate), partner context (e.g. romantic partner or not), and parental attitudes toward adolescent sexuality (Meier, 2007; Vasilenko, 2017; Vrangalova & Savin-Williams, 2011).

Pre-existing individuallevel characteristics may also be of importance. Longitudinal studies suggest that depressive symptoms and other indicators of poor psychological health may predict subsequent sexual harassment victimization (Dahlqvist et al., 2016; Skoog & Kapetanovic, 2021).

These perspectives may also be relevant to understanding sexual experiences and violations in online settings. As with offline sexual experiences, their implications for health may depend on the developmental, interpersonal, and socio-cultural context in which they occur and how they are perceived by the young person. Relevant factors may include whether the experience is perceived as normative among peers, the relationship to the person involved, and the adolescent's age and developmental stage at the time of the experience. Although the present study accounted for age at assessment, we could not examine age at onset of the different experiences due to substantial missing data on the age of onset variable. Pre-existing individual differences in mental and somatic health may also play a role. While our study could not establish temporal or reciprocal associations, associations with mental and somatic symptoms remained significant after accounting for pre-existing differences in mental and somatic health, suggesting that they were not solely driven by poorer health preceding these experiences. In online settings, the relational context and subjective appraisal of an experience may be particularly important. For example, receiving sexual content from a same-age romantic partner may be perceived as positive and age-appropriate, whereas receiving similar content unsolicited from an unknown person may evoke discomfort or uncertainty. Qualitative research suggests that adolescents may experience unsolicited nude images with discomfort, feel pressure to reciprocate, or find it difficult to say no (Mishna et al., 2023). This variation in how online sexual experiences can be perceived may help explain why associations with mental and somatic symptoms remained even after the most clearly coercive or abusive experiences were removed from the cumulative exposure score. The remaining experiences may encompass both exploratory behaviors that form part of normative sexual development and experiences that are ambiguous, unwanted, uncomfortable, or otherwise negatively perceived. Thus, the association between the trimmed cumulative exposure score and higher symptom levels may partly reflect differences in the context and subjective meaning of these experiences that were not captured by the exposure measure itself.

Subjective Appraisals and Regret

Hypothesis 2 was supported: greater exposure to online sexual experiences and violations was associated with higher levels of both peak and current subjective distress when thinking about what happened. As with the previous hypothesis, this may suggest that with increasing numbers of online sexual experiences, the probability of experiencing subjectively distressing events increases. What remains a critical question is which factors determine whether an event is appraised retrospectively as distressing or not.

In this study, we examined regret as one such factor. Hypothesis 3, stating that the association between greater exposure and subjective distress in response to what happened, would be stronger in individuals who reported regret, was not supported. The lack of moderation may indicate that regret, at least as measured in this study, captures only a limited aspect of adolescents' retrospective appraisal of how distressing the experience was.

Experimental research on offline sexual scenarios suggest that perceived distress is shaped by multiple factors including consent, wantedness and pleasure (Hills et al., 2020), and future studies should explore these factors in the context on online sexual experiences. Additionally, the regret item in our study was framed as "Have you ever regretted having done this", thereby foregrounding the adolescent's own actions. It is possible that other factors, that instead direct attention to "what the other person did" may be another useful avenue to pursue. Examples of such factors are invasiveness and exploitativeness, introduced by the 3-D theory (Emery et al., 2023). Invasiveness refers to transgressions of normative boundaries around the body or the (sexual) self, while exploitativeness refers to being instrumentally used by another person for their own purposes or self-gratification. These constructs may be especially relevant in online settings, where affordances of digital platforms can alter the meaning and consequences of an experience after it has taken place. For example, sharing sexual content with someone one is romantically interested in may initially be consensual and pleasurable. Unlike many offline sexual interactions, digital sexual content can be screenshotted, redistributed, or recontextualized without the sender's knowledge. If the recipient later shares the content with peers to enhance his social status, the young person may retrospectively appraise the experience as invasive and exploitative. In other words, future research should thoroughly explore factors that impact the intensity of subjective distress experienced, which digital affordances enable these factors and how such risks can be limited.

Gender Differences

Girls had higher odds of exposure to sexual experiences and violation settings in an online context. This finding is consistent with gendered patterns documented in offline adolescent contexts, where girls tend to report more unwanted sexual attention and harassment, as well as sexual violence (Kaltiala, 2022; Stoltenborgh et al., 2011). Literature regarding gender differences in online settings is more mixed. Some studies indicate that girls are more likely than boys to experience a wide range of sexual encounters and violations in online settings (Finkelhor et al., 2024; Gámez-Guadix et al., 2023). However, a recent large meta-analysis of representative surveys including data from 123 countries found no major gender differences in online sexual victimization (Fry et al., 2025), suggesting that patterns of risk may differ from those observed in traditional offline settings. Similarly, a survey on online sexual child sexual solicitation and abuse among young adults found higher rates among women for some experiences, particularly unwanted sexualized interactions, although effect sizes were small, whereas other experiences were reported at similar rates by men and women (Chauviré-Geib et al., 2026). This is partly in line with our findings, which indicate girls encounter higher rates of online sexual experiences and violations, although when explored at item-level, effect sizes were mostly small. However, in our study, gender was associated with perceptions of being forced or tricked into the experience and both measures of subjective distress related to that experience with small-to-moderate and moderate effect sizes.

There are several possible explanations for these observed differences. First, it is possible that girls encounter experiences in online settings that genuinely involve more coercion or discomfort. Alternatively, boys may be less willing to disclose sexually abusive experiences, as documented in offline settings (Hietamäki et al., 2024). That may help explain why boys in our sample were less likely to provide complete data on sexual experiences and violations and why, among exposed youth, boys were less likely than girls to provide ratings of subjective distress related to online experiences. Research on sexual trauma in men shows that disclosure and labeling oneself as a victim may violate traditional masculine norms of being strong and invulnerable (Pilkington et al., 2026). That may have also influenced boys' reported levels of subjective distress and regret in our study. Finally, gender differences identified in this study may also reflect gendered social meanings and consequences attached to sexual experiences. A recent qualitative study provides insights into how seemingly similar events such as sharing a nude image, can serve very different purposes and have different social consequences depending on gender (Ringrose & Regehr, 2023). In that study, sexual double standards created victim-blaming and shaming for girls, but not for boys. Similar processes may explain why a larger proportion of girls in the present study report regret. This means that while comparing prevalences between boys and girls is informative, it is important to be aware that these experiences can bear different meanings and consequences.

Continuity of vulnerability across offline and online contexts

In this study, a history of sexual abuse by an adult in offline settings was associated with substantially higher odds of having sexual experiences or being exposed to sexual violations in online settings. Thus, Hypothesis 4 was supported, in line with the polyvictimization framework, which suggests that exposure to one form of victimization increases risk of experiencing other forms of victimization across contexts (Finkelhor et al., 2007). However, because the outcome used in this study captures a broader range of online sexual experiences, the observed continuity across offline and online contexts should be interpreted as likelihood of encountering sexualized online interactions, not solely as revictimization. This finding also aligns with the work by Turner and colleagues (2023), who identified early offline sexual abuse as a particularly strong predictor of online sexual abuse.

In their study, several forms of early life adversities were linked to increased odds of online sexual abuse, but these associations were fully mediated through risky online behaviors. In contrast, early offline sexual abuse retained a direct effect on online sexual abuse, even when accounting for the mediating role of risky online behaviors. This unique and direct effect may reflect lasting psychological injury that may leave children vulnerable in other contexts, also digital platforms, including distorted self-concept, distorted sense of own value, and reduced belief that one has control over one's life (Finkelhor & Browne, 1985; Turner et al., 2023).

Together, the findings by Turner and al. (2023) indicate that adolescents with history of early life adversity may benefit from digital safety education that aims to prevent risky behaviors in online settings. However, youth with a history of offline sexual abuse may need to receive trauma-informed support that address broader psychosocial vulnerabilities such as distorted self-concept, self-worth and powerlessness (Finkelhor & Browne, 1985).

Limitations and Future Directions

The cross-sectional design of the main analyses limits conclusions about directionality.

Although sensitivity analyses adjusting for mental and somatic symptoms assessed two years earlier reduce the likelihood that the observed associations are entirely explained by pre-existing symptom differences, they do not establish temporal ordering or causality. The associations may reflect several processes: online sexual experiences and violations may contribute to subsequent symptoms. Alternatively, adolescents with more symptoms may be more likely to engage in sexual activities or remain in risky online settings. Future studies should therefore use longitudinal designs, in which both online sexual experiences and symptoms are assessed repeatedly over time. Such designs, combined with advanced statistical approaches such as random-intercept cross-lagged panel models, would make it possible to examine temporal ordering and reciprocal associations between exposure and symptoms.

Second, although the present study assessed subjective distress related to online sexual experiences and violations, as well as selected subjective appraisals such as regret or feelings of being tricked, threatened or forced into the experience, these measures captured only a limited part of the adolescents' subjective experience. Future studies should therefore expand questionnaires with more nuanced measures that assess both contextual factors and their personal meaning such as perceived agency, consent, peer pressure or perceived social consequences, for example. Such measures would help clarify when online sexual experiences constitute normative sexual exploration and when it becomes harmful.

Third, attrition analyses indicated that boys were less likely than girls to provide complete data on the online sexual experiences and violations items. This differential missingness suggests that prevalence estimates, particularly among boys, should be interpreted with caution. Similarly, among exposed youth, boys were less likely to provide ratings of subjective distress. Future research should examine how questions about online sexual experiences and their subjective appraisals can be phrased in ways that facilitate disclosure among boys as well as girls.

Conclusion

Nearly half of adolescents in this population-based sample reported at least one sexual experience or violation in online settings by age 16, underscoring that digital platforms are important developmental contexts in which young people navigate sexuality, intimacy, vulnerability and potential harm. This also suggests that such experiences are not rare or marginal events affecting a small subgroup of adolescents, but relatively common experiences. Higher frequency of such experiences was linked to more mental and somatic health symptoms, as well as more negative subjective appraisals of these experiences. Finally, girls, older adolescents, and individuals with a history of sexual abuse by an adult in offline settings had higher odds of reporting sexual experiences and violations in online settings.

Together, these findings underscore the need for strategies to create safer online contexts that facilitate healthy exploration and support adolescents in navigating and being conscious of the specific risks and opportunities digital platforms have.
