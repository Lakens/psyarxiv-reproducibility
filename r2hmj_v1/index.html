Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Innovative and accessible strategies are needed to address mental health burden among university students. Nature-based interventions (NBIs), which involve therapeutic activities conducted in nature spaces, are emerging to promote student wellbeing on university campuses. This review examined the range of NBIs implemented within universities and their reported benefits using a systematic search and narrative synthesis methodology. Evidence was searched from database inception to August 2026, identifying twelve studies representing eleven trials across five countries. Interventions involved forest therapy (n=5), mindfulness-based programs (n=4) adapted for outdoor campus settings, and therapeutic horticulture interventions (n=3).

Evidence suggests that forest therapy is associated with reductions in stress and anxiety and improvements in mood, attention and overall wellbeing. Nature-integrated mindfulness programs were linked to reduced depressive symptoms, rumination, stress and anxiety, alongside enhanced wellbeing, mindfulness and connection to nature. Preliminary evidence suggests on campus therapeutic horticulture interventions may be associated with improvements in anxiety among university students and represent an accessible and affordable way to build nature and social connection. While findings are promising, evidence remains limited and of weak quality, highlighting the need for further robust research on implementation and sustainability within university contexts.

Introduction

Young people experience a disproportionate burden of mental ill-health, with the onset of many mental disorders occurring before the age of 25 years (Solmi et al., 2022), posing a significant threat to their developmental and productive life trajectories (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2025; McGorry et al., 2023). This burden has been acutely felt by universities, where high rates of mental disorders have been reported among students internationally (Auerbach et al., 2018; Osborn et al., 2022). In Australia, prevalence studies indicate university students experience disproportionately high rates of mental health concerns compared to the broader population (Stallman & Shochet, 2009; Teo et al., 2025). Consistent with this, in 2017 Orygen -the National Centre of Excellence in Youth Mental Health in Australia -reported that over half of students aged 16-25 were experiencing high levels of psychological distress, and more than one-third had reported thoughts of self-harm or suicide (Orygen, 2017). These problems have further escalated during and after the COVID-19 pandemic (Dingle et al., 2024).

Multiple factors contribute to elevated rates of stress and other mental health concerns among university students (Broglia et al., 2021). Many university students are navigating the transition from adolescence to adulthood, which typically involves balancing academic, social and financial demands (Lin & Huang, 2014; Logan & Burns, 2023; Zochil & Thorsteinsson, 2018). Some students also experience aspects of tertiary study as stressful, such as exams and other academic pressures, increased independent learning requirements, and forming new relationships with an academic community (Limone & Toto, 2022). Further, online learning has persisted in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic (Broadbent et al., 2023), yet many students report difficulties adjusting to virtual classes and feeling isolated and disconnected from the university environment (Akpen et al., 2024; Dingle et al., 2022). Certain student cohorts are particularly vulnerable to stress and anxiety, including international students (Maharaj et al., 2024) and those relocating from rural and remote areas (King et al., 2011; Mulder & Cashin, 2015).

Data suggest that student counselling services within university settings globally are experiencing unprecedented demand (Abrams, 2022; Broglia et al., 2021; Priestley et al., 2022), and in Australia, young people are presenting with more severe and complex needs (Andrews, 2019). Additionally, certain cohorts of students require support, yet do not helpseek, with stigma (Broglia et al., 2021; Cage et al., 2020) and the challenges of navigating university support services identified as key barriers (Universities UK, 2015). Research suggests there is particularly low help-seeking among male students and those with higher levels of depression (Zochil & Thorsteinsson, 2018). Moreover, qualitative accounts suggest that current university mental health and wellbeing services may not meet students' treatment preferences (Priestley et al., 2022).

In response, universities worldwide are pursuing whole-of-university approaches to promote student mental health and wellbeing (Canadian Association of College and University Student Services & Canadian Mental Health Association, 2013; Universities UK, 2015). In Australia, the University Mental Health Framework (Orygen, 2020) advocates a settings-based approach, embedding mental health and wellbeing initiatives across the whole university. This framework recognises that universities, while complex settings, offer opportunities to build holistic student health by enhancing connectedness, facilitating participation, and providing supports tailored to student needs and preferences. It also recognises that Australian university campus spaces can enhance mental health through safety and equity of access, sensory environments, and access to nature (Orygen, 2020).

Access and exposure to green spaces, including green spaces within and beyond university campuses, are associated with a range of mental health benefits for university students, including improved mood and quality of life, reduced stress and anxiety (Föllmer et al., 2021; He et al., 2024; Holt et al., 2019; Meredith et al., 2020; Reese et al., 2020; Zhu, et al., 2025). In a study of Chinese university students, perceived naturalness of the campus environment was positively associated with self-rated health and psychological restoration (Liu et al., 2018). Thus, nature spaces on campus (also known as 'academic greenspace'; Windhorst & Williams, 2015) may offer opportunities to deliver interventions that harness the mental health benefits of nature, such as nature-based interventions (NBIs).

Nature Based Interventions

NBIs promote health and wellbeing by engaging participants in semi-structured therapeutic activities in natural or green spaces (Shanahan et al., 2019), including forest bathing, ecotherapy, and therapeutic horticulture (Coventry et al., 2021). Reviews suggest that purposeful use of nature as a therapeutic agent benefits young people through improved connectedness, self-esteem, confidence, positive affect, stress reduction, and resilience (Cooley et al., 2020; Roberts et al., 2019). For example, a study among Australian university students found that participants that completed a three-week nature exposure intervention (n=42) experienced significant reductions in stress compared to a waitlist control group (Payne et al., 2020). Despite these potential benefits, green campus spaces remain underutilised globally as settings for the delivery of NBIs (Föllmer et al., 2021). Expanding NBIs within mainstream university services may be particularly relevant for student populations, as evidence suggests that young people are generally receptive to nature-based approaches for supporting wellbeing (Fernee et al., 2019). Additionally, NBIs support universities' sustainability goals, as they rely on the development and care of on-campus gardens, trails, and forested areas (Patton et al., 2020).

The Current Study

A key barrier to current NBI implementation is the limited understanding of the feasibility and effectiveness of different NBIs in university settings (Meredith et al., 2020). As such, this review aims to explore the breadth and types of NBIs that have been implemented within university campus spaces and examine their psychological wellbeing benefits for students. The findings will guide future nature-based campus initiatives that aim to support the wellbeing of university students and the development of sustainable campuses.

Methods

Design

This review was guided by the updated Cochrane recommendations for rapid reviews of effectiveness (Garritty et al., 2024). A rapid review is suitable for emerging research areas where understanding existing gaps and future research directions are of particular interest. A review protocol was developed and registered with PROSPERO (Zhou et al., 2023). A narrative synthesis of identified evidence was performed to answer the research question.

Findings were reported according to PRISMA guidelines (Page et al., 2021).

Search Strategy

A systematic and comprehensive search strategy was developed using Boolean logic (AND/OR) including a combination of key terms and database-specific subject headings (e.g. MESH terms) to identify studies examining the application of NBIs within university campus settings. The following electronic databases were searched: MEDLINE Complete (EBSCO), PsycINFO (EBSCO), Embase (OVID), CINAHL (EBSCO) and Cochrane Central (NMHRC). Search terms were organised into two major categories: NBIs and university populations (see Supplementary Materials A for full search strategy).

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined a priori according to the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study Design). The population of interest was enrolled university students. Interventions included NBIs delivered on general university campus grounds. Studies were required to report one or more psychological wellbeing outcomes. Studies of any design that were published in English were eligible for inclusion. Non-peer-reviewed studies and other grey literature were excluded.

Study selection

Searches were initially conducted in 2023 using Covidence (Version 2) and subsequently updated in 2025 and August 2026 to identify newly published literature. For the search in 2023, following the removal of duplicate records, two reviewers (CZ and NH) independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria, with disagreements resolved through discussion and, where necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (MOS).

Full-text articles were then retrieved and independently assessed for eligibility by the same two reviewers. The authors of three identified records were contacted to clarify aspects relevant to eligibility (e.g., whether studies used the same or different participant pools). Records identified through the 2025 and 2026 search updates were screened independently by HC and KG using the same eligibility criteria and review procedures and were incorporated into the review where eligible. For all searches, any disagreements were reviewed by MOS, who made the final determination regarding inclusion. Reference lists of key reviews (e.g. He et al., 2024) were also hand-searched to identify additional eligible studies not identified through the database search strategy. The review reflects evidence available up to August 2026.

Risk of Bias (Quality) Assessment

The Quality Appraisal Tool for Quantitative Studies, developed by the Effective Public Health Practice Project (Thomas et al., 2004), assessed study quality for all included studies.

Quality scores were assigned based on six categories: selection bias, study design, confounders, blinding, data collection methods, and withdrawals and dropouts. The assessment was performed by one reviewer (SM or KG) and verified by a second (MC or HC).

Data Extraction and Analysis

Data were extracted by one reviewer and checked for accuracy by a second reviewer according to the following study characteristics: (i) authors/date/country of publication, (ii) aims, (iii) study design, (iv) sample information, (v) intervention information, (vi) scales/measures used, (vii) outcomes and main findings, (viii) limitations and (ix) quality appraisal. Data were then examined according to the key research questions, with a focus on the types and wellbeing outcomes of NBIs being delivered on university campuses globally. A narrative synthesis was then undertaken to provide an integrated interpretation of the findings and guide future research and implementation.

Results

The search strategy yielded 1212 records after duplicates were removed. Following title and abstract screening, 67 studies were identified for full-text review, of which 56 were excluded based on the selection criteria. Main reasons for exclusion included a focus on nature exposure alone (including walking interventions without associated therapeutic activities), rather than the implementation of a prescribed NBI (e.g., Ballew & Omoto, 2018; Shrestha et al., 2021); an NBI being delivered for students off-campus (e.g., Djernis et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2024); the intervention being delivered in connection with a Forestry, rather than a general university campus (e.g., Yang et al., 2024); or due to ineligible study type (e.g., Vitagliano et al., 2023; protocol paper). A total of twelve studies were included in the final review. Two studies report on the same two trials within them (Wang et al., 2026a, b) and one study reports qualitative findings (Kim & Shin, 2021) from two individual trials (Kim et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2021), therefore representing eleven unique trials of NBIs on a university campus for enrolled students. Scope of NBIs Adopted on University Campuses for Student Wellbeing

Three primary NBIs were identified: (1) forest therapy or forest bathing (Kang & Shin, 2020; Kim et al., 2020; Kim & Shin, 2021; Kim et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2023); (2) mindfulnessbased intervention protocols undertaken in campus greenspaces (Ibes et al., 2018; Ibes & Forestell, 2020; Nisbet et al., 2019; Owens & Bunce, 2022); and (3) therapeutic horticulture (D'Andrea- Brooks et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b).

(1) Forest Therapy or Forest Bathing Forest therapy (also 'forest bathing' or 'shinrin-yoku') is a NBI that involves guided immersion in forested surroundings to promote mental and physical health (Kotte et al., 2019) and is a recognised evidence-based public health practice in Japan, South Korea and China (Hansen et al., 2017). Five studies from four unique trials, all conducted at Chungbuk National University in South Korea, examined the inclusion of forest therapy on campus greenspaces with the aim of promoting the wellbeing of university students. Guided (Kang & Shin, 2020; Kim et al., 2020) and self-guided (Kim & Shin, 2021; Shin et al., 2023) group-based forms of forest therapy were evaluated, with two studies examining the differential effects between them (Kim & Shin, 2021; Shin et al., 2023).

Across the studies, results indicated that participation in both group-guided and selfguided forest therapy programs led to a range of positive benefits compared to controls, including improved mood/positive emotion (Kim et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2021; Kim & Shin, 2021; Shin et al., 2023), physical and mental relaxation (Kim & Shin, 2021), subjective wellbeing (Kim et al., 2021), and attention and reflection (Kim & Shin, 2021), and reduced stress (Kang & Shin, 2020; Kim et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2021) and anxiety (Kim & Shin, 2021; Shin et al., 2023). Notably, one study, which compared qualitative findings from group and self-guided programs, suggested that group-guided forest therapy promoted more positive social and interpersonal benefits, while self-guided programs better enhanced mindful experiences (Kim & Shin, 2021).

(2) Mindfulness-based intervention protocols undertaken in nature Four studies examined mindfulness-based interventions in natural outdoor campus settings on university campuses across the US, UK and Canada. Across these, brief mindfulness and meditation practices were found to improve student wellbeing, mood, and stress outcomes when compared to controls. Owens and Bunce (2022) reported that self-guided meditation integrating elements of compassion-focused therapy, mental imagery, and forest bathing reduced depressive symptoms and rumination and enhanced wellbeing. Similarly, Nisbet et al. (2019) found that both mindfulness practice and nature exposure independently improved mood and nature connectedness. Finally, Ibes and Forestell (2020) demonstrated that spending time outdoors reduced mood disturbance overall, irrespective of whether students engaged with meditation or not; however, the greatest benefits were observed when meditation was combined with nature exposure. Of note, benefits were also evident for meditation conducted indoors. Complementing these findings, qualitative data from Ibes et al. (2018) indicated that brief campus-based mindfulness and nature activities were positively received and associated with stress relief, positive emotions, and sense of connection among students and staff. The on-campus greenspace therapeutic horticulture intervention included multi-sensory engagement with plants and plant materials, guided meditation and group discussions, and was compared with the same intervention conducted indoors, as well as off-campus forest therapy, and control groups. Therapeutic horticulture conducted in campus greenspace was associated with significant pre-post intervention reductions in stress and anxiety symptoms across both trials (Wang et al., 2026a, b). Whilst non-significant, the authors noted trending improvements across anxiety measures over multiple sessions, suggesting that repeated engagement may be associated with cumulative mental health benefits (Wang et al., 2026b).

Quality Appraisal

Studies were appraised using the Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies (Thomas et al., 2004), with all studies receiving a global rating of weak. The primary methodological limitations were consistent across studies: most relied on self-selected samples of university students and participation rates were rarely reported. While study design was typically rated as moderate, descriptions of randomisation were poorly detailed, and confounders were generally uncontrolled and not assessed at baseline. Given the nature of NBIs, participants were aware of their intervention status; outcome assessors were also typically not blinded to participants status (with the exception of Owens & Bunce, 2022). In contrast, data collection methods were a relative strength, with most studies employing validated and reliable instruments, such as the Profile of Mood States (McNair et al., 1981), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (Spielberger et al., 1983), or Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (Tennant et al., 2007), supported by Cronbach's alpha values typically above .80. Reporting of withdrawals and drop-outs was consistently rated as weak, with many studies failing to provide numbers and reasons for attrition.

Discussion

The World Health Organization (WHO, 2017) has emphasised that expanding the use of public greenspaces has the potential to play a vital role in promoting health and healing, and that universities are well placed to contribute through intentional campus design and programming. Nevertheless, academic greenspaces remain underused resources for promoting student mental health and wellbeing (Föllmer et al., 2021). This review aimed to identify and critically appraise existing evidence on the use of NBIs within university campus settings.

Although interest in campus greenspace NBIs is growing, we found that research in this area remains limited, with only twelve studies identified. Approximately half of these studies explored the benefits of on-campus forest therapy at a single university, while a small majority focused on mindfulness-based programs -traditionally delivered indoors -that were adapted for outdoor campus environments, and two studies assessed therapeutic horticulture interventions in campus garden spaces. Collectively, the findings suggest that NBIs may offer meaningful mental health benefits for university students, but further evidence is required to understand their feasibility and value within higher education settings.

Our review identified two primary forms of NBIs currently employed as part of university greenspace wellbeing initiatives, identifying these as potentially feasible approaches for future implementation: (1) forest therapy and (2) mindfulness-based interventions adapted for outdoor campus settings. The review also identified a potential for therapeutic horticultural activities to support university students in campus garden spaces, where mindfulness is identified as a key component (Pantiru et al., 2024). Overall, the findings suggest that students engaging in on-campus forest therapy reported significant reductions in stress and anxiety, alongside improvements in attention, mood and overall psychological wellbeing. This aligns with recent reviews that suggest that forest therapy may be particularly beneficial for young people (Pavlovic & Connolly, 2023), including university students (e.g., He et al., 2024).

Additionally, students engaging in nature-integrated mindfulness programs reported improvements in a range of outcomes, including reduced depressive symptoms, rumination, anxiety and stress, as well as enhanced wellbeing, energy, mindfulness and nature connection.

These findings reflect recent evidence demonstrating that mindfulness-based interventions can improve psychological wellbeing among university students and reduce levels of anxiety, depression and rumination (Dawson et al., 2020; González-Martín et al., 2023; Johnson et al., 2023).

Emerging evidence also suggests that mindfulness practices undertaken in nature environments may offer additional benefits beyond those associated with mindfulness alone (Djernis et al., 2019; Dong et al., 2025). Several theoretical frameworks are proposed for the enhanced benefits of mindfulness undertaken in nature settings (Lymeus et al., 2019; Macaulay et al., 2022). Most prominently, the Attention Restoration Theory (ART) posits that nature settings are inherently restorative because they offer distance from daily routine, psychological demands and distractions, while simultaneously engaging the mind through 'soft fascination'-a gentle, effortless form of present-moment attention (Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989).

Similarly, Shin et al. (2023) argued that restorative nature-based therapies on campus, such as forest therapy, have the potential to counter various challenges associated with urban living for university students, such as noise and air pollution, which are known to negatively affect mental health.

Future Directions

Despite suggesting the overall acceptability of campus-based NBIs to students, the included studies revealed a lack of detailed data on the key barriers and enablers to implementation and improving access to campus-based NBIs. For example, Ibes et al. (2018) emphasised the low cost and relatively simple installation of mindfulness interventions along campus trails, yet specific recommendations to feasibly implement NBIs and address logistical, financial or institutional challenges on campus were limited. Kim et al. (2021) stressed the need for universities to raise students' awareness of the psychological benefits of nature-based therapies, and D'Andrea- Brooks et al. (2025) suggest combining campus community gardens with university mental health programs to bolster the role of nature-based therapeutic activities in educational settings. Furthermore, Shin et al. (2023) suggested that self-guided forest therapy interventions may enhance accessibility for university students by addressing challenges associated with guided sessions, such as scheduling and resource constraints. This reflects evidence that students prefer interventions that take account of their schedules by being brief and accessible (Bamber & Schneider, 2020). Future research should consider the co-design of campus-based NBIs in partnership with students, noting that participatory approaches can improve acceptability and uptake by ensuring interventions are tailored to their needs and preferences (Hagen et al., 2025). Across the included studies, the duration of NBIs varied, ranging from brief single-session formats (one to 20 minutes) to longer duration multi-session interventions (up to 2 hours). Given the small number of studies, specific recommendations regarding the optimal length or duration of campus-based NBIs cannot yet be made.

Nevertheless, the overall acceptability of NBIs reported by students across all included studies encourages future examination of both their benefits and implementation factors that might support greater uptake within university settings and across more diverse groups of students. Notably, research also suggests that students may view interventions in natural settings as less stigmatising (Flies et al., 2023), thereby enhancing their engagement and benefits. Certain groups of students are known to be particularly poor at help-seeking, including male and international students (Sagar-Ouriaghli et al., 2020; Yee & Ryan, 2023; Zochil & Thorsteinsson, 2018). A better understanding of whether these cohorts, as well as other equity and diverse groups, may be more open to campus NBIs is also needed. Theoretically, NBI approaches draw on more holistic frameworks of wellbeing, such as the Social Emotional Wellbeing Framework (SEWB; Gee et al., 2014), which underpins the Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander conceptualisation of health. Indigenous frameworks for health such as the SEWB highlight connection to nature, or Country, as an inherent driver of wellbeing (Biles et al., 2024). As such, NBIs may also represent more culturally aligned approaches for Indigenous students, with the potential to address their lower rates of access to current university-based mental health support, warranting further attention.

Finally, there remains a general lack of understanding regarding the potential of NBIs to advance campus sustainability, despite their potential to promote environmental responsibility and engagement towards broader sustainability initiatives (D'Andrea- Brooks et al., 2025). Universities occupy a unique position to lead climate change mitigation and response efforts, through leveraging both their intellectual and practical resources (Barnett-Itzhaki et al., 2025), including the expansion and regeneration of nature campus spaces.

Evidence indicates that higher levels of nature connection are associated with increased proenvironmental behaviours, which in turn are positively linked to wellbeing (Sollis et al., 2025).

Consequently, the inclusion of NBIs within university mental health and wellbeing offerings not only encourages the creation and maintenance of inclusive and accessible nature spaces on campus but also has the potential to promote greater environmental awareness, encourage sustainable behaviours and alleviate climate change-related distress and anxiety among students (Wamsler et al., 2018; Windhorst & Williams, 2015). Given the rising rates of climate distress and anxiety among university students (Cameron & Kagee, 2025; Tavolacci & Ladner, 2024), an expansion of research into the role of NBIs within whole-of-university student mental health and wellbeing strategies is strongly recommended.

Limitations

While aimed at providing a comprehensive appraisal of the use of NBIs in the higher education sector, this review is limited by the number of studies identified. Included studies were concentrated within a small number of universities, with almost half conducted at a single institution in South Korea, thereby limiting the potential generalisability of findings. No studies were conducted in Australia. Additionally, the methodological quality of included studies was rated as weak overall, with a general lack of controlled studies published to date. Grey literature was not explored, which means that potentially other campus-based NBIs or relevant implementation recommendations were not identified. In addition, several studies that may offer notable perspectives were outside the scope of this review. For example, several studies have examined the outcomes of NBIs conducted in nature settings adjacent or nearby to, rather than within, university campuses (e.g., Djernis et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2024), while others have examined the psychological benefits of general exposure to on-campus green spaces but did not conduct a NBI so were not included (e.g., Shrestha et al., 2021).

Conclusion

The growing mental health burden among university students highlights the need for innovative, accessible wellbeing strategies. This review provides preliminary support for broadening greenspace initiatives -specifically forest therapy, nature-based mindfulness and therapeutic horticulture interventions -as part of a more comprehensive and holistic university mental health strategy. Situated within the university environment, these programs may be especially accessible to university students and account for their various academic, social and extracurricular demands. When delivered in campus settings, NBIs may also contribute to the environmental sustainability of universities and promote their roles in addressing the health challenges associated with urbanisation. Increased uptake of NBIs is called for, alongside future research that focuses on optimising their design, acceptability, feasibility and sustainability within the university context.
