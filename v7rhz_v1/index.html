How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Purpose. The short UPPS-P (SUPPS-P) is a widely used, gold-standard measure capturing five facets of impulsivity, including negative urgency, (lack of) premeditation, (lack of) perseverance, sensation seeking, and positive urgency. However, most validation studies have been conducted in relatively small convenience samples, few utilize multi-wave data, and no formal psychometric evaluation of the SUPPS-P has been conducted in a Danish sample. Procedures. Up to 2,745 adolescents (65.46% female, Mage=16.77 [SD=0.58] at baseline) completed the Danish SUPPS-P, as well as measures of substance use, self-efficacy, wellbeing, and internalizing symptoms at four timepoints over a 12-month period. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to evaluate fit of the established SUPPS-P five-factor structure and test measurement invariance across biological sex. Convergent, discriminant, and predictive validity; internal consistency; and test-retest reliability were evaluated for each subscale. Main Findings. Replicating past studies, a five-factor model best fit the data, but several items loaded weakly on the lack of perseverance and sensation seeking factors. Measurement invariance across biological sex was established. Positive correlations with substance use, negative correlations with self-efficacy and well-being, and subscale-specific correlations with internalizing symptoms supported convergent and discriminant validity. Correlations of baseline SUPPS-P scores with external variables up to 12 months later supported predictive validity. Internal consistency was acceptable for all subscales (ω=0.67-0.81) and moderate intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC=0.51-0.69) supported adequate testretest reliability. Conclusions. The SUPPS-P is suitable for assessing impulsivity in Danish adolescents over time, though further research is needed to optimize the measure's psychometric properties in this group.

1. Introduction

Impulsivity reflects a tendency to "overlook important information prior to making a decision, take unreasonably high risks, and opt for immediate rather than delayed payoffs in intertemporal choices" [1] and is related to a wide range of psychiatric conditions and health risk behaviors, including substance use [2]. The dominant model of impulsivity posits five distinct facets, including negative urgency, positive urgency, (lack of) premeditation, (lack of) perseverance, and sensation seeking [3, 4]. Negative and positive urgency reflect a propensity to act rashly under intense negative and positive affective states, respectively [5]. Lack of premeditation and lack of perseverance, conceptually categorized under deficits in conscientiousness, reflect the tendency to act without thinking and difficulties with remaining focused on a task, respectively [5, 6]. Sensation seeking reflects the tendency to seek out novel and exciting experiences [5]. A large literature supports the five-factor structure of impulsivity, with clearly differentiable but correlated facets comprising this behavioral construct [3, 4, 7].

The UPPS-P [4] is a widely used, gold-standard metric of impulsivity validated for use in individuals age 13 years and older. The measure includes 59 self-report items that capture the five theorized impulsivity subscales; the validated short form, the SUPPS-P, comprises 20 items capturing the same five domains [8]. The SUPPS-P has been validated in adolescent [9] [10] [11] [12] [13], young adult [8, 14], and adult samples [15, 16]; clinical [17, 18], student [8, 19, 20], and community-based samples [9, 12, 21, 22]; and Latin American [16, 18], European [12, [19] [20] [21] [23] [24] [25], East Asian [13, 26, 27], and Middle Eastern populations [28] [29] [30]. Though there are notable exceptions [9, 12, 16], a vast majority of validation studies, including the original SUPPS-P validation studies [8, 19, 20] and studies validating translations of the SUPPS-P, are relatively small and cross-sectional (N=188-743) [11, 21, 22, 24, 28, 31]. Several of these studies have explored convergent and discriminant validity with substance use and psychopathology variables [8, 19, 22, 31], though only small subset has leveraged longitudinal data to evaluate test-retest reliability [19, 26, 30]. Further, most validation studies are conducted in convenience samples of college students [8, 15, 19, 20, 27] or other groups that have specifically opted in to or sought out participation in a research study [9, 21, 22, 29]. This selfselection of study participants naturally results in samples that tend to report higher levels of agreeableness and conscientiousness [32], as well as higher levels of health-promotive behavior at baseline [33] -traits often negatively associated with impulsivity and propensity for engaging in risky behavior. Evaluating the psychometric properties of the SUPPS-P in a realworld sample of participants who did not actively seek out participation in a research study on health behaviors would aid in establishing its validity in a more representative population.

Recent large-scale psychometric research on the SUPPS-P has called for further validation studies on translations of the measure [16]. Notably, research on the SUPPS-P in Scandinavian samples is limited. There has been only one Scandinavian validation study to date, conducted in a sample sample of 343 Swedish young adults [31]. Further, only one small study (N=109) has examined impulsivity in Danish adolescents within the UPPS-P framework [34], and no formal psychometric evaluation of the validity or reliability of this measure has been conducted in a Danish sample. In fact, Danish is not yet counted among the 26 languages to which the SUPPS-P has been formally translated or the 13 languages in which it has been formally validated [16].

1.1 Present Study

As the SUPPS-P is one of the most widely used measures of impulsivity and is predictive of a wide range of psychiatric outcomes, it is important to comprehensively establish its psychometric properties in large samples, multi-wave data, and real-world settings.

Leveraging data from a large-scale, longitudinal study of substance use in Danish high school students [35], this study aimed to conduct a psychometric evaluation of the Danish translation of the SUPPS-P, including evaluation of factor structure; measurement invariance across biological sex; convergent, discriminant, and predictive validity; and internal consistency and test-retest reliability. In line with a broad literature, we anticipated that these data would reflect the established five-factor structure of the SUPPS-P [8]; that established measurement invariance across sex would replicate in this sample [9, 16]; that SUPPS-P subscales would be positively correlated with substance use, negatively correlated with self-efficacy and mental well-being, and that negative urgency, positive urgency, and lack of perseverance would be correlated with internalizing symptoms [19, 36] while sensation seeking would be uncorrelated or negatively correlated with internalizing symptoms [22, 37]; that this pattern of association would hold between SUPPS-P subscales as measured at baseline and external variables as measured up to 12 months later; and that the SUPPS-P would demonstrate acceptable internal consistency and test-retest reliability over the course of the 12-month study period.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Participants and Procedures

Data were drawn from a parent substance use intervention study in Danish high school students [35, 38]. The original trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID NCT06018389) and the Danish Data Protection Agency. Institutional review board (IRB) approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee at Aarhus University, Denmark (ID BSS-2023-039).

In spring 2023, 64 public high schools were invited to participate in the study via email.

Sixteen schools from 13 Danish cities agreed to participate. Eligibility criteria for students included being an incoming first-year student in August 2023, at least 15 years of age, and able to understand and speak Danish. As the age of consent for research participation in Denmark is 15, students were able to independently consent to participation. Students and their parents/guardians received written information about the study at least one week before the study team visited campuses to conduct enrollment procedures. Information included that participation was voluntary and that students had the right to withdraw at any time. Consent was provided ahead of survey administration. Surveys were completed on-site in schools during class periods at five waves (baseline, 2-, 6-, 9-, and 12-months) with members of the research team present at each survey administration. The present study included 2,745 students who provided SUPPS-P data at baseline and participated in at least one of the follow-ups.

2.2 Measures

2.2.1 Short UPPS-P (SUPPS-P) Impulsive Behavior Scale

The English version of the 20-item SUPPS-P [8] was used as the basis for translation to Danish. Two native Danish speakers fluent in English independently translated all items into Danish, discussed the translations, and came to agreement. Danish items were then backtranslated by a native English and Danish speaker. Two senior team members then finalized the translation. The final version of the Danish SUPPS-P is available upon request.

Items were rated on a 1 (agree strongly) to 4 (disagree strongly) scale and mean scores were calculated for each subscale. Where indicated, items were recoded such that higher scores reflect higher impulsivity. The Danish SUPPS-P was administered at baseline and 6-, 9-, and 12-month follow-ups.

2.2.2 Substance Use

2.2.2.1 Alcohol Use.

Frequency of intoxication in the past 30 days was assessed via a single item querying "How many times how you gotten drunk from drinking alcohol within the past 30 days?" Respondents could select options ranging from 0-30. Students who reported no alcohol use in the past 30 days were coded as having 0 drunk days. Number of binge drinking days, maximum drinks per drinking occasion, and total drinks in the past 30 days were assessed using the Timeline Follow-Back (TLFB) (32), a calendar-based assessment that prompts recall of substance use patterns by referencing dates, weekends, and notable events (e.g., semester start or holidays). The TLFB is the gold standard for assessing quantity and frequency of substance use in the adolescent age group, with the computer-based version demonstrating comparable reliability to interview-based formats [39, 40].

2.2.2.2 Nicotine Use.

Among students who endorsed past 30 day nicotine use, frequency of smoking cigarettes and frequency of vaping in the past 30 days were assessed via single items querying "Think about the last 30 days. How many days have you: smoked cigarettes?" and "Think about the last 30 days. How many days have you: used e-cigarettes/vape/puff bars?" Respondents could select options ranging from 1-30. Students who did not endorse nicotine use in the past 30 days were coded as having 0 use days.

2.2.3 Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy was measured via three items from the World Health Organization Health

Behavior in School-age Children survey. Questions included "How often can you find a solution to problems if you try hard enough?", "How often can you achieve what you set your mind to?", and "How often do you succeed in learning what you want at school?" Items were rated on a 1 (always) to 5 (never) scale.

Items were reverse coded such that higher scores reflect higher self-efficacy, and a mean score was subsequently derived from the three items.

2.2.4 Mental Well-Being

Mental well-being was measured via the World Health Organization Well-Being Index, a 5-item measure assessing frequency of feeling cheerful and in good spirits, calm and relaxed, active and vigorous, refreshed and rested after waking, and as though daily life has been filled with things of interest over the past two weeks. Items were rated on a 0 (at no time) to 5 (all the time) scale and summed to create a total score.

2.2.5 Internalizing Symptoms

Internalizing (depression and anxiety) symptoms were measured via the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-4, a 4-item measure consisting of a 2-item depression scale and a 2-item anxiety scale [41]. Frequency of symptoms over the past two weeks are rated on a 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) scale. Items were summed to create a total symptom score.

2.3 Analytic Plan

Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 [42] and Mplus version 9 [43]. Inter-item correlations for all 20 SUPPS-P items and item-total correlations within each SUPPS-P subscale were estimated using baseline data. To examine whether the established factor structure of the SUPPS-P replicates in this sample of Danish adolescents, four confirmatory factor models were fit using data from the baseline measurement occasion. This sequence of models mirrored that in the English SUPPS-P validation study [8]: 1) a one-factor model, wherein all 20 SUPPS-P items load onto a single "impulsivity" factor, 2) a five-factor model, with one factor corresponding to each subscale and freely estimated intercorrelations between factors, 3) a higher-order five-factor model, with one factor corresponding to each subscale and a higher-order "impulsivity" factor, and 4) a three-factor hierarchical model, with negative and positive urgency as lower-order factors subsumed under a higher-order "emotion-based rash action" factor and lack of perseverance and premeditation as lower-order factors subsumed under a higher-order "deficits in conscientiousness" factor. As the SUPPS-P items are measured on an ordinal scale, all models were estimated using weighted least squared mean variance (WLSMV) with robust standard errors to account for clustering within schools.

Evaluation of model fit was based on the comparative fit index (CFI) [44], the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) [45], the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) [46], and the root meansquare error of approximation (RMSEA) [47]. Assessment of model fit was based on accepted cutoffs in the literature: CFI ≥ .95 (good fit) or ≥ .90 (acceptable fit), TLI ≥ .95 (good fit) or ≥ .90 (acceptable fit), SRMR ≤ .06 (good fit) or ≤ .08 (acceptable fit), and RMSEA ≤ .06 (good fit) or ≤ .08 (acceptable fit) [46, [48] [49] [50] [51]. The chi-square ( 2 ) statistic is reported per convention, but models were not rejected on the basis of a significant  2 due to its sensitivity to sample size [51].

Configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance across biological sex were evaluated within the best-fit factor model. Configural invariance tests whether the factor structure holds across groups and imposes no equality constraints on parameters. Fit of the configural model was evaluated by the same criteria as the confirmatory models described above. Metric invariance tests whether factor loadings are equal across groups by constraining loadings to equality across groups and evaluating deterioration in model fit compared to the unconstrained configural model. Scalar invariance tests whether factor means are equal across groups by further adding equality constraints to item intercepts and evaluating deterioration in model fit compared to the metric model. Strict invariance tests whether residual variances are equal across groups by further adding equality constraints to residual variances and evaluating deterioration in model fit compared to the scalar model. Comparison of these nested models was conducted using the difftest function within Mplus, which performs a corrected  2 difference test that is appropriate for application to models for which the reported  2 is mean and variance adjusted. Due to the corrected  2 test's sensitivity to sample size, additional criteria of Δ CFI of ≤0.010, Δ TLI of ≤0.010 (where a decrease beyond this threshold would suggest deterioration of fit), Δ SRMR of ≥0.030, and Δ RMSEA of ≥0.015 (where an increase beyond this threshold would suggest deterioration of model fit) were used as the primary metrics by which to compare models [16, [52] [53] [54]. Unpaired t-tests were used to evaluate mean differences of each subscale across males and females at each timepoint. Spearman rank correlations between SUPPS-P subscales and external correlates (substance use, self-efficacy, mental well-being, internalizing symptoms) were estimated using baseline data to evaluate convergent and discriminant validity. Predictive validity was evaluated by examining correlations between the SUPPS-P subscales at baseline and substance use, self-efficacy, mental well-being, and internalizing symptoms at 6-, 9-, and 12-month follow-ups.

Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω were calculated to evaluate internal consistency of each SUPPS-P subscale at each measurement occasion. Test-retest reliability was evaluated via intraclass correlations (ICCs) between SUPPS-P subscale scores across all measurement occasions, as well as pairwise ICCs for baseline and each follow-up occasion. ICCs were estimated using multinomial mixed effect models with random intercepts to account for repeated measures within students and school-level clustering. Evaluation of test-retest reliability was based on established thresholds for ICCs wherein <0.50 is poor, 0.50-0.75 is moderate, 0.75-0.90 is good, and >0.90 is excellent [55].

Reliabilities for all subscales met or exceeded acceptable thresholds at 6-month follow-up (ω=0.72-0.83), 9-month follow-up (ω=0.75-0.85), and 12-month follow-up (ω=0.73-0.88). Test-retest reliability was evaluated via ICCs for each SUPPS-P subscale, captured by both an overall ICC across timepoints and pairwise ICCs between baseline and each follow-up. Overall ICCs indicated moderate reliability for all scales (ICC=0.51-0.69). Pairwise ICCs between baseline and each follow-up similarly showed moderate reliability for negative urgency (ICC=0.48-0.51), lack of premeditation (ICC=0.55-0.61), sensation seeking (ICC=0.65-0.66), and positive urgency (ICC=0.52-0.57); lack of perseverance again showed borderline poor reliability (ICC=0.46-0.48). As expected, ICCs showed small decreases at more distal timepoints.

4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the most comprehensive validation studies of the Short UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale in an adolescent sample, and the first to validate the Danish version of SUPPS-P. This study was strengthened by the large sample size and multi-wave design, permitting robust estimation and evaluation of psychometric properties of the SUPPS-P over time. In line with a large literature on translations of the SUPPS-P [8, 12, 16, 19-21, 23-26, 28, 31], the data supported a five-factor structure with established measurement invariance across biological sex. Patterns of correlation with substance use, self-efficacy, mental wellbeing, and internalizing symptoms supported convergent and discriminant validity. Correlations of baseline SUPPS-P scores with these external variables measured up to 12 months later supported predictive validity. Internal consistency was generally acceptable across subscales and moderate intraclass correlation coefficients supported acceptable test-retest reliability.

Though the results presented here support the five-factor structure of the SUPPS-P, attention to the performance of the lack of perseverance scale in this sample is warranted.

Specifically, lack of perseverance had the weakest item-total correlations, several weak factor loadings, the weakest relationships with external validators (and did not correlate with internalizing symptoms, as it does in a vast majority of validation studies) [13, 18, 21, 26, 27], and the lowest internal consistency and test-retest reliability. This pattern is notable as, by many established thresholds, the lack of perseverance subscale performed at or below borderline acceptability in present sample. There is some evidence that the performance of this subscale can be worse in adolescents [57], which is likely attributable to executive functioning, cognitive control, and capacity for sustained attention still being in development during this stage [58] [59] [60], but a handful of other studies of SUPPS-P translations have shown that the lack of perseverance subscale also performed more poorly than the others in young adult and adult samples [13, 31]. In a validation study of the Chinese SUPPS-P, this was attributed to psycholinguistic factors that subtly altered the meaning of a particular item ("I finish what I start") [13]; however, this item performed adequately in capturing the lack of perseverance factor in the present study. In a validation study of the Swedish SUPPS-P, relatively poor reliability of the lack of perseverance scale was attributed to differences in sample characteristics (non-students) compared to the validation study of the English SUPPS-P (undergraduate students) [31]. Given that the present study was conducted in a sample of students, the non-student nature of the Swedish sample cannot explain the relatively poor performance of the lack of perseverance subscale.

It is notable that one of the few other validation studies of a SUPPS-P translation that observed low reliability for the lack of perseverance subscale is that in another Scandinavian sample, and that this study was also the only other to find no association between lack of perseverance and internalizing symptoms [31]. This may suggest that there is a broader cultural influence impacting how lack of perseverance manifests in Scandinavian populations and/or how these items are interpreted and understood in this cultural context. The SUPPS-P items capturing lack of perseverance focus on emotional discomfort with incomplete tasks (e.g., "unfinished tasks really bother me") and behavioral persistence toward task completion (e.g., "I finish what I start"); notably, the two emotion-based items evidenced weak loadings (λ=0.252-0.490), while the behavior-based items performed well (λ=0.657-0.859). In a culture that places strong emphasis on work-life balance, and in which it is generally acceptable to leave a task unfinished in favor of maintaining such balance and personal well-being [61], aversive emotional experiences related to incomplete tasks may be less common and less relevant to capturing (lack of) perseverance than more behaviorally-oriented indicators. Indeed, replacing the two poorly-performing emotion-based items with two items from the self-efficacy measure that conceptually reflect behavior-based perseverance ("How often can you find a solution to problems if you try hard enough?" ; "How often can you achieve what you set your mind to?") significantly improved model fit ( 2 (160)=1283.792, p<.0001; CFI=0.917; TLI=0.901; SRMR=0.067; RMSEA=0.051 [0.048, 0.053]); these items also had much stronger loadings (λ=0.670-0.713). As the SUPPS-P items were originally developed within the American cultural context, which is extremely work-driven and places significant social and emotional value on productivity [62], it is possible that this cultural contrast may contribute to a "disconnect" with the emotion-based lack of perseverance items when applied to a Danish population [61].

Several items on the sensation seeking factor also evidenced relatively weak loadings, though this pattern appears to be attributable to the age of the sample rather than potentially salient differences in the functioning of the items in Scandinavian culture. The "I would like to learn to fly an airplane" item has been shown to perform poorly in adolescents, attributed to learning to fly an airplane potentially being of less interest to youth than it was when these items were originally developed [57]. This item was derived from the Sensation Seeking Scale published in 1964 [63], when air travel was likely seen as more novel and exciting than it is in today's society, in which flying is far more routine and accessible. Further, recent research has argued that activity-based items are generally poor indicators of sensation seeking and suggests that they should be discarded entirely [64]. In line with this, the factor loading for the other activity-based SUPPS-P item ("I would enjoy the sensation of skiing very fast down a high mountain slope") was only slightly stronger than that for the "I would like to learn to fly an airplane" item (λ=0.550 vs. λ=0.452) and was substantially weaker than the loading for the "I quite enjoy taking risks" item (λ=0.795). The item "I welcome new and exciting experiences and sensations, even if they are a little frightening and unconventional" (λ=0.486) performs well in adolescent samples when the phrasing has been modified to be more developmentally appropriate ("I like new, thrilling things, even if they are a little scary") [9, 57], but similarly performs poorly compared to other items when the phrasing of the original SUPPS-P is retained [23]. However, given that the loadings for these items were borderline acceptable by some recommended thresholds [56], it is arguable whether the scale should be modified from the existing version that is widely used and very well-established. However, development of an updated version with examples more relevant to contemporary society coupled with modified phrasing more appropriate for adolescents may be viable ways to address these potential shortcomings. Further studies in novel samples will be required to determine if the patterns observed here are idiosyncratic to these data, or if these particular items require modification to more precisely measure sensation seeking in Danish adolescents.

4.1 Limitations

These findings should be interpreted in light of limitations. First, this sample was drawn from a high school-based study. As such, students who dropped out or opted to attend vocational school are not captured here. Second, although a member of the study team was present for all survey administrations, it is possible that the classroom environment in which the survey was administered impacted how students responded to the survey. Third, phrasing from the adult SUPPS-P was retained, which may have impacted the performance of some items in this adolescent sample, as described above. Finally, measures within the survey were not randomized, such that positioning of the SUPPS-P within the larger survey may have impacted how students responded to questionnaire.

4.2 Conclusions

This study presents initial validation of the Danish version of the Short UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale [8], supporting its suitabililty for assessing impulsivity in adolescents.

Although the best-fit model identified here was consistent with a large literature supporting a five-factor solution and the SUPPS-P showed acceptable validity and reliability by several metrics, results also indicated that this measure could be further optimized to capture impulsivity in Scandinavian adolescents. Integrating cultural considerations and modifying the language of some items in the Danish SUPPS-P to be more developmentally appropriate for the adolescent age group are two viable avenues to futher improve the performance of this measure.
