Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract

Infant visual attention-most often operationalized through looking behavior-is a foundational method and explanatory construct in the study of early cognition. Cultural psychology has established that attention is organized by culturally structured practices and localized meanings, but this work has focused largely on children and adults, and the extent to which culturally patterned experience shapes the endogenous attentional processes that looking measures are used to index in infancy remains under-specified. This review focuses on the first two years of life and on four components of endogenous attention that are routinely inferred from looking behavior: focal versus contextual allocation, orienting speed versus accuracy, novelty versus familiarity orientation, and persistence versus flexibility. We distinguish cultureshared meanings, practices, and organized forms of everyday activity-from demographic indicators such as nationality, language background, race, and socioeconomic status, which are coarse proxies for proximal experience. Synthesizing cross-national studies alongside research on multilingual, interracial, and socioeconomic experience, we show that components of infant attention vary with experience and task context in ways that do not map cleanly onto reified group categories. We identify a gap between research documenting effects of specific experiences and research examining culture directly, and we propose a culturally situated model in which endogenous attentional mechanisms are calibrated through culturally patterned input across development. This approach preserves the value of looking-time and eye-tracking methods while strengthening their construct validity, measurement invariance, and generalizability. Public significance statement: Infants' looking behavior is widely used to understand early learning, memory, and perception. This paper shows that visual attention is also shaped by culturally patterned experiences, including language exposure, social diversity, and socioeconomic context. Recognizing these influences can help developmental scientists interpret infants' behavior more accurately, avoid treating differences as deficits, and build theories and methods that better represent children growing up in diverse environments.

Sociocultural Determinants of Infant Visual Attention

Infant attention-most often operationalized through looking behavior-is a central explanatory construct for a wide range of early mental processes, from perception and learning to memory, expectation formation, and emerging social cognition (Aslin, 2007; Colombo, 2001; Fantz, 1956; Oakes, 2023).

Because infants cannot reliably report what they know, prefer, or predict, researchers have long treated systematic variation in visual attention (e.g., fixation, dwell time, saccades, disengagement) as an inferential lens into a range of latent mental processes (for a recent review, see Hunter et al. 2025). Psychological processes operationalized by visual attention measures have been interpreted as catalysts for learning (Oakes, 2023) and neurodevelopment (Johnson, 1990). Over the last 50 years, infant looking time has emerged as the methodological backbone of infant psychological development.

A longstanding reliance on infant visual attention has often rested on a particular interpretive assumption. This assumption is not that experience is irrelevant, but that looking behavior can be treated as sufficiently comparable across infants and samples to support broad developmental inferences unless a specific experiential factor is under investigation. The assumption is especially consequential when looking measures are used to characterize developmental change, compare populations, or extrapolate from restricted samples to infants universally. Indeed, infant research has often presumed that early behavioral responses provide insight into species-typical or universal psychological processes because infants are viewed as having relatively little prior experience (Singh, 2026, in press). Yet, as Haith (1998) astutely observed, by three months of age an infant has already accumulated approximately 800 hours of waking experience and made between three and six million eye movements, providing extensive opportunity for visual learning and adaptation. This experience is also culturally organized from the outset: infants encounter variation in linguistic input, caregiving routines, nutrition, and social and physical affordances that is structured by family life, institutions, and broader cultural practices, and such patterned conditions shape development even before birth (Singh, in press). Infants are therefore never truly "acultural," and the visual attention they display may reflect culturally situated experience rather than an experience-independent starting point.

We do not claim that prior work has ignored experience-dependent attention. Rather, we identify an interpretive default in which contextual influences remain peripheral unless they are explicitly measured or theorized. Assessing the validity of this assumption is further complicated by evidence from meta-analyses showing that even basic demographic and contextual information-including where a study was conducted-is often incompletely reported in infant research (Singh, Cristia, et al., 2023; Singh & Rajendra, 2024). Such omissions make it difficult to situate looking measures within infants' developmental contexts, evaluate assumptions of comparability and universality, and determine how sampling characteristics may shape the interpretation and generalizability of findings.

Foundational work has long shown that perceptual experience, stimulus familiarity, maturation, and task demands shape infant looking behavior (e.g., Colombo, 2001; Fantz, 1964; Johnson, 1990; Kidd & Hayden, 2015). Cultural psychology has, in parallel, established that attention is organized by culturally structured practices and meanings. In adults, East Asian participants attend more to context and to relations among elements, whereas North American participants attend more to focal objects (Chua et al., 2005; Kitayama et al., 2003; Masuda & Nisbett, 2001; Nisbett et al., 2001). In childhood, comparable differences have been reported in relational versus object-based matching and in object recognition (Kuwabara & Smith, 2012 , 2016), in children's attention to focal objects versus backgrounds and its alignment with parents' culturally dominant modes of attention (Senzaki et al., 2016; Senzaki & Shimizu, 2022), and in how children manage attention to multiple ongoing events (Chavajay & Rogoff, 1999; Correa-Chávez & Rogoff, 2009; see also Brown, 2011; Rogoff, 2003). The claim that culturally patterned environments shape attentional development is therefore not new.

The present review makes three contributions that this literature leaves open. First, it focuses on infancy. Most cross-cultural research on attention has studied children from the preschool years onward, as well as adults, often using verbal, categorization, or free-viewing tasks. Whether culturally patterned differences in attention are already present in the first two years before children produce the descriptions and narratives that reveal cultural modes of attention later in development (Senzaki et al., 2014 (Senzaki et al., , 2016) ) has only recently become an empirical question (e.g., Heise et al., 2025; Köster et al., 2023; Tu et al., 2025; Waxman et al., 2016). Second, it focuses on endogenous attention processes, and specifically on the the components of attentional control that looking-time paradigms are designed to index, rather than on broad cognitive styles. Third, it focuses on measurement. Because looking behavior is the primary dependent variable from which infant learning, memory, and expectation are inferred, culturally patterned variation in its components has direct consequences for how infant findings are interpreted, compared, and generalized. What remains less developed, and what we aim to provide, is a component-level account of how sociocultural experience interacts with endogenous attentional systems to shape these dependent measures.

We use endogenous attention to refer to the internally guided control of attention-selecting, sustaining, and shifting attention on the basis of the infant's expectations, goals, and prior learning-as distinct from exogenous attention, in which attention is captured by factors such as the physical salience of stimuli (Colombo, 2001; Colombo & Cheatham, 2006). Across the first year, attention shifts from predominantly exogenous, stimulus-driven orienting toward increasing endogenous control, supported by the maturation of cortical attention networks (Johnson, 1990; Posner & Rothbart, 2007). Endogenous attention is therefore the level at which accumulated experience would be expected have an impact. We concentrate on four endogenous components that are routinely measured with looking-time and eyetracking paradigms: (a) the allocation of attention between focal objects and their context; (b) the control of orienting and disengagement; (c) novelty versus familiarity orientation; and (d) persistence versus flexibility in sustaining or redistributing attention. Studies are reviewed when they inform one or more of these components.

Our scope is intentionally circumscribed. We focus on these four components in infants from birth to approximately 24 months, rather than on every cognitive process inferred from looking time or on specialized social-attention processes such as gaze following. We also do not claim that all looking or eye-tracking measures are equally susceptible to sociocultural variation; some measures appear robust across markedly different contexts (Gredebäck et al., 2025). Nor do the concerns we raise apply only to cross-group comparisons. Studies of a single cultural group are also conducted within a particular cultural ecology, and the same interpretive questions arise whenever findings from that group are generalized to infants more broadly or treated as evidence of universal processes (Nielsen et al., 2017; Singh, 2026).

Within-participant designs can control for stable experiential differences among infants in a sample, which can make cultural dependency less consequential for these designs, but they do not establish that the task engages the same construct, or yields effects of the same magnitude, in infants raised under different conditions. Cultural dependency becomes most critical when researchers compare groups, pool samples, interpret baseline looking differences, test generalizability, or assume that a task engages the same construct across sociocultural contexts. And without basic demographic data, it is difficult to evaluate any of these possibilities.

We do not argue that infant attention is primarily exogenous or that culturally patterned experience replaces endogenous theories of attentional development. Rather, we adopt an interactionist position. Contemporary accounts already conceptualize alerting, orienting, and executive attention as biologically constrained systems that develop through continuous interaction with experience (e.g., Colombo & Cheatham, 2006; Posner & Rothbart, 2007). Our central question is therefore not whether attention is endogenous or experience-dependent, but how endogenous attentional systems become calibrated through culturally patterned environments across development and how these interactions can form a part of theory.

Across the literatures reviewed below, we use the four components defined above to interpret sociocultural variation in attentional allocation: (a) focal versus contextual processing, (b) speed versus accuracy, (c) novelty versus familiarity orientation, and (d) persistence versus flexibility. We first review cross-national studies, which most directly address culture but measure it coarsely. We then review, more briefly, research on multilingual, interracial, and socioeconomic experience. These literatures do not study culture as such. They are included because each isolates a dimension of proximal experience-variability of linguistic input, social diversity, and environmental predictability-that is often bundled with culture in cross-national comparisons. Our coverage is also necessarily constrained by how the primary studies have operationalized culture: almost exclusively through demographic proxies such as country of residence, language background, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, rather than through direct measures of the shared values, beliefs, socialization goals, caregiving practices, and everyday routines that constitute culture. We then consider the gap between these two bodies of work, synthesize the findings at the level of attentional components, and propose a culturally situated model of infant visual attention with practical implications for both large collaborative studies and typical single-laboratory designs.

A component-level framework for sociocultural variation in infant visual attention

Culture has been conceptualized in several ways, and the choice of definition shapes what counts as evidence of cultural influence. Symbolic and interpretive approaches define culture as shared systems of meaning through which people interpret experience (Bruner, 1990; Geertz, 1973). Cultural psychology emphasizes that culture and mind are mutually constituted, such that psychological processes take shape through participation in meaningful practices (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Shweder, 1991). Sociocultural approaches to developmentl locate culture in participation in organized, routine activities, including the ways in which caregivers structure and guide children's attention (Chavajay & Rogoff, 1999; Rogoff, 2003). Ecological and sociodemographic approaches emphasize that cultural values and learning environments shift with conditions such as urbanization, formal schooling, and economic change (Greenfield, 2009). Research on cognition and perception has characterized cultural variation in terms of independent versus interdependent models of the self and analytic versus holistic systems of thought (Kitayama & Uskul, 2011; Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Nisbett et al., 2001). For infancy specifically, the developmental niche framework identifies three subsystems through which culture reaches the developing child: the physical and social settings of daily life, culturally regulated customs of childcare, and the psychology of caregivers (Super & Harkness, 1986; see also Keller, 2007).

Building on these perspectives, we use culture to refer to shared meanings, practices, values, interactional routines, and organized forms of everyday experience. Nationality, language background, race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status are not equivalent to culture. They are best understood as coarse indicators that covary with culturally organized experience, such as caregiving routines, linguistic variability, social density, and environmental predictability, but also with factors that are not cultural in this sense, such as material resources and health. The developmental niche framework is particularly relevant here because each of its subsystems can be translated into measurable proximal variables. An important empirical charge is to move from broad indicators toward more precise measures of the proximal experiences that plausibly tune attention, and to be explicit about whether a given study examines culture or a correlate of it.

Cross-national comparison studies on infant visual attention

Cross-national comparisons provide one window onto early attentional variation, but they should not be interpreted as direct tests of culture by themselves. National context is a broad proxy that can covary with caregiver practices, stimulus familiarity, urbanicity, language input, testing context, and analytic decisions. Cross-national designs also face well-documented methodological limitations. They confound culture with many co-occurring differences, often treat variation within countries as error variance, typically sample only two populations so that any difference has many possible sources, and can mistake differences in how a measure functions in context for differences in the construct it is meant to index (Matsumoto & Yoo, 2006; van de Vijver & Leung, 1997; see also Henrich et al., 2010; Nielsen et al., 2017). We therefore use cross-national studies not to argue that nations impose fixed attentional styles, but to illustrate how culturally patterned environments may be associated with separable components of attention.

Historically, much of this work draws on the distinction between analytic and holistic cognition. In Nisbett and colleagues' account, analytic thought involves detaching objects from their context, attending to their attributes, and reasoning about them with rules, whereas holistic thought involves attending to the field as a whole and to relations among its elements (Nisbett et al., 2001). Object-versus background-oriented attention is a narrower construct. It refers to the relative allocation of attention to focal objects versus their surrounding context, typically measured as fixation time to foreground versus background regions (Chua et al., 2005; Masuda & Nisbett, 2001). The two are related but not equivalent: holistic processing also concerns how objects are integrated with their context and with one another, which allocation measures do not capture directly. A higher proportion of looking to the background could therefore reflect contextual integration, but it could also reflect weaker engagement with the focal object or broader scanning without integration (see Tu et al., 2025).

In childhood, cross-cultural differences have been reported in relational versus object-based matching (Kuwabara & Smith, 2012), configural versus feature-based object recognition (Kuwabara & Smith, 2016), and attention to focal objects versus backgrounds during scene description, together with evidence that parents communicate culturally dominant modes of attention to their children (Senzaki et al., 2016; Senzaki & Shimizu, 2022). Cross-linguistic work also suggests early differences in how young children construe objects and substances (Imai & Gentner, 1997). Cross-linguistic effects on attention are not uniform, however. For example, Hagihara et al. (2026) found that Japanese-and English-speaking 2-to 5-year-olds largely interpreted heard action verbs in terms of similarly narrow action categories, regardless of how broadly or narrowly each language's verbs apply. The authors suggest that early verb learning builds on prior perception-action experience and that cross-linguistic effects may emerge later in development.

Notably, some cultural differences in older participants emerge only when a task requires verbal description or narrative, and not during passive viewing (Senzaki et al., 2014 (Senzaki et al., , 2016)). This raises the question of whether differences in attentional allocation can be observed in infancy, when looking-based measures are the primary window onto attention.

Recent cross-national eye-tracking work has investigated variation in foreground versus background processing, particularly in comparisons involving Eastern and Western contexts. Heise et al. (2025), for example, found that 15-month-olds in Japan devoted relatively more attention to background information than infants in the United States when viewing both faces and cartoon scenes, with the difference in the cartoon task most pronounced for background objects with eyes. In a separate bookreading task, Japanese caregivers verbally referred to background details more often than U.S. caregivers, although caregivers' context sensitivity was not correlated with their infants' looking, paralleling evidence from older children that parents communicate culturally dominant modes of attention (Senzaki et al., 2016). Waxman et al. (2016) similarly reported that 24-month-olds in the United States and China attended to dynamic scenes in largely similar ways. Their attention diverged only briefly, during the initial baseline viewing of the test scenes and not in the subsequent response period: infants in the United States looked relatively more to the scene in which the object had changed, whereas infants in China looked relatively more to the scene in which the action had changed. At the same time, stimulus familiarity must be treated as a methodological and theoretical moderator. Jurkat et al. (2020) showed that in older children, cultural differences in context-sensitive attention varied depending on whether stimuli were familiar or unfamiliar to participants, underscoring that cross-cultural effects can be amplified, reduced, or reversed by stimulus selection.

Complementing this behavioral evidence, Köster et al. (2023) provide converging neurophysiological support from the first year of life: using EEG frequency tagging with 12-month-olds in Austria and Japan, they found stronger neural signatures of object processing in Austrian infants and accentuated background processing in Japanese infants. In a separate interaction phase, Austrian mothers also pointed out object (versus background) elements more often than Japanese mothers (for evidence that these differences are less consistent in early childhood, see Köster et al., 2018, who found mixed and sometimes reversed differences among 5-year-olds in Germany, Japan, and rural Cameroon).

Extending this line of work, Tu et al. (2025) compared 9-month-olds in Sweden and Zimbabwe and found culturally distinct patterns of visual attention that did not fit a simple holistic-analytic contrast.

Swedish infants scanned more broadly, looked relatively longer at focal objects than at background information, and shifted gaze between focal elements and background more frequently than Zimbabwean infants. They therefore combined an analytic emphasis on focal objects with broad exploration and frequent shifting, features typically associated with holistic viewing. Zimbabwean infants made fewer fixations and looked less overall, and devoted relatively less attention to focal objects. Within the Zimbabwean sample, greater poverty and stronger caregiver relatedness were associated with more distributed, holistic-looking patterns.

Beyond differences in scene parsing, cross-national work also points to variation in the core orienting processes that govern how quickly and accurately infants shift attention. DeBolt et al. (2025)compared spatial orienting in 6-to 9-month-old infants in rural Malawi and suburban California using the Infant Orienting With Attention (IOWA) task, an attentional cuing paradigm. In both samples, infants fixated targets more quickly and accurately after valid cues and more slowly and less accurately after invalid cues, indicating that the basic signature of cue-driven orienting was shared across contexts. Relative to US infants, however, Malawian infants took longer to fixate the targets but were more accurate overall.

This pattern indicate a different trade-off between speed and accuracy across contexts.

Earlier work comparing 9-month-old infants in rural Malawi and Finland found a partially overlapping pattern (Forssman et al., 2017). As in DeBolt et al. (2025), Malawian infants showed slower gaze-shift latencies, averaged across tasks. In contrast to DeBolt et al., however, they were less successful at visual search when the target appeared alone or among distractors of one kind, although the groups did not differ in the most demanding conjunction condition. Malawian infants were also less likely to disengage from happy and fearful faces, but not from non-face control stimuli, which suggests that this difference concerns attention to faces rather than disengagement in general. Forssman et al. (2017) cautioned against interpreting these differences as cognitive disadvantages, noting that unfamiliarity with screen-based testing could contribute and that the groups did not differ on more naturalistic observational measures. Together, these studies suggest that the fundamental mechanisms of infant orienting are present across contexts, while their speed and accuracy may be shaped by infants' lived experiences, by the demands of the particular task, and by their interaction.

Comparison studies on infant visual attention based on multilingual exposure

At the outset, we note that language background is not a proxy for culture; many bilingual infants share cultural practices with monolingual peers in the same community. We include this literature because language exposure varies within cultural groups and isolates one proximal dimension of experience-the variability of linguistic input-that cross-national comparisons sometimes bundle with culture. Lookingtime measures are widely used to infer infants' underlying knowledge and learning, yet the same magnitude or pattern of looking can arise from different attentional processes. Classic accounts emphasize that infants' visual preferences shift from familiarity to novelty as a function of encoding, developmental stage, and task difficulty (Hunter & Ames, 1988; Roder et al., 2000; see also Kosie, Zettersten et al., 2024). Within that tradition, novelty preferences are often used to infer memory or discrimination. The point of the studies reviewed here is not that novelty preferences cease to be informative about memory, but that the timing, threshold, and strength of novelty orientation may themselves be shaped by infants' prior experience, including exposure to multiple languages.

Evidence from visual habituation and paired-comparison paradigms shows that bilingual exposure is associated with differences in both the rate and direction of attentional change. In a study of Singaporean 6-month-olds, monolingual and bilingual infants showed comparable total fixation during visual habituation, but bilingual infants exhibited steeper habituation slopes and a reliable novelty preference at test when discriminating similar visual stimuli (Singh et al., 2015). This pattern is consistent with rapid encoding alongside a stronger orientation toward novel information in bilingually exposed infants. Because many infant studies interpret longer looking to novelty as evidence of discrimination, surprise, or memory, these findings indicate that experience can influence how readily such responses are expressed, even when the measure remains theoretically meaningful.

Language experience also modifies the temporal dynamics of attention. Using gap-overlap and switching tasks, D 'Souza et al. (2020) showed that bilingually exposed infants disengaged from central stimuli more quickly and shifted attention to peripheral or changing stimuli sooner than monolingual peers.

Within bilingual infants, disengagement speed was associated with the amount of second-language exposure, and faster disengagement was associated with more frequent attentional switching. These results indicate that bilingual infants sample visual information more broadly, consistent with adaptation to environments in which input is more variable and less predictable (see D 'Souza & D'Souza, 2021; Singh, 2021; Singh, Barr et al. 2024).

Similar effects emerge in visual preference paradigms designed to assess novelty orientation. In a side-by-side task in which one stimulus remained constant while the other changed across trials, bilingually exposed infants showed earlier and more persistent preferences for the changing stimulus than monolingual infants, whose novelty preference emerged more gradually over time (Singh, Kalashnikova, & Quinn, 2023). Importantly, the strength and timing of novelty preferences were related to degree of secondlanguage exposure, indicating a graded effect of language experience on attentional allocation. These findings are particularly relevant for studies that use novelty preference as an index of learning, as they show that baseline preferences for novelty can differ systematically across infants before any experimental manipulation is introduced.

Anticipatory-looking paradigms further illustrate how language experience can influence the interpretation of gaze behavior. Although early reports suggested bilingual advantages in anticipatory control (Kovács & Mehler, 2009), subsequent work has shown that group differences may lie less in anticipatory accuracy than in how rapidly infants orient once a salient outcome appears. In a replication attempt, Kalashnikova et al. (2021) found no bilingual advantage in anticipatory looks but observed that bilingual infants fixated a novel visual reward preferentially relative to monolingual infants. This pattern is consistent with enhanced responsiveness to novel stimuli rather than advantages in executive control. Downstream consequences may extend to learning and generalization. In deferred imitation tasks, bilingual infants generalize learned actions across greater changes in visual features than monolingual infants (Barr et al., 2020; Brito & Barr, 2012 , 2014), although these tasks index memory flexibility rather than attention per se. Taken together, these findings indicate that language experience may tune attentional systems that underlie commonly used behavioral measures of nonlinguistic (i.e., visual) attention, and that measuring linguistic variation can clarify multiple dimensions of visual attention (Singh, Barokova et al., 2024).

Interracial face experience and attentional tuning

The literature on interracial face experience is included to clarify the boundary between domainspecific and domain-general effects of experience on attention. Face perception is often treated as a specialized domain, and the other-race effect has classically been interpreted in terms of perceptual narrowing and face-processing experience; it should not be collapsed uncritically with object-based looking paradigms. It is relevant here insofar as it informs whether sociocultural experience influences the endogenous operations through which visual information is selected, maintained, and disengaged.

Across multiple studies, infants raised in relatively homogeneous racial environments tend to show early attentional biases toward, and selective discrimination of, own-race faces (Kelly et al., 2005; 2007; 2009). Increased interracial exposure can attenuate or alter these biases. Bar-Haim et al. (2006) reported that 3-month-olds' own-race visual preference was modulated by exposure to other-race individuals, and Gaither et al. (2012) showed that monoracial and biracial infants differed in face processing and scanning patterns. In a multiracial context, Singh, Phneah et al. (2022) found that both societal-level diversity and caregiving experience were associated with infants' race-related looking preferences.

Recent work also points to attentional mechanisms within the face domain itself. Markant, Oakes, and Amso (2016) used a spatial cueing task to experimentally bias Caucasian 9-month-old infants' attention toward own-or other-race faces and found that infants discriminated the faces that were the focus of attention, regardless of race. This suggests that differences in attentional engagement, rather than a loss of sensitivity to other-race faces, may be one mechanism through which experience shapes face discrimination.

A more speculative question is whether such attentional dynamics attributed to experience with social (racial) categories generalizes beyond faces. D 'Souza, Rajendra, and Singh (submitted) examined infants' interracial contact as a predictor of performance on a gap-overlap attentional disengagement task using non-face stimuli. In 9-to 11-month-old bilingual infants, greater interracial contact predicted more efficient attentional disengagement when shifting gaze from a central object to a competing peripheral object. Two interpretations remain possible. On a domain-specific account, interracial experience recalibrates attention within the social domain, altering how infants allocate attention to faces and social categories while leaving broader object-directed attention largely intact. On a domain-general account, frequent exposure to socially and perceptually variable faces may also support more flexible orienting or disengagement across visual contexts. Distinguishing between these possibilities will require studies that jointly measure infants' social exposure, attentional dynamics, and learning across both social and non-social tasks. This distinction is important for the present framework because a culturally situated model should specify the scope of an experiential effect rather than assume that all experience-dependent changes generalize across domains.

Infant visual attention and socioeconomic status

Socioeconomic status (SES) has become a central construct in research on early attention because it indexes access to material resources and introduces associated systematic variation in infants' everyday experiences, including environmental predictability, caregiver interaction patterns, and exposure to cognitive stimulation (Duncan et al. 1994). A central construct in this section is environmental entropy, by which we mean the degree of unpredictability, variability, and informational disorder in infants' everyday environments. Entropy may include household noise, crowding, interruption, visual clutter, caregiver availability, and instability in routines. It is not synonymous with SES, although the two may covary.

Treating entropy as a proximal feature of experience helps distinguish material resources from the structure of attentional demands in the infant's environment.

Early evidence for SES differences in fixation comes from longitudinal work examining infant attention during free-play contexts. Clearfield and Jedd (2013) followed infants from higher-and lower-SES households (classified by maternal education) longitudinally at 6, 9, and 12 months during free play with either a single toy or an array of six toys. Higher-SES infants showed more focused attention to toys overall and greater increases in focused attention in the more complex six-toy condition. Lower-SES infants showed more off-task looking at every age and in both conditions, and were less likely to modulate their attention according to stimulus complexity, most clearly at 12 months and in measures of disengagement and attention to people. Clearfield and Jedd interpreted these patterns as early attention deficits, speculating about nutritional mechanisms and calling for early intervention, consistent with much of the historical framing of SES effects.

Studies of disengagement and orienting provide a more mechanistic view of fixation dynamics.

Studies using gap-overlap paradigms indicate that SES relates to how infants balance maintaining fixation against shifting attention, but these relations are not uniform across studies, samples, or ages. For example, Siqueiros Sanchez et al. (2021) found that among 5-month-old Swedish infants, those whose mothers had more education showed slower gaze-shift latencies in the overlap condition (and in the baseline condition), although SES was unrelated to the disengagement difference score. The authors suggested, with caution, that slower latencies may not indicate poorer performance in infancy, given that faster gaze shifts may come at a cost of accuracy, but they did not measure accuracy directly and noted that the effect was small. These findings contrast with studies using other paradigms that report attentional disadvantages for lower-SES infants (e.g., Clearfield & Niman, 2012; Lipina et al., 2005).

An important interpretive question raised by these findings concerns the functional significance of attentional regulation in context. Differences in sustained engagement or modulation with complexity need not reflect deficits in attentional capacity; they may instead reflect calibration to environmental demands.

For example, if lower-SES environments are more perceptually cluttered, less predictable, or characterized by higher levels of background activity and interruption, broader sampling or heightened vigilance may be advantageous for monitoring multiple information sources rather than sustaining prolonged focus on a single object. From this perspective, modulation of fixation across experimental conditions could reflect an attentional pattern tuned to contexts in which sustained focus is not consistently rewarded, motivating mechanistic work that targets the engagement-disengagement equilibrium and its expression relative to contextual demands.

Longitudinal evidence further highlights the need to distinguish between disengagement speed and disengagement failure. For example, Moyano et al. (2023) found no significant associations between a composite SES index measured at 6 months and visual disengagement at any age, although SES showed a trend toward fewer disengagement failures at 16-18 months. When individual SES components were examined, higher parental occupational status at 6 months was associated with fewer failures to disengage at 16-18 months, whereas parental education and income-to-needs were not. In hierarchical regression models, the SES index at 6 months predicted fewer disengagement failures at 16-18 months even after accounting for disengagement performance at 9 months. The authors interpreted these findings as consistent with prior work linking higher SES to more focused attention and greater attentional flexibility in infancy (Clearfield & Jedd, 2013; Clearfield & Niman, 2012; Lipina et al., 2005) and with later SES-related differences in attentional control (Hackman & Farah, 2009).

Infants' home environments also vary in ways that SES does not fully capture, particularly household disorganization and chaos, which may exert independent and sometimes counterintuitive effects on attention. In Moyano et al. (2023), SES and chaos made independent contributions to later disengagement, and, contrary to the authors' hypothesis, higher levels of chaos at 6 months predicted fewer disengagement failures at 16-18 months. The authors proposed that unpredictable home environments may foster a more reactive attentional style and heightened sensitivity to contextual information, which could facilitate disengagement from a central stimulus but might undermine attentional control in situations that require ignoring competing information. Consistent with this account, a related study found that 2-to 4year-olds from more disorganized homes made more correct anticipations in complex, context-dependent visual sequences (Moyano et al., 2022). By contrast, Tomalski et al. (2017) found that among 5.5-montholds from predominantly middle-class families, greater parent-reported household chaos was associated with longer looking, and thus slower processing, of complex but not simple stimuli in a habituation task.

Family income did not predict looking in this model, although it did when household size was used as an indirect measure of crowding. The authors interpreted chaos as detrimental to processing speed under high task demands.

In trying to reconcile these findings, associations between household chaos and infant visual attention are not uniformly directional; instead, they appear vary by which attentional component is measured and the structure of the task. In habituation paradigms indexing processing efficiency, higher chaos predicts slower processing under high stimulus demands (e.g., longer peak looks to complex scenes; Tomalski et al., 2017) that could reflect attentional overwhelm, whereas in gap-overlap paradigms indexing disengagement, higher chaos predicts fewer failures to disengage by toddlerhood (Moyano et al., 2023), a pattern that may reflect a more reactive, vigilance-oriented attentional style rather than enhanced sustained processing. More work is needed to clarify when attentional patterns function as context-sensitive adaptations that support learning, versus when they may carry downstream processing costs in settings that reward sustained attention.

One example of such a component-based approach comes from studies of object-and feature-based attention. Werchan et al. (2019) examined how 3-to 5-and 9-to 12-month-olds resolve competition between whole-object representations and salient perceptual features (color, orientation, and motion), and explored whether family income related to this process. Higher income was associated with greater featurebased attention among younger infants and, as a trend, with greater object-based attention among older infants, but only when color competed with object identity; income was unrelated to performance on orientation or motion trials. These feature-specific effects may reflect interactions between SES-related experience and the developmental status of particular visual processing pathways. From this perspective, SES does not globally enhance attention but may shape attentional selection as representational systems mature. The authors proposed that income may influence the number and variability of infants' visual input through access to books and toys, and that caregivers may therefore direct infants' attention in a manner that supports their findings.

Evidence linking early attention to later self-regulatory outcomes, together with emerging intervention work, underscores the developmental relevance-and potential malleability-of early differences in visual attention. In a large longitudinal sample of children from predominantly low-income, nonurban communities, poverty-related risk was negatively associated with infant sustained attention at 7 and 15 months, which in turn prospectively predicted pre-K executive function as well as teacher-rated effortful control and emotion regulation; infant sustained attention partially mediated associations between early poverty-related risk and each self-regulatory outcome (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2019). Complementing these observational findings, intervention evidence suggests that core components of visual attention may be responsive to changes in infants' ecological conditions. In a pilot study nested within a supplementary feeding trial in rural Sierra Leone, Leppänen et al. (2022) found that infants with moderate acute malnutrition showed a nonsignificant trend toward slower attention shifting at baseline (longer saccadic reaction times) relative to non-malnourished peers, but showed significant improvement relative to agematched controls after four weeks of supplementary feeding. This suggests that early orienting dynamics can change over relatively short time scales when poverty-linked constraints such as undernutrition are addressed.

These findings motivate closer attention to the mechanisms through which socioeconomic context shapes early attentional organization. Beyond material resources, social factors such as caregiver-infant interaction may influence fixation by scaffolding attention toward relevant targets during play and everyday routines, thereby supporting the development of endogenous attentional control. In parallel, stress-related physiological processes associated with socioeconomic adversity may affect arousal regulation, producing differences in vigilance, distractibility, or persistence of fixation depending on contextual demands.

Importantly, these pathways are likely probabilistic rather than deterministic and may operate differently across attentional components and developmental periods (Blair and Raver, 2012).

From correlates of experience to culture

The literatures reviewed above differ in what they can tell us about culture. Cross-national studies come closest to examining culture directly, but they typically operationalize culture as country of residence and rarely measure the meanings, practices, or caregiving routines thought to produce attentional differences. Research on multilingual, interracial, and socioeconomic experience often measure a specific experiential variable with some precision-such as the proportion of exposure to each language, the amount of interracial contact, or household chaos-and report graded associations with attention within a single society. But they rarely ask whether these experiences carry different meanings in context, or are embedded in different practices, across cultural communities, and so they speak to effects of experience rather than effects of culture. This division of labor leaves a gap. On one side are studies showing that culture, broadly indexed, is associated with infant attention but that do not identify the experiences responsible; on the other are studies that identify experiences associated with attention but do not situate them culturally. Closing the gap would involve examining the developmental niche directly-the settings of daily life, customs of care, and caregivers' beliefs and practices (Super & Harkness, 1986)-alongside attentional outcomes, within and across cultural communities, and testing whether the same proximal experience is associated with the same attentional component in different settings (Matsumoto & Yoo, 2006). The synthesis below is organized by attentional components so that evidence from both kinds of studies can be compared at the level at which it is most informative.

Component-level synthesis of sociocultural research on infant visual attention

We next synthesize the literature by decomposing infant visual attention into its component processes, allowing evidence from diverse sociocultural contexts to be evaluated according to the attentional operations it informs. Across national contexts, variation in infant visual attention does not map cleanly onto simple contrasts such as WEIRD versus non-WEIRD, nor can it be explained by reified typologies such as analytic equals Western and holistic equals non-Western.

Instead, cross-national evidence may be most useful when interpreted at the level of attentional components. Some findings implicate focal versus contextual processing; others implicate speed-accuracy trade-offs, orienting efficiency, or the informativeness of particular dependent variables. Findings on interracial face experience illustrate why the scope of an observed effect must be specified rather than assumed. Greater experience with other-race faces may alter attention specifically within the face-processing system-for example, by changing how readily infants discriminate, scan, or maintain attention to faces from different racial groups. Alternatively, such experience may shape broader attentional operations, such as the speed with which infants disengage from a stimulus, shift attention, or flexibly allocate attention across competing inputs. Evidence from a face-based task alone cannot distinguish these possibilities. Establishing a domain-general effect therefore requires showing that the same attentional pattern extends beyond faces to nonsocial or differently structured stimuli. This distinction constrains claims appropriately while preserving the theoretical significance of the literature: sociocultural experience may tune face-specific perceptual processing, broader mechanisms of attentional control, or both, but the underlying nature of the effect must be determined through comparisons across tasks and stimulus domains. SES-related findings further show that environmental influences on attention are not linear or uniformly directional. Socioeconomic context appears to shape how infants regulate engagement and disengagement under varying levels of perceptual and informational load. In some contexts, sustained fixation and demand-sensitive modulation align with higher SES; in others, broader sampling, faster updating, or reduced disengagement failure are associated with greater household unpredictability or chaos. These patterns imply that attentional behaviors commonly treated as markers of maturity or efficiency may instead reflect calibration to local levels of environmental entropy, with functional consequences that depend on task demands and developmental timing.

The reviewed evidence is necessarily constrained by the small number of available studies, heterogeneity in designs, and uneven measurement of culture-relevant variables. Apparent inconsistencies may reflect genuine differences in how attentional systems are calibrated across environments, but they may also arise from unmeasured cultural variables, stimulus familiarity, testing context, analytic choices, or mismatches between tasks and the attentional components they engage. This is precisely why a culturally situated model must be component-specific, measurement-conscious, and cautious in its claims. The model rests on four principles. First, looking behavior should be interpreted at the level of attentional components rather than as a unitary measure. Fixation duration, novelty preference, latency to shift, disengagement failure, gaze dispersion, pupil dilation, anticipatory looking, and gaze following do not index the same construct. Second, demographic variables should be treated as important but imperfect proxies for proximal experience, rather than as explanations in themselves. They provide essential information for situating samples and evaluating generalizability, but should be supplemented by more direct measures of culturally patterned experience-such as caregiving routines, social diversity, household organization, and environmental predictability-and integrated into the interpretation of attentional behavior. Third, methodological features-including immediate task context, stimulus familiarity, perceptual salience, task structure, calibration quality, exclusion criteria, and analytic decisions-may interact with sociocultural experience and with individual differences such as temperament and arousal regulation. Identifying these interactions depends on adequately capturing the relevant exogenous, contextual, and individual-level factors rather than treating them as background variation. This requires a more situated approach in which a broader range of environmental and participant characteristics is measured and integrated into the interpretation of attentional behavior (see Singh, Barokova et al., 2024, for a framework to capture infants' demographic information). Fourth and related, the functional value of an attentional pattern cannot be inferred from the behavior alone; it depends on the affordances and demands of the environments in which that pattern develops and is deployed.

This framework complements endogenous accounts of attentional development. Models of alerting, orienting, and executive attention emphasize biologically constrained systems that mature over time and interact with experience (Posner & Rothbart, 2007). A culturally situated model extends this view by asking how culturally patterned input shapes the developmental calibration of those systems.

Endogenous capacities constrain what infants are able to attend to, but experience can shape what infants preferentially attend to, how readily they disengage, when novelty or familiarity is prioritized, and whether persistence or flexibility is more functional in a given ecology.

This framework also clarifies what it means to ask whether looking behavior is a reliable index of cognition. Reliability and validity are not global properties of looking time; they are properties of particular measures used for particular inferential purposes. A novelty preference may validly index discrimination or memory in a specific paradigm while also being moderated by infants' prior novelty orientation, language environment, or stimulus familiarity. Similarly, slower disengagement may index immature control in one context but strategic persistence in another. Rather than treating these possibilities as threats to looking-time methods, a culturally situated model treats them as testable hypotheses about construct validity.

The meaning of looking itself may also vary across cultural contexts. Communities differ in how caregivers organize infants' attention-for example, in the relative emphasis placed on engagement with the caregiver versus engagement with objects and ongoing events (Bornstein et al., 1992; Chavajay & Rogoff, 1999)-and in infants' familiarity with screens, laboratory settings, and unfamiliar adults. The same duration of looking may therefore reflect interest, wariness, or habitual patterns of engagement to different degrees in different samples, and dependent variables derived from the same paradigm can differ in how informative they are across settings (Beckner et al., 2024). These are possibilities to be evaluated empirically.

Measurement invariance is therefore central (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; van de Vijver & Leung, 1997; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). When researchers compare infants across cultural, linguistic, socioeconomic, or racialized contexts, it is important to ask whether the task engages the same attentional construct in each group and whether the dependent variable has the same meaning across contexts. This does not require abandoning cross-group comparisons. It requires designing them so that stimulus familiarity, task affordances, and analytic choices are not coarsely interpreted as cultural effects on the basis of difference or as evidence of universality on the basis of similar responses, and so that robust measures can be distinguished from measures that are expected to vary with experience. A related concern is the valence attached to looking behavior. Treating faster disengagement, longer looking, or greater focus on focal objects as a sign of developmental advancement assumes that the same pattern is adaptive everywhere. Supporting that interpretation requires much broader sampling and an account of what a given looking pattern accomplishes in the real-world contexts where infants actually develop. This does not require abandoning cross-group comparisons. It requires designing them so that stimulus familiarity, task affordances, and analytic choices are considered relative to their functionality in context.

Large-scale collaborative efforts such as ManyBabies provide one powerful route toward this goal because they can harmonize protocols, diversify samples, and test moderation across laboratories.

However, culturally situated models cannot depend only on large consortia. A single-laboratory study can also implement the framework by taking several concrete steps: specifying the attentional component targeted by the paradigm; measuring relevant proximal experiences and individual differences such as language variability, caregiver routines, household chaos, stimulus familiarity, social diversity, temperament, and arousal regulation, using available instruments such as household chaos scales (Matheny et al., 1995), detailed language-exposure measures (de Bruin, 2019), standardized demographic questionnaires (Singh, Barokova et al., 2024), and head-mounted cameras that capture infants' everyday visual input (e.g., Fausey et al., 2016); reporting demographic variables consistently; checking whether stimuli are similarly familiar and salient across participants; considering moderation by experience when sample size permits; and interpreting group differences relative to task demands rather than as default evidence of ability, deficit, or universality.

The framework also points beyond passive viewing in the laboratory. Laboratory paradigms are valuable because they isolate specific attentional operations, but infants' everyday attention unfolds in multimodal, socially embedded contexts that include caregiver behavior, bodily activity, objects, routines, noise, and competing demands. Naturalistic methods, including head-mounted eye tracking, home observation, video-based coding, and caregiver-report measures of routines and environments, can help determine whether laboratory patterns generalize to real-world attentional ecology. Conversely, laboratory tasks can help identify which components of real-world attention are shaped by particular forms of experience.

In sum, moving toward culturally situated models of infant visual attention allows the field to retain the explanatory power of looking-based methods while strengthening their theoretical precision.

The central claim is not that infant attention is infinitely malleable, nor that culture explains all variation in looking behavior. It is that attentional measures are produced by developing infants situated in structured environments, and that the field can make stronger inferences when endogenous mechanisms, proximal experience, task context, and measurement validity are modeled together.
