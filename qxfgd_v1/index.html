Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Distinguishing problematic from non-problematic chemsex, or placing experience on a continuum of severity, generates limited information about why similar drug use can lead to different experiences and support needs. We propose considering how the circumstances of an encounter work together and change over time. Method: This think piece presents a selective conceptual synthesis of chemsex research and assessment approaches, informed by minority stress theory, drug, set and setting, functional analysis, and addiction science. Empirical studies inform the domains and the proposed relationships between them. Results: Six questions (who, what, why, with, where and when) describe the person, substances, functions, relationships, settings and timing within a changing chemsex configuration. Digital activity may connect these through partner contact, planning and support, and is an important emerging consideration. Three fictional vignettes illustrate different responses to comparable exposure. The person and therapist could each map the configuration, compare accounts and identify a focus for formulation and therapy. Conclusions: Assessment should determine how pleasure, protection and harm arise through interacting circumstances. The six Ws direct attention to these relationships and to differences between the person's and therapist's accounts. Participatory development and longitudinal evaluation are needed to establish whether this approach improves shared understanding, decisions and care.

Introduction

Chemsex describes the intentional use of particular psychoactive substances before or during sexual activity to facilitate, intensify or prolong the experience. In the United Kingdom, the term emerged within men who have sex with me (MSM) communities and commonly refers to methamphetamine, synthetic cathinones (mostly mephedrone, 4-MMC) and gamma-hydroxybutyrate or gamma-butyrolactone (GHB/GBL), although sessions may involve other drugs (McCall et al., 2015; Stuart, 2019). Figure 1

illustrates

Stuart's culturally specific account by placing chemsex within sexualised drug use (SDU), which in turn sits within broader patterns drug use.

The boundaries of what constitutes chemsex nevertheless vary between studies.

Definitions differ in drug lists, populations and sexual intention, ultimately changing who is recruited and which experiences are studied (Amundsen et al., 2024; Maxwell et al., 2019). Language also carries local meanings. Party and play (PnP) and high and horny (HnH) are used across several regions (Barber et al., 2026; Dennermalm et al., 2021).

More recently, Witzel et al. (2026) distinguish "hi-fun" in Thailand from sex at homepas and incidental sex with drugs, in contrast with more "westernised" definitions of chemsex.

Pleasure, intimacy and belonging can coexist with overdose, psychiatric symptoms or sexual harm (Bourne et al., 2015a; Mundy et al., 2025). Acknowledging these different trajectories can be confusing, particularly when two people with seemingly similar demographics and patterns of chemsex engagement go down different pathways of pleasure versus harm. Recognising non-problematic engagement challenges the assumption that participation itself is pathological and was a relatively early contribution to the field (Stuart, 2019). A continuum of harm permits greater nuance, although suggesting an individual occupies a single position on a single spectrum of possible harms may still compress different experiences and support needs. Stuart (2019) already recognised a middle ground and individual agency. The Problematic Chemsex Journey connects loneliness and seeking connection with changing experiences through to severe harm, allowing for different courses (Platteau et al., 2019). A later self-control framework attends to pleasure and personal goals (Platteau et al., 2022). The Pharmacosex Wheel adds detail about the sexual effects sought from drugs and combinations (Platteau et al., 2025). More recently, attempts at psychological formulation asks how a person's difficulties develop and are sustained. Barber et al. (2026) describe the 5 Ps (presenting difficulties and predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors). Our use of six Ws draws on questions already used in functional analysis of substance use (Reichert et al., 2021), with attention to how the components of a chemsex configuration interact and change over time to create unique, indivdualised patterns. This assessment models proposes examining the configuration of influences operating at a particular time (Figure 2). A 5 Ps formulation may explain that configuration, while the six Ws attend to how each influence changes with the others. A relationship may sustain intimacy while increasing access to drugs; poor sleep may change the response to an invitation otherwise declined. Considering these relationships can identify a focus for help, to be explored through formulation and therapy.

Materials and methods

This think piece is a selective narrative synthesis and conceptual proposal, drawing on empirical chemsex research alongside minority stress theory (Meyer, 2003), drug, set and setting (Zinberg, 1984), and addiction science. Chat GPT 6.1 was used to enhance readability of earlier drafts.

Results

The six Ws

We propose taking the configuration at a particular time as the unit of assessment (Figure 3). Each W describes influences whose significance depends on the others and is adapted from conventional functional analysis techniques (Reichert 2021). Drug exposure acquires different implications when a person is exhausted, seeking relief from shame or dependent on a partner for somewhere to stay. These circumstances also 'Who' concerns the resources and demands a person brings to chemsex.

Who: the person and their current capacity

Current mental state, sleep and physical health sit alongside longer-standing life experiences, coping and support. These may affect whether someone recognises deterioration, communicates a boundary or recovers well after a session. For instance, 'anxiety' may precede use, ease temporarily during sex or intensify afterwards dependent on 'who' the participant is.

Minority stress theory describes how prejudice, anticipated rejection, concealment and internalised stigma place demands on mental health (Meyer, 2003). Among men interviewed after a chemsex-related HIV risk, Pollard et al. (2018) describe drug use and sexual risk through accounts of marginalisation and the gay scene. Mundy et al.'s (2025) qualitative synthesis places shame and loneliness alongside belonging and intimacy.

These accounts make social adversity relevant to understanding capacity, while retaining the value of temporary freedom from self-consciousness when other opportunities for connection feel restricted.

Trauma requires a substantive place in this account of chemsex. Tan et al. (2021) interviewed 33 treatment-seeking men with sexualised drug-use histories in Singapore. Quantitative research also complicates the assumption of a uniform psychiatric profile. Bohn et al. (2020) compared 280 men reporting chemsex with men reporting no sexualised substance use apart from alcohol or nicotine. Depression and anxiety scores were higher in this German convenience sample, but differences were small and the proportions reaching screening thresholds did not differ significantly.

It is also important to consider the distinction between trait and state. Rejection, grief or poor sleep may temporarily undermine limits that usually work, while knowledge of dosing, bodily awareness and confidence in seeking help may support someone experiencing distress. Recording changes from baseline helps distinguish an unusually difficult encounter from longer-standing difficulties, while retaining the social origins of strain.

What: substance use patterns and reinforcement 'What' describes pharmacological exposure and how it is organised. Substance names and mechanisms alone may obscure differences in dose, route, combinations and redosing. Methamphetamine can sustain arousal and activity, while mephedrone combines stimulant and empathogenic effects. GHB/GBL may reduce inhibition and alter sexual sensation but carries substantial overdose risk. Ketamine adds dissociation and analgesia; cocaine, MDMA and other substances may also feature (Giorgetti et al., 2017; Maxwell et al., 2019; Platteau et al., 2025) 'What' also concerns the sex taking place, including kink, sexual roles and whether particular practices feel possible only with substances. Interviews with men engaging in chemsex describe drugs enabling desired sex through confidence and disinhibition, alongside intensified sensation and sexual adventure (Weatherburn et al., 2017).

Professionals should ask whether a substance changes arousal or bodily sensation, eases discomfort or helps someone express desires otherwise constrained by shame.

There is a possible interaction here with neurodivergence, particular in individuals for whom sexual activity may be challenging for sensory reasons that the substances in question alter. Placebo-controlled work in healthy men found that GHB increased sexual arousal and lowered the threshold for erotic perception (Bosch, Havranek, et al., 2017).

Related work reported increased self-rated body and emotion awareness alongside greater perfusion of the right anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex (Bosch, Esposito, et al., 2017). This invites discussion of how drug effects and sexual meaning shape the experience, and which parts of it the person wants to preserve or change. Maxwell et al.'s (2019) review of 38 studies describes varied drug-use and injecting patterns, with differences in definitions and sampling limiting any single prevalence estimate. In the Budd study, 11 app users recorded 63 chemsex events and combined drugs in 95% of them (Platteau et al., 2023). Event reports capture combinations that a lifetime drug list may miss. The Pharmacosex Wheel considers the sexual effects sought from these combinations (Platteau et al., 2025).

Exposure also has a learned history. Incentive-sensitisation theory distinguishes motivational wanting from hedonic liking, while addiction research describes how cues and repeated action sequences can influence pursuit and control (Berridge & Robinson, 2016; Everitt & Robbins, 2016). Applied to chemsex, these accounts raise questions about whether arousal, a notification or a familiar room prompts urges or preparation before a drug is even taken. Continued pursuit with diminishing enjoyment may differ from deliberate use that remains pleasurable, making the person's account of enjoyment and the urge to continue important to understanding the encounter. Chen et al. (2020) found differences between men with methamphetamine dependence and controls in responses to methamphetamine and sexual cues, involving the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and anterior insula respectively. The distinct cue responses raise questions about how sexual arousal and drug-related urges relate to one another within an encounter. We explored the wider neuroimaging and neurophysiological evidence in a recent scoping review (Naulls et al., 2026).

We propose describing the organisation of use alongside its extent. The same frequency can encompass measured doses, reactive redosing or a change in route, with different implications. A reduction in one form of harm need not coincide with less frequent participation; conversely, fewer sessions can conceal more hazardous combinations.

These changes may identify where support is needed.

Why: functions and meanings

Why concerns what chemsex enables and the importance of that experience to the person. In interviews with 30 gay men in South London, Weatherburn et al. (2017) distinguished using drugs to enable desired sex from using them to intensify the qualities (Ahmed et al., 2016). This links with the 'what' domain -particularly given findings that GHB can alter the threshold for erotic perception and may influence interoception through altered activity in the insula (Bosch et al 2017; Naulls 2026).

It is also important to acknowledge that the role chemsex plays in a person's life can Reducing exposure alone may leave the principal difficulty untouched, while preserving pleasure may actually be part of or a means to reducing harm.

With: relationships, consent and power

With treats relationships as active influences on the configuration. Other people can affect access to drugs, the pace of a session and what happens if someone becomes unwell. A trusted partner may measure doses or respect an agreed end point. Equally, familiarity may make refusal easier or harder. Enquiry therefore needs to attend to the person's agency within the relationship/s and how it changes during the encounter/s.

Qualitative accounts describe social expectations, connection and differing perceptions of sexual risk (Ahmed et al., 2016; Bourne et al., 2015b; Mundy et al., 2025). These shared meanings may affect whether belonging depends on matching others' pace or an experienced friend makes limits easier to maintain. Mundy et al.'s synthesis also describes coercion involving access to drugs. Control of drugs or accommodation can constrain choice, especially when leaving threatens access to a network or somewhere to stay.

The exploratory Budd study illustrates the importance of that level of detail in any assessment of harm profiles. Across 63 event reports from 11 participants, sexual peer pressure was reported in 31 events and personal boundaries were 'disregarded' in 24 (Platteau et al., 2023). Repeated reports from this small group captured experiences that a general description of consensual participation could miss. Enquiry should follow how boundaries concerning sex, substances, routes, partners and recording are communicated as intoxication changes, including whether pausing or leaving is possible.

The same person (who) using the same substances (what) in the same location (where) may be less able to communicate preferences because of who they are with (with).

Relationships might extend beyond those present. A friend who knows someone's location, a partner who supports recovery, or a community organisation may all offer continuity. Digital contact can help negotiate boundaries before meeting, but subsequent pressure or abrupt separation may alter the experience. Removing a network without replacing its support may create another difficulty. We propose examining how care and power operate together, as the same relationship may sustain connection, increase access to substances and affect whether limits can be kept. Relational agency is therefore an outcome to examine even when exposure is unchanged, and psychodynamic principles may be especially helpful in this regard.

Where: physical, digital and structural settings

Where concerns the conditions that make particular actions possible. Residential gatherings, clubs, darkrooms, saunas and other sex-on-premises venues create different conditions for drug use and sexual contact. A private home may offer greater control over dosing, rest and who is present, whereas anonymity, limited visibility and venue rules may shape behaviour in a sauna or darkroom. In our mixed methods study of GBMSM venue drug use, participants described punitive drug policies encouraging 'preloading' or concealed use, alongside movement from clubs into prolonged residential 'afters' which may be more hidden and susceptible to introducing harms (Naulls et al., 2025).

Assessment should consider how layout, staff responses and closing times affect the encounter as people move between settings, including into residential spaces.

A familiar space may offer privacy, food, medication and somewhere to rest, while also carrying strong learned associations that fuel cravings. Moving between crowded or unfamiliar spaces may make a planned exit harder. In 88 interviews with gay and bisexual men in Australia, Drysdale et al. (2020) describe crystal methamphetamine use across varied settings and relationships, challenging a uniform account of chemsex risk. Their Digital activity extends the environment across anticipation, meeting and recovery. This is an area that has become increasingly relevant since the emergence of chemsex when considering harms but also has potential to contribute to the creation of protective tools or interventions. So-called 'cam-chemsex' has emerged, often involving individuals using drugs and engaging in virtual chemsex activity. The potential for harm in this space given the isolation needs to be carefully monitored, and unfortunately case reports of mortalities have recently emerged (Pélissier-Alicot, 2026) . Online images and invitations, or even notifications, may become part of searching, travelling and entering a chemsex setting; continued app use may introduce partners or interrupt an intended end point. In Wang et al.'s (2020) Hong Kong cohort, 407 of 600 men completed six-month follow-up. Exposure to online information supportive of SDU was associated with subsequent SDU after adjustment, including baseline use. This prospective association makes the content and timing of contact worth examining alongside existing intentions to use.

Digital support also depends on whom people can turn to for help. In Strong et al.'s (2025) app study in Taiwan, 20 participants completed one-month follow-up and 13 were interviewed. Participants wanted time planning and broader life goals addressed; some lacked someone to whom they could disclose use for emergency-contact functions.

Relationships therefore affected whether a feature was usable. Physical and digital environments are connected conditions of care: a safety plan depends on the required space, contact or service being available.

When: session course, recovery and overall trajectory

When concerns how a configuration develops within and between encounters. Session counts collapse a planned evening and an unplanned period extending into work into the same unit. Timing, duration and intervals influence the demands on the person and the opportunity to recover. We propose reconstructing when an intention becomes a plan, when limits change and what sustains continuation. Renewed invitations or redosing may displace an intended end point; identifying the transition makes the pattern more intelligible than a frequency total.

Sleep connects timing to capacity to continue involvement. Experimental sleep research describes impairments in attention and emotional regulation (Krause et al., 2017). Bohn et al. (2020) reported that 13.2% of their chemsex group described hearing voices or paranoid experiences after chemsex. Mental health reviews also describe variation between studies and pre-existing psychiatric difficulties (Íncera-Fernández et al., 2021; Moreno-Gámez et al., 2022). These accounts support exploring when symptoms occur in relation to the person's usual state, drug exposure and recovery. We hypothesise that extended wakefulness and stimulant exposure may interact within this sequence, making timing useful to understanding an apparent psychiatric change.

Recovery includes physical care and the emotional aftermath, as well as resuming medication, work and relationships. Interviews with 15 Swedish men who travelled to Berlin described self-defined rules for balancing pleasure and safety in recreational and sexualised drug use, learnt through peers and online sources; knowledge did not consistently become practice (Dennermalm et al., 2021). A protected recovery day therefore depends on resources in the other domains. Continued invitations, isolation or nowhere safe to rest may make an intended pause difficult. We hypothesise that incomplete recovery can also change capacity for the next encounter, linking sessions even when doses remain stable.

Longer trajectories should be reconstructed from turning points, including loss, relationship change or new support. Rhythms around weekends, paydays or rejection may reveal when earlier contact would be useful from a supportive practitioner or when proactive risk planning may be beneficial. Importantly, the same frequency can accompany improved recovery, increasing isolation or longer sessions. Change should be assessed against what matters to the person, including restored sleep and valued parts of life. Repeated assessment can test whether recovery changes precede symptoms or renewed use, allowing for effects in either direction.

Illustrative configurations

The following fictional vignettes illustrate how the configuration might guide clinical work.

Each involves stimulant and GHB/GBL use in overnight sexual settings, which in itself may be an oversimplification of the heterogeneity of chemsex presentations in practice.. In the first, a man plans infrequent sessions with a trusted network for pleasure and exploration. He measures doses, avoids injecting, discusses consent and protects the following day for recovery. Acute drug risks remain. Support could reinforce overdose precautions and recovery while considering what might disrupt these protections.

In the second, a man seeks connection when social anxiety is high. He meets unfamiliar partners through apps, struggles to hold boundaries under intoxication and follows further invitations as sessions extend. Dosing is similar to the first example, but support may focus on consent, an exit plan, app boundaries and other routes to connection.

In the third, a man describes trauma-related distress, loneliness and chronic sleep loss.

Reactive dosing includes injecting methamphetamine, and closely spaced sessions span several different chemsex settings. He seeks relief rather than exploration and reports intense shame afterwards. Acute safety, sleep, nutrition and continuity of care take priority. Trauma-informed work and alternatives can follow as stability improves, alongside urgent medical or safeguarding action when indicated. Support needs to address the relief chemsex offers as well as its costs.

Discussion

The framework's main proposition is that domains modify one another. The same exposure may have different consequences depending on relationships, motives and recovery; the same person may move between configurations without increasing drug use. A familiar home might support safety while carrying powerful cues, just as a trusted network might offer care while increasing access to substances. Whether a component protects, harms or does both depends on its place within the configuration.

Analyses should test specified interactions and include agency, pleasure and connection alongside adverse events. Recurring configurations would describe relationships that may change over time. Limitations The literature uses uneven definitions and often relies on convenience samples and cross-sectional self-report. The six labels overlap and may compress structural or biological influences. Their proposed clinical use needs testing, particularly whether exploring relationships between domains changes the priorities identified through assessment. Assessment also depends on trust and what can be communicated. Embodied pleasure or implicit learning may be difficult to describe, and people may reasonably withhold information from services. The literature centres on gay and bisexual men; use with other communities requires development with them and attention to the terms they use. Criminalisation, discrimination and inaccessible services also require action beyond individual clinical work.
