Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The stereotype that "men are funnier than women" is culturally pervasive. In a large preregistered online study (n=966), participants rated 12 jokes delivered by a male or female AI-generated voice. To control for differences in delivery, cadence, and intonation, we used a speech-to-speech transformation to transform jokes spoken by a female into a male version and vice versa. Using a Bayesian analysis, we found strong evidence that the apparent gender of the speaker does not affect the perceived funniness of the joke (BF=0.023). We found no interaction between the original and apparent voice gender on masculinity ratings, indicating that vocal gender cues were effectively eliminated when voices were transformed to the opposite gender, reinforcing the merit of this gender manipulation method. Further, men had significantly higher agreement that being funny is a masculine trait than women (M D =0.73, p adj <.001), and participants with more male than female friends had significantly higher agreement with this belief than those with equal numbers of male and female friends (M D =0.68, p adj <.001) or more female than male friends (M D =0.91, p adj <.001). In all, apparent vocal gender does not appear to influence humour evaluations directly, but exploratory findings suggest the apparent vocal gender and delivery may shape broader impressions of speakers (such as perceived confidence and charisma). And individual differences such as gender and proportion of male friends may shape gendered attitudes and expectations toward humour.

Is a joke funnier if it is told by a man? A novel paradigm using AI-generated voices

The stereotype that "men are funnier than women" is widespread in both popular media (e.g. Hitchens, 2007) and in lay beliefs held by men and women (Hooper et al., 2016; Mickes et al., 2012). For example, men are more commonly identified as the funnier gender, and funny cartoon captions are more likely to attributed to male authors, while unfunny captions are attributed to female authors (Hooper et al., 2016; Mickes et al., 2012). This raises an important question: does this stereotype reflect genuine gender differences in humour production, or does it partly arise because equivalent humour is evaluated differently when it is believed to come from a man rather than a woman? These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. Humour is inherently evaluative: a joke is funny only to the extent that an audience finds it funny. However, evaluations may reflect more than the content of a joke and the quality of its delivery. They may also be shaped by assumptions about the speaker. If men are expected to be witty, confident, intelligent, or socially dominant, while women are expected to be less humorous or face greater social costs for performing humour, identical material may be interpreted differently depending on the perceived gender of its source. This possibility is especially important because humour can signal socially valued traits, including competence, status, creativity, and affiliation (Bear & Miron-Spektor, 2024).

One explanation for gendered attitudes towards humour comes from evolutionary theory. Humour production, along with language, has been proposed as an indicator of cognitive fitness (Greengross & Miller, 2011; Miller, 2001; Miller, 2006). From this perspective, humour and other cognitively demanding displays may have evolved through mutual mate choice as signals of underlying mate quality. Sexual selection theory further predicts greater female selectivity in mate choice given women's greater minimum reproductive costs (Trivers, 1972). Consistent with the cognitive fitness indicator theory, women tend to report a preference for humorous partners, whereas men tend to report a preference for partners who appreciate their humour (Bressler et al., 2006; Li et al., 2009).

However, evidence from real-life contexts does not show that the attractiveness of humour production or humour receptivity differs reliably by sex (Wainwright et al., 2023). Thus, mate-preference findings alone do not establish that men are inherently funnier, nor do they explain why people may systematically credit men more readily for humour. A contrasting explanation is provided by social role theory, which proposes that gender differences in behaviour and beliefs partly reflect the historically unequal social roles occupied by men and women (Eagly & Wood, 2012). Because men have traditionally occupied more positions of economic and social power, gender stereotypes may associate men with traits considered useful for high-status roles, such as assertiveness, confidence, and competence. Humour may become gendered when it is culturally associated with these traits.

Expectations that men should be funny may then influence both behaviour and evaluation: men may be more encouraged to attempt humour, and in turn, audiences may be more likely to recognise, reward, and remember humour when it is attributed to men.

Although gendered expectations may shape both the expression and reception of humour, much existing research has examined whether men and women differ in their ability to produce humour. A meta-analysis by Greengross et al. (2020), including 5,057 participants, reported a small-to-moderate average difference favouring men in humourproduction ability, with an effect size of d = 0.32. Across the evaluated studies, men's humorous output was rated as funnier than women's even when judges were unaware of the producer's gender. However, most studies assessed humour with constrained laboratory tasks, particularly cartoon-caption writing tasks analogous to the New Yorker Cartoon Caption Contest (e.g. Feingold & Mazzella, 1993; Greengross & Miller, 2011). Other paradigms have included completing a humorous résumé (Howrigan & MacDonald, 2008), generating funny responses to unusual prompts, and joke-completion tasks (Nusbaum et al., 2017). Although some of these measures correlate with cartoon-caption performance, they require specific written, visual, and contextual skills and may not adequately represent the spontaneous and socially embedded humour of everyday interaction.

Research also suggests that apparent gender differences in humour production can be shaped by factors other than humour ability itself. Silvia et al. (2021) found that women rated their own humour attempts as less funny and expressed lower confidence in their performance than men. In contrast, Mickes et al. (2012) found no difference in the actual funniness of men's and women's cartoon captions in one study, despite strong beliefs that men were funnier. Caldwell and Wojtach (2019) further showed that humour self-efficacy mattered: when participants completed an easy practice task intended to increase confidence, women's captions were rated funnier than men's; when the practice task was difficult and presumably lowered confidence, men's captions were rated funnier. Although men's captions were rated funnier overall in that study, these findings indicate that confidence and task conditions may moderate observed gender differences in constrained humour-production tasks.

Evidence also indicates that gender stereotypes influence humour perception. In a memory paradigm, Mickes et al. (2012) showed participants cartoon captions that were rated high and low on humour, along with the authors' genders. When later asked to recall the author's gender, participants of both genders disproportionately remembered funny captions as male-authored and unfunny captions as female-authored. Hooper et al. (2016) replicated this pattern, supporting the conclusion that people's beliefs about gender can shape whom they credit for humorous material. However, these studies did not test whether the same humorous content is rated as funnier when recipients believe that it was produced by a man rather than a woman.

A small number of studies have manipulated characteristics of humour producers, but none have directly isolated the effect of speaker gender on ratings of spoken joke funniness. Reich et al. (2021), for instance, used written dating vignettes to examine responses to a failed humour attempt by a male or female target. The vignettes did not contain humorous material, but participants evaluated the male target's humour mistake as more severe and rated him as less likeable and competent than the female target. Langley and Shiota (2023) used video dating profiles in which male and female confederates gave either humorous or non-humorous responses; humour affected ratings of targets, but speaker gender was not the central experimental question. Similarly, Yeo et al. (2022) manipulated the gender, race, and professional identity of comedians performing a science-based stand-up routine. Although professional comedians were coached to keep pacing, tone, emphasis, and cadence consistent, individual speakers necessarily differed across many vocal dimensions, and humour ratings were not the central outcome.

Most humour in everyday interaction and in media is spoken. Speech is therefore a crucial context for studying how gendered beliefs about humour may operate. Yet it presents a methodological challenge: researchers must distinguish a bias in how listeners evaluate men and women from genuine differences in speakers' delivery. Asking men and women to perform the same jokes does not fully solve this problem, because perceived funniness can depend on subtle features of speech-including cadence, intonation, emphasis, timing, pitch, and timbre-that are difficult to standardise across human speakers (e.g. Langley & Shiota, 2023; Yeo et al., 2022). Consequently, an observed difference in ratings could arise from speaker gender, delivery quality, or both.

The present study uses artificial intelligence (AI) to address this limitation.

Specifically, we use AI text-to-speech and speech-to-speech tools to create one-liner jokes in apparently male and female voices. The stimuli are then gender-transformed, enabling the same underlying vocal delivery and pacing to be presented with either a male or female apparent gender. By counterbalancing the original AI voice and the direction of gender transformation, the design also controls for differences associated with a particular generated voice or transformation process. This method makes it possible to test whether identical spoken jokes are judged differently solely because listeners perceive the speaker as male or female. The study additionally assesses whether the apparent gender of the voice influences evaluations of warmth, charisma, competence, confidence, and attractiveness, using the same design. And we investigate whether individual differences, such as gender, and proportion of male friends influence gendered attitudes towards humour.

Method Participants

We obtained 1126 responses by our preregistered stopping date. After excluding participants who were under the age of 18, did not indicate English as a first language, failed attention or comprehension checks, or did not complete the survey, we were left with 966 valid responses (exceeding our preregistered goal of 900). Out of the valid responses, 535 identified as female, 396 as male, 31 as non-binary/third gender, and 4 preferred not to disclose their gender. 597 participants were recruited from Prolific and compensated approximately $1.25 AUD for completing a 5-minute survey. Another 135 participants were from the [hidden] first-year participant pool and were awarded 0.5 research credits for their participation. 234 participants were recruited using flyers placed around the University of Queensland campus, through Reddit, or Facebook. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 78 (M = 29.91, SD = 12.27, median = 26.00).

The sexual orientations of participants were diverse: 719 identified as heterosexual/straight, 48 as gay/lesbian, 144 as bisexual, 28 selected an option not listed, and 27 preferred not to say or did not respond. Participant ethnicities were also diverse: 9 Aboriginal/Torres Strait Islander, 35 African, 39 African American, 620 Caucasian, 76 East Asian, 22 Latino, 3 Melanesian/Pacific Islander, 15 Middle Eastern, 33 selected an option not listed, 8 preferred not to say. A majority of participants (91%) were from Western Englishspeaking countries (382 Australia, 176 Canada, 9 New Zealand, 87 United Kingdom, 225 United States of America). Ethics approval was granted by the Low and Negligible Risk Committee at the University of Queensland (2024/HE000002).

Design

We conducted a 2x2 between-subjects experimental design using an online study. We manipulated the gender of the originally spoken voice (male or female AI) and the apparent gender of the voice that participants actually hear (male or female). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four conditions, ensuring that each condition had an approximately equal number of participants across the gender categories. We note that we changed the design of our study from a mixed to a between-subjects design out of concern of a confound in the original within-subjects condition. Details of this change are described in the preregistration (https://osf.io/hu8b4/?view_only=b7cc7a115ef1447e94e859dc468e5441).

Materials

Jokes

We initially asked 21 friends and colleagues (7 males, 13 females, and 1 non-binary/third gender) to rate the funniness, offensiveness, and the edginess of 33 one-liner jokes 1 in a survey. This larger pool of one-liner jokes were selected to be gender-neutral (i.e. could be plausibly delivered by any gender). From this pool, we selected the funniest-rated jokes provided that fewer than 5 out of 21 respondents rated these as offensive (selected jokes are reported in Table 1).

1 Jokes were sourced from joke compilation websites, Reddit, and Youtube.

Audio

We used Elevenlabs' text-to-speech and speech-to-speech tools to generate our stimuli (ElevenLabs, 2024). We chose what we considered to be the most generic female (Matilda) and male voice (Chris) with American accents. We chose American accents because they are widely familiar in Western society due to the prominence of American media.

We created the original voice stimuli using the text-to-speech tool where the jokes were delivered by Matilda and Chris, respectively. To enhance humour in delivery, we added punctuation in the text input and used Audacity (Audacity Team, 2022) to edit audio to improve comic timing. We then created gender-transformed conditions for each original voice using the speech-to-speech tool to transform jokes spoken by Chris to a voice spoken by Matilda or vice versa. This ensured that the delivery of each joke was maintained across the apparent gender conditions. We verified the consistency of the joke delivery in the original and transformed conditions using Audacity (see OSF materials).

Measures and Procedures

We advertised a 'Joke Rating Study' aimed at investigating the humorousness of audio jokes and individual attitudes toward humour. After participants answered demographic questions, participants were randomly assigned into one of the four voice conditions. We then checked whether participants could listen to and comprehend English audio before beginning the study. To avoid any implicit cues about the true purpose of the study, all audio presented was consistent with the voice condition that participants were assigned to.

Participants were then presented with each of the 12 jokes in random order and asked to rate each joke on a scale of 1 to 7 (1 = not funny at all,3 = a little funny,5 = pretty funny,7 = very funny). Two jokes contained comprehension check questions to verify that the participant was listening to the joke. After all jokes had been rated, participants were asked to listen to a neutral statement spoken by their assigned voice: "I never have trouble sleeping when there's a storm because the sound of rain helps me to fall asleep." Participants were asked to rate their agreement (1 = strongly disagree to7 = strongly agree) on statements starting with, "Their voice sounded…" on the following traits: "attractive", "confident", "warm", "competent", "charismatic", and "masculine". The item "Their voice sounded masculine" was included as a manipulation check.

We asked participants to rate themselves (1 = strongly disagree to7 = strongly agree) on the following statements: "I am funny", "I find other people funny", "I prefer a partner who makes me laugh", "I prefer a partner who laughs at my humour", "Being funny is a masculine trait". Participants were then asked to choose the statement that they agreed with: "Men are funnier than women", "Men and women are equally funny", or "Women are funnier than men", and to select which of the following statements were true about themselves: "I have more male friends than female friends", "I have an equal number of male and female friends", or "I have more female friends than male friends".

Data analyses

For the primary analyses investigating the role of apparent gender on funniness ratings, we conducted a multilevel model analysis using the 'lme4' package (Bates et al., 2011) and a Bayesian analysis using the 'brms' package (Morey & Rouder, 2024) in R (R Development Core Team, 2021). We included random intercepts for the joke ID and participant ID as each participant rated all 12 jokes. We also included random intercepts for nationality, ethnicity, sexual orientation, and the source of recruitment. A significance threshold of α = .05 was used for all analyses.

Exploratory analyses examined whether perceived personal characteristics of the speaker varied as a function of apparent voice gender, original voice gender, and the gender of the participant. A 3-way ANOVA was conducted for each characteristic (masculinity, confidence, attractiveness, warmth, competence, and charisma). Significant ANOVA main effects were followed up using Tukey's HSD. We note that only male and female participants were retained in these analyses due to insufficient sample size in the other gender categories.

Results

In our main preregistered analysis, we investigated whether there was a main effect of the apparent gender of the voice on funniness ratings, i.e. Are jokes told by a male voice funnier than jokes told by a female voice? While controlling for participant gender, an interaction between the gender of the participant, the apparent gender of the voice, and the gender of the original voice, we found no significant difference in the funniness ratings of jokes told by apparently male and female voices, γ(SE) = -0.03(0.08), t(942.20)= -0.32, p = .745, 95% CI = [-0.19, 0.13]. A sensitivity power analysisfoot_0 revealed that 0.012 was the minimum effect size that could be detected with 80% power given a sample size of n = 966.

Using a Bayesian analysis, we found strong evidence of no difference in funniness between jokes told by a male or a female voice (BF = 0.023, % error = 0.33%)foot_1 . We found no evidence of such an interaction between the gender of the participant and the apparent gender of the voice on ratings of funniness (γ(SE) = -0.06(0.13), t(941.93)= -0.49, p = .622, 95% CI = [-0.31, 0.19]). That is, there was no gender difference in the tendency to find jokes told by male or female voices funnier. We also found no evidence of a difference in funniness when jokes were originally voiced by a male or female AI voice, γ(SE) = -0.02(0.06), t(938.73)= -0.30, p = .762, 95% CI = [-0.14, 0.10]. We found that on average, men rated jokes 0.39 units (out of 7) funnier than women, γ(SE) = 0.39(0.09), t(945.52)= 4.23, p < .001, 95% CI = [0.21, 0.57].

A manipulation check confirmed that participants accurately perceived the apparent gender of the voice: participants who were assigned an apparent male voice rated the voice significantly more masculine (M = 5.68 out of 7) than those assigned an apparently female voice (M = 1.90 out of 7) (F(1,962) = 2319.09, p < .001). There was also a main effect for the gender of the original voice such that a voice originally spoken by the male AI-voice (regardless of gender transformation) was rated more masculine (M = 3.96) than a voice originally spoken by a female AI-voice (regardless of gender transformation) (M = 3.63) (F(1,962) = 16.68, p < .001), which we attribute to differences in speech delivery. We found no evidence of an interaction between the gender of the original voice and the gender of the apparent voice on ratings of the voice's masculinity (Figure 1) (F(1,962) = 1.16, p = .281).

The presence of an interaction would indicate that the gender of the original voice was confounded with the perceived masculinity of the transformed voice, e.g. if the graveliness of Chris' voice was carried through to the female-transformed voice. Here, we found that ratings of vocal masculinity were independently influenced by the apparent gender of the voice and the delivery of the original voice. on mean ratings of voice masculinity with standard error bars.

On average, the jokes were somewhat funny, with an overall mean rating of 3.15 out of 7 (SD=1.72). The average ratings for each joke are reported in Table 1. Most men (65%) and women (79%) believed that "men and women are equally funny", while 33% of men and 10% of women agreed that "men are funnier than women", and 2% of men and 11% of women agreed that "women are funnier than men" (Table 2). While most men and women believed that men and women are equally funny, approximately a third of men believed that men are funnier than women.

In all, we only found a consistent main effect of apparent gender of the voice for perceived confidence; a male-sounding was perceived as significantly more confident than a female-sounding voice (M D = 0.18, F(1, 923) = 5.35, p = .021). There was a significant main effect of apparent gender on ratings of charisma (F(1,923) = 17.31, p < .001), but this difference was explained by a significant interaction between the apparent and original gender of the voice. A male voice transformed to a female voice was rated significantly less charismatic than the untransformed male voice (M D = 0.69, p adj < .001) while there was no significant difference between the female to male transformed voice and the untransformed female voice (p adj = .700). There were no other significant main effects for the apparent gender of the voice for warmth, competence, and attractiveness ratings (p ≥ .098) (full details are reported in the Supplementary materials).

Discussion

In a large sample (n = 966), we found strong evidence that jokes delivered by an apparently male voice were rated as no funnier than jokes delivered by an apparently female voice, even when controlling for the delivery of the jokes. Most men (65%) and women (79%) in our sample agreed that "men and women are equally funny" in contrast to past findings (Caldwell & Wojtach, 2019; Hooper et al., 2016; Mickes et al., 2012), though there was still a general tendency for men (33%) and women (10%) to agree that "men funnier than women". Also, participants' gender and the proportion of male friends they had were independently associated with agreement that "being funny is a masculine trait". Finally, our study demonstrates that using an AI speech-to-speech transformation can successfully manipulate the apparent gender of a voice while controlling for delivery.

Much research has focussed on whether men and women have different humour production ability (e.g. Greengross et al., 2020). Other studies have found that individuals misattribute the authorship of funny cartoon captions to male authors and unfunny captions to female authors (Hooper et al., 2016; Mickes et al., 2012). However, no studies have explicitly tested whether the same humorous material and delivery is regarded funnier when it is spoken by a man compared to a woman. Our large study is the first to examine this problem while controlling for joke delivery. Asking participants to rate the funniness of audio jokes allows for assessment of humour perception more in line with how humour is typically consumed interpersonally or through the media, compared with past humour studies (e.g. cartoon captioning).

In the current study, we successfully manipulated the apparent gender of the voice delivering the joke while controlling for both the content and delivery of humour. There was no evidence of an interaction between the apparent gender and original gender of the voice on ratings of voice masculinity. In other words, specific gender cues of the original speaker were not retained when voices were transformed to the opposite gender (Figure 1). A neutral statement originally delivered by a male voice (Chris) was rated more masculine than a statement originally delivered by a female voice (Matilda), indicating that delivery alone affects the perceived masculinity of a voice. We believe that using AI speech-to-speech transformations is an effective way to manipulate gender while controlling for the delivery of speech. Given that variation in speech delivery can affect the perceived voice masculinity, past studies using confederates to deliver humorous content may be confounded by differences in delivery rather than humour content alone (e.g. Evans et al., 2019; Langley & Shiota, 2023; Yeo et al., 2022).

While we report no evidence of an interaction between the original and apparent gender of the voice on ratings of vocal masculinity, our findings are limited to the voices "Chris" and "Matilda". In the earlier stages of this study we intended to use "Rachel" to voice jokes, but we soon realised that her voice was not conducive to delivering humorous content (see amendment to preregistration). It is also unclear as to whether other male and female voice combinations would speech-to-speech transform as well as "Chris" and "Matilda".

Furthermore, while the voices were sourced from real individuals{ElevenLabs, 2026 #378}, we did not ask participants to what extent they thought the voice was real and whether this could affect perceptions relating to gender -a human construct.

Another limitation of our study was that we only used one voice for each gender.

Voices are distinct and can vary across many dimensions (e.g. pitch, formant, timbre). While we selected voices that we qualitatively deemed to be generic, we cannot be certain that other voices would have produced similar results. This limitation is not dissimilar to other confederate studies that only use one confederate of each gender (e.g. Evans et al., 2019).

However, a substantial advantage of our paradigm, which attenuates the limitation, is that we effectively performed a within-delivery manipulation, such that we could apply a different voice while ensuring the same delivery of jokes. In addition, our use of one-line jokes may not represent typical humour that is communicated in an everyday interpersonal context.

However, these short jokes have the advantage of conveying humour quickly and with minimal context, which helps reduce any extra variability that longer jokes or anecdotes might introduce through complex scenarios or assumptions about the speaker.

We acknowledge that the jokes in our study lean toward a masculine humour style given their edgy or inappropriate content (Hofmann et al., 2020), and most of our jokes originated from male comedians (Table 1). Therefore, these jokes may not be congruent with humour typically expected from womenfoot_3 . Research in organisational contexts suggests that women who behave and communicate in gender-incongruent ways can face negative evaluations and discrimination (Evans et al., 2019; Rudman, 1998; Rudman et al., 2002).

However, there are also instances where women are evaluated or received more positively than men when they use humour in organisational and social contexts (Langley & Shiota, 2023; Miron-Spektor et al., 2023). Even though our study featured primarily masculine jokes, we found that neither the apparent gender of the voice nor gender differences in the delivery of the jokes affected how funny the joke was.

We found some evidence that evaluations of speaker characteristics reflect genderrelated biases and expectations. After accounting for original voice gender, participant gender, and possible interactions, apparently male voices were rated as significantly more confident than apparently female voices. This pattern is consistent with gender stereotypes that associate men with more dominant traits. However, apparent voice gender did not show consistent main effects across other assessed traits such as warmth, competence, charisma.

There was also evidence that evaluations depended on the relation between apparent gender and vocal delivery. Gender-transformed voices were rated as less attractive than untransformed voices, and an apparently female voice was rated as less warm when it was originally delivered by a male voice. These findings suggest that a mismatch between the apparent gender and delivery-related vocal features lead to more negative evaluations.

However, the effects varied across traits, and it remains unclear which particular features of speech produce such perceived mismatches. For instance, originally male voices were rated as more confident than originally female voices, indicating that delivery-related characteristics also influenced evaluations. In all, these findings highlight the value of separating apparent speaker gender from vocal delivery: comparisons between different male and female speakers cannot determine whether perceived differences reflect gendered expectations, delivery differences, or a combination of the two. Although exploratory, the present findings suggest that listeners evaluate voices not only according to apparent gender, but also according to stylistic features of speech and the perceived congruence between these features and gendered vocal cues.

A majority of our sample believed men and women to be equally funny, though there was a general tendency to believe that "men are funnier than women". And on average, both men and women disagreed with the statement "being funny is a masculine trait" with mean scores falling below the midpoint of4 = neutral (Table 3), though men had higher agreement compared to women (p < .001). Our sample had substantively different beliefs to those reported in other studies that sampled university students from Western English-speaking countries (Hooper et al. (2016): n = 228; Mickes et al. (2012): n = 72) and Mturkers (Caldwell and Wojtach (2019): n = 369). The age of our participants did not explain our observed discrepancies; neither participant age (p = .186) nor an interaction between participant age and gender were associated with agreement of "being funny is a masculine trait" (p ≥ .227). There was also no difference in these attitudes across our three recruitment sources (SONA, Prolific, word of mouth) (p = .128). Participants' beliefs about which gender is the funniest and their agreement with the statement that 'being funny is a masculine trait' was not associated with the rated funniness of jokes, regardless of whether the voice was originally male or female, or whether the voice sounded male or female.

In light of our findings, it is possible that the stereotype that "men are funnier than women" and funniness being a masculine trait could be driven by other factors beyond the immediate judgement of humour. Men report (Myers et al., 1997) and have been observed to engage in higher rates of humour attempts (Robinson & Smith-Lovin, 2001), perceive their attempts to be more humorous (Myers et al., 1997; Silvia et al., 2021), and identify as being funny more so than women (Silvia et al., 2021). These social cues, which may not necessarily signal humour quality, could artificially inflate perceptions of funniness in men. Our findings reinforce this idea; participants who reported predominantly male friends had a higher agreement with the statement that "being funny is a masculine trait", even when controlling for their gender. Individuals' perceptions of humour may be influenced by those with whom they are most likely exchange humour. Alternatively, those who believe that funniness is a masculine trait may actively choose to have more male friends (whom they would consider to be funny). Further analyses support the idea that social exposure influences stereotype agreement; participants who were randomly assigned an apparently female voice had a significantly lower agreement with "being funny is a masculine trait" while controlling for relevant variables. In either case, our findings indicate that gendered attitudes toward humour could be influenced by social contexts rather than biases in humour perception.

Whether there is any truth to the stereotype that "men are funnier than women" remains an unresolved problem. Here, we found evidence that there is no difference in the funniness of jokes delivered by male voices compared to female voices. While previous studies suggest that men outperform women on comic captioning tasks, we cannot know whether this difference generalises to a naturalistic context, and it is unclear how humour ability would be measured given the subjective and context-dependent nature of humour judgements. Some of our secondary findings suggest that gender stereotypes around humour are associated with social exposure to individuals of a particular gender, and that the apparent gender of the voice, vocal delivery, and a combination of the two affect evaluations of speakers. Future studies should further explore the topic in this vein.
