Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Social networks are important for child mental health but have been under-researched in middle childhood. In three linked studies, we co-developed the My World Game (MG) with relevant users and conducted preliminary validation. MG aims to explore the social worlds of children aged 5-12. Although usable with any child, it is designed with children who have complex social networks, such as those in care, in mind. In study 1, we conducted user testing of an MG prototype. Questionnaires with parents and interviews with children found the child's perception of their social network differed from their parent's, emphasising the importance of the child's voice. In study 2, an online version of MG was co-produced with care-experienced young people and research nurses in online and face-to-face workshops.

Transcripts of the first 12 baseline MG interviews with children participating in a UK randomised controlled trial were thematically analysed. This facilitated development of a quantitative rating scale with seven domains rated 1 (low) to 5 (high): Engagement (in the social network); Feeling Understood; Consistency (in personal relationships); Rich Connections; sense of Ownership (of people and places in the social network); Hierarchical Attachments and Confidence and Clarity (in describing the social network). In study 3, the rating scale was refined through further co-production to ensure adequate content validity.

Analysis of 28 double-rated MG interviews established inter-rater reliability. Finally, we developed a support manual and standard operating procedures for MG use in future studies.

INTRODUCTION

Supportive relationships in a child's life can positively influence their mental health and (Sen & Broadhurst, 2011) resilience (Butler et al., 2022), but social networks are under researched for children in middle childhood (~5-12years old) (Zinn et al., 2017).

Children adopted or who have been in care ("care-experienced children") have often experienced significant adversities, such as abuse and neglect, that may impact their ability and opportunity to build and maintain supportive social networks. Multiple transitions, unsupportive networks and/or the continual breakdown of supportive networks (Towse, Cooper, & John-Legare, 2019) are common, yet many care experienced children still form and maintain strong relationships (Zeanah et al., 2016).

Children who have been maltreated are at higher risk, compared to peers, of developing psychiatric disorders across the lifespan (Murphy et al., 2020) (Li et al., 2016; Murphy et al., 2020). The abuse and neglect-related psychiatric disorders, Reactive Attachment Disorder (RAD), and Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder (DSED) can further impair children's ability to form, navigate and maintain social relationships (Davidson et al., 2024; Talmon-Knuser, Gonzalez-Sala, Lacomba-Trejo, & Samper-Garcia, 2023) After severe adversity, "social thinning" can result in an increasingly narrow network of social relationships (McCrory, Foulkes, & Viding, 2022). In addition, changes of care placement can disrupt the ability to form relationships well into adulthood (Baldwin et al., 2023; Maguire et al., 2024; Mandavia et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2022) Social support, connectedness and family cohesion are important protective factors (Butler et al., 2022; Fritz et al., 2018) and meaningful social relationships are associated with mental health (Butler et al., 2022; Rodriguez et al., 2021), resilience (Graber et al., 2016; van Harmelen et al., 2021), wellbeing (Hellfeldt et al., 2020; Shaheen et al., 2019) and positive functioning (Harmelen et al., 2017). Multisystemic perspectives on resilience have focused on how individuals identify and use resources around them to cope with ongoing challenges, of which social relationships form an important part (Ungar et al., 2023). Middle childhood, as the child's focus shifts from parental relationships towards peers, is a crucial time in which to support children develop connections, which can act as a buffer against potential effects of adversity (Brent et al., 2014; Cohen & Wills, 1985). An evaluation of the child's social world should therefore be included in clinical and research assessments, both as a measure of risk and as a predictor of treatment outcome. This is particularly true in high-risk populations, such as children who have experienced abuse or neglect and those who have grown up in care.

EXISTING MEASURES

It is challenging to capture children's social worlds when the child lives in a complex environment, for example where the child is in the care system (Magalhães & Calheiros, 2017). In some existing tools, children are asked to describe a "father" or "mother", who might not be the primary care giver of a child in care. Other tools, have focused on a single domain, such as kin or peers or use stigmatising languages such as "contact" to talk about relationships with parents a child does not live with (Healy et al., 2024). The recently developed PROMIS paediatric peer and family relationships scales use open language (such as "family"/"other kids"), but may lack the flexibility to adapt to a child's specific and complex social contexts (Blackwell et al., 2022). Recently, calls for community engagement in design and implementation of research have increased (Burgess et al., 2023). We saw a need to codevelop a measure of social networks with young people at high risk of experiencing challenges in their social networks -those with experience of the care system.

We describe the development and preliminary validation of the My World Game (MG) a new tool for examining social connectedness in middle childhood co-produced by researchers, young people with care experience, and research nurses. The tool was created in 3 linked studies: initial development and user testing (study 1); co-production of the online version of MG with care-experienced young people and research nurses and preliminary development of a quantitative rating scale (study 2); refinement and testing of the quantitative rating scale (study 3). This measure would be suitable to explore social networks in any child but was specifically developed with those children who have complex social worlds in mind.

STUDY 1: DEVELOPMENT AND PILOTING METHODS

Participants were recruited by email from a database of people who had previously agreed to receive invitations to studies. Inclusion criteria were: being age 8 to 11 and developing typically.

The University of Glasgow Medical, Veterinary and Life Sciences School Ethics committee approved the study.

Interviews were aided by visual methods, audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. Four concentric circles were printed onto felt fabric to produce a "fuzzy felt" game. In the innermost circle the word 'You' was printed. Additionally, icons representing people, buildings and other aspects of lives were printed on felt and cut into square tokens. These icons have hook-and-loop strips attached to them, so that the participant could place them on the concentric circle as appropriate. The people icons were very simplistic representations, consisting of a colourful body and a black oval head.

Procedure

During a home visit, the participating parent was given a parent questionnaire to fill in while the child was being interviewed, asking them to write in the names of the people who they believed were important to the children into 3 concentric circles ("Who are the people that you believe are most important to them?").

The child interview was guided by a semi-structured interview schedule adapted from the convoy mapping procedure developed by Kahn and Antonucci (Kahn et al., 1980). Children identified sources of support for different scenarios (e.g.: "Who would you want to be with most if you hurt yourself?"). The questions were aided by the concentric circles printed on felt. The children were asked to place the tokens on this 'social map'. The inner most circles included "any person or persons that you feel so close to that it's hard to imagine life without them". The middle circle included "people to whom you may not feel quite that close, but who are still very important to you". The outer most circle included "people who you haven't already mentioned who are close enough and important enough in your life that they should be placed in your diagram". Lastly, the children were asked questions about their neighbourhood, living conditions and transport options and asked how much each affect their relationships with others. Interviews lasted ~10 to 20 minutes and were transcribed verbatim.

Analysis

Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) was used since IPA is well suited to making sense of the personal and social world (Smith & Osborn, 2003).

Participants

Four female participants aged 8-11, two white, two mixed race (White and Asian) took part with their mothers.

Themes

All participants were quick to understand the task and appeared engaged with the game.

While some were shy at the start, others were happy to start sharing quickly.

In every case the parent placed more people (especially extended family) on their map of their child's social world than the child placed on the felt map.

The children showed a rich understanding of their social world and took care with decisions of who to place and where to place them.

Identifying and using support.

Most participants confidently identified sources of support and examples of when they used it. Friends were as frequently selected as family, for support. The individuals named as being available for support were more often in the inner circle then the middle circle.

Children often identified living close by, length of relationship, frequency of contact and sharing activities as factors as to why a relationship was closer or less close.

The interview schedule enabled a rich discussion, and the visual aid was intuitive for the children to represent their social world. DISCUSSION

The My World Game (MG) has been co-produced with care experienced children and research nurses, ensuring its content validity, feasibility and acceptability for both the target population and administrators. The MG uses open language and visuals, acknowledging the powerful role these can play to stimulate children's descriptions of their relationships. It is child-centred, allowing the child to take an active role in defining who and what belongs in their social world. This allows for a simultaneous exploration of related concepts such as quality of relationships, feelings of belonging, connectedness and social capital. User-friendly guidance ensures that the implementation of the tool is feasible across research and clinical settings.

Interviews and the co-production sessions showed that consistent and secure relationships were important to child participants. Often, and in line with previous research (Varnish et al., 2025), the primary caregivers were identified as key relationships, however, not exclusively.

RATING SCALE

The My World Game Rating Scale assesses social connectedness across six domains, with the key indicators of social connection and quality of relationships generated solely from the subjective experiences of participants.

The domains are: Engagement, Feeling Understood, Consistency, Rich Network of Connections, Ownership, Hierarchical Attachments and Confidence and Clarity. All indicators were mentioned often and clearly have significance for the child's perception of their social world.

Engagement is a key indicator of enthusiasm for, or reluctance towards, taking part in activities. It also includes the ability of the child to engage in fun and free play, with a sense of safety (or lack of). Research shows that a sense of safety is important for positive relationships (Santos et al., 2023) and that fun and play promote wellbeing and development (Sando et al., 2021).

Feeling Understood captures, the propensity for the child's relationships to be a place where joy is shared, support is offered and care is provided. These indicators have been consistently identified as essential for positive relationships by children in care (Crumé et al., 2021; Logan-Greene et al., 2023; Sprecher et al., 2021). Consistency was named, by child participants and YPAG collaborators, as a key indicator of the quality of relationships throughout. Positive relationships with people, places or animals are characterised by reliability, predictability and reciprocity (Hawk et al., 2018).

Rich Network of Connections captures the importance of a variety of connections that are important in childhood. While family relationships are regarded to have greater importance over peer relationships in early childhood, peer relationships and relationships with pets are part of a rich and protective social world (Muela et al., 2017).

Ownership describes a sense of belonging to the places and spaces a child occupies and participates in, and crucially, a belonging of those to the child. Children in unstable environments, including care experienced children, often move or experience multiple placements (Connell et al., 2006; Konijn et al., 2019). Multiple placements are associated with problems in forming and sustaining relationships, and with poorer health and wellbeing (Maguire et al., 2024). A strong sense of ownership and active participation in relationships therefore is an important protective factor.

Hierarchical Attachments refers to the degree to which the child participant went specifically to primary caregivers when distressed or wanting to share joy. It is an important indicator, especially for children at risk of Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder (DSED), characterised by indiscriminate behaviour (Davidson et al., 2024). The child's struggles to differentiate between primary caregiving and other relationships could potentially delay the formation of secure attachments (Minnis, 2018; Schoemaker et al., 2020; Zeanah et al., 2002).

Lastly, the Confidence and Clarity captures the fluency, clarity and agency with which the child describes their social world. Secure attachment is linked with a child's confidence to explore unknown situations and relationships (Ainsworth & Bell, 1970) as well as with narrative fluency when describing caregiving relationships (Gastelle & Kerns, 2022), so this indicator therefore being a proxy measure of the child's perceived security.

Each of these domains demonstrated reasonable inter-rater reliability. User testing allowed us to develop standard operating procedures for future MG use.

CO-PRODUCTION

The co-production of the tool ensured the tool is sensitive and relevant to children's lived experience. Further, YPAG participation allowed triangulation with research team thematic analysis and offered a unique insight into the transcripts. This influenced how and what decisions were made whilst creating the rating scale and during its refinement. User testing with research nurses enabled ongoing refinement of the rating scale and supporting material for increase acceptability to both administrators and child participants.

PRACTICAL AND CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

Our findings have wide ranging implications for research and clinical settings. Existing measures of social relationships might not be appropriate for children and young people with care experience, as their social worlds contain complexities underrepresented in previous tools. Co-production with young people added significant value. Co-design with research nurses was also crucial in ensuring the practicality and feasibility of using such a tool.

LIMITATIONS

Firstly, while the online version of the tool increases reach, it also led to the presence of caregivers during the interview. This, at times, influenced the answers of the child. The technical limitations of Microsoft Teams and Canva mean some nurses or participants might need to additional support to use this software effectively. Lastly, despite the interview guide, the style of interviewing varied between research nurses, leading to potentially different child responses. Nonetheless, the themes emerged across all interviews, and all interviews could be rated, indicating that despite the differences there was sufficient similarity. The differences in interview styles also reflect a variability that will likely exist in real-world implementation.

CONSTRAINTS ON GENERALITY

This paper presents a co-produced interview and rating scale that intends to measure the quality of a Their perspectives enriched the interview and rating scale guide and helped ensure that the MG remained as open as possible to capturing social worlds reflecting different realities. We therefore expect the tool to be appropriate for a variety of contexts in which the perspective of a child on their social world is helpful. Further studies in different cultural contexts are needed to validate the use of this tool.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the My World Game (MG) offers an open yet structured way to assess a child's perception of their social world and relationships. This tool can be used to assess a child's risk or resilience factors to mental health issues, as well as monitor success of interventions.

Development of the MG highlighted the need for the voices of children to be heard and integrated into research about them. Future research should include the MG in longitudinal studies and trials to assess how well it can monitor changes in a child's social world. Engagement

High levels of engagement are observed when the child can actively participate in a variety of activities and relationships. This will be visible by the differing relationship groups present in the child's map (e.g., immediate family, sibling relationships, friends, school/club relationships, extended family, etc). Children who cherish the ability to engage in a variety of fun activities and have the opportunity for free play exhibit high levels on this scale. People, places, community clubs and pets are seen to be facilitators of fun and a rich collection of these on the child's map is a critical facet for high levels of engagement. Feelings of safety commonly encourage engagement in fun and free play and is an important factor in high levels of this scale. Where the child feels safe and secure engaging in relationships and groups, they may 'poke fun' or 'have a laugh' within the safety of specific important relationship. Engaging and participating in enabling spaces which encourage the notable relationships in the child's life to flourish, meaning that engaging with and having placing meaning of spaces are crucial indicators to high levels in this scale.

Children enjoy sharing and exhibiting the different possessions which bring them fun as a means of engaging in new and existing relationships and appear to use this to build stronger connections. As such, personal space and possessions can allow children to actively engage with others through the things that interest them. Technology can also provide a mean by which children can engage with others in a fun and free manner.

Feeling Understood

Feeling understood occurs when the child has a clear understanding of who and what they can and wish to share with others. Children evidencing high levels on this scale will utilise different individuals in differing ways (e.g., participants may naturally seek caregiver support in times of sadness but engage others when sharing joy). Children able to identify the role specific individuals within larger relationship groups (friends, whole family etc) score highly within this scale, with those who cannot see beyond the larger relationship groups in which they exist scoring lower. Where specific relationships appear on the map is not of great significance to feeling understood, for example, children my place a friend in the outer circle of their map but identify that friend as someone who they would choose to share joy or successes with. Feeling understood therefore can be harboured within various relationships at varying levels with individual relationship qualities being crucial for high levels on this scale.

Having peers at the same age and stage on the child's map creates a platform for connection and feeling understood as the context in which humour, successes and challenges exist better comprehended by those at similar life stages or with similar life experiences.

The child who can clearly articulate who and why they share joy with is seen as a critical element to high levels of feeling understood as the context of the joy is perhaps better understood by those who have had or are going through similar life experiences, something which builds connection. Whereas consolation in times of sadness does not necessarily require context, it requires time and comfort.

Indices of high levels of feeling understood may include, but are not limited to: 1.

Multiple strong relationships which understand the context of good news.

2. Friends with similar experiences and sense of humour.

3. Differentiating between support networks or individuals to share good news and/or have fun with.

4. Knowing who provides emotional support.

5. Fluency in describing self and relationships.

6. A variety of trusting relationships.

Indices of lower levels of feeling understood may include, but are not limited to: 1.

High level relationships only (e.g., groups relationships as 'friends' or 'family') and not discussing specificity of individual relationships.

2. Not able to articulate what relationships provided emotionally.

3. Sparce map.

4. Withdrawn, hesitancy and/or avoiding answering questions.

Consistency

This scale reflects the degree of consistency in the child's life. In general, high consistency scores are given where there is evidence of lasting and consistent places and people in the child's life. Such consistency is often reflected in reoccurring contact in the proximal relationships for the child. As such, home and school are commonly enabling spaces for these relationships to flourish. The main indicator of consistency for participants researched was their caregivers. High levels in this scale are often reflected in the caregiver relationship, these relationships may be described as supportive in some form and placed in nearest circle to them on their map. However, this is not in isolation and participants may evidence high levels of consistency through relationships with places, such as their home, school and family/friendship groups.

Despite consistency commonly being evidenced through the caregiver relationship, it can also appear for some children in diverse ways, coming beyond their caregiver, in the form of longdistance or community relationships with peers and adult role models.

Consistency may also be evidenced through challenging interactions during the interviews.

Consistent relationships often set boundaries (to which participants respond) and/or provide a platform to practice conflict resolution. High levels of consistency in the participants lives result in the child feeling able and confident to explore the world independently.

The key concept in scoring consistency is the degree to which the child reflects and describes the reoccurring and lasting relationships with the people and places evidenced on their map.

Indices of high levels of consistency may include, but are not limited to:

1. Verbal descriptions of current consistent relationships.

2. Verbal descriptions of current consistent places.

3. Reflection on the past meaning of consistent relationship with people and place.

4. Hope of desire for meaningful relationships to continue in the future.

5. Descriptions of reciprocity and love in relationships.

6. Being unprompted or speaking with confidence about the meaning of relationships with people, places and/or animals.

7. Confident to explore world outside the home, independently from parents.

8. Places described as enabling (e.g. I get to spend time with my best friend at school).

9. The identification of role models present in the child's life.

10. Clear boundaries and the child taking part in dialogue to resolve conflict.

Indices of lower levels of consistency may include, but are not limited to: 1.

No depth of meaning within relationships.

Ownership

The reciprocation of love and affection is a marker for ownership being present in the child's life. Alongside this, the ownership of relationships can be reflected in the child's language (describing relationships as 'my mum, my cat, my friend' etc as opposed to simply describing them as 'teacher, auntie' etc) exhibiting that the child is playing an active role in these relationships. Participants may also have a special bond with animals, and these can be seen as a way in which participants can express and receive love. Animals can provide more than just the reciprocation of love: children value the opportunity to be a caregiver (e.g., thinking about what animals need when designing their map). This may also be transferred to the important human relationships in participants lives. Ownership can be evidenced when the child takes time to consider the people they put on their map (e.g. I will make you that colour because it is your favourite or I will put you near this building as you like to go there) although, this may also be elicited by the participant when discussing the achievements of 'my friends' (or any other important relationship), such as recognising abilities at school, sports etc. Furthermore, ownership may occur visually with the child enacting the caregiver with any adult present during the interview (passing them something they need or 'telling them off'). This may also happen with animals, if a child is stressed or feeling under pressure from a specific interview question, they may seek the emotional support of their pet.

Excitement and enthusiasm when talking about people and pets.

2. Ownership of relationships, e.g., by using "my".

3. Volunteering information about people and pets.

4. Caring for/considering the feelings/desires of other beings.

5. Animals present during the interview with the child.

6. Reciprocation of love and affection.

Lack of adjectives to describe people, places and/or animals.

Hierarchical Attachments

This scale represents the manner in which the child differentiates between the meaning of the relationships in their lives. Children may group relationships (e.g., mum and dad together, auntie and uncles together) however, high levels of hierarchical relationships occur when the child gives meaning of specific individual relationships. Lower levels on this scale are identified whereby the child is indiscriminate between these groups (e.g., everyone close to the centre of the map or trying to include the interviewer within the map) or even struggling to differentiate between relationship groups and the individual value of each relationship.

Hierarchical attachment is not measured by how many people are on the map but instead on the value of each relationship. Higher levels of hierarchical attachment may be visually evident, with the child having differing relationships placed across the multiple levels of their map. A crucial element of hierarchical attachment is the child's ability to identify the relationship that provide emotional solace, particularly when hurt, upset or emotionally vulnerable.

Indices of high levels of hierarchical attachments may include, but are not limited to:

1. Having different levels of trust and safety with people.

2. Identification of how and why they receive support.

3. Identification of people with differing relationships to me.

4. Able to articulate why specific relationships occur on different level of the map.

This scale provides the child the basis to engage in relationships with people and places and build meaning without worry or anxiety navigating this independence. Visually children may exhibit this by having agency in their engagement with the interviewer, providing clear and concise responses and requiring little or no prompts from the caregiver. However, the child may also use any potential interviewer or caregiver interactions as a means of asserting themselves (disagreeing with caregiver on who should be on the map and/or detailing with confidence why certainly individuals, places or animals are present). Individual play can be seen as a way from the child to be confident to remove themselves from a group setting to recharge with the aim of connecting with others in the future, as the best version of themselves. The child may also provide clarity on the type of play or generally engagement they enjoy and know how, where and when this can be sought to allow them to develop their wellbeing. This may also be exhibited by the child showcasing the toys, possession, drawing and/or pets (that have meaning) to the interviewer or the caregiver. coding system consists of seven scales (Engagement, Feeling Understood, Consistency, Rich Network of Connections, Ownership, Hierarchical Attachments and Confidence and Clarity) each reflect levels of social connection for children in permanent foster or adoptive care placements with MAPP. All seven scales are rated on a five-point rating scale and based on concurrent review of the interview, transcript and/or video recording. Specific scores are based on the interviewer's weighing of high-and low-level indicators of; Engagement, Feeling Understood, Consistency, Rich Network of Connections, Ownership, Hierarchical Attachments and Confidence and Clarity, that the participants evidence vocally or physically. Midpoint scores are acceptable under this system and a comment box under each scale provides interviewers the ability to add context to their chosen score. Definitions of each scale and example of scale items are included later within this document. Indicators for each scale may occur at different points during the interview. Indicators do not necessarily relate to specific questions.
