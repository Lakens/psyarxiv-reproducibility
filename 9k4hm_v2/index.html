Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

Who is the language learner represented in language acquisition research? Despite the prevalence and diversity of multilingual childhoods, theories of early language development remain grounded in monolinguals from Euro-American contexts, while multilingual research has concentrated on limited populations/settings. This review examines multilingual language development during the first three years across Asia and Africa, attending to diversity within and between regions. Research on input, speech perception, word recognition, and vocabulary indicates that multilingual infants flexibly adapt to linguistic ecologies but vary in developmental pathways. We argue that multilingualism should be conceptualized not as a categorical characteristic, but as a multidimensional ecology structured by language combinations, caregiving arrangements, social functions, socioeconomic conditions, and colonial histories. We conclude with a research agenda that broadens empirical and linguistic coverage, improves measurement of multilingual experiences, and centers community-informed priorities. A globally adequate science of language development requires building theory from children's diverse multilingual realities.

Highlights

• Current theories of language development underrepresent multilingual childhoods in Asia and Africa.

• Evidence across early language domains reveals diverse developmental pathways shaped by multilingual language ecologies.

• The world's language learners are more diverse than current theories: building a global science of language development requires moving beyond monolingual and Western assumptions.

Child Multilingualisms Beyond WEIRD Contexts: Evidence from Asia and Africa

In studying language development, a basic question is who is the language learner? Much of the field has implicitly answered that question by centering the monolingual child from Euro-American contexts as the de facto norm. Yet this norm presents a striking contrast to the demographic realities and contexts that most children inhabit. Notably, Asia and Africa are extraordinarily diverse regions linguistically, culturally, historically, and politically. These populous regions of the world are where most of the world's language learners live, although they remain barely represented in published studies on language learning (Kidd & Garcia, 2022; Singh et al., 2022). Instead, it is children from specific Euro-American contexts whose trajectories are most often narrated in textbooks, incorporated into assessments, and treated in clinical and therapeutic settings as reflective of the normative course of language development (Namboodiripad et al., 2026).

Although there has been an increased focus on understanding the multilingual learner, this literature has centered world regions and contexts within those regions that are wellrepresented in developmental research (Rocha-Hidalgo & Barr, 2023; see also Snijders et al., 2026). This narrows the representation of multilingual learners to those localized in North America and regions of Western Europe (Bylund et al., 2024). In effect, this narrow evidentiary base has both empirical and theoretical consequences. Empirically, it artificially narrows the empirical foundation of the field. Theoretically, it reproduces an essentialist view of language learners that obscures meaningful variation in children's experience. This in turn hinders scientific progress by normalizing highly specific forms of language learning that are not globally representative (Namboodiripad et al., 2026). This issue is especially relevant to the significant underrepresentation of Asia and Africa in the literature because these regions encompass much of the world's linguistic diversity: multilingualism within these regions has been shaped by social and institutional histories that differ in important ways from those of the contexts that dominate the literature. Although estimates vary according to how languages are classified, Africa is home to approximately onethird of the world's languages, and Asia contains more than 2,300 living languages; together, the two regions account for well over half of the world's linguistic diversity (Eberhard et al., 2024; UNESCO, 2025). In many parts of Asia and Africa, children grow up navigating local, regional, national, liturgical, and colonial languages, with those languages often serving distinct roles across home, school, religion, government, and media (Bhatia & Ritchie, 2012; Kamwangamalu, 2012; Lee & Li, 2013). These language ecologies have been profoundly shaped by colonial and postcolonial histories, which have structured language hierarchies, access to schooling and thus to national languages, and the prestige associated with particular linguistic codes (Makoni & Pennycook, 2023; Mohanty, 2010; Thiong'o, 1986). Additionally, many settings across Africa and Asia are characterized by sociolinguistic superdiversity: multilingual practices are distributed across speakers, relationships, and institutions and are shaped by mobility, political history, and unequal access to linguistic resources (Blommaert & Rampton, 2011; Lanza, 2021).

Bypassing these populations and contexts means bypassing opportunities to truly understand language development at a global scale.

The urgency of orienting towards Asia and Africa as sites of discovery about multilingualism is heightened by ongoing and projected demographic and migratory change (Singh & Killen, submitted). United Nations population projections indicate that over the coming decades Africa will experience the most rapid population growth in the world, while Asia will remain the most populous region even as its growth slows (United Nations, 2024). Over the next 30 years, the population of sub-Saharan Africa is projected to increase by roughly 79%, reaching 2.2 billion, while Asia will continue to contain a very large share of the world's children and families (United Nations, 2024). At the same time, UNICEF has documented record levels of child displacement globally, with 47.2 million children displaced by conflict and violence by the end of 2023 and further displacement in 2024 associated with intensifying conflicts in places such as Sudan, Myanmar, and the State of Palestine (UNICEF, 2025). These shifts impact language science because they will increase the visibility, scale, and diversity of multilingual childhoods both within Asia and Africa and across diasporic communities around the world.

They will also reshape language contact as migration brings children into new social and institutional configurations and gives rise to increasingly hybridized forms of multilingualism organized across borders, communities, and shifting senses and sites of belonging (Singh & Killen, submitted). The populations most underrepresented in theories of language development are therefore becoming ever more central to the present and future contexts of language learning. The implications of this underrepresentation extend beyond the populations to which existing findings can be generalized. Research conducted predominantly in Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) contexts can also shape which features of language development are treated as theoretically central. These concerns operate at three related but distinct levels: the underrepresentation of multilingual populations limits the diversity of evidence available; the resulting gaps in evidence leave the generalizability of existing findings across linguistic and sociocultural contexts insufficiently tested; and they constrain opportunities to evaluate whether current theoretical accounts adequately explain language development across diverse multilingual environments. For example, models and measures that prioritize direct speech from a primary caregiver may inadequately capture environments in which infants encounter language through multiple caregivers and overheard interactions. Similarly, characterizing multilingual experience primarily in terms of exposure to individual languages may overlook how speakers, interactional contexts, and the social functions of languages organize children's opportunities for learning. These concerns do not necessarily invalidate existing accounts of language acquisition; rather, they raise questions about the conditions under which their assumptions and predictions generalize.

Evidence from multilingual populations across Asia and Africa provides an opportunity to investigate these questions. We use the term multidimensional language ecology to emphasize that multilingual experience encompasses not only the languages children hear and the amount of exposure to each, but also the people and contexts through which exposure occurs, the functions and status of different languages, and the socioeconomic and historical conditions that shape language use. This perspective shifts the focus from treating multilingualism as a single developmental category to investigating which dimensions of children's linguistic environments predict particular developmental outcomes, and under what conditions. The theoretical contribution of this review is therefore not simply to broaden the populations represented in developmental research, but to identify how variation in multilingual experience can help test the generality of existing explanations and motivate more precise, context-sensitive predictions about language development. Thus, this review proceeds from the premise that centering multilingual development in Asia and Africa is necessary for a robust and generalizable science of early language acquisition. Following this brief historical framing, we turn to an empirical review focused on the first three years of life, a period that is especially informative because it captures the earliest stages of input processing, speech perception, word recognition, and vocabulary growth, before later schooling and formal literacy more heavily reorganize children's language experience. Within that review, we examine the nature of multilingual input, including family structures and networks of care; responsiveness to input; early speech perception, including discrimination, perceptual narrowing, and word-object associations; word recognition, especially preferential-looking work; and early word learning, including vocabulary inventories and related measurement challenges. We then consider what this literature means for broader theories of language development, with particular attention to the limits of monolingual defaults, the barriers that have constrained progress in this area, and possible solutions for building a more globally representative theory of language acquisition. By focusing on Asia and Africa, while recognizing their immense internal diversity, this paper argues that multilingualism should be treated as a central empirical and theoretical lens on the question of exactly who the language learner is.

Literature Search and Scope

We used a targeted literature search to identify research on early multilingual language development in Asian and African contexts, focusing on children aged 0-36 months. Studies with broader age ranges were eligible if their samples included children within this range.

Searches were conducted using Google Scholar, combining broad terms (e.g., multilingualism, bilingualism) with terms related to the developmental domains covered in this review (e.g., language input, responsiveness to input, speech perception, word recognition, and word learning). We also used regional terms (e.g., Asia, Africa) and the names of individual countries to identify relevant research. No publication-date restrictions were applied. To identify additional work, we examined the reference lists of relevant publications and circulated a call for relevant studies and preprints through the COGDEVSOC listserv. This review was designed to synthesize and illustrate research on early multilingual development across diverse Asian and African contexts, rather than to provide an exhaustive systematic review of all studies. The literature discussed was selected for its relevance to the developmental domains and broader theoretical questions addressed in the review. Structure of Language Input and Caregiving

Early language input in many Asian and African contexts is characterized by distributed caregiving and exposure to multiple languages within and across speakers. Importantly, these environments vary substantially not only from monolingual contexts, but also within multilingual populations themselves, raising questions about how differences in input shape early language development and how methodological tools and findings from more homogeneous, WEIRD samples generalize to these settings.

Among the Gusii of Kenya, for instance, LeVine and LeVine (1988) documented a caregiving model organized around physical proximity and rapid responsiveness to infant distress -through holding, soothing, and breastfeeding -rather than verbal engagement. Even during breastfeeding, mothers rarely looked at or spoke to their infants, consistent with a parenting ethos oriented toward producing a quiet, manageable child rather than a verbally stimulated one. Gusii mothers were very responsive to their infants' distress signals, but not to nondistressed vocalizations (LeVine, 2004; Richman et al., 1992). Similarly, Mesman et al. (2018) observed that in forager and agrarian communities in the Philippines, the Republic of Congo, and Mali, sensitive caregiving was predominantly expressed through physical means rather than through the verbal and affective channels that dominate Western conceptions of responsive interaction. Taken together, these findings suggest that in many non-Western caregiving environments, infants receive input structured quite differently from the face-to-face, conversationally rich interactions that standard models of early language acquisition implicitly assume.

These differences in caregiving practice are partly grounded in parental ethnotheories: culturally shared beliefs about child-rearing goals that shape the everyday environments infants inhabit (Harkness & Super, 1992) which vary substantially across cultures (Harkness et al., 2010). In many African and Asian contexts, where caregiving goals are more commonly oriented around physical wellbeing, obedience, and social integration, ethnotheories may similarly depress the centrality of direct verbal engagement with preverbal infants (LeVine, 2004).

Additionally, much of the developmental literature, particularly in WEIRD contexts, assumes a model in which primary caregiving is concentrated within the nuclear family, with parents serving as the dominant source of language input (Fernald et al., 2017). In contrast, research from Asia and Africa has long documented the prevalence of alloparenting, in which caregiving responsibilities are distributed across a wider network of individuals, including grandparents, siblings, extended family members, domestic helpers, and other community members (Dagan & Sagi-Schwartz, 2021; Keller et al., 2005; Lamm et al., 2023; LeVine, 2004; Seymour, 2013; Singh, Phneah et al., 2022). In such contexts, infants are not only exposed to a greater number of speakers, but also to greater variability in how and when language is used, as well as in which languages are spoken by different members of the caregiving network.

Recent work from African contexts illustrates the implications of this structure for multilingual input. Omane, Benders, and Boll-Avetisyan (2025), for example, examined language exposure in Ghana, where multilingualism is the norm. They administered caregiver interviews and collected language exposure logbooks from 121 families of 3-12-month-old infants in Accra. Their findings indicate that infants are exposed to between two and six languages on a regular basis, with no consistent use of one-parent-one-language (OPOL) strategies commonly documented in WEIRD populations. In fact, most people spoke more than one language to the infants sampled. Moreover, 14% of infants were exposed to a language not spoken by their parents via older siblings, and 26% were exposed to one or two additional languages from relatives other than parents. Language input is thus distributed across a network of caregivers, including parents, siblings, and extended family members, each of whom may use multiple languages with the infant. Notably, the study also documented substantial exposure to Ghanaian English, often occurring through indirect input such as overheard speech rather than direct caregiver-infant interaction. English is Ghana's colonial language and remains the official language used in formal education and government business, reflecting its continued institutional role within Ghana's multilingual society. This pattern also highlights how colonial languages can shape children's language environments in multilingual African contexts, where their institutional status may contribute to exposure beyond direct caregiver-infant interaction.

Evidence from Malaysia further illustrates how multilingual input is shaped by both caregiving arrangements and parents' language choices. Low et al. (2010) surveyed 100 mothers of children aged 6-36 months from middle-to upper-middle-socioeconomic backgrounds in the Chinese community of Penang. The mothers reported using multiple languages, including Mandarin, English, Malay, Hokkien, and Cantonese, across different social domains.

Importantly, language use with children did not simply mirror language use within the household: although 51% of mothers reported using Hokkien at home with other family members, only 21% reported using it with their children. In contrast, English use increased from 38% with other family members to 73% with children. Furthermore, 70% of mothers reported using more than one language with their children, most commonly Mandarin and English. These patterns suggest that parents' language choices for their children may differ from their own everyday language practices, reflecting the perceived educational and socioeconomic value of particular languages. The study also highlights the role of other caregivers: only 33% of children were reported to be cared for by their mothers during weekdays, while others were primarily cared for by grandparents, babysitters, or nursery staff. The study illustrates how input in multilingual households can be shaped by the intersecting influences of caregiving networks, parental language choices, and broader sociolinguistic hierarchies.

Despite growing recognition of such variability, the empirical literature on early language input in Asia and Africa remains limited. As a result, existing models often fail to capture key dimensions of variability that characterize multilingual environments, including the number of languages in circulation, the distribution of those languages across speakers, and the balance between direct and indirect forms of input. This gap underscores the need for approaches that explicitly account for diversity within multilingual populations, rather than treating multilingualism as a uniform condition.

Responsiveness to Input

Responsiveness to Input learning outcomes is a key question in research on early multilingual development.

How children's sensitivity to variation in linguistic input influences early language

Much of the existing literature has implicitly treated North American infant-directed speech (IDS) as a normative or even optimal form of input although this literature draws disproportionately from monolingual learners from specific Euro-American contexts (Weinstein & Baldwin, 2024).

Related assumptions often extend beyond speech style itself to broader models of interaction, emphasizing one-to-one exchanges between a primary caregiver (typically the mother) and the infant in conjunction with object manipulation and labeling (Cristia, 2023; see also Little et al., 2016). In multilingual environments, however, input may be distributed across several caregivers and speakers, many of whom use more than one language with the child (Low et al., 2010; Omane, Benders, & Boll-Avetisyan, 2025). Infants' responses to speech also appear to reflect both broadly shared sensitivities and adaptation to the structure of their experience: bilingual and monolingual infants show comparable overall preferences for IDS, while the strength of bilingual infants' preference varies with exposure to the language variety used in the stimuli (Byers-Heinlein et al., 2021).

One possibility is that infants' responsiveness to speech may be robust across diverse linguistic environments. Tsui et al. (2026) examined African infants' responses to North American English IDS and found that both monolingual and bilingual infants aged 3 to 15 months, drawn from multilingual environments across six African countries, showed a preference for North American English IDS over adult-directed speech. The result admits several interpretations: the preference could reflect properties of IDS that are shared across communities, familiarity with IDS-like features in local caregiving, or a novelty response to an unfamiliar register, and the design does not distinguish among them. However, it is important to note that the stimulus itself is rooted in a North American linguistic register that many infants are not regularly exposed to in naturalistic settings. As such, the findings raise broader questions about the extent to which developmental research continues to rely on WEIRD-derived benchmarks when investigating populations whose linguistic experiences differ substantially from those contexts.

This approach connects to prior debates surrounding the use of Euro-American standards to investigate language learning in Africa. Although Weber et al. (2017) did not examine multilingual infants specifically, the study is relevant because it tested monolingually-based assumptions about language promotion in a multilingual African context. 1 In rural Senegal, the researchers evaluated a parenting program that encouraged caregivers to increase verbal engagement with young children, drawing on North American evidence linking child-directed speech to language development. The program increased caregiver talk and improved several language-related outcomes. However, Morelli et al. (2018) argued that the intervention was grounded in assumptions derived primarily from Western, middle-class populations and may have undervalued local caregiving practices. They emphasized that communication and learning in many communities occur through participation in multiparty interactions, observation, gesture, and other nonverbal forms of engagement rather than through intensive one-to-one verbal exchanges between mothers and infants. This debate highlights that the social organization of communication shapes the forms of input available to children and that, in multilingual communities, input may be especially complex because it is distributed across multiple speakers, languages, activities, and interactional settings. Under these conditions, the assumption that a single form or quantity of input is universally optimal for maximizing infants' responsiveness is unlikely to be adequate; both research questions and intervention targets should instead be grounded in locally organized communicative practices.

Another theme is that infants' input environments are themselves highly variable, and that this variability is structured by everyday interactional contexts. El Amin and Brown (2024) examined the child-directed speech of Lebanese mothers of bilingual children across book 1 Wolof communities exist within a highly multilingual national ecology. Wolof is Senegal's principal lingua franca, French is the official language and dominates formal education and administration, and languages such as Pulaar, Sereer, Joola, Mandingue, and Soninke are also widely used. Code-switching and mixed Wolof-French practices are common, although individual and community language repertoires vary considerably. reading, toy play, and parent-selected activities. They found that both the quantity and quality of input differed by context, with book reading eliciting greater lexical diversity and higher rates of labeling and attention-directing language, while interactions during parent-selected activities involved more action directives. More directly relevant to multilingual input -despite instructions to speak only Arabic, language mixing, or code-switching, accounted for approximately 21% of mothers' utterances. Although the study did not examine infants' responses to these differences, it highlights the variability of the input available to them, both in its communicative functions and in its language composition. This raises the question of whether infants' responsiveness varies depending on the interactional context and characteristics of the speech they encounter.

Taken together, these findings suggest that responsiveness to input cannot be understood in terms of a single, privileged input type that gives rise to a uniform developmental trajectory.

Instead, infants appear to be attuned to the specific patterns of variability that define their linguistic environments. At the same time, the empirical literature remains limited and is based on a relatively small number of studies. This raises important questions about generalizability: whether observed preferences reflect universal properties of early perception or are shaped by methodological choices that do not fully capture the diversity of multilingual input.

Speech Perception

Early exposure to multiple languages raises the question of whether the trajectory of speech perception -how infants detect and become sensitive to the sound contrasts used in language -is altered. In particular, researchers have examined perceptual narrowing -the process by which infants become increasingly attuned to contrasts that are relevant in their linguistic environment while losing sensitivity to non-native distinctions (Werker & Tees, 1984).

The ability to discriminate sounds that distinguish words has largely been studied using vowels and consonants in Indo-European languages and among monolinguals (Kalashnikova et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2014; Singh, Rajendra, & Mazuka, 2022). However, many languages in the world, especially those used in Asia (e.g., Mandarin, Vietnamese, Thai) and Africa (e.g., Zulu, Akan/Twi-Fante, Yoruba) are tonal, meaning that beyond vowels and consonants, lexical tone is used to distinguish words (Best, 2019; Yip, 2002). Evidence from multilingual populations, and from languages that rely on other phonological dimensions such as lexical tone and vowel harmony, suggests a more complex picture: rather than following a single, uniform trajectory, speech perception appears to be shaped by the structure, variability, and distribution of the linguistic input infants receive.

One broad insight from this literature is that multilingual infants do not simply show delayed or extended perceptual narrowing, but instead track multiple phonetic systems in parallel. From birth, bilingual infants are able to discriminate between their languages, and over development they establish sensitivity to multiple sets of phonetic contrasts, reflecting the distributional properties of their input (e.g., Burns et al., 2007; Byers-Heinlein et al., 2010; Singh et al., 2017). This challenges the idea of a single developmental pathway in which sensitivity is lost uniformly, and instead suggests that perceptual organization is shaped by the need to accommodate multiple linguistic systems. In this sense, variability in the input -across languages, speakers, and contexts -plays a central role in determining which contrasts are maintained and how they are represented.

A second theme is that the phonological features that structure perceptual development differ across linguistic environments, raising questions about the generalizability of existing models. Much of the classic literature has focused on consonants and vowels, yet many of the world's languages rely on other features, such as lexical tone. Evidence from multilingual contexts in Asia suggests that tone perception may follow a different developmental trajectory.

For example, in a large-scale, multi-site study, Kalashnikova et al. (2024) examined 462 infants at 5, 10, and 17 months across diverse linguistic contexts (including Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore and Malaysia) and found that sensitivity to Cantonese tone contrasts was maintained across age and similar among monolinguals and bilinguals, even among infants without direct exposure to Cantonese. Similarly, work with English-Mandarin bilingual infants in Singapore, where bilingualism is the norm, shows that exposure to a tonal language supports the continued functional use of tone in word learning, whereas infants learning only non-tonal languages show a decline in sensitivity over time (Singh et al., 2014). Together, these findings suggest that developmental accounts based primarily on consonant and vowel perception may not fully capture how infants learn to process phonological features that play different roles across languages. The developmental trajectory of perceptual sensitivity may depend not only on multilingual experience, but also on which phonological features are meaningful within the languages infants encounter.

Moreover, evidence from African multilingual contexts further highlights the importance of considering a broader range of phonological systems and input conditions. Studies of Ghanaian infants show that infants can use vowel harmony, a constraint requiring vowels within a word to share certain features, as a cue to speech segmentation (Omane et al., 2024; Omane, Boll-Avetisyan, & Benders, 2025). Notably, this ability was not significantly modulated by the degree of exposure to languages with vowel harmony, suggesting that infants may be able to use these regularities for segmentation even with limited experience.

A third insight is that perceptual sensitivities do not map straightforwardly onto later language use, highlighting a dissociation between discrimination and functional deployment.

Although multilingual infants often maintain sensitivity to a wider range of phonetic contrasts (e.g., Graf Estes & Hay, 2015; Kuhl, 2010; Petitto et al., 2012), this sensitivity is not always immediately recruited for word learning. For example, Singh and Tan (2020) demonstrated that bilingual infants in Singapore maintained sensitivity to non-native Hindi contrasts longer than monolinguals (at 14 and 19 months versus only 14 months). However, this sensitivity declined by 24 months and did not consistently support word learning. These findings highlight a distinction between detecting phonetic differences and using phonological information to establish or recognize word meanings. This suggests that perceptual narrowing is not simply a loss of sensitivity, but a reorganization in how phonological information is used, with different trajectories for discrimination and lexical integration. Multilingual experience may therefore extend or reshape perceptual sensitivity without necessarily altering when and how those sensitivities are functionally deployed.

Taken together, this body of work suggests that speech perception development is shaped by the diversity of linguistic input, rather than following a single, uniform trajectory of perceptual narrowing. One interpretation of these patterns is that early multilingual acquisition promotes a form of perceptual flexibility, whereby infants maintain sensitivity to a broader range of phonological contrasts for longer periods of development. Theoretically, this flexibility has been argued to reflect an adaptive response to the increased variability and reduced predictability of multilingual environments, which may encourage infants to distribute attention across a wider range of cues while converging on linguistic structure (e.g., Singh, 2021; Werker & Hensch, 2015). At the same time, the findings reviewed here highlight several distinct aspects of speech processing: infants' ability to discriminate phonetic contrasts, developmental changes in perceptual sensitivity, the use of phonological regularities to segment speech, and the recruitment of phonological information to distinguish word meanings. These abilities are related, but evidence of sensitivity to a contrast does not necessarily establish that infants use it for segmentation or word learning.

A key question for future research is how these processes relate to one another across different multilingual environments. Moreover, this remains a relatively small and uneven literature. Much of the existing work -particularly research on lexical tone and word learningcomes from urban, high-resource settings such as Singapore, limiting our ability to generalize these findings to other multilingual populations. Future research will need to move beyond treating multilingualism as a categorical distinction and instead identify the specific dimensions of multilingual experience (e.g., number of languages, distribution across speakers, functional roles) that shape perceptual development. Doing so will be essential for developing models of speech perception that capture not only differences between monolingual and multilingual learners, but also the substantial variability within multilingual populations themselves.

Word Recognition

A central question for research on early lexical development is how children learn to recognize words. Although infants' sensitivity to phonological contrasts provides a foundation for this process, recognizing words requires them to determine which phonological cues are relevant to identifying a word in a particular language. Research with multilingual infants therefore offers insight into how children weigh competing phonological cues, respond to mispronunciations, and use linguistic information during real-time word recognition. Evidence from multilingual populations suggests that early word recognition is robust, but the phonological cues infants rely on, the efficiency with which they deploy them, and the developmental timetable by which those cues are prioritized may all be shaped by multilingual experience. We therefore organize this section around cue weighting: how infants prioritize consonant, vowel, and tone information during recognition, and how that prioritization changes with experience and development.

One broad lesson from this work is that multilingual infants show early flexibility in how they map phonological variation onto meaning. In prior studies conducted in Singapore, important differences emerged between multilingual and monolingual learners. Singh et al. (2016), for example, found that English-Mandarin bilingual infants in Singapore tested at 12-13 months used tone in a language-specific way when learning new words, treating tone variation as lexically relevant in Mandarin but not in English. By contrast, monolingual Mandarin-learning infants of the same age, despite being able to perceptually discriminate tonal contrasts, did not yet use tone to distinguish word meanings; this emerged later, by 17-18 months. One possible interpretation of this contrast is that multilingual experience may influence the developmental timing of infants' use of phonological information during word learning. Rather than supporting a simple deficit or delay account, these findings suggest that multilingual infants can flexibly calibrate which dimensions of speech are relevant for word learning in different linguistic systems. One possible interpretation is that phonological variation across languages need not create confusion for multilingual learners, as the framing of such variation as "phonological conflict" implies, although these findings come from one bilingual population and do not test this account directly.

The most direct evidence on cue weighting suggests that multilingual experience may alter not the possibility of successful word recognition, but the weighting and timing of phonological cues during online processing. Also in Singapore, Wewalaarachchi et al. (2017) showed that both monolingual Mandarin infants and bilingual English-Mandarin infants at 24 months recognized correctly pronounced words and, in aggregate, rejected vowel, consonant, and tone mispronunciations. Yet the fine-grained time-course analyses revealed meaningful differences in how recognition unfolded. Bilingual infants were slower and less efficient in recognizing correctly pronounced words and were less sensitive to tone mispronunciations during real-time lexical selection. The two groups also differed in the hierarchy of cues they appeared to privilege: monolingual Mandarin learners were least sensitive to consonants and similarly sensitive to vowels and tones, whereas bilinguals showed greatest sensitivity to vowels, intermediate sensitivity to consonants, and least sensitivity to tones. Seen in broader perspective, these findings may indicate that multilingual development does not undermine word recognition so much as redistribute attentional weight across competing phonological dimensions, particularly when children are learning languages that assign different functional roles to those dimensions. This reweighting is also not static. Rather, the available evidence suggests that the relative importance of phonological cues continues to shift across development. Singh et al. (2015) found that among bilingual Mandarin-learning children, younger children showed greater sensitivity to tonal mispronunciations than to consonant or vowel variation, whereas preschoolers showed the reverse pattern. In that sense, multilingual children offer a particularly clear window into the dynamic nature of word recognition, suggesting that the phonological structure of lexical representations can be recalibrated over time in relation to changing language experience. Recent work from Ghana extends this literature to an understudied African tone language. Omane et al. (2026) examined wordform recognition in multilingual infants aged 11-14 months learning Akan, a tone language, alongside other languages, including Ghanaian English. In a familiar-word recognition task, infants listened longer to familiar Akan wordforms than to pseudowords, demonstrating recognition of familiar words in Akan. However, when familiar words were presented with either consonant or vowel mispronunciations, listening times did not differ significantly, providing no evidence of the vowel bias previously reported in infants learning Asian tone languages. Neither familiar-word recognition nor sensitivity to mispronunciations was modulated by infants' relative exposure to Akan. These findings suggest that the weighting of phonological cues may not be uniform across tone languages. Together with findings from Singapore, this work highlights the importance of examining how languagespecific phonological properties interact with developmental and experiential factors in shaping early lexical processing.

A related theme is that infants' word recognition appears to accommodate variability, including variability that extends beyond experimentally controlled mispronunciations. Singh and Cheng (2023) found that both monolingual and bilingual 24-month-olds recognized words across correctly produced, assimilated, and mispronounced forms, with no differences across trial types. Infants can thus tolerate substantial phonological variation in natural speech, an ability that is especially consequential in multilingual environments where variability across speakers, languages, and interactional contexts is common. Here again, multilingual learners push the field beyond a monolingual default by underscoring that flexibility and tolerance for variation are central aspects of how language understanding operates.

At the same time, this literature makes clear that multilingualism is not the only source of variation that matters for early word recognition. Rajendra et al. (2025) found that socioeconomic status (SES) was a robust predictor of performance in a preferential looking task among infants from diverse multilingual and multi-ethnic backgrounds in Singapore.

Importantly, SES and multilingual status are also often correlated, especially in research involving language-minority or immigrant families, making it difficult to disentangle the effects of multilingual experience from the broader social and material conditions in which it occurs (Calvo & Bialystok, 2014; Hoff, 2013; Meir & Armon-Lotem, 2017). More broadly, scholarship on bilingual and multilingual development has emphasized that language status and SES frequently co-occur in ways that can obscure their distinct contributions unless both are carefully measured and theorized (De Cat, 2021). Multilingual development therefore unfolds within broader social and environmental ecologies that shape processing outcomes alongside language experience. This reinforces the need for models of early word recognition that situate children's processing within intersecting conditions of language input, socioeconomic opportunity, and social context.

Taken together, these findings suggest that research on multilingual infants does more than extend an existing literature to new populations. This work suggests that multilingual experience can reveal the flexibility, contingency, and developmental plasticity of early word recognition itself. Across studies, word recognition remains robust, but the cues children prioritize, the efficiency with which they process speech, and the way lexical representations are reorganized over time may all depend on the structure of their linguistic environments. At the same time, this remains a small literature, and the existing evidence comes almost exclusively from Singapore, with one study from Ghana. That concentration may limit how broadly they can be generalized. More globally diverse research will be necessary to determine whether most of the patterns documented here reflect multilingual development more broadly or are partly specific to the linguistic, institutional, and sociocultural conditions of Singapore.

Word Learning

How does variability in multilingual input translate into vocabulary outcomes? Addressing this question requires moving beyond assumptions derived from single-language contexts and considering how differences within multilingual environments shape lexical development. For example, one widely cited pattern in early word learning is the noun bias -the tendency for children to acquire more nouns than verbs early in development. However, this pattern does not generalize across languages. For instance, children learning Mandarin have been shown to acquire verbs at comparable or even earlier rates than nouns (Frank et al., 2021), suggesting that lexical development is shaped by language-specific structural and input-related factors. These differences become even more complex in multilingual contexts. Although multilingual children often have smaller vocabularies in each individual language compared to monolingual peers, their total or conceptual vocabulary across languages is typically comparable (e.g., Byers-Heinlein et al., 2024; De Houwer et al., 2014; Junker & Stockman, 2002; Pearson et al., 1993). This distinction is theoretically important because it challenges deficit-based interpretations of multilingual development and instead reframes it as a redistribution of lexical knowledge across linguistic systems. Crucially, however, this redistribution is not uniform: the relative size of vocabularies in each language depends on patterns of exposure, use, and social value assigned to each language.

Measurement practices further complicate this issue. Widely used tools such as the MacArthur-Bates Communicative Development Inventory (CDI) have been adapted into many individual languages, but they are primarily designed for monolingual assessment. In multilingual environments, where children may be exposed to and use multiple languages fluidly, such tools may underestimate vocabulary knowledge if not administered in all of the child's languages, which can be methodologically challenging and unfeasible. Efforts to reduce assessment burden have included the development of traditional short-form CDIs (Fenson et al., 2000), model-based very short forms (Chai et al., 2020), and computerized adaptive approaches (Kachergis et al., 2022). Although these approaches offer ways to make vocabulary assessment more efficient, they do not necessarily address the distinct challenges of multilingual assessment, including adapting instruments across languages, establishing their validity for multilingual populations, and capturing children's knowledge across multiple languages.

Efforts to adapt these tools illustrate both their potential and their limitations. For example, Vogt et al. (2015) adapted the CDI into three languages spoken in Mozambique (Changana, Ronga, and Portuguese) to assess vocabulary development in both rural monolingual and urban multilingual communities. While the adapted instrument proved useful, vocabulary outcomes were strongly shaped by contextual factors such as socioeconomic status (SES, which was higher in the urban sample, was linked to larger expressive vocabularies), caregiving practices (caregiving by siblings or other adults, which is more common among rural populations, was linked to larger receptive vocabularies), and health (health problems like Malaria and HIV, which are more prevalent among rural populations, were linked to smaller receptive and expressive vocabularies). These findings highlight that even within the same national context, differences in children's environments can lead to markedly different developmental trajectories, underscoring the need to treat multilingualism as a heterogeneous experience rather than a single category.

In response to these challenges, alternative approaches have begun to reconceptualize vocabulary measurement in ways that better capture multilingual experience. Coffey et al. (2025) developed a vocabulary checklist for multilingual families in Ghana, where children were exposed to up to four languages. Instead of assessing each language separately, parents reported whether their child understood or produced each word in any of their languages, yielding a single measure of conceptual vocabulary. Their findings showed that children from multilingual homes had higher conceptual vocabularies on average, illustrating how different measurement approaches can reveal patterns that would be obscured by single-language assessments.

In addition, vocabulary development in multilingual contexts is shaped not only by linguistic exposure, but by the broader social and institutional environments in which languages are embedded. Using an adapted parent-report vocabulary questionnaire based on the CDI, Ohana et al. (2024) examined vocabulary development in 24-48-month-old Yiddish-Hebrew bilingual children growing up in ultra-Orthodox communities in Israel where Yiddish functions as an in-group (and minority) language and Hebrew as the majority language in society. Results showed significantly larger vocabularies in Yiddish than in Hebrew across the sample, reflecting the strong support for Yiddish within the home, schooling system, and community identity. This overall pattern of results contrasts with many other bilingual contexts in Israel, where bilingualism is typically balanced. Instead, the linguistic ecology of ultra-Orthodox communities sustains Yiddish as the primary language of daily interaction, despite it being a minority language in the broader societal context. Similarly, in Singapore, Singh et al. (2021) showed that vocabulary size in English -the dominant community language -was strongly predicted by the proportion of English exposure, as well as by socioeconomic factors. The authors developed English vocabulary norms using a locally adapted parent-report checklist based on the CDI framework, and proposed that in Singapore's multilingual context, a single-language measurement of the community language could be adequate for assessing bilingual/multilingual development. English functions as the primary language of education and is widely used across domains of public life, making it a language to which most children are consistently exposed. In addition, some lexical overlap may exist between English and children's heritage languages, and parents may be particularly attentive to their children's English vocabulary given its perceived importance for schooling and future opportunities. For these reasons, measuring vocabulary in a community language can provide a useful benchmark for multilingual development in this context. At the same time, as acknowledged by the authors, this approach is not universally applicable. In other multilingual settings where exposure is more evenly distributed across languages, or where no single language occupies such a dominant societal role across the population, assessing vocabulary in only one language may provide an incomplete picture of children's lexical knowledge.

These findings illustrate that bilingual development is not only shaped by exposure to multiple languages, but also by social identity and institutional practices that structure children's everyday language environments. Consequently, developmental trajectories can vary substantially even among multilingual children within the same national context. This work also highlights that vocabulary development is embedded within broader environmental conditions that intersect with multilingual experience. SES and the home literacy environment, for example, have been shown to shape early vocabulary outcomes, with richer literacy practices partially mediating the relationship between SES and vocabulary size (Singh et al., 2022). These findings suggest that SES differences in early lexical development may operate through everyday language and literacy experiences available to children in the home.

Together, these findings highlight that variability in vocabulary development is a function of both whether children are multilingual and of specific characteristics of their multilingual environments. Across contexts, vocabulary outcomes are shaped by differences in language exposure, community norms, sociolinguistic hierarchies, and broader environmental factors such as SES and home literacy practices, consistent with established evidence. These studies' contributions lie in illustrating how these factors operate in multilingual environments that are underrepresented in developmental research. Ultimately, capturing multilingual vocabulary development requires tools that account for the distribution of input across languages, the functions those languages serve, and the social contexts in which they are used. More broadly, incorporating evidence from diverse linguistic and sociocultural contexts challenges the generalizability of conclusions drawn from WEIRD populations and calls for theoretical frameworks that can accommodate the full range of variation in children's language environments.

Summary of Review

Multilingual development in Asia and Africa is central to theories of early language acquisition. Across the studies reviewed, infants were often exposed to multiple languages, multiple speakers, and caregiving arrangements that extended beyond the nuclear family. At the same time, language experiences varied substantially both within and across contexts. This variability was reflected across developmental domains. Research on speech perception highlighted the importance of phonological features such as lexical tone and vowel harmony, which are common in many Asian and African languages but have received comparatively little attention in the broader literature. Studies of word recognition showed that multilingual infants flexibly adjusted their use of phonological cues in response to the languages they were learning.

Research on vocabulary development demonstrated that language outcomes were shaped not only by patterns of exposure, but also by factors such as socioeconomic circumstances, educational systems, and the social roles different languages occupied within children's communities.

A common thread runs through these findings: the features of multilingual childhoods that shape early input show up as explanations for how infants and children perceive, recognize, and learn words. The evidence reviewed across language input, responsiveness to input, speech perception, word recognition, and vocabulary development consistently demonstrates that many assumptions inherited from monolingual and WEIRD populations do not readily generalize to the linguistic realities experienced by large numbers of children worldwide. Early language development occurs under conditions that differ in important ways from those typically represented in developmental research.

Theoretical and Methodological Implications

More broadly, the evidence reviewed here suggests that theories of early language development should be organized around language ecology rather than a monolingual default. This perspective shifts attention away from questions of whether multilingual children differ from monolingual children and toward questions concerning which dimensions of linguistic experience matter, under what conditions, and through which developmental mechanisms.

Moreover, methodologically, this variability highlights that the tools, benchmarks, and measures that researchers use to study children require proper adaptation to capture complex multilingual exposure, distributed caregiving arrangements, and diverse forms of communicative interaction without assuming that models developed in WEIRD populations are universally applicable (Fernald et al., 2017). Many of the standard tools and benchmarks in the field were built around assumptions -one language, one primary caregiver, direct speech -that do not hold for many of the world's children. Adapting these tools is a necessary step for making the field's methods match the realities of multilingual childhoods. These limitations also have clinical implications: when speech-language therapists use assessments developed and normed for monolingual populations with multilingual children, children's language abilities may be misrepresented, potentially contributing to the misidentification of language disorders (Bedore & Peña, 2008; Mdlalo et al., 2019). This is a particular concern in multilingual contexts where culturally and linguistically appropriate assessment tools are limited, including parts of Africa and Southeast Asia (Mdlalo et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2026).

At the same time, an important conclusion emerging from this review is that the empirical literature remains remarkably limited. Much of the existing evidence comes from a small number of urban, relatively high-resource settings, particularly Singapore, Accra, and a handful of other research sites (see Figure 1). Given the immense linguistic, cultural, and socioeconomic diversity that characterizes both Asia and Africa, these studies provide only a partial picture of multilingual development. Expanding research beyond these contexts is therefore essential for developing a more comprehensive and representative understanding of multilingual development.

In general, these findings have important implications for how multilingual exposure is conceptualized and measured. In particular, thresholds commonly used in WEIRD-based research to classify children as multilingual (e.g., requiring at least 25-35% exposure to a given language) are difficult or impossible to apply in contexts where children are regularly exposed to three or more languages. Although certain thresholds are often misattributed to Pearson et al. (1993), subsequent research has shown that there is no consistent exposure cutoff at which bilingual children begin to resemble monolinguals. For example, receptive vocabulary outcomes have been shown to fall within the monolingual range at exposure levels as low as 35-40%, whereas expressive vocabulary may require substantially higher exposure (Thordardottir, 2011), and other studies report comparable performance only at much higher levels of exposure (e.g., 60% or more; Cattani et al., 2014). Moreover, differences observed at one developmental stage may attenuate over time (DeAnda, Arias-Trejo et al., 2016; Friend et al., 2017). Taken together, this body of work suggests that fixed thresholds may obscure meaningful variation in children's linguistic experience. In response, recent approaches have emphasized modeling multilingual exposure as a continuous construct (Kremin & Byers-Heinlein, 2021).

Additionally, commonly used measures of early language input in WEIRD research tend to prioritize direct, caregiver-to-child speech (e.g., Byers-Heinlein et al., 2020; DeAnda, Bosch et al., 2016). In Africa, parent-child interactions in agrarian communities have been documented to differ from Western styles which encourage direct communication (e.g., LeVine, 2004; LeVine & LeVine, 1988; Richman et al., 1992). While studies with WEIRD populations have emphasized the need for direct language input to facilitate children's language learning, infants who are rarely spoken to directly, such as Tseltal infants, demonstrate similar comprehension of words as Western infants (Foushee & Srinivasan, 2024). In contexts where language input is more distributed across speakers and interactional settings, indirect exposure (e.g., overheard speech) may constitute a substantial portion of infants' linguistic experience and should be accounted for in measurement approaches. children included in the present review across Asia and Africa. Agenda for Future Research

The evidence reviewed here points toward a research agenda organized not around multilingualism as a single developmental condition, but around the diversity of multilingualisms found across Asia and Africa. Consistent with the focus of this special issue, the central task is to explain variation both between and within multilingual populations: how children's developmental pathways differ according to the languages they encounter, the speakers and institutions through which those languages are accessed, and the social, historical, and material conditions that organize their use. Several of the questions we raise extend beyond the infant literature reviewed above, and this is in essence, the point. Questions about how infants learn the social organization of their multilingual input remain largely unexamined in early language development research. These include how languages and varieties are distributed across speakers and settings, how language choice shifts with the interlocutor, how contact varieties coexist with standardized institutional varieties, and how registers communicate social meaning. Yet these questions are central to the communicative realities of many children in Asia and Africa. Their absence from the infant literature is itself an important finding of this review: it reflects the extent to which research on early language development has been organized around input that is assumed to consist of discrete, named languages delivered by a small number of caregivers in dyadic contexts (see Stengelin et al., 2026). Addressing these questions will require greater cross-pollination between developmental psycholinguistics and fields that have long studied language as socially situated practice, including sociolinguistics, linguistic anthropology, and language socialization research (e.g., Blommaert & Rampton, 2011; Lanza, 2021). These traditions offer theoretical frameworks and methods for analyzing how linguistic variation is tied to social identity, setting, and power. Integrating sociolinguistic and sociopragmatic perspectives with experimental and longitudinal approaches to early development may therefore be one of the most generative directions for building theories that reflect the multilingual ecologies in which most of the world's children learn language.

We identify five priorities for advancing this agenda. First, research must broaden the empirical geography and social range of the populations studied. Second, multilingual experience should be measured as a multidimensional ecology rather than reduced to a categorical designation or exposure threshold. Third, theories must be tested against a wider range of linguistic structures and everyday communicative practices. Fourth, language learning must be situated within the social and institutional systems that give languages different functions and value in context. Finally and most critically, these changes should inform which questions are treated as theoretically central. Each recommendation is described in turn.

Broaden the Range of Multilingual Populations and Ecologies Studied

The first priority is to expand the geographic and sociocultural range of empirical evidence. As Figure 1 makes clear, the current literature is concentrated in a small number of sites, with Singapore accounting for a disproportionate share of studies and only scattered representation elsewhere in Asia and Africa. These sites have generated foundational findings, but they are no substitute for two vast and heterogenous continents containing immense variation in language histories, demographic structures, educational systems, colonial influences, and culture. In future research, as samples diversify, Asia and Africa should be treated neither as homogeneous regions nor as a single contrast with "the West." Rather, their internal heterogeneity should be used to identify the dimensions of language ecology that explain developmental variation. This broader agenda also requires closer attention to caregiving networks. Much existing research assumes that input is organized primarily through one or two parents (see a critique of the notion of the dyadic default; Stengelin et al., 2026). The studies reviewed here show that infants may learn from siblings, grandparents, domestic workers, neighbors, and other community members, each of whom may use more than one language. Research designs should therefore sample the network around the child rather than only the primary caregiver. Relevant questions include how children track languages across multiple speakers; whether consistency at the level of a relationship, activity, or setting matters more than consistency at the level of an individual speaker; and how learning changes when a language is heard mainly through siblings, overheard conversation, religious practice, or community interaction. Longitudinal and comparative studies could test whether distributed caregiving produces different pathways to segmentation, word recognition, and lexical growth than the concentrated caregiver-child dyads that dominate current theory. Such studies would extend longstanding calls to understand development within culturally organized systems of activity rather than treating context as a background variable (Harkness & Super, 1992; Keller et al., 2005; Lamm et al., 2023).

Broader sampling must also be accompanied by a sustainable research infrastructure.

One-off data collection by visiting researchers is unlikely to produce the cumulative evidence needed for theory-building. Progress will depend on long-term collaborations with scholars and communities in the regions studied, investment in local laboratories and training, multilingual research teams, and publication practices that recognize locally generated questions and expertise. Multisite consortia can help distinguish findings that are site-specific from those that generalize across language ecologies, but they should not require every site to implement a method developed elsewhere without adaptation. A globally representative science will require both coordinated measurement and room for locally meaningful constructs, tasks, and interpretations (Medin et al., 2017).

Measure Multilingual Experience as an Ecology Rather Than a Category

A second priority is to replace categorical definitions of multilingualism with richer descriptions of children's experience. Fixed exposure thresholds -often 25% or 30% of waking input -have had considerable staying power because they make samples easy to classify. Yet they are poorly suited to children who encounter three, four, or more languages, whose exposure changes across days and caregivers, or whose languages serve sharply different functions.

Available evidence does not identify a single cutoff at which children become meaningfully "bilingual," and the exposure associated with monolingual-range performance differs across receptive and expressive outcomes and across developmental periods (Cattani et al., 2014; Thordardottir, 2011). Exposure should therefore be modeled continuously and multidimensionally rather than used only to assign children to groups (Kremin & Byers-Heinlein, 2021; Singh et al., 2021). This shift also requires studying language learning in vivo: as it unfolds within the activities, relationships, communicative practices, and material settings of children's everyday lives (Casillas, 2023). Casillas argues that theories of language development should be linked more directly to the diverse experiences through which children actually encounter and use language, rather than relying primarily on simplified measures of input or decontextualized laboratory interactions. From this perspective, multilingual experience is not adequately represented by a set of percentages reflecting exposure. It is an ecology comprising languages, speakers, settings, activities, and interactional roles, whose configuration changes across time and space. Two children with the same proportional exposure may therefore inhabit fundamentally different learning environments.

Figure 2 summarizes this multidimensional language-ecology framework. At its center is the child's everyday language experience, which comprises three interrelated dimensions: the network of speakers and caregivers through whom languages are encountered, the interactional contexts in which they are used (including speech directed to the child and speech that is overheard), and the amount and stability of exposure to each language. These dimensions are in turn shaped by the status and social functions of each language, by the institutions that assign languages different roles, and by socioeconomic conditions, all of which are situated within longer histories of mobility, colonialism, and language policy. The framework proposes that developmental outcomes in speech perception, word recognition, and vocabulary are best predicted not by multilingual status as a category, but by how these dimensions are configured for a given child, and how that configuration changes over time. At minimum, measurement should capture the amount of exposure to each language, the number and identity of speakers, the settings and activities in which each language occurs, the distinction between speech directed to the child and speech available through participation or overhearing, and the stability of these patterns over time. It should also record language mixing, dialect and accent variation, and the social functions and institutional status of each language.

Parent interviews remain valuable, but naturalistic recordings, daylong sampling, structured diaries, and repeated assessments can reveal variability that a single global estimate may obscure. For example, children receiving the same proportional exposure may hear very different absolute quantities of speech, and the balance between direct and overheard input may differ substantially across languages and days (Orena et al., 2020). Measures should be validated locally because automated systems and coding conventions trained on North American English may perform unevenly across languages, acoustic environments, and multiparty interactions.

Vocabulary assessment presents a related challenge. Administering separate monolingual instruments in every language can underestimate conceptual knowledge, impose heavy burdens on families, and become impractical when children encounter several languages. Future work should compare language-specific, total-vocabulary, and conceptual-vocabulary approaches and determine which measures best predict later communication, learning, and school participation in different ecologies. Instruments developed in Ghana and Mozambique show the value of adapting measures to local multilingual realities rather than merely translating an English checklist. In some contexts, a dominant community language may provide a useful benchmark; in others, no single language adequately represents the child's knowledge. Measurement decisions should thus follow from the local distribution and functions of languages rather than from a universal assessment template. In situ task development, interviewing with caregivers, and iterative piloting are essential, even though this requires substantially more time than direct translation.

Expand the Linguistic Phenomena that Theories are Required to Explain

A third priority is broader linguistic coverage. Theories of early language development have been built largely from a narrow subset of the world's languages and have consequently privileged the contrasts and structures most salient in those languages. Research in Asia and Africa makes it possible to test developmental accounts against lexical tone, vowel harmony, clicks, extensive morphological marking, reduplication, ideophones, and other properties that remain uncommon in the infant literature. The studies reviewed here already show that perceptual narrowing does not follow a single timetable across phonological dimensions and that multilingual infants can recruit tone and vowel harmony in ways that are not predicted by models centered on consonants and vowels in Indo-European languages. The next step is not simply to document performance in additional languages, but to ask which learning mechanisms generalize across typologically distinct systems and which depend on the statistical, functional, and social organization of particular features.

Research should likewise treat language mixing, translanguaging, dialect variation, and multi-dialectical input as ordinary properties of input rather than departures from an idealized code. In many multilingual communities, speakers draw flexibly on repertoires that do not map neatly onto named-language boundaries. Children may hear one lexical item embedded in another language, encounter several local varieties of a national language, or shift registers across home, school, religion, and media. These patterns raise questions that are rarely central in mainstream infant research: How do infants infer linguistic structure when boundaries between named languages are socially meaningful but not consistently marked in speech? How do they learn that the same speaker may use different varieties with different interlocutors? When does variation support robust recognition, and when does it create uncertainty? How are lexical representations organized when words circulate across languages or when a local contact variety, such as colloquial Singapore English, coexists with a standardized institutional variety?

Situate Multilingual Development Within Social and Institutional Systems

A fourth priority is to analyze multilingual development within the social structures that assign languages unequal value. Language exposure is neither randomly nor evenly distributed.

It is organized by schooling, labor, migration, religion, media, family structure, and histories of colonial governance. A language may be frequent in the home but excluded from formal education; another may be rarely used in intimate interaction yet carry disproportionate economic and institutional value. These asymmetries shape caregiver choices, children's opportunities to use each language, and the meanings attached to proficiency. Accordingly, socioeconomic status, language status, and multilingual exposure should not be treated as independent covariates to be controlled for. Their dynamic and sometimes unpredictable intermingling with language use is often itself part of the developmental phenomenon to be explained (De Cat, 2021; Hoff, 2013; Mohanty, 2010).

Future studies should examine how language trajectories change at institutional transitions, including entry into childcare or school, migration to a new community, displacement, and changes in family employment or caregiving. Longitudinal work can identify when a home language is maintained, displaced, or reorganized and how these shifts influence speech processing, vocabulary, identity, and family communication. Research should also examine the mechanisms through which language hierarchies operate: differential access to books and media, teachers' language practices, caregiver beliefs about mobility and opportunity, stigma attached to nonstandard varieties, and policies that recognize or exclude particular languages. These factors are especially consequential for assessment and intervention. A child's limited performance in the language of testing may reflect restricted opportunity or a mismatch between the assessment and the child's repertoire rather than a language-learning deficit (Core & Hoff, 2015).

Build Theory from the Diversity of Multilingual Populations

Most fundamentally, the diversity of multilingual ecologies in Asia and Africa should be treated not only as a gap in sampling but as a resource for theory-building. The multilingualecology framework (Figure 2) identifies dimensions of children's language environments that are often confounded in the populations on which current theories are based. In many WEIRD samples, the number of caregivers, the amount of child-directed speech, the number of languages, the status of those languages, and socioeconomic resources tend to covary.

Multilingual ecologies in Asia and Africa often separate these factors. They therefore make it possible to test assumptions that are built into existing developmental theories. Aim I: Testing the generality of input-dependent mechanisms.

This aim concerns the proximal layer of the framework, specifically speaker and caregiving networks and interactional context (Table 1, Assumptions 1 and 3). Many accounts of early word learning assume that learning is driven primarily by speech directed to the child within dyadic interaction. Distributed multilingual ecologies allow this assumption to be tested directly. How do infants identify and organize languages when exposure is distributed across parents, siblings, grandparents, domestic workers, and community members, many of whom use several languages with the same child?

How do infants learn from languages that they hear frequently in overheard or multiparty interaction but that are seldom addressed directly to them? Evidence that infants rarely spoken to directly show comparable word comprehension (Foushee & Srinivasan, 2024) suggests that theories privileging direct input may describe one effective pathway rather than a necessary condition. Theories should therefore specify which properties of input (quantity, directedness, speaker diversity, or consistency within relationships or settings) are proposed to drive learning.

They should also state what pattern of outcomes across ecologies would count against them.

Aim II: Specifying how the social organization of input enters learning. This aim concerns the outer layers of the framework, specifically language status and institutional factors, and how they shape the input children receive (Table 1, Assumption 5). Current theories largely treat languages as discrete codes and input as a linguistic signal. In many multilingual communities, however, the boundaries between languages and varieties are fluid in everyday speech and can be marked by schooling, religion, ethnicity, or the state. How do infants represent such languages and varieties? When a standardized institutional language coexists with regional languages, contact varieties, dialects, or heritage languages, how do children learn not only their linguistic structures but also their different social reach and communicative value? These questions require theories to specify whether and how information about speakers, settings, and social meaning becomes part of children's linguistic representations. As noted above, addressing them will require integrating sociolinguistic and sociopragmatic perspectives with developmental approaches.

Aim III: Testing developmental timing and reorganization. This aim concerns the temporal dimension of the framework and the combinations of languages children are exposed to (Table 1, Assumption 2). Multilingual ecologies also change over time in ways that can test assumptions about the stability of early representations. How are early representations reorganized when children enter school in a language used infrequently at home, when migration alters the speakers and languages in their environment, or when a high-prestige language gradually displaces a family or community language? Similarly, typological combinations make it possible to ask whether developmental timetables such as perceptual narrowing are general or feature-specific. Examples include acquiring tonal and non-tonal languages, or languages with different phonotactic and morphological systems. How do these combinations shape perceptual organization, segmentation, and lexical representation? Together, these questions treat multilingualism not as a binary child characteristic but as a system of developmental ecologies whose variation can be used to test theoretical claims.

Finally, theory-building depends not only on how questions are tested but on which phenomena are treated as theoretically central. Which questions are pursued should therefore not be determined exclusively by researchers located at the traditional centers of language science.

ManyLabs Africa offers an instructive example. Rather than beginning with a canonical Western finding selected for replication in Africa, Adetula and colleagues invited African researchers to nominate studies concerning concepts or problems originating in African contexts, developed by African scholars, and previously investigated on the continent. Candidate studies were then evaluated in relation to their local relevance, multisite feasibility, and reproducibility, including whether people in participating countries would consider the topic important (Adetula, 2024).

This bottom-up process shifts local researchers from the role of data collectors or cultural adapters to that of agenda setters and theory builders.

Applied to early multilingual development, a comparable model would involve researchers, caregivers, educators, clinicians, and community organizations in identifying the outcomes and questions that matter locally. Communities might prioritize, for example, maintaining intergenerational communication, preparing children for a transition into a different language of schooling, understanding the developmental consequences of language displacement, supporting acquisition of a minoritized or primarily oral language, distinguishing linguistic difference from clinical disorder, or documenting learning through siblings and multiparty participation. Such priorities may not correspond precisely to the contrasts most often studied in Euro-American developmental laboratories, but that is precisely their theoretical value. Participatory and locally led research can reveal phenomena that imported paradigms overlook, while reducing extractive research relationships and ensuring that collaboration builds durable local capacity and produces reciprocal value (Adetula et al., 2022; Weinstein et al., 2026).

Conclusion

We began with a basic question: Who is the language learner? The learner most clearly portrayed by contemporary language science is still disproportionately a monolingual child from a Euro-American, urban, relatively highly educated setting, interacting directly with one or two primary caregivers and acquiring a standardized language that is well represented in research, assessment, and schooling. That learner is globally unrepresentative, yet has been institutionalized as the normative model of language development. Similarly, studies on multilingual children remain concentrated in Euro-American contexts yet are often taken to represent the diverse category of multilingual learners. The evidence reviewed here describes children whose input is distributed across speakers and settings, whose languages differ in structure and social reach, and whose learning unfolds within histories and institutions that organize multilingualism in diverse and context-situated ways.

Evidence from Asia and Africa reveals the diversity of multilingualisms that theories of language acquisition must incorporate and explain. Infants may encounter two languages or six, through one caregiver or an extended network, in sharply separated domains or through fluid mixing, and under social conditions in which the languages they hear differ substantially in institutional recognition, prestige, and future value. These configurations cannot be collapsed into a single multilingual category. They constitute different developmental ecologies, each offering evidence about how children identify structure, allocate attention, build lexical representations, and adapt to variation.

Who, then, is the language learner? The learner is not the singular child most clearly portrayed by contemporary language science, nor is the multilingual learner simply a monolingual child with an additional language added. Across Asia and Africa, key sites of discovery about multilingualism, learners participate in multiple multilingualisms, shaped by distinct linguistic structures, caregiving arrangements, colonial and political histories, and relations of power. A representative science of language development therefore requires more than extending existing theories to new populations. It requires building theories capable of explaining the diversity of multilingual populations and the multiple developmental pathways through which children learn language.
