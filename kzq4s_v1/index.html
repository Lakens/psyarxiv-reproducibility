Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: College students face high rates of mental health problems including depression and stress. Digital interventions offer accessible support, but adherence remains challenging. Autonomy may enhance motivation, yet its role in studentfocused digital interventions is not explored yet in a randomized controlled trial.

Objective: This study examines whether enhancing autonomy in a guided digital CBT-based mental health intervention for college students (LifeHack) improves satisfaction, adherence, and mental health outcomes.

Methods: In a two-armed randomized trial, 575 students were assigned to either a structured intervention or an autonomy-enhanced version in which participants could select topics of interest. Assessments were conducted at baseline and 5 weeks later (post-intervention). Primary outcomes were general satisfaction (CSQ-8) and working alliance with the program and e-coach (WAI-I); secondary outcomes included adherence, mental well-being (MHC-SF), depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), and quality of life (MHQoL).

Results: Participants were moderately satisfied with the intervention and guidance, and showed small-to-moderate improvements in mental well-being, depressive and anxiety symptoms, and quality of life from baseline to post-intervention (all p < .001; Cohen's d ranging 0.24-0.41). However, no significant differences were observed between conditions on satisfaction, adherence or clinical outcomes.

Conclusions: Allowing users to freely choose lesson topics did not enhance satisfaction, adherence, or clinical outcomes compared with a structured lesson sequence. Nonetheless, participation in LifeHack was associated with improved mental health, indicating potential intervention effectiveness regardless of the autonomy features. Future research should test more comprehensive autonomysupportive designs and compare LifeHack with inactive control conditions to establish intervention effectiveness.

1. Introduction

Student life presents many challenges, including rigorous academic demands, increased responsibilities, financial constraints, and limited social support (Mulaudzi, 2023; Thompson et al., 2016). A recent meta-analysis reports that over a quarter (26.3%) of college students experience mental health problems, such as symptoms of depression and stress (Leow et al., 2024). Furthermore, the onset of common mental health problems, including anxiety, eating disorders, and addiction disorders, often occurs between the ages of 17 and 25 (Solmi et al., 2022). Poor student mental health is associated with several undesirable outcomes, including academic drop-out (Thomas et al., 2021), self-harm (Kiekens et al., 2023), and, in the worstcase scenario, suicide (Li et al., 2020). These high prevalence rates and detrimental consequences make student mental health a significant cause of concern.

Psychological interventions aimed at improving poor mental health in students have been shown to be effective. An umbrella review of meta-analyses found that such interventions can reduce mental health complaints in student populations (Cuijpers et al., 2021). When comparing different types of therapy, CBT was found to be the most effective in reducing symptoms of mental health problems such as depression and anxiety (Madrid-Cagigal et al., 2025). Additionally, colleges increasingly seek to support not only students with mental health disorders, but also the broader student population with mild symptoms or even without symptoms, given that the absence of mental illness does not necessarily imply the presence of positive mental well-being (VanderWeele et al., 2026). Mental health problems and positive mental well-being are increasingly understood as related but distinct dimensions, implying that support for students should ideally aim not only to reduce symptoms, but also to strengthen mental well-being more broadly.

However, access to traditional mental health support poses substantial barriers for college students. Students often report difficulties accessing services, long waiting lists, and concerns regarding privacy (Boonstra et al., 2023). Additionally, they may worry about stigmatization, underestimate the severity of their problems, prefer to manage difficulties independently, or perceive a lack of time to engage in treatment (Czyz et al., 2013; Ebert et al., 2019). As a result, college students may be more receptive to alternative forms of mental health support than traditional face-to-face therapy provided by licensed professionals.

Digital mental health interventions may help reduce several of these barriers and appear particularly suitable for student populations. Such interventions are typically highly accessible, cost-effective, and offer a high level of anonymity (Andersson & Titov, 2014; Ebert et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2023). Although not focused exclusively on students, meta-analyses comparing digital and traditional or face-to-face treatments for a range of mental health problems, including depression, anxiety, stress, and eating disorders, suggest that these formats can achieve comparable outcomes (Cuijpers, Noma, et al., 2019; Esfandiari et al., 2021; Hedman-Lagerlöf et al., 2023).

Though not researched as extensively, comparisons of treatment format within the student population yield similar outcomes: digital treatments generally aren't inferior to traditional therapy (Koelen et al., 2024; McKenzie et al., 2025). Nevertheless, digital interventions also face challenges, most notably difficulties related to user engagement and adherence (i.e. high drop-out rates), which may limit their overall effectiveness (Donkin et al., 2011; Oti & Pitt, 2021).

Autonomy, which is the experience of having choice and ownership over one's actions, is widely recognized as a key driver of sustained, self-endorsed behavior (Ryan et al., 2008; Ryan & Deci, 2020). Research shows that providing individuals with meaningful choice enhances intrinsic motivation, effort, and task performance across a range of contexts (Patall et al., 2008). Furthermore, interventions that actively support autonomy have demonstrated small but significant positive effects on physical health behavior change, with autonomy functioning as an important mechanism underlying these effects (Ng et al., 2012; Sheeran et al., 2020). In the context of mental health, however, the explicit integration of autonomy as a deliberate design element in digital interventions remains relatively underexplored.

Recent reviews of psychotherapy innovations further suggest that, while many digital and therapeutic approaches show promise, no single advancement is expected to dramatically improve outcomes on its own. Instead, progress in both effectiveness and uptake is likely to result from multiple, incremental improvements across intervention design, delivery, and personalization (Cuijpers et al., 2026). In this context, autonomy-enhancing features such as offering participants choices can be considered one such incremental improvement.

In the current study, we developed a new guided digital intervention (LifeHack) for the general college student population aimed at improving their mental health, which includes such an incremental improvement. In an autonomy-enhanced version, participants can choose the topics they find to be most relevant. The other version follows a structured format in which topics have to be completed in a predefined order. To investigate the impact of autonomy, these two version are compared headto-head in a randomized controlled trial. Our primary outcome is satisfaction, and the secondary outcomes are guidance satisfaction, adherence (number of lessons completed), general mental well-being, depressive symptoms, anxiety, and overall quality of life.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study design

This study was a two-arm randomized trial among college students seeking to improve their mental health, comparing a structured versus an autonomy-enhanced version of a guided e-health intervention (LifeHack). Assessments were conducted at baseline (T0) and five weeks later (post-intervention (T1). This study was conducted within the Caring Universities Consortium; a collaboration between several large Dutch colleges. The Caring Universities Consortium is affiliated with the World Health Organization (WHO) World Mental Health International College Student Initiative (WMH-ICS) (Cuijpers, Auerbach, et al., 2019). Ethical approval was granted by the VU Scientific and Ethical Review Board (reference number 2020.088) and the trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID NCT05596396).

2.2 Participants and Enrollment

Students enrolled in any of the participating universities were eligible to register for LifeHack. Participants were recruited through several channels. First, through oncampus marketing efforts (e.g. posters, flyers) as well as social media posts. Second, staff members including student psychologists and student mentors were encouraged to refer students to LifeHack. Lastly, Caring Universities through a yearly WMH-ICS survey sent to all students via email and informating them about LifeHack. Participant recruitment and data collection took place from November 2021 to October 2023. No compensation was offered for participation.

2.3 Inclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: participants had to 1) be aged 16 years or older, 2) be currently enrolled as a student or PhD candidate at a university within the Caring University Consortium, 3) report no active suicidal ideation, and 4) provide informed consent. Suicidal ideation was measured by asking whether the participant had considered or made plans to end their life. If answered 'yes', participants were asked how likely they were to act on these plans. Participants that responded with "somewhat likely" or "very likely" were excluded from the intervention and referred to more appropriate mental healthcare.

2.4 Intervention

2.4.1 Contents

LifeHack is a CBT-based online intervention designed to improve the mental health of the general student population. It consists of 12 lessons which can be categorized into four themes: emotional well-being, studies, social life, self-esteem, along with an introductory and concluding lesson (see Table 1).

The lesson topics were selected in a two step process: first, clinical psychology experts from the Caring Universities Consortium created a longlist of mental healthrelated topics they considered beneficial for students. Second, this list was presented to a group of students (n=156) who were asked to identify the most relevant topics. The topics selected by the most students were incorporated into the program.

Each lesson was developed using cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) as a general framework, while also drawing on principles from positive psychology, behavior change approaches, and interpersonal skills and incorporating student input. Each lesson contained a mix of psychoeducation, reflective questions, interactive exercises, and homework assignments. In order to ensure the program was engaging and appealing, we used text as well as visuals in the form of illustrations, infographics and videos. Each lesson took around 30 minutes to complete. The intervention could be accessed on any device that had internet connection.

2.4.2 Guidance

All LifeHack participants received text-based guidance by an e-coach. The ecoaches were master students Clinical Psychology from the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam who received training by and supervision from a psychologist with ecoaching experience. During registration, a participant could choose one of three ecoaches based on a photograph and short biography. Each time a participant completed a lesson, they would receive a written message from their e-coach regarding that lesson. The coaching process was as follows: an e-coach would read a participant's answers to the reflective questions and assignments in a lesson. They would then write a coaching message within three working days using motivational interviewing techniques by making affirmations, asking open ended questions, and using reflections (Hall et al., 2012). The e-coach would send this message to the participant via the platform, who would then receive an email notification that a new coach message is available to them upon logging into the intervention.

2.5 Differences between the interventions

To examine the effect of the principle of autonomy on intervention adherence, this study compared two versions of LifeHack that contained exactly the same content and guidance, but they differed in the way the content was offered.

In the first version, the 'structured' condition, autonomy was limited. Participants had to go through the categories in a fixed order, which can be seen in Table 1 (i.e. from top to bottom). Completing the first lesson of a category, as indicated by an asterisk (*) in Table 1, would unlock the other two lessons in that category as well as the first lesson of the next category. In order to reach a later category, participants would have to complete at least the first lesson of all previous categories. For example, if a participant was interested in the lesson 'Overcome perfectionism & fear of failure' from the fourth category, they would first have to complete all other asterisked lessons before they could access it. In this condition, autonomy was limited as participants were not allowed to choose which lessons they wanted to complete and in which order.

In the second version of LifeHack, referred to as the 'autonomy' condition, participants had full autonomy over topic-choice. They could access all categories and lessons upon completing the introductory lesson. They could choose which lessons they wanted to complete and which to skip, as well as how many and in which order.

2.6 Randomization

Participants were randomly allocated to either the structured or autonomy condition. We used 1:1 block randomization via an automated online system built in with the Caring Universities platform, stratified by gender and university. Conditions were automatically assigned upon completion of the baseline assessment. This automated procedure ensured that the allocation was blinded.

2.7 Assessment measures

2.7.1 Sociodemographic information

We assessed the following sociodemographic data: age, gender, marital status, nationality, current university, education level (Bachelor, Master or Ph.D.), and current usage of psychotherapy (yes/no) and/or psychofarmaca (yes/no).

2.7.2 Primary outcome measure: General satisfaction

Participant's satisfaction with the intervention was measured using the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8) (Larsen et al., 1979). The CSQ-8 assesses overall satisfaction, including perceived quality and service, usefulness, and whether participants would reuse or recommend the intervention. It comprises eight items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (sum score 8-32), with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction. Though there are no official categories or cut-offs to interpret total scores, a recent article considered a score between 8-20, 21-26 and 27-32 as low, medium and high satisfaction respectively (Jilani et al., 2023). Another paper stated a total score of 24-32 indicates high satisfaction (Ozmen et al., 2023).

Example items include 'How would you rate the quality of service you received?', 'To what extent has our service met your needs?', 'If you were to seek help again, would you come back to our service?' and 'If a friend were in need of similar help, would you recommend our service to him or her?'.

The CSQ-8 has shown a good fit, high reliability and construct validity for online interventions (Boß et al., 2016). The Dutch version was found to have the same properties as the original questionnaire and was deemed suitable for use in the Netherlands (de Brey, 1983; De Wilde & Hendriks, 2005).

2.7.3 Secondary outcome measures

2.7.3.1 Guidance satisfaction

Participant's satisfaction with the e-coach was measured with the Working Alliance Inventory for guided internet interventions (WAI-I) (Gómez Penedo et al., 2020). It comprises 12 questions on a 5-point Likert scale (sum score 12-60) and a higher score demonstrates greater satisfaction. A score between 36 and 47 indicates sufficient satisfaction, and a score above 48 indicates high to very high satisfaction with the e-coach. The psychometric characteristics of the WAI-I were found adequate (Gómez Penedo et al., 2020).

2.7.3.2 Adherence

In this study, adherence was measured by calculating how many of the 14 lessons were completed by participants, which was tracked by the digital platform.

2.7.3.3 General mental health

General mental health was measured using the Mental Health Continuum -Short Form (MHC-SF) (Keyes, 2002). This instrument assesses mental health across three dimensions: emotional well-being (e.g. happiness, life satisfaction.), social well-being (e.g. feeling part of a community, contribution to society) and psychological wellbeing (e.g., self-acceptance, purpose in life, autonomy). It comprises 14 items on a 6-point Likert scale (sum score 0-70) and a higher score indicates better mental health. The MHC-SF has shown high internal reliability and moderate test-retest reliability in a Dutch sample (Lamers et al., 2011).

2.7.3.4 Depressive symptoms

Symptoms of depression were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) (Kroenke et al., 2001). It comprises 9 questions on a 4-point Likert scale (sum score 0-27) and a higher score indicates a higher level of depression. The PHQ-9 has shown high sensitivity, specificity, test-retest reliability and validity (Kroenke et al., 2001; Wittkampf et al., 2007).

2.7.3.5 Anxiety

Symptoms of anxiety were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7) (Spitzer et al., 2006). It comprises 7 items on a 4-point Likert scale (sum score 0-21) with higher scores indicating more severe anxiety. The GAD-7 has shown high sensitivity, specificity, internal consistency, test-retest reliability and validity (Löwe et al., 2008; Rutter & Brown, 2017; Spitzer et al., 2006).

2.7.3.6 Quality of life

Quality of life was measured using the Mental Health Quality of Life questionnaire (MHQoL) (van Krugten et al., 2022). It comprises 7 questions on a 4-point Likert scale (sum score 0-21), where a higher score indicates better quality of life. The MHQoL has shown good validity, test-retest reliability and internal consistency (Cronbach's α= 0.85) (van Krugten et al., 2022).

2.8 Sample size calculation

We could find no literature on the effect of autonomy on treatment satisfaction, nor research that directly compares interventions that promote autonomy with those that don't. Therefore, we based our power calculation on a two-tailed difference between means with an effect size of .3 (Cohen, 2013) which is small-to-moderate but still clinically meaningful. Our analysis indicated a total sample size of 580 (290 per arm) (Faul et al., 2007).

2.9 Data analyses

IBM SPSS version 30 was used for the statistical analyses. All participants that were randomized were included in the primary analyses in accordance with the Intent-to-Treat principle (ITT).

First, we examined differences in baseline characteristics between the two study arms (i.e., the structure and autonomy conditions) to assess the success of randomization. We also compared study completers and non-completers to determine whether attrition may have introduced systematic bias. These analyses were conducted using chi-square tests and independent-samples t-tests.

For the satisfaction analyses, missing data were handled using multiple imputation with 60 imputed datasets generated via Fully Conditional Specification (FCS) and pooled results were computed using Rubin's rules. We examined differences between our two arms in satisfaction (CSQ-8, WAI-I) and adherence with independent sample t-tests. We calculated effect sizes (Cohen's d) manually based on the pooled results and interpreted using the benchmarks of 0.2 (small effect), 0.5 (moderate effect), and 0.8 (large effect) (Cohen, 2013).

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used to examine the effects of program version, time, and their interaction on all clinical oucome scores (i.e. MHC-SF, PHQ-9, GAD-7, MHQoL). The LLMs were conducted on the original data (i.e. not imputed), consistent with evidence that mixed-model analyses of longitudinal data can validly accommodate missing values without the need for multiple imputation (Twisk et al., 2013). We included sociodemographics (age, gender, marital status, nationality, university, education level, psychotherapy and/or medication) and baseline clinical scores as potential covariates in our analyses as these factors have been shown to possibly moderate the effectiveness of interventions (Ebert et al., 2013; Karyotaki et al., 2018; Reins et al., 2020). Models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were calculated from the LMM and we examined both time*group as well as time effects. Effect sizes were calculated manually based on the EMMs.

Lastly, we performed additional sensitivity analyses and repeated all the analyses for two subgroups. First, we did a Complete Case Analysis (CCA) analyzing only participants with complete data at post-measure. Second, we analysed only participants that had completed at least one lesson, and were therefore guaranteed to have been exposed to the autonomy-manipulation.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Participants

A total of 910 participants were assessed for eligibility between November 2021 and October 2023. Of these, 145 dropped during baseline assessment, 152 dropped out during account creation, and 38 were excluded due to active suicidal ideation and referred to other forms of mental health support (GP, psychologist).

575 participants were randomised and make up our total sample of which 286 participants were randomized to the 'structure' condition and 289 to the 'autonomy' condition A participant flow diagram can be seen in Figure 1. The average age of the total sample (n = 575) was 22.6 (SD = 4.33) and most participants were female (80.7%). The majority was from the Netherlands (62.1%) or another European country (29.7%), and were currently in their first bachelor year (32.2%) or master's (37.4%). Most participants were single (61.7%) and were currently not receiving psychotherapy or medication (83.1%). On average, participants indicated moderate general mental health (MHC-SF M = 38.04) and quality of life (MHQoL M = 13.21), and had mild levels of depression (PHQ-9 M = 8.33) and anxiety (GAD-7 M = 7.29).

We compared baseline characteristics (i.e. age, gender, nationality, university, education level, marital status, current professional help, PHQ-9, GAD-7, MHC-SF, MHQoL) between the two arms. Participants in the 'autonomy' condition scored higher on baseline anxiety (M= 7.8) and depression (M= 8.8) compared to the 'structured' condition (M=6.8 and 7.8 respectively). Full details on the full sample as well as the comparison of the two arms can be found in Table 2.

3.2 Study drop-out

Of the 575 participants who started the study, 217 (37.7%) completed the post-test questionnaires and 358 (62.3%) did not. Study drop-out between the two conditions was comparable: 60.4% in the autonomy condition and 64% in the structure condition. Study completers and non-completers did not differ significantly in age, gender, nationality, marital status, professional help, or baseline clinical characteristics (all p > .05). Significant differences were observed only for education level (p = .004): completers were more frequently doing a Master's and less frequently in their first year Bachelor compared to study non-completers. Full details on the comparison of study completers and non-completers can be found in Appendix 1.

3.3 Primary outcome: General satisfaction

We conducted an independent samples t-test to compare participant general satisfaction (CSQ-8) at post-test between the two conditions.

There was no significant difference in CSQ-8 scores between the structured condition (n = 286, M = 22.11, SE = 3.20) and the autonomy condition (n = 289, M = 22.44, SE = 3.19) (Cohen's d=.07). See Table 3.

3.4 Secondary outcomes

3.4.1 Guidance satisfaction (WAI-I)

Guidance satisfaction (WAI-I) between conditions was also compared using an independent samples t-test. Scores did not differ significantly between the structured condition (n = 286, M = 41.60, SE = 8.14) and the autonomy condition (n = 289, M = 42.15, SE = 7.17) (Cohen's d=.04). See Table 3.

3.4.2 Adherence

Adherence was defined as 'the number of lessons completed'. In the structured condition (n=286), participants completed an average of 2.36 lessons (SD = 3.13), with a median of 1. In this condition, 107 participants (37.4%) completed zero lessons, 57 (19.9%) completed one lesson, and 122 (42.7%) completed 2 or more lessons. In the autonomy condition (n=289), participants completed an average of 2.21 lessons (SD = 3.29), with a median of 1. A total of 119 participants (41.2%) completed zero lessons, 55 (19%) completed one lesson, and 115 (39.8%) completed 2 or more lessons.

We compared adherence across our two arms with an independent samples t-test.

Though participants completed slightly more lessons in the structured condition than the autonomy condition, this difference was not significant: structured condition (n = 286, M = 2.36, SD = 3.13) vs. the autonomy condition (n = 289, M = 2.21, SD = 3.29): t(573)=.569, p=.569.

3.4.3 Clinical outcomes: General mental health, quality of life, depression and anxiety

The analyses yielded a significant time-effect for all clinical outcomes. Participants across both versions of the intervention showed a significant reduction in PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores, and a significant increase in MHC-SF and MHQoL scores from baseline to post-intervention, all p < .001. All effect sizes (Cohen's d) were small to moderate. Full details can be found in Table 4. When comparing the structured and autonomy conditions, there were no significant differences in post-test scores on all clinical measures (all p > .05) after adjusting for baseline clinical scores and sociodemographic variables (age, gender, nationality, university, marital status, medication). See Table 5 for full details.

3.4.4 Sensitivity analyses

Our main analyses were performed on all randomized patients. We then repeated all analyses on two subgroups of students. First, a complete case analysis (CCA) including only students who completed post-test measures. Second, examining participants that completed at least one lesson.

The results for both subgroups were very similar to those of the total sample: there were no significant differences between groups on satisfaction, adherence and mental health outcomes. Across groups, there was a significant time effect for all mental health outcomes.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Principal results

The present trial examined the effects of autonomy in a newly developed, guided digital CBT-based intervention (LifeHack) aimed at improving mental health in college students. Participants received one of two versions of LifeHack that differed only in the degree of autonomy. At baseline, participants displayed moderate general mental health problems as well as mild levels of depression and anxiety, although patients in the autonomy condition showed slightly more symptoms than those in the structured condition.

Participants indicated being moderately satisfied with the general intervention as well as with the guidance and there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups. There were also no significant differences in the number of completed lessons (M=2.3). Across both conditions, participants showed statistically significant improvements over time in general mental well-being (d=.24), quality of life (d=.41), depressive symptoms (d=.36) and anxiety symptoms (d=.38) but there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups.

Sensitivity analyses using complete-case analyses and only participants that completed at least one lesson yielded patterns consistent with the main results, further strengthening the conclusion that enhancing autonomy by letting participants choose topics of interest within the intervention did not significantly enhance satisfaction, adherence or clinical outcomes in the present study.

4.2 Comparison to Prior Work

The absence of between-group differences did not confirm our hypothesis that increasing autonomy would enhance satisfaction, adherence, and clinical outcomes. To our knowledge, no studies directly examined the effect of autonomy in digital mental health interventions, however, autonomy was shown to have small positive effects on physical health behavior change (Sheeran et al., 2020). One possible explanation for our lack of findings regarding autonomy relates to the presentation of the content in the autonomy condition: displaying all 14 topics simultaneously may have increased cognitive load or induced choice paralysis, potentially offsetting the intended benefits of autonomy (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000). Additionally, common elements across conditions (e.g., content and guidance) may have overshadowed any incremental benefit of autonomy.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of recent work emphasizing that revolutionary progress in digital mental health is unlikely to result from single, isolated innovations. Instead, improvements are expected from the accumulation of incremental refinements in intervention design and delivery (Cuijpers et al., 2026). The autonomy manipulation in the present study can be viewed as one such incremental refinement. While theoretically grounded and practically relevant, the manipulation may have been insufficient to produce measurable effects. Our findings therefore do not undermine the relevance of autonomy as a motivational principle, but rather highlight the challenge of detecting small, additive effects in real-world digital interventions.

Participant satisfaction in the present study was in line with prior digital intervention research. Studies on interventions across a range of mental health complaints (i.e. stress, depression, procrastination) for college students report moderate to high satisfaction rates (Ozmen et al., 2025; Rahmadiana et al., 2021; Terp et al., 2022).

This further strengthens the notion that digital interventions are generally acceptable to college students.

While adherence rates were in line with findings from similar open-access online mental health interventions offered within the Caring Universities Consortium (Amanvermez et al., 2023; Ozmen et al., 2025), they were on the lower side when compared with other student mental health interventions (Harrer et al., 2018; Palacios et al., 2018; Rith-Najarian et al., 2024). However, these latter studies either offered financial compensation for study participation, only included participants with elevated clinical symptoms who may have been more motivated to engage with the treatment, or used automated daily motivational SMS reminders. The present study was for the general student population who averaged only mild levels of depression and anxiety, and we offered no compensation nor phone-based reminders. These differences likely contributed to the comparatively lower adherence observed in our study. However, our participants improved from pre-to post-test, and while we did not have an inactive control, our findings may be an indication that even a relatively low adherence can still yield beneficial effects and is therefore acceptable.

Consistent with meta-analyses showing that (digital) mental health interventions can effectively improve outcomes in students (Cuijpers et al., 2021), the present study found that participants from both versions of LifeHack showed small to moderate improvements in well-being, depression, anxiety, and quality of life from pre-to posttest, though we cannot directly attribute these to the intervention due to the lack of an inactive control group.

4.3 Strengths and limitations

The present study has several limitations. The first limitation concerns the operationalization of the construct of 'autonomy'. Autonomy was implemented as the ability to choose intervention topics, which may represent a relatively narrow manipulation of the construct. The intervention may have benefited from additional autonomy-enhancing features such as goal-setting, task options and feedback options (Alberts et al., 2024). Additionally, presenting all topics simultaneously may have increased cognitive load or choice paralysis (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000), potentially reducing engagement. Consequently, the autonomy manipulation in the present study may not have been sufficiently impactful to produce measurable effects.

A second limitation is the high attrition rate (37.7% completion). High attrition can introduce bias when participants who discontinue differ systematically from those who complete the study, potentially affecting the validity of the findings (Nunan et al., 2018). The observed attrition may partly reflect characteristics of the study design: the intervention was implemented in a naturalistic setting and offered as a service for students, and participants may not have been highly motivated to complete assessments. While this low-threshold and scalable design increases ecological validity and accessibility, it may also reduce engagement with the assessments, contributing to higher attrition and potential attrition bias. However, no meaningful differences were observed between study completers and non-completers, suggesting that the risk of attrition bias may be limited.

A key strength of the present study is its novel theoretical contribution. We experimentally isolated a single autonomy-supportive design element and compared two otherwise identical interventions that differed only in this element. Although autonomy is widely recognized as an important factor in physical health behavior change (Sheeran et al., 2020), to our knowledge, prior research has not directly tested the effect of a clearly defined autonomy-supportive component within a controlled design. The present study contributes to a still limited body of research examining the specific mechanisms that foster motivation and enhance the effectiveness of digital interventions.

Another notable strength is the ecological validity of the study. The present intervention was delivered as part of a large-scale mental health platform available to college students across the Netherlands, rather than under highly controlled trial conditions. This real-life implementation ensures the generalizability of our findings to similar large-scale, routine care settings (Glasgow et al., 1999). It also suggests the potential feasibility of integrating mental health interventions within the infrastructure of higher education, indicating that such approaches may have the capacity for wide-reaching impact in student populations (Andersson & Titov, 2014; Ebert et al., 2018). Given the increasing mental health needs among college students (Auerbach et al., 2018), the ability to implement and evaluate interventions for students is both timely and critical.

4.4 Directions for future research

We have several recommendations for future research. First, the optimization of the autonomy-supportive intervention. To improve the process of topic selection, we recommend incorporating a tool into LifeHack to help participants identify the most relevant topic(s) to their current needs rather than presenting all 14 at once and potentially creating clutter and mental overload. Such a tool could analyse a participant's baseline assessment data and make a suggestion for most relevant topic. In addition, participants could read a one-sentence description of each lesson to select the most relevant one(s), which would then become the focus of the intervention. To further support autonomy, we recommend incorporating a brief goalsetting exercise within each lesson, allowing participants to identify specific goals and reflect on their personal reasons for change (Alberts et al., 2024). Lastly, participants could be offered meaningful choices in how they complete exercises (e.g., a writing reflection or a guided audio exercise), enabling them to select the format that best fits their preferences or context (Alberts et al., 2024).

A second recommendation for future research is to evaluate LifeHack's effectiveness as a mental health intervention for students. Although this study primarily examined the role of autonomy, participants did show significant improvements across outcomes in both conditions. However, the absence of an inactive control group prevents causal attribution of these improvements to the intervention, as alternative explanations such as regression to the mean or spontaneous recovery cannot be ruled out (Mohr et al., 2009). Future research should therefore employ a randomized controlled design with a wait-list or no-treatment condition to establish the intervention's effectiveness.

5. Conclusions

The present trial did not find evidence that adding a single autonomy-supportive feature to a guided digital mental health intervention for students improved satisfaction, adherence, or clinical outcomes.

These findings suggest that introducing a single autonomy design element such as allowing participants to choose lesson topics may be insufficient to produce measurable improvements in engagement or outcomes. Rather, meaningful gains in digital mental health interventions may require the cumulative effect of multiple design features.

Nonetheless, this study represents an important initial step in applying autonomy within digital mental health interventions, offering valid insights from a real-world university setting and offering clear directions for future intervention development and research.
