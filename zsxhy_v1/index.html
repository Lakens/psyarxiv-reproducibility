Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Next to acoustic cues, lexical stress can be communicated visually through the timing of beat gestures. This study investigated for the first time how auditory and visual lexical stress cues are weighted in meaningful sentences. Minimal stress pairs of Spanish verb forms (clasiFIco, "I classify" vs. clasifiCÓ, "(s)he classified") were embedded in sentence contexts that biased towards either member of the pair. Stress was manipulated acoustically along a continuum, and visually by aligning a beat gesture to the penultimate or last syllable. Native listeners selected the pronoun (yo "I" or ella "she") they felt fit best with the sentence. Listeners were sensitive to acoustic cues, as indicated by both responses and response times. While gesture alignment did not significantly affect pronoun selection, response times were shorter when the gesture matched the semantic context bias. This study thus shows that in full sentences, congruent (vs. incongruent) gestural timing affects word recognition.

Introduction

In face-to-face speech comprehension, a listener is tasked with combining rich acoustic and visual signals in the context of a discourse (Holler & Levinson, 2019). Indeed, the acoustic and visual components of the speech signal are processed together and affect each other, as demonstrated by the classic McGurk effect, in which some listeners perceive an illusory syllable ("da") after hearing someone say "ba" while simultaneously seeing them produce "ga" (McGurk & MacDonald, 1976). More representative of natural communication, it has been shown that seeing mouth movements and gestures facilitates speech comprehension in noise (Drijvers & Özyürek, 2017; Maran et al., 2026; Sumby & Pollack, 1954), and that questions accompanied by gestures elicit faster responses (Holler et al., 2018). Given the multimodal nature of human communication, it is key for audiovisual experiments to approach naturalistic situations (Holler & Levinson, 2019; Perniss, 2018).

Prosody, the dimension tested in the present study, is one aspect of communication that is particularly audiovisual (Prieto et al., 2025; Guellaï et al., 2014; Prieto et al., 2015). This holds even at the word level: lexical stress is communicated acoustically through suprasegmental cues, such as syllable duration (Cutler, 2005), and often co-occurs with visual signals, such as manual gestures, eyebrow movements, and head nods (Bosker & Peeters, 2021; Leal-Arenas & Huensch, 2024; Scarborough et al., 2009). Nevertheless, research on lexical stress perception has traditionally focused on the auditory aspect (e.g., Soto-Faraco et al., 2001; Cutler & Van Donselaar, 2001; Van Donselaar et al., 2005; Reinisch et al., 2010; Zahner et al., 2019). Moreover, multimodal studies remain largely limited to investigating audiovisual stress perception in isolated words or uninformative carrier sentences that do not represent the semantically rich utterances that occur in everyday speech (e.g., Bujok et al., 2025; Rohrer et al., 2025; Leal-Arenas & Huensch, 2024; Bosker & Peeters, 2021).

This study addressed these limitations, investigating the audiovisual perception of lexical stress in full, meaningful sentences with semantic constraints. In particular, this study examined the relative contribution of acoustic, visual, and abstract linguistic cues to the perception of lexical stress. To do so, acoustic and gestural markers of stress were independently manipulated for Spanish verb forms, which were embedded in semantically constraining sentences. That is, these sentences contained an initial adverbial phrase providing a semantic cue for one specific stress pattern on the verb form. This way, the current study tested how multimodal components of the speech signal are weighted and integrated with each other, and with predictions generated by preceding sentence context. Moreover, the use of an audiovisual paradigm with full sentences offers a better representation of natural communication.

Background

Lexical stress refers to the relative prominence of one syllable in a word, compared to the other syllables in that word (Cutler & Jesse, 2021; Cutler, 2005). In free-stress languages, such as English, Dutch, and Spanish, the stressed syllable of a word is not restricted to a specific position in that word. As a result, these languages allow minimal lexical stress pairs, in which words differ only in the position of lexical stress (e.g., English OBject vs. obJECT, where capital letters denote stress). In these cases, stress is an essential cue to the lexical identity of a word. Also apart from minimal pairs, stress provides useful information to the identity of the word. Indeed, corpus analyses on English, Dutch and Spanish show that including lexical stress information reduces the number of lexical items embedded in other words (Cutler et al., 2004; Cutler & Pasveer, 2006; Van Heuven & Hagman, 1988). Spanish stands out especially in this regard: the average number of embedded competitors per word in Spanish is 2.32 when only considering a word's segments. However, this 'embedding rate' drops drastically to 0.73 stress is taken into account (e.g., CAra "face" is not an embedded competitor of caraMElo "caramel" if stress is considered; Cutler et al., 2004). Lexical stress information may therefore be used in word disambiguation, facilitating speech comprehension. Indeed, several behavioural experiments have shown that lexical stress is immediately taken into account during speech perception. Even for isolated syllables, Dutch and Spanish listeners have been shown to perform above chance when instructed to decide whether a spliced-out syllable is stressed or not (Cutler & Van Donselaar, 2001; Torreira et al., 2014).

Listeners of these two languages were also faster to respond in lexical decision tasks when they were offered an auditory prime that matched the target in stress pattern, than when the target and prime mismatched (Soto-Faraco et al., 2001; Van Donselaar et al., 2005). For example, when Spanish participants heard the fragment PRINci-(/ˈpɾin.θi/) as the acoustic prime, they were faster to respond to the visually presented PRINcipe "prince", compared to when an unrelated control condition was offered as the auditory prime. Conversely, if the target word mismatched the stress pattern produced in the auditory prime (e.g., prinCIpio "beginning" following the PRINci-prime), participants were slower relative to the control condition. For Spanish, these effects were even of similar size as compared to segmental mismatches (e.g., using word pairs like sardina "sardine" and sardana "sardana" -a type of dance; Soto-Faraco et al., 2001). The immediate effect of stress information on word processing is further underscored by eye-tracking studies. For example, when Dutch participants heard a word (e.g., okTOber "October") they were more likely to look at the corresponding word rather than its competitor (OCtopus "octopus") well before the onset of the disambiguating final syllable (Reinisch et al., 2010). Suprasegmental information conveyed by lexical stress thus has immediate implications on word retrieval processes, showing both facilitatory and inhibitory effects, and is taken into account not after, but during online speech perception.

Phonetically, lexical stress is most commonly realised through suprasegmental characteristics, such as movement in F0 and increases of syllable duration and intensity (Cutler, 2005; Cutler & Jesse, 2021; Rietveld & van Heuven, 2016), though individual variability in stress production exists (Severijnen et al., 2024). In some languages, such as English and Catalan, lexical stress is also implemented segmentally. Specifically, (some) vowels are reduced to schwa in unstressed syllables (Cutler, 2015; Nadeu, 2014; Prieto & Ortega-Llebaria, 2006). In other languages, such as Spanish, vowel reduction plays a smaller role -also because the schwa sound does not exist in the Spanish vowel inventory (Cutler & Jesse, 2021). In Spanish, lexical stress is primarily realised through syllable duration, though F0 and intensity naturally covary with it (Ortega-Llebaria & Prieto, 2007; 2011). Nevertheless, Torreira et al. (2014) found that there is considerable acoustic overlap between stressed and unstressed syllables in Spanish.

In addition to acoustic dimensions, lexical stress can also be communicated through visual signals, such as manual 'beat' gestures (Bosker & Peeters, 2021). Beat gestures are brief, biphasic movements of the hand that occur very frequently in natural communication and are used to convey emphasis rather than semantic meaning (McNeill, 1992; Shattuck-Hufnagel & Ren, 2018). Beat gestures tightly align temporally with accentuated words and syllables (Leonard & Cummins, 2011; Krahmer & Swerts, 2007), allowing listeners to use them as a cue to both phrasal and lexical stress in speech perception (Krahmer & Swerts, 2007; Bujok et al., 2025; Rohrer et al., 2025; Bosker & Peeters, 2021). Indeed, beat gestures can attract lexical stress perception to the syllable during which the gesture is produced (Bosker & Peeters, 2021; Bujok et al., 2025; Rohrer et al., 2025). For example, when presented with identical acoustic tokens, a Spanish listener is more likely to perceive HAblo "I speak" with initial stress when the apex of the gesture (the point of maximal extension of the hand) is aligned to the first syllable of the word. Conversely, when it is aligned to the second syllable, the listener is more likely to perceive haBLÓ "(s)he spoke" with final stress (Rohrer et al., 2025). Moreover, when the difference in acoustic prominence between the stressed and unstressed syllables is less pronounced, the effect of the beat gesture on lexical stress perception is even larger (Bujok et al., 2025; Rohrer et al., 2025; Maran et al., 2025) and more consistent within listeners over time (Cos et al., 2024). Beat gestures thus seem to provide reliable evidence to the location of lexical stress, complementing that of the acoustic signalespecially when the acoustic signal is less reliable. These studies on beat gestures investigated stress perception in a multimodal, and therefore more naturalistic setting compared to a speech-only situation. Nevertheless, research on audiovisual stress perception so far remains limited in that it focused on isolated words or words in carrier sentences only (e.g., "Now I say…"). It therefore remains unclear to what extent beat gestures influence lexical stress perception in more naturalistic sentences.

Moreover, in addition to acoustic and visual markers of lexical stress, it is possible that stress processing is also affected by abstract linguistic predictions generated from the sentence context. In Spanish, for example, the minimal stress pair clasiFIco / clasifiCÓ "I classify / (s)he classified" represents a difference in verb tense. A sentence starting with a reference to a specific point in time (e.g., anoche "last night") may cause the listener to be more expectant of a verb form with a corresponding tense (i.e., clasifiCÓ), affecting the processing of the subsequent acoustic and visual signal of the verb form itself. The current study addressed these issues, first asking what role beat gestures play in lexical stress perception for words that are embedded in complete utterances and second, how the effect of acoustic and visual cues to stress interacts with prior semantic constraints.

To interpret the relative contribution of acoustic and visual markers of stress on the one hand, and the importance of predictions from the sentence context on the other, the current study adopted a cue integration framework proposed by Martin (2016). In this framework, cues of different levels of linguistic representation are combined after weighing them according to their estimated reliability in the current situation. The model assumes that lower-level ('signalbased') cues (e.g., the acoustic signal, phonemes and words) and higher-level, abstract cues (ranging from phrase structure to discourse context, pragmatic meaning and world knowledge) interact with each other to bias the processing of the upcoming speech signal. As such, the model is particularly suited for interpreting the role of semantic context (an abstract linguistic cue) and its effect on the subsequent processing of acoustic and gestural information (signal-based cues). While the framework has received support from both behavioural (Kaufeld, Naumann, et al., 2020; Kaufeld, Ravenschlag, et al., 2020; S. W. Y. Li et al., 2026) and neuroimaging studies (e.g., Bai et al., 2022; Martin, 2018), the current study is the first to apply the framework to visual cues (i.e., signal-based cues from a different modality).

Finally, at least for words in isolation, it has been shown that audiovisual lexical stress perception is subject to considerable individual variability. Some listeners appear to primarily weigh the auditory component, while others are guided to a larger extent by gestural information (Cos et al., 2024; Rohrer et al., 2025). This individual variation does not seem to correlate with measures of phonological or visual working memory (Rohrer et al., 2025). By means of exploration, the current study tested the role of musical aptitude as another potential source of individual variation. Previous research has demonstrated a relationship between musical skills and prosodic perception (for a meta-analysis, see Jansen et al., 2023). Some studies have even suggested that music and speech processing use shared mechanisms (e.g., Perrachione et al., 2013). Given the acoustic properties of lexical stress and the rhythmic nature of beat gestures, it is possible that measures of musicality can indeed be correlated with acoustic or visual effects in lexical stress perception.

Current study

The current study tested whether the timing of a beat gesture affects the perception of lexical stress for words that are embedded in semantically constraining or unconstraining full Spanish sentences. Spanish is a pro-drop language, usually omitting the pronoun marking the subject.

Moreover, all regular verbs ending in -ar contain several minimal stress pairs (e.g., clasiFIco vs. clasifiCÓ), making lexical stress perception exceptionally relevant. Beat gestures were aligned to the main verb form of a sentence (e.g., En este momento, clasifico las plantas según su tipo, "In this moment, I classify the plants according to their type", with the verb form marked in boldface), presented as an audiovisual stimulus. Through video and audio editing, we manipulated the acoustic cues to lexical stress (creating a continuum ranging from clasiFIco "I classify" to clasifiCÓ "(s)he classified"), as well as the alignment of a beat gesture to either the penultimate syllable or the last syllable. Finally, the semantic content of the initial phrase of the sentence varied, providing a bias to a present-tense reading (e.g., En este momento "at the moment"), past-tense reading (e.g., El pasado abril "last april") or no-tense bias in particular (e.g., Diligentemente "diligently", serving as a baseline condition). The semantic content of the sentence context thus served as an abstract linguistic cue.

Most studies on audiovisual stress perception asked participants which member of the minimal pair they perceived (i.e., HAblo or haBLÓ; Rohrer et al., 2025; see also Bujok et al., 2025; Bosker & Peeters, 2021; Leal-Arenas & Huensch, 2024). The current study adopted a different task to avoid drawing explicit attention to the verb form, which may lead listeners to boost the weighting of the local acoustic and visual cues relative to the context bias cue. In the current study, participants therefore responded to the stimuli by selecting the pronoun that they thought "fit best with the sentence", choosing between yo "I" and ella "she". In addition to these categorisation responses, response times were collected as a sensitive online measure of processing efficiency related to cue integration. The independent manipulation of acoustic, visual, and semantic cues allowed for the examination of the interplay and relative weighting of each combination of cues, as well as the formulation of a hypothesis for each cue by itself.

H1 -Audio effect.

With respect to the acoustic continuum, we expected to observe a gradual decrease in the likelihood of a yo-response being given when the audio changed stepwise from stress on the penultimate syllable (e.g., clasiFIco "I classify", congruent with yo) to stress on the last syllable (clasifiCÓ, incongruent with yo and congruent with ella; Rohrer et al., 2025; Bujok et al., 2025; Bosker & Peeters, 2021). In terms of response times, we expected that clear audio would elicit faster responses than ambiguous (i.e., continuum-medial) audio steps.

H2 -Beat gesture effect.

Analogous to previous research (Rohrer et al., 2025; Bujok et al., 2025; Maran et al., 2025; Cos et al., 2024; Bosker & Peeters, 2021), we expected beat gestures to affect lexical stress perception, also when the emphasised word was embedded in a full sentence. In other words, listeners would be more likely to perceive stress on a syllable when it had a beat gesture aligned to it. In terms of response times, there was no reason why one beat alignment condition would be associated with faster responses. However, it was possible that participants would respond faster if the beat gesture alignment matched with the acoustic cues to stress (e.g., a gesture falling on the last syllable of a word with clear final stress). This would be expressed through an interaction between audio (continuum) step and beat alignment.

H3 -Semantic context effect.

The semantic content of the sentence context was also expected to affect the perception of the verb form, as the sentence context provides a reliable cue to the tense of the verb. Compared to a sentence context without bias, sentences with a present tensebias were expected to elicit higher proportions of yo-responses, as the present-tense verb form (e.g., clasiFIco) is first person singular. Past tense-biasing sentence contexts would be associated with fewer yo-responses. Semantic context itself may not be associated with differences in response times; however, it is possible that responses are slower when the sentence context provides an expectation that is later mismatched by either the audio (e.g., clear clasifiCÓ audio following a present tense-biasing sentence context) or the beat gesture (e.g., a beat gesture aligned to the last syllable, cueing clasifiCÓ following a present tense sentence context).

The cue integration framework (Martin, 2016) predicts that cues of different levels of linguistic abstraction are integrated rapidly, so that high-level, semantic cues may also affect the processing of signal-based cues from the subsequent speech signal. Moreover, the relative weight by which cues are combined may change depending on their reliability. As such, it is possible that beat alignment is more influential when acoustic markers of stress are ambiguous, and therefore less reliable (cf. Rohrer et al., 2025; Bujok et al., 2025). Furthermore, it is possible that perception processes on the verb form are more efficient if several or all cues are in agreement, providing unified evidence for one member of the minimal stress pair and resulting in faster and less variable responses (see also the cumulative-cue hypothesis; Ambrazaitis & House, 2022). If this is the case, we expect to see shorter response times in those cases were acoustic and gestural cues match with each other or with the bias from the sentence context, compared to when different cues communicate different stress patterns.

Lastly, the current study targeted individual differences between listeners in their cue integration strategies through measures of musical aptitude. To explore this possibility, participants completed two modules of the Profiles of Musical Perception Skills (PROMS) test (Law & Zentner, 2012; Strauss et al., 2024). From this test, which contains modules on the perception of various characteristics of music and has undergone extensive validation, the current study tested Rhythm and Accent. The selection of these modules was motivated by the rhythmic nature of the beat gestures and the fact that musical accent, like lexical stress, involves relative differences in prominence. Moreover, these specific PROMS test modules have previously been adopted in several studies on gesture and prosody (e.g., Van Maastricht et al., 2022; P. Li et al., 2020; Baills & Prieto, 2023; Baills et al., 2018). However, as the data were collected at a later stage, these analyses are exploratory in nature.

Methods

All data and analysis scripts for this study will be made available in an open-access data repository upon publication. The stimuli for this study are available upon request after the article has been published.

Participants

A total of 90 participants aged between 18 and 40 (42 female, 46 male, 2 other; M Age = 28.69, SD = 4.85, range: 21-40) was recruited through Prolific (https://www.prolific.com/). All participants were native speakers of Spanish, born in Spain, and self-reported to be located in Spain while taking part in the study. All participants reported having normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no hearing impairments or language deficits. The same sample of participants was subsequently invited to take part in a second session, in which their musical abilities and aptitude were tested. Sixty-six participants returned to take part in this test (31 female, 33 male, 2 other; M Age = 28.68, SD = 4.77, range: 21-40).

On average, there were 94.6 days between sessions (SD = 32.7). For both sessions, participants provided informed consent as approved by the [ethics assessment committee, redacted for review]. Participants received £4 for their participation in the main experiment, and another £2 when they also participated in the second session.

Materials

The audiovisual materials for the main experiment were based on sixteen Spanish verbs with a minimal lexical stress pair in their conjugation (e.g., clasiFIco "I classify" vs. clasifiCÓ "(s)he classified"). The verbs were selected based on their high frequencies according to the News on the Web (NOW) component of the Corpus del Español (Davies, 2016). Additionally, the selected minimal pairs did not allow alternative stress patterns (e.g., word groups like the noun NÚmero "number", and the verb forms nuMEro "I number", and numeRÓ "(s)he numbered" were excluded) or meanings (words like caMIno meaning both the verb form "I walk", as well as the noun "path" were also excluded).

For each minimal pair, a set of three sentences was created (see Table 1 for an example set). All sentences had the same fixed structure, starting with an adverbial phrase, followed by the verb form. The sentence ended with a direct object noun phrase or an additional adverbial phrase, depending on the transitivity of the verb. In each sentence set, two sentences started with an adverbial phrase that was exclusively congruent either with the present tense form, or the past tense form. The third sentence of each set functioned as a neutral sentence context, in which both verb tenses were acceptable. Two native speakers of Castilian Spanish evaluated all sentences, making sure that the present-and past-biasing sentence contexts were only acceptable with a verb form in the corresponding tense, and that the neutral sentence contexts were acceptable with both tenses. Audio manipulation and audio-only pretest

Following Rohrer et al. (2025), three suprasegmental cues were manipulated: syllable duration, F0 contour, and mean syllable intensity. The audio component of each recorded sentence was manipulated and pre-tested to ultimately create a 5-step perceptual continuum between the stress patterns of the two verb forms (i.e., stress on the penultimate syllable or on the last syllable). Specifically, only the final two syllables of the verb form were manipulated to incrementally approach the acoustic characteristics of the verb form with the opposite stress pattern.

For each sentence, the boundaries of the final two syllables of the verb form were annotated manually in Praat. We then used a Praat script adapted from Rohrer et al. (2025) to linearly interpolate syllable duration, F0 contour, and average syllable intensity between the original recording and the opposite stress pattern. These characteristics were applied in 10% increments, resulting in 11 audio steps. For each sentence, the interpolation was applied to the original audio recording. In other words, the audio of present tense verb forms from the Present and Neutral Sentence Contexts was manipulated towards the acoustic characteristics of the corresponding form in the Past Sentence Context. Conversely, the audio from the Past Sentence Context verb was manipulated towards the standards of the Present Sentence Context verb form. The continua were relabelled such that the form with penultimate stress, cuing present tense, was mapped onto step 1 while the form with stress on the last syllable was mapped onto step 11. With each step in the continuum, the duration, F0 contour and intensity of a syllable changed together, forming a gradual change between the two stress patterns. See Section 1 of the Supplementary Information in the open data repository for a more detailed description of the acoustic manipulation.

The isolated manipulated verb forms were tested in an auditory-only pretest, with the aim of sampling a uniform perceptual continuum for each sentence. From each 11-step continuum, the middle 9 steps were tested. To reduce the duration of the test session, the 16 verbs were divided into two lists of 8, so that every participant would evaluate all stimuli for half of the verbs. A total of 39 native speakers of Castilian Spanish (M Age = 30.82, SD = 4.66) each evaluated all continua and audio steps for one of the experimental lists (8 verbs * 3 sentence contexts * 9 audio steps = 243 stimuli). In an online 2-AFC task, they were asked to indicate for each verb form which stress pattern they perceived (e.g., clasiFIco or clasifiCÓ).

These participants were subsequently excluded from the main audiovisual experiment.

Based on the results of the pretest, three audio steps were selected for each sentence to serve as the ambiguous steps in the ultimate 5-step continuum. This was done by calculating the proportion of responses indicating stress on the penultimate syllable for each audio step and selecting the three audio steps that were cumulatively as close as possible to 75%, 50% (fully ambiguous) and 25% response proportions (Figure 1). The average proportion of penultimate stress responses of all selected audio steps was .51; the average penultimate stress response proportion of the most ambiguous audio steps was also .51. Both highlight the gradual, evenly spaced nature of the resultant perceptual continua.

To maximize the naturalness of the stimuli, the face of the speaker was not masked in these stimuli -in contrast to Rohrer et al. (2025). However, to maintain the coordination between the lip movements of the speaker and the audio, it was not possible to simply shift the alignment between the audio and video components of the stimulus. Because the two video strips had to be merged again later, the beat gesture could be moved only by a discrete number of frames (rounding off considering the frame rate). 1 To delay a beat gesture apex, the first frame of the video was duplicated to enable the desired shift. To make the apex occur earlier, the first number of frames of the video were removed, and the last frame was duplicated in equal measure. This way, the beat apex approached the vowel onset as closely as possible. Finally, the manipulated video was recombined with the "face layer", and the corresponding manipulated audio stimulus was added.

All in all, there were 16 verbs, each with 3 sentence contexts, 5 audio steps, and 2 beat alignment conditions (aligned to the penultimate or ultimate syllable), for a total of 480 unique stimuli. The stimuli had an average duration of 3.37 seconds (SD = 0.36 s, range: 2.52 -4.24 s). To avoid an overly long experimental session, the stimuli were again divided into two lists by matching them in terms of lexical frequency according to frequencies reported in the NOW component of the Corpus del Español (Davies, 2016). Finally, 24 catch trials were added to each list, in which the beat alignment, auditory stress pattern and sentence context bias all supported the same stress pattern. In these catch trials, a large white cross was superimposed over the speaker's face. Participants were asked to respond by pressing the space bar when they encountered a catch trial. Since catch trials deviated from normal trials only visually, they allowed us to verify whether participants focused visually on the stimuli.

PROMS task

To explore individual variability in the way auditory and visual cues interact in lexical stress perception, the current study employed some modules from the Profile of Music Perception Skills (PROMS) test battery (Law & Zentner, 2012). The test was designed to provide a swift and objective assessment of a listener's ability to discern contrasts in several musical aspects (melody, pitch, timbre, tuning, rhythm, accent, tempo, and loudness). Each of these aspects is assessed through a module consisting of trials with an AAX structure, where the participant decides if the third fragment is the same as, or different from the first two, which are identical.

The participant responds on a scale with response options "definitely same", "probably same", "don't know", "probably different", and "definitely different", and receives one point in case of a correct 'definitely' response, 0.5 point in case of a correct 'probably' response, and 0 points otherwise. The test has been extensively validated for use with relatively few items, and with a subset of modules (Strauss et al., 2024; Zentner & Strauss, 2017); in the current study, we tested the modules Accent and Rhythm, as they were expected to be relevant skills specifically for the perception of beat gesture timing and stress perception in general. In total, the PROMS scores for a given individual could range from 0 to 18, as there were 18 trials.

Together, the two PROMS test modules took approximately 10 minutes to complete.

Procedure

The experiment was built and hosted online using Gorilla (Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020). After participants provided consent, they were asked to adjust the audio volume to a comfortable level. The use of headphones was required and checked using a short intensity-discrimination task (Woods et al., 2017).

The main experiment consisted of a 2-AFC task, where participants saw the video stimulus and selected their response from two options. A proxy task for the perception of lexical stress was employed, in order not to draw attention to the verb form itself, or one specific (auditory or visual) modality. Since the two verb forms of the minimal pair are each congruent with a different subject (1st vs. 3rd person singular), we asked our participants to indicate which pronoun (yo "I" or ella "she", congruent with clasiFIco or clasifiCÓ, respectively) they thought fit best with the sentence. Ella was chosen over él ("he"), to ensure that adjectives with a feminine gender marker matched both the pronoun and the gender of the speaker (in case the "I"-form was perceived). The response times of the 2-AFC responses were recorded as well, counting from video onset. Participants had the option to respond at any time once the video stimulus started playing, ensuring the collection of very sensitive response times.

A trial started with a blank screen for 500 ms, followed by a fixation cross for another 500 ms. Then, the video stimulus started playing automatically. The video phase of a trial always had a duration of 4250 ms, to accommodate the longest video stimulus (4.24 s). The entire stimulus was shown, even if a participant responded while it was still playing. If a response had not been provided during the video phase, the trial continued to a blank answer screen, where participants had another 3000 ms to respond.

Participants were instructed to press F for a yo-response, J for an ella-response, and the spacebar whenever they encountered a catch trial. The response options were visible throughout the entire trial, and were respectively shown on the left, right, and bottom middle sides of the screen. There was a brief practice block of 4 trials, including 2 catch trials, using verbs from the other list of the experiment. The stimuli were offered in a random order. There were two breaks with a maximum duration of 1 minute and the experiment had a median duration of 36 minutes.

Statistical analyses

Four participants were removed from the data set because they failed to provide accurate responses to more than one third of the catch trials. For the remaining data, categorisation responses were recoded with yo responses as 1 and ella responses as 0, to be used as the dependent variable in logistic regression models. Response times were calculated relative to verb offset, as that was the moment at which all cues to lexical stress were available to the participant. Responses with negative reaction times (i.e., responses given before verb form offset; 0.83%), as well as outliers outside of 3 SD from the grand mean, were removed from all analyses (1.94% in total).

Three data sets were analysed separately using R (R Core Team, 2026). For each, a logistic mixed-effects regression model was fit on the categorisation responses, and a linear mixed-effects regression model was fit on the log-transformed response time data, using the lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest packages (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). First, to assess whether gesture alignment affects lexical stress perception in full sentences like it does in words in isolation and words in carrier sentences, we analysed the trials with a neutral (nonbiasing) sentence context. Second, to see how semantic information affected the subsequent processing of acoustic and visual cues to lexical stress, we compared the trials with presenttense and past-tense biasing sentence contexts. The three sentence context conditions were also analysed all together; this model is available in Section 2 of the Supplementary Information.

The exploratory analyses including measures of musicality were also based on all three contexts combined.

The third data set consisted of the non-biasing sentence context trials of the current study, combined with data of a previous study (Cos et al., 2026), which used exactly the same stimuli and procedure, but exclusively employed non-biasing sentence context trials. This comparison was deemed relevant because of the current study's many conflicting stimuli (in which, for example, the sentence context biased towards the present-tense verb form while the acoustic cues indicated the past-tense stress pattern). The comparison with a data set without such incongruencies allowed us to assess how conflicting stimuli affected stress perception and cue weighting throughout the experiment. This comparison is included in Section 3 of the Supplementary Information.

Between the models based on categorisation responses and response times, different interactions were of interest. With respect to the categorisation responses, we expected to find a larger effect of beat alignment when audio was ambiguous, and therefore less reliable (following previous studies; Bujok et al., 2025; Rohrer et al., 2025; Maran et al., 2025). In the case of response times, however, there was no reason to expect that one beat alignment condition would be faster than the other when audio was ambiguous. Instead, we expected that response times would be faster when beat alignment and audio cues provided matching evidence for a specific lexical stress pattern. The same held for agreement between sentence context and beat alignment, and sentence context and audio continua. Specifically, when the sentence context provided a bias for the present-tense verb form, we expected faster response times when the suprasegmental cues or gesture alignment indicated stress on the penultimate syllable, compared to when these cues would emphasize the last syllable. Conversely, when the sentence context biased to the past-tense verb form, we expected faster response times if suprasegmental cues or beat alignment stressed the last syllable rather than the penultimate syllable. Because of this, the regression models fitted to categorisation responses included interactions between beat alignment and a separate factor for audio ambiguity, whereas models fitted to response times included interactions with the audio continuum itself. For the sake of brevity and overview, the exact model structures, including relevant two-way and three-way interactions for each of the regression models are detailed in Table 2. In terms of random effects, every model was fitted with random intercepts for both participants and verbs. Next, every fixed factor was entered for random slopes. If a model would not converge, the random slopes exhibiting the least variance were removed, and the model was fit again. Non-biasing sentence context trials

The first research question of this study was whether the alignment of a beat gesture can affect the perception of lexical stress when the critical word is simply embedded in a full sentence.

To this end, the first analyses focused on those trials from the experiment that contained a neutral sentence context that did not bias to any stress pattern by itself.

Categorisation response analyses

The model analysing neutral sentence context trials of the current experiment revealed a significant effect of Audio Step (β = -1.558, SE = 0.041, z = -37.974, p < .001), indicating that the proportion of yo-responses decreased as the audio gradually became more consistent with an ella-response (i.e., stress on the last syllable). The combination of the significant intercept (β = -0.974, SE = 0.404, z = -2.413, p = .016) and the significant effect of Audio Ambiguity (β = 0.379, SE = 0.164, z = 2.320, p = .020) reveal that there was a global tendency for selecting the ella-response. Crucially, the effect of Beat Alignment was not significant (β = -0.048, SE = 0.125, z = -0.388, p = .698), nor was its interaction with Audio Ambiguity (β = -0.028, SE = 0.110, z = -0.252, p = .801). Response time analysis

To improve the normality of the model's residuals, the response times were log-transformed.

The linear regression model fit on the response times revealed a significant effect of Audio Ambiguity (β = -0.109, SE = 0.010, t = -10.980, p < .001), which indicates that responses were faster when the acoustic cues to stress were clear. Response time analysis

As before, response times were log-transformed. As with the model analysing the categorisation responses, the trials with a past-biasing sentence context were used as the reference level. The model revealed a significant effect of Audio Step (β = -0.040, SE = 0.004, t = -9.250, p < .001), indicating that responses were faster as the audio gradually changed from stress on the penultimate, to stress on the last syllable (i.e., when the acoustic cues matched the sentence context mapped onto the intercept; Past). In addition, there was a significant effect of Audio Ambiguity (β = -0.067, SE = 0.010, t = -6.486, p < .001), suggesting that responses were generally slower if the acoustic cues were ambiguous between the two stress patterns.

Interestingly, the effect of Beat Alignment was significant (β = -0.055, SE = 0.010, t = -6.486, p < .001), showing that responses were faster when the beat gesture was aligned to the last syllable than when it was aligned to the penultimate syllable (i.e., when Beat Alignment matched the Past Sentence Context). The interaction between Beat Alignment and Audio Step was not significant, however (β = -0.007, SE = 0.009, t = -0.778, p = .436), suggesting no evidence for faster responses when acoustic and visual cues matched in the stress pattern they conveyed. The interaction between Beat Alignment and Audio Ambiguity also was not significant (β = 0.029, SE = 0.016, t = 1.773, p = .076), indicating no evidence that the effect of Beat Alignment on the response times was larger when the acoustic marking of stress was more ambiguous.

The model revealed no significant effect of Sentence Context on RTs (β = 0.008, SE = 0.023, t = 0.343, p = .733). However, several interactions including Sentence Context were significant. These included interactions with Audio Step (β = 0.105, SE = 0.006, t = 17.195, p < .001), Audio Ambiguity (β = -0.035, SE = 0.012, t = -3.043, p = .002), Beat Alignment (β = 0.118, SE = 0.033, t = 3.624, p < .001) and the three-way interaction with Beat Alignment and Audio Ambiguity (β = -0.049, SE = 0.023, t = -2.136, p = .033) -indicating that these effects were significantly different going from past-biasing to present-biasing sentence context trials.

To investigate these effects for the present-tense biasing sentence context, a relevelled model was fit, using Present Sentence Context as the reference level. This model also revealed significant effects of Audio Step (β = 0.065, SE = 0.004, t = 15.068, p < .001) and Beat Alignment (β = 0.063, SE = 0.023, t = 2.740, p = .006), notably in the opposite direction from the model with Past Sentence Context as its reference level. Specifically, response times were shorter when either the acoustic or gestural cues indicated stress on the penultimate syllable rather than the last syllable, matching the prediction from the Present Sentence Context -as opposed to the Past Sentence Context, where response times were shorter when the acoustic and gestural cues indicated stress on the last syllable, matching its respective temporal prediction.

Because of the reversed effect of Beat Alignment, the estimate for its interaction with Audio Ambiguity had a negative instead of a positive estimate. At the Present Sentence Context reference level, however, this interaction was also not significant (β = -0.020, SE = 0.016, t = -1.246, p = .213). The interpretation of the interaction is therefore the same as with the Past Sentence Context reference level, namely that the effect of Beat Alignment was not larger with ambiguous compared to clear audio. Lastly, the main effect of Audio Ambiguity was significant -in the same direction as the original model, though numerically larger (β = -0.102, SE = 0.010, t = -9.892, p < .001). Discussion

This study investigated how acoustic and visual cues to Spanish lexical stress are integrated with each other and with semantic predictions generated by the sentence context. Specifically, we were interested in the effect of beat gesture alignment on the perception of lexical stress for words that are embedded in full, meaningful sentences, thus taking a step forward in terms of naturalistic language use. To this end, participants evaluated audiovisual stimuli of a native speaker of Spanish producing complete sentences accompanied by a beat gesture aligned to either one of the two last syllables of the verb form. They indicated which pronoun they thought fit best as the subject of the sentence, which was taken to reflect which stress pattern they perceived. All of three cues (suprasegmental cues, gesture alignment, and semantic information) were expected to affect the proportion of responses for the two pronouns. In addition, we expected to find larger effects of gesture alignment when other cues were less informative (in the case of ambiguous audio or a non-biasing sentence context). Response times were also analysed, as it was hypothesised that trials with converging cues would be associated with faster response times, compared to trials containing mismatching cues. Finally, this study explored musical skills as a potential source of individual variation in the integration of audiovisual information from the speech signal, using two modules from the PROMS task (Law & Zentner, 2012).

Effects of suprasegmental cues on lexical stress perception

The analyses revealed a robust effect of the acoustic continuum on pronoun selection, confirming the hypothesis that listeners would be sensitive to suprasegmental information during lexical stress perception. As acoustic markers of stress gradually changed from indicating stress on the penultimate, to stress on the last syllable, participants were ever less likely to select the pronoun matching the penultimate stress pattern. This held for all sentence contexts, including those that provided a semantic bias to one specific stress pattern. However, the post-hoc exploratory analysis suggested that there are individual preferences for acoustic or semantic cues, which may indicate a difference in cue weighting strategies.

The response time analyses revealed that participants generally responded faster when stress was clearly marked acoustically, than when it was ambiguous -irrespective of potential biasing information from the sentence context. On top of this, when the sentence context supported a prediction for a verb tense, and as a result, one specific stress pattern, faster response times were observed when the acoustic cues to stress matched the predicted stress pattern, than when the acoustic cues mismatched it. Taken together, the results showed that listeners were sensitive to suprasegmental stress information. Moreover, this cue is informative enough to have an influential weight even when being integrated with linguistic predictions, if those can reliably be made. In other words, acoustic cues are not overruled by semantic information in lexical stress perception.

Effects of beat gesture alignment on pronoun selection

When it comes to pronoun selection, the analysis of pronoun responses provided no evidence for an effect of gesture alignment -contrary to our second hypothesis. At least during a task of pronoun selection, a beat gesture aligning with either one of the two last syllables did not result in significantly more responses of the pronoun matching the stress pattern indicated by the gesture. In this regard, the results of the current study seem to mismatch a range of studies that found such a gesture alignment effect on stress pattern categorisation for isolated words, both in Spanish and Dutch (Bujok et al., 2025; Rohrer et al., 2025; Maran et al., 2025; Cos et al., 2024), or for words in carrier sentences (Bosker & Peeters, 2021). Furthermore, the hypothesised trend that a larger effect of beat alignment on stress perception would be observed when the acoustic cues to stress were less reliable (cf. Rohrer et al., 2025; Bujok et al., 2025; see also Ye et al., 2025) was also not observed. Given the more naturalistic nature of the stimuli of the current study, which featured complete and meaningful utterances, these findings may be taken to suggest that the visual cue of beat gestures is weighted relatively lightly when it comes to the perception of lexical stress in complete utterances, at least when going by the behavioural responses.

The apparent reduction in the effect of gesture alignment is not straightforward to explain. One explanation might be the task adopted in the current study, which probed stress perception indirectly by asking listeners to select the pronoun that best fit the sentence.

Previous studies, on the other hand, simply asked participants which member of the minimal pair they perceived (in the case of Rohrer et al., 2025; e.g., HAblo or haBLÓ, including the formatting). The current study deviated from this paradigm to avoid explicitly drawing attention to the verb form as well as the acoustic modality, as participants might have strategically disregarded the rest of the sentence (including its biasing context) after getting used to the structure of the stimuli. It is therefore possible that the additional morphological evaluation of which pronoun better suited the perceived stress pattern obscured a categorisation effect.

There are several studies that do report effects of beat gestures for words embedded in sentences. However, these studies have focused mostly on phrasal stress, and how beat gestures may be used to emphasize words (i.e., not individual syllables), marking them as important or putting them in focus (Krahmer & Swerts, 2007; Leonard & Cummins, 2011; Dimitrova et al., 2016; Biau & Soto-Faraco, 2013). Some other studies report that beat gestures may help disambiguate syntactically ambiguous or complex sentence structures (Biau et al., 2018; Holle et al., 2012). Finally, it has been shown that listeners were faster to respond in a lexical decision task when the target word had a beat gesture aligned to it -though no difference was observed depending on whether the gesture was aligned to the canonically stressed syllable or not (Bujok et al., 2026; Maran et al., 2026). In these strands of research, beat gestures seem to have a meaningful effect, also when the words they are aligned to are embedded in sentences. As such, it is possible that beat gestures, in more naturalistic situations, are primarily interpreted as markers of information structure or even as paralinguistic markers, signalling that challenging or unexpected speech elements are coming up.

A related explanation is that beat gestures are a less reliable cue to lexical stress because it is more difficult to determine where exactly the gesture is aligned at the syllable level compared to the word level. That is, the temporal difference between aligning a beat gesture to one or the other syllable within a word is much smaller than the difference between aligning it to one or the other word in a sentence. Indeed, the current study, featuring a more naturalistic speech rate compared to studies using isolated words, exhibited a much smaller timing difference between beat conditions compared to the only other study on beat gestures and lexical stress in Spanish (Rohrer et al., 2025). In this older study, the timing difference between beat alignment conditions was on average 303 ms, compared to 170 ms in the present study.

The tighter timescale may have made it more difficult to determine where exactly the beat gesture was aligned. In turn, the cue of beat gesture alignment may have been treated as less reliable, causing it to be weighted only lightly compared to previous studies. Importantly, it is unlikely that the participants were unable to determine where the gesture was aligned at all, given the difference in response times between beat alignment conditions (discussed below in greater detail). Future studies may investigate whether the timescale is indeed an important factor in cue weighting, or if beat gestures naturally pertain primarily to higher-level prosodic or pragmatic domains (such as focus marking or 'warning' for unexpected elements in the speech signal).

Effects of beat gesture alignment on response times

As opposed to the pronoun selection responses, the response time analyses revealed that for trials with a sentence context that supported a prediction for one specific stress pattern, participants generally responded faster when the gesture was aligned to the syllable that matched the stressed syllable of the prediction. For example, in the case of present-tense biasing elements like En este momento "At the moment", creating a prediction for a presenttense (clasiFIco) stress pattern, participants responded faster if there was a beat gesture aligned to the penultimate syllable instead of the last syllable. These findings indicate that the listeners were sensitive to the syllable-level timing of the beat gesture, even if this cue was not weighted heavily enough to determine the listener's categorisation response.

There are several studies that showed that listeners are faster to respond to multimodal vs. unimodal utterances. For example, it has been shown that questions are answered faster if they are accompanied by semantically constraining gestures (including iconic and deictic gestures; Holler et al., 2018; ter Bekke et al., 2024), and that participants were faster at indicating whether they felt addressed or not when a stimulus was combined with an iconic gesture (Nagels et al., 2015). With respect to lexical access, it has been shown that participants were faster to relate primes and targets when the stimuli contained iconic gestures and speech that matched, rather than mismatched (Kelly et al., 2010). Because the current study did not feature any stimuli without gestures altogether, it cannot be determined whether matching gesture timing facilitated word recognition, or if a mismatching gesture timing disrupted it.

Nevertheless, the current study does show that listeners are sensitive to the subtle difference in timing of beat gestures when it comes to perceiving lexical stress. Taken together, it seems that, also in naturalistic sentences, native Spanish listeners are sensitive to (mis)matching beat gesture timing, even if this cue is perhaps weighted so lightly that it is unable to alter the listener's ultimate pronoun response.

The current study only used minimal lexical stress pairs, and the candidate syllables for bearing lexical stress were always adjacent. As such, it is possible that beat gestures have an even larger impact on word perception in cases where the candidate syllables to lexical stress are non-adjacent, as is the case for many embedded words and non-minimal pairs (e.g., Spanish

CAra "face" vs. caraMElo "caramel", Cutler et al., 2004). It is also possible that gesture alignment would be weighed more heavily in the cue integration process if other (e.g., suprasegmental) cues are unreliable or even absent, such as in noisy communicative settings or accented (learner's) speech. On the other hand, it is likely that natural speech exhibits larger variability in gesture-speech alignment (see Loehr, 2012), which may counteract the potential increased reliability of gestural cues in noise. Finally, in everyday communication, a speaker may employ a range of simultaneous visual signals to convey stress and emphasis, combining gestures with head and eyebrow movements (Holler & Levinson, 2019). As such, the current results -obtained with stimuli isolating the contribution of particular visual cues -may underestimate the potential of visual signals to communicate lexical stress in real conversations.

Exploratory analysis of musical aptitude

Lastly, the exploratory analyses using measures of musicality revealed that participants with higher PROMS scores exhibited a stronger effect of suprasegmental cues on pronoun selection.

Moreover, listeners with stronger musical skills were faster at responding whenever the acoustic cues to stress were clear, compared to participants with lower scores. Several other studies reported that stronger musical skills correlate with higher accuracy in lexical stress discrimination when it is clearly marked (see Jansen et al., 2023), though most of these studies focus on language learners -possibly because native speakers are expected to achieve overwhelmingly high accuracies (e.g., Podlipský et al., 2026; Nayak et al., 2022; Schwab & García, 2026; Degrave, 2017). None of these studies have tested native listeners at perceiving ambiguously marked stress, however; the current study tentatively suggests that in native listeners, musicality may provide an advantage in lexical stress processing, but only when lexical stress is clearly marked rather than ambiguous. This finding requires more rigorous investigation. The PROMS scores of musicality never interacted with any effect involving beat gesture alignment.

Conclusions and outlook

This study investigated audiovisual lexical stress perception in native speakers of Spanish, implementing stress through suprasegmental cues and beat gestures, for words embedded in full, meaningful sentences. Crossing these cues enabled us to investigate how multimodal cues are integrated during lexical stress perception. The results revealed that Spanish listeners are sensitive to acoustic cues when it comes to lexical stress perception of words in full sentences, even in the face of clear biases from the sentence context. Moreover, when combined with a semantic prediction, responses were faster when a beat gesture was aligned to the expected, rather than the unexpected syllable. Unlike previous studies using isolated words and unpredictive contexts, the gesture cue was not strong enough to change the listener's response, suggesting that the visual cue of gestures receives only limited weight in cue integration when it comes to lexical stress perception in connected speech. Future studies may investigate the circumstances under which visual cues exhibit a more influential weighting in cue integration, including speech in noise (reducing the reliability of acoustic cues), at lower speech rates (potentially increasing the reliability of gestural cues), or with different populations (e.g., second language learners who may benefit more from a transparent marker of lexical stress).

Conversely, if beat gesture alignment primarily affects the process leading up to the response more so than the response itself, it may be interesting to see how beat gestures affect the neurological processes behind lexical stress processing.
