Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Adolescents' expectancies and subjective task value beliefs for STEM decline across secondary school, yet few trials have compared different intervention components for enhancing these beliefs within the same trial. This four-arm randomized trial, framed by situated expectancyvalue theory, compared brief online interventions focused around goal setting, role modeling, their combination, and an active control. Participants were 855 Year 9 students (401 female, 454 male; M age = 14.68) across six Australian schools (3 urban, 3 rural). Outcomes were expectancy, intrinsic value, utility value, and perceived cost for STEM overall and, in exploratory analyses, for biology, chemistry, and physics. Compared to the control condition, the goal-setting-only condition produced higher post-intervention expectancy (Δ = 0.17, q = .034) and intrinsic value (Δ = 0.18, q = .034) but not utility value. Role modeling alone produced no significant benefit after statistical correction. The combined condition raised expectancy but also perceived cost. No gender interaction was significant. In rural (compared to urban) schools, exploratory analyses showed that, relative to the active control, goal setting alone raised utility value (Δ = 0.46, q < .001) and role modeling alone raised expectancy (Δ = 0.11, q < .001).

Exploratory subject-specific analyses showed similar goal-setting gains. Results suggest that a brief, structured online goal-setting sequence can raise adolescents' immediate STEM expectancy and intrinsic value, warranting longer-term follow-up.

STEM participation builds the scientific and technical capabilities needed for innovation, productivity, and responses to challenges such as public health and environmental sustainability (Department of Industry, Science and Resources, 2024), and demand for STEM-qualified workers in Australia continues to grow (Jobs and Skills Australia, 2024). Whether students enter STEM pathways depends not only on capability but on whether they expect to succeed and see STEM as interesting, useful, and worth pursuing, beliefs that predict later subject choices, aspirations, and careers (Guo et al., 2015; Toh & Watt, 2022). These expectancies and values often decline across secondary school while perceived costs become more salient (Gaspard et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2018). These changes occur shortly before Australian students confirm senior subject choices in Year 10, so supporting STEM motivation before then may broaden participation (Guo et al., 2015). STEM participation is also uneven across students and educational contexts. Female students remain substantially underrepresented in physics despite much stronger representation in biology (Australian Mathematical Sciences Institute, 2025; Marsh et al., 2019), a pattern consistent with evidence that stereotypes and identity-related beliefs shape how students evaluate different pathways within STEM (Cheryan et al., 2017). Students in rural areas also encounter narrower senior-subject offerings, less access to specialist teachers, and fewer visible examples of STEM careers than their urban peers (Holden & Zhang, 2024), which may further narrow their participation in STEM.

Brief psychosocial interventions offer one way to strengthen STEM motivation. Rather than changing curriculum content, they target students' beliefs, values, and interpretations of their academic experiences (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020; Harackiewicz & Priniski, 2018), and they have produced favorable effects on interest, achievement, persistence, and course-taking (Brisson et al., 2017; Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009). Despite these encouraging findings, three limitations constrain what this evidence can say about adolescents approaching consequential STEM choices (Rosenzweig & Wigfield, 2016). First, the strongest evidence comes from U.S. undergraduate courses (Asher et al., 2023; Harackiewicz & Priniski, 2018), and school-based trials have targeted a single subject, typically mathematics (Brisson et al., 2017; Gaspard et al., 2015). It remains unknown whether a brief intervention can shift beliefs about STEM as a broader domain, and whether effects are shared across biology, chemistry, and physics, which differ markedly in participation and gender composition. Second, interventions are usually evaluated as complete packages and rarely compared with each component delivered alone, so which components move which beliefs, and whether combining components within a fixed session adds benefit or dilutes an otherwise effective component, cannot be determined (Harackiewicz & Priniski, 2018; Linnenbrink-Garcia et al., 2026). Third, most school trials, unlike many college trials that randomize individual students online, randomize intact classrooms and deliver activities in person, so whether such activities retain their effects when delivered online, in a standardized format, within one scheduled class session is untested. The present study addressed all three gaps.

1.1. Theoretical Framework: Situated Expectancy-Value Theory

Situated expectancy-value theory (SEVT; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) explains achievement-related choices in terms of students' beliefs about whether they can succeed and the value they attach to particular activities, extending the original expectancy-value model (Eccles et al., 1983). Expectancy refers to students' beliefs about their likelihood of succeeding in a domain. Subjective task value includes intrinsic value, or the interest and enjoyment derived from an activity; utility value, or its usefulness for future goals; attainment value, or its importance to the self; and perceived cost, or perceptions of what someone must give up to do a task, including the effort, opportunity costs, and emotional demands associated with participation (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020; Flake et al., 2015). These beliefs are examined separately, as an intervention may change some and not others, and cost is not simply the inverse of positive value (Flake et al., 2015).

Under the "situated" view, these beliefs are shaped by students' experiences, social contexts, and interpretations of particular domains (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), so brief interventions are more likely to shift immediate appraisals than enduring dispositions, and those appraisals may differ across STEM overall, biology, chemistry, and physics, and by gender and school setting.

1.2. Goal Setting

Goal setting provides a structured way for students to translate broad aspirations into concrete and attainable actions. Goal-setting theory proposes that specific goals can direct attention, mobilize effort, and promote strategy use more effectively than vague intentions (Locke & Latham, 2002). In educational settings, proximal and attainable goals have been shown to support students' self-efficacy, achievement (Bandura & Schunk, 1981), and academic performance (Morisano et al., 2010), and implementation intentions can strengthen goal pursuit by specifying how students will respond when opportunities or obstacles arise (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). Personal-best goal setting likewise supports science aspirations (Martin et al., 2014) and problem solving (Ginns et al., 2018), though through repeated goals rather than a single brief activity.

The present study adapted these principles to a brief STEM-focused intervention that could be completed within one class session. Students first reflected on the relevance of STEM to their own lives (a prompt drawn from utility-value interventions; Hulleman et al., 2010), then constructed a SMART goal (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound), evaluated whether the goal was achievable, identified strategies for dealing with distraction and difficulty, and formulated an implementation-intention plan. Brief interventions integrating these elements have rarely been examined in secondary-school STEM with motivation as the immediate outcome.

Within SEVT, constructing an attainable goal and appraising whether it can be achieved may strengthen expectancy (Bandura & Schunk, 1981). A goal may also strengthen utility value if it helps students connect STEM to their lives and futures (Hulleman et al., 2010). Framing a self-generated goal around confidence, usefulness, or enjoyment may support intrinsic value (Locke & Latham, 2002; Martin et al., 2014). Cost could move in either direction, as planning for difficulty and distraction may reduce perceived barriers (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006), whereas appraising the effort a goal requires may make those demands more salient.

1.3. Role Modeling

Role models are people whose success shows others what is possible and how to achieve it (Morgenroth et al., 2015). They can shape students' motivation by providing concrete examples of people who have succeeded in domains that students may consider for themselves (Bandura, 1977; Dasgupta, 2011; Morgenroth et al., 2015).

In STEM, where stereotypes about who belongs, who can succeed, and who is interested in STEM (Master et al., 2016) shape self-concepts and aspirations, counter-stereotypical models may strengthen identification with the field (Dasgupta, 2011). Exposure to women in STEM has been associated with stronger STEM self-concept and identification among female students (Stout et al., 2011), and brief role-model interventions have produced positive motivational effects in some studies (Buckley et al., 2022; González-Pérez et al., 2020). Effects are not uniformly positive, however, as portrayals that emphasize effort and the development of ability and interest produce different responses from portrayals of exceptional talent (Bagès & Martinot, 2011; Gladstone et al., 2024), and models perceived as exceptional or psychologically distant can undermine anticipated success (Cheryan et al., 2011). Role models appear most motivating when students see them as attainable, similar to themselves, and successful through effort (Gladstone et al., 2024; Morgenroth et al., 2015). Within SEVT, a relevant and attainable model may raise expectancy through vicarious evidence of success (Bandura, 1977), raise utility value by making future pathways concrete (Morgenroth et al., 2015; Sjaastad, 2012), support intrinsic value when STEM fits students' possible identities (Dasgupta, 2011; Stout et al., 2011), and lower perceived cost by showing how its demands can be managed.

1.4. Moderation by Gender and School Location

Intervention effects may vary by gender, as female students often report lower expectancy and higher perceived cost in some STEM fields despite comparable achievement (Marsh et al., 2019; Watt et al., 2012). Female STEM role models may be particularly motivating for female students when perceived similarity strengthens identification (Dasgupta, 2011; Stout et al., 2011), although stereotypical or psychologically distant models can have unintended negative effects (Cheryan et al., 2011). Goal setting may also offer greater scope for improvement among students who start from a lower base (Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009; Schippers et al., 2015), as female students often do on these beliefs. Accordingly, both components were expected to benefit female students more than male students. School location may also moderate effects. The narrower STEM opportunities in rural settings noted above may shape how useful and attainable STEM appears to rural students.

Where pathways to STEM careers are less visible, rural students have fewer chances to consider what a STEM future could look like for them, so goal setting may strengthen the perceived usefulness of STEM for personal goals (Hulleman et al., 2010), and role models may make possible futures more visible and attainable (Morgenroth et al., 2015; Sjaastad, 2012). With little prior evidence, location was examined as an exploratory moderator.

1.5. The Present Study and Hypotheses

The present study tested whether two brief online activities, goal setting (GS) and role modeling (RM), could shift Year 9 students' expectancy, intrinsic value, utility value, and perceived cost for STEM. Students were individually randomized within classes to one of four conditions in a 2 × 2 factorial design (GS only, RM only, combined GS + RM, or active control).

Within one scheduled class session, students completed a baseline survey (T1), their assigned intervention, and an immediate posttest survey (T2). As the two activities target different SEVT processes, combining them was expected to offer a stronger option, delivering both approaches in one session (Harackiewicz & Priniski, 2018; Linnenbrink-Garcia et al., 2026). Within a fixed session, however, combining them reduces the dose of each, so the combined condition tested whether they add value under a realistic time constraint. The study asked whether the two components, alone or combined, improved students' overall STEM motivation (RQ1), whether their effects differed by gender (RQ2), and whether they differed by school location (RQ3). Gender moderation was predicted, whereas location was examined without a directional prediction.

More adaptive motivation was defined as higher expectancy, intrinsic value, and utility value and lower perceived cost. H1 and H2 addressed RQ1 and H3 addressed RQ2, whereas RQ3 and the subject-specific analyses were exploratory (Table S1).

H1 (components). Students in conditions containing GS, and students in conditions containing RM, will report more adaptive overall STEM motivation (higher expectancy, intrinsic value, and utility value, and lower cost) than students in conditions without that component.

H2 (conditions vs. control). Students in each intervention condition (GS only, RM only, and combined) will report more adaptive overall STEM motivation than students in the active control.

H3 (gender). The benefits of GS and of RM will be larger for female than for male students. Where this moderation is observed, the H2 contrasts will be examined separately for female and male students.

>Method

2.1. Participants

Participants were 865 Year 9 students randomized in six secondary schools (3 urban, 3 rural) in Victoria, Australia; the 855 analyzed students were 401 female and 454 male (M age = 14.68 years, SD = 0.55). Ten students who reported a nonbinary gender identity were excluded (Section 2.8), so the primary analysis is a modified intention-to-treat analysis. Participant flow follows the CONSORT guideline (Schulz et al., 2010; Figure S1), which also gives sample sizes by condition. An a priori power analysis in G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) targeted approximately 200 students per condition, providing 80% power to detect medium effects at α = .05.

The schools were non-government (Catholic and independent), three rural (n = 480) and three urban (n = 375), and all had Index of Community Socio-Educational Advantage values above the national mean of 1,000 (Australian Curriculum, Assessment and Reporting Authority, 2025). In the Australian context, rural refers here to schools in regional Victoria and urban to schools in metropolitan Melbourne. All students were taking compulsory Year 9 science. The study was approved by a university Human Research Ethics Committee.

2.2. Study Design and Procedure

Data were collected between July and August 2025 using REDCap during a single scheduled class session. Parents provided consent through each school's online consent platform, and students provided assent before participation. Students first completed demographic questions and the baseline (T1) survey and were then individually randomized with REDCap's randomization module, using an unblocked allocation table generated by computer script before data collection; research staff could not view the table or anticipate the next allocation. Students and teachers were not informed of condition assignments. Allocation was unrestricted, and arm sizes ranged from 185 to 251, χ²(3) = 11.35, p = .010, although baseline means on all dependent measures were similar across conditions (Table 1).

Students then completed their assigned condition's online content and, immediately afterward, completed the posttest (T2) survey. Each condition was scheduled for 35 minutes and included access to an interactive condition-specific chatbot (GPT-4o; Tables S2 and S3). Figure 1 shows the intervention design, allocation to condition, and procedural timeline (Panel A) and the content and dose of each of the four conditions (Panel B; see also Table S4). For the role-model videos, students were required to remain on each video page for at least one minute before advancing.

Students who missed the scheduled session (e.g., through excursions, illness, or technical difficulties) completed the same sequence when they next attended and were randomized the same way. These 192 newcomers (22.5%) were evenly distributed across conditions, χ²(3) = 0.57, p = .904, and school locations, χ²(1) = 2.11, p = .147.

2.3. Intervention Conditions

As shown in Figure 1B, students in the goal-setting-only condition watched three introductory videos and reflected on the relevance of STEM to their lives. They then constructed a SMART goal aimed at their confidence in, the usefulness of, or their enjoyment of STEM and appraised its attainability and required effort. Finally, they chose strategies for distraction and difficulty and wrote an implementation-intention ("if-then") plan (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).

In the role-modeling-only condition, students viewed five career-profile videos (about 3 min each) from the Australian Academy of Technological Sciences and Engineering (ATSE), each featuring a woman in an engineering or science career. After each video, students rated the career on expected success, interest, usefulness, and cost manageability, using items adapted from Gladstone et al. (2024), and at the end chose the video they found most inspiring. The combined condition delivered abbreviated versions of both: three of the five videos and the goalsetting sequence up to the SMART goal (Table S4). In the active control condition, students watched four TED-Ed videos on history and art and answered brief response items after each, matching the other conditions in delivery, video use, chatbot access, and session time without STEM content.

The four subject blocks, and the items within each block, appeared in random order at each wave to limit order and fatigue effects. Items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), and Table S5 lists every item. 2.5. Manipulation Checks

Immediately after the intervention, students answered two yes/no items adapted from Schweickle et al. 2.6. Analytic Strategy

Intervention effects were estimated using longitudinal latent structural equation models in R 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024) with the lavaan package version 0.6-20 (Rosseel, 2012). Separate models were fitted for expectancy, intrinsic value, utility value, and cost, with biology, chemistry, physics, and STEM-overall latent factors represented at T1 and T2. Correlated uniquenesses were specified for parallel items across waves and subject domains.

For each outcome, the T2 latent factor was regressed on intervention condition and the corresponding T1 latent factor, adjusting for gender, newcomer status, and school location (rural vs. urban, assigned from the school record). Models used robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR), full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) for missing data, and cluster-robust standard errors with school specified as the clustering variable. Posttest item-level intraclass correlations (ICCs) ranged from .000 to .133 (median = .048; Table S7). Fixed-factor scaling set each T1 latent variance to 1, so factorial coefficients (β) and model-implied contrasts between conditions (Δ) are in T1 baseline-SD units.

Goal setting and role modeling were effect-coded (-0.5 = absent, +0.5 = present), and gender was coded (0 = female,1 = male). As the combined condition contained abbreviated components, factorial main effects average across implementation doses. Planned contrasts compared each intervention condition with the active control. The planned contrasts ask whether each condition outperformed the active control, and the factorial effects whether a component helped on average across the conditions that included it. As registered, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) was applied within each main effect and contrast, across the four STEM-overall outcomes and, separately, the 12 subjectspecific outcomes; the same rule was applied to the moderation analyses. These adjusted p values are reported as q, and q < .05 was treated as significant. Following the registration, the exploratory RM × GS interaction and the contrast between the combined condition and the average of the two single-component conditions, which estimate how the two components combine, are reported with unadjusted p values and 95% CIs; the same applies to their three-way interactions with gender and location. Moderation was tested by adding product terms between each effect-coded component and gender or school location to the same models.

Longitudinal invariance (configural, metric, and scalar models) and invariance across gender (multigroup models) were tested for each construct, with ΔCFI ≥ -.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ .015 as criteria (Chen, 2007; Cheung & Rensvold, 2002). Scalar invariance held across waves and gender for all four constructs (Table S8), so effects were estimated on the scalar models.

2.7. Missing Data

Posttest data were complete for 570 to 590 students, depending on the outcome. Retention differed by condition, from 55.8% in the goal-setting-only condition, where a video fault stopped some students progressing, to 72.6% in the role-modeling-only condition, χ²(3) = 19.13, p < .001 (Figure S1). FIML estimation assumed that data were missing at random (MAR) conditional on the model variables (the baseline factor of each outcome, condition, gender, newcomer status, and school location). Although MAR cannot be verified directly, missingness was associated with baseline motivation, which was included in the models (Figure S1), and attrition due to the video fault was unlikely to depend on students' motivation. As preregistered, complete-case analyses were conducted as a sensitivity check. Observed means and standard deviations by condition are in Table 1, and correlations among the study variables in Table S9.

2.8. Preregistration and Transparency

The hypotheses and analysis plan were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (link withheld for anonymized review; an anonymized copy is provided for reviewers) on June 5, 2026, after data collection but before the research team accessed the data. Analyses are labeled confirmatory only where the registration specified a direction. The RM × GS interaction, the combined-versus-average contrast, subject-specific effects, and location moderation were registered as exploratory, without directional predictions (Table S1).

Four aspects of the analysis depart from the registration. Ten students who reported a nonbinary gender identity were excluded because the models included gender as a binary covariate; the manipulation checks were not preregistered; FDR correction was extended to the moderation analyses; and the within-location arm contrasts, subject-level location analyses, and RM × GS × location interaction were added after registration (Table S1).

3. Results

>Results >RQ1: Effects on Overall STEM Expectancy, Values, and Cost

3.1.1. Goal Setting

Conditions containing goal setting reported higher expectancy (β = 0.11, 95% CI [0.04, 0.17], q = .003) and intrinsic value (β = 0.12, [0.05, 0.19], q = .003) than conditions without it, but not higher utility value (β = 0.10, [-0.05, 0.25], q = .190; Table 2). The GS-only condition likewise exceeded the active control on expectancy (Δ = 0.17, [0.03, 0.32], q = .034) and intrinsic value (Δ = 0.18, [0.04, 0.33], q = .034) but not utility value (Δ = 0.17, [-0.09, 0.43], q = .271). For cost, the factorial goal-setting estimate indicated higher perceived cost (β = -0.16, [-0.27, -0.05], q = .007), but the GS-only condition did not differ from the active control (Δ = -0.14, [-0.49, 0.22], q = .447), so only the combined condition significantly exceeded the active control in perceived cost (Section 3.1.3). For goal setting, H1 and H2 were partially supported.

3.1.2. Role Modeling

Role modeling did not improve any outcome. No factorial role-modeling effect survived correction. The RM-only condition did not differ from the active control on any outcome (all q ≥ .893; Table 2). 3.1.3. Combining the Components

The combined condition exceeded the active control on expectancy (Δ = 0.08, [0.02, 0.14], q = .027) but not on intrinsic value (q = .177) or utility value (q = .179), and it was the only condition with significantly higher perceived cost than the active control (Δ = -0.18, [-0.33, -0.04], q = .027). For the combined condition, H2 was partially supported. Exploratory factorial tests showed that the combined condition was neither more nor less effective than its two components would predict, as no RM × GS interaction was significant (all p ≥ .117; Table 2), and the combined condition did not differ from the average of the two single-component conditions on any outcome (all p ≥ .501; Table S10).

3.1.4. Subject-Specific Effects

In exploratory subject-specific analyses (Table S11), goal setting again raised expectancy in biology and chemistry and intrinsic value in biology and physics (β = 0.05 to 0.12, all q ≤ .020; GS-only contrasts Δ = 0.11 to 0.24, all q ≤ .003), but not utility value. Factorial estimates also suggested higher cost with goal setting in biology and chemistry and lower intrinsic value with role modeling, but no corresponding contrast with the active control survived correction.

3.2. RQ2: Do These Effects Differ by Gender?

No GS × gender or RM × gender interaction survived correction (all q ≥ .514 for GS and ≥ .070 for RM; Table S12), so H3 was not supported; exploratory within-gender contrasts are in Table S13 and Figure 2A. Tested arm by arm, gender differences in the GS-only and RM-only contrasts with the active control were also nonsignificant (all q ≥ .619; Table S14).

The exploratory RM × GS × gender interaction was not significant for any outcome (all p ≥ .333). No subject-specific factorial component × gender interaction survived correction (Table S15). In the arm-specific contrasts, goal setting alone raised chemistry intrinsic and utility value for female but not male students (gender differences q < .001 and .010; Table S14).

3.3. RQ3: Do These Effects Differ by School Location?

Of the eight factorial component × location interactions, only RM × location for cost survived correction (β = -0.35, [-0.61, -0.08], q = .039), but neither within-location rolemodeling effect on cost was significant (Table S16). Compared to the active control group, the GS-only condition raised utility value in the rural schools (Δ = 0.46, q < .001) but not the urban schools (Δ = -0.08, q = .376; difference = 0.54, 95% CI [0.28, 0.80], q < .001), and the RM-only condition raised expectancy in the rural schools (Δ = 0.11, q < .001) but not the urban schools (Δ = -0.03, q = .721; difference = 0.15, [0.07, 0.22], q = .001) (Figure 2; Table S17). The exploratory RM × GS × location interaction (significant, uncorrected, for expectancy, intrinsic value, and utility value) indicated that the combined condition fell below the average of the two single-component conditions in the rural schools but not in the urban schools (Table S17).

Within subjects (Tables S18 and S19), the rural advantage of the GS-only condition for utility value held in biology, chemistry, and physics (differences = 0.29 to 0.42, all q ≤ .024), as did the RM-only advantage for physics intrinsic value (0.10, q = .046). Only 4 of the 24 exploratory subject-level factorial interactions survived correction, and they showed no consistent direction (Table S18).

3.4. Sensitivity Analyses

The complete-case analysis (N = 570; Table S20) reproduced the pattern of the primary analysis. The factorial goal-setting effects on expectancy and intrinsic value remained significant after correction, and the utility-value effect remained nonsignificant. As in the primary analysis, only the combined condition showed higher perceived cost than the active control (q = .032).

The GS-only contrasts on expectancy and intrinsic value were as large as in the primary analysis (Δ = 0.19 and 0.20 vs. 0.17 and 0.18) but no longer survived correction (q = .063), consistent with the loss of power from a sample one third smaller.

4. Discussion

This study asked whether a brief online intervention could shift adolescents' STEM expectancy, values, and cost, and which component was responsible. Goal setting raised expectancy and intrinsic value, both in the factorial analyses and in the direct comparison of goal setting alone with an active control. Similarly, in the exploratory subject analyses, goal setting raised expectancy in biology and chemistry and intrinsic value in biology and physics. Utility value did not change on average in any subject or overall, although exploratory analyses suggested that goal setting raised it in the rural schools, and that role modeling alone raised expectancy there. Brief role-model videos produced no reliable average benefit, the combined condition added no benefit beyond the average of the two single-component conditions, and the registered gender interactions were not supported.

4.1. Goal Setting Raised Expectancy and Intrinsic Value

The goal-setting sequence may have made future engagement both more meaningful and more actionable, as students connected STEM to their lives, appraised a specific goal, and planned how to pursue it. Appraising one's capability to reach an attainable goal may strengthen expectancy by making progress seem manageable (Bandura & Schunk, 1981), and generating a personally relevant goal may support intrinsic value through ownership of the activity (Koestner et al., 2008; Locke & Latham, 2002; Martin et al., 2014). These findings extend SEVT-based intervention research, which has mostly targeted utility value through relevance writing (Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009), by showing that a single goal-setting session can shift expectancy and intrinsic value. Because SEVT treats these beliefs as situated, such immediate shifts are a plausible first step toward later STEM choices.

4.2. Utility Value and School Context

Goal setting did not significantly raise utility value on average. Its estimates were about as large as those for expectancy and intrinsic value but much less precise, so the average effect on utility value is uncertain rather than absent. The exploratory location analyses offer one explanation. Goal setting alone raised utility value markedly in the rural but not the urban schools, and this rural advantage appeared in each of biology, chemistry, and physics. Students in rural settings, with narrower senior STEM offerings and fewer visible pathways (Holden & Zhang, 2024), may have had more to gain from connecting STEM to their own goals. Access to STEM opportunities was not measured, however, so this difference should be treated as a lead for replication rather than as an established rural-urban effect. The utility-value factor also combined usefulness with general importance, so the design cannot separate a change in perceived usefulness from broader valuing of STEM.

4.3. Role Modeling Did Not Improve Motivation on Average

Neither the role-modeling component nor the role-modeling-only condition improved any STEM-overall outcome. Brief exposure to assigned role models may be insufficient to shift motivation for current STEM learning, particularly as role models are most influential when seen as relevant, relatable, and attainable (Morgenroth et al., 2015), perceptions that exposure alone may not foster and that were not assessed here.

One exploratory finding is a possible exception. In the rural but not the urban schools, the role-modeling-only condition raised STEM expectancy relative to the active control, consistent with evidence that a relevant model's success can strengthen beliefs that success is attainable (Bandura, 1977), particularly for students with little exposure to STEM professionals (Sjaastad, 2012). However, the factorial role-modeling effect did not differ by location, and location was confounded with school, so this finding warrants caution. Future studies could test more personalized or repeated role-model exposure and measure similarity, identification, and attainability as mechanisms.

4.4. Combining Components Within a Brief Intervention

Delivering both components within one fixed session required reducing the dose of each, so the combined condition included three of the five role-model videos and a goal-setting sequence without the attainability appraisal, strategy selection, and implementation-intention plan. It raised expectancy relative to the active control, so the abbreviated package retained some benefit, but its estimated gains were smaller than those of goal setting alone and no larger than the average of the two single-component conditions. The design therefore cannot tell whether role modeling added little or abbreviating goal setting weakened an effective component.

The combined condition was also the only condition in which perceived cost was significantly higher than in the active control. One explanation is that the abbreviated sequence omitted strategy selection and implementation planning, the elements most directly aimed at managing difficulty and distraction, so students may have registered the demands of STEM without a plan for meeting them. Cost-focused interventions have similarly raised perceived cost at times when they made students' challenges and competing demands salient (Rosenzweig et al., 2022). The factorial goal-setting estimate pointed in the same direction, but the goal-settingonly contrast was imprecise and not significant, so whether the full sequence also raises perceived cost remains open.

4.5. No Evidence of Gender Moderation

Contrary to the registered hypotheses, neither intervention component showed significantly greater benefits for female than male students on the primary STEM-overall outcomes. In exploratory arm-level contrasts, goal setting alone raised female, but not male, students' intrinsic and utility value for chemistry, suggesting that a brief goal-setting activity may help female students find chemistry more interesting and useful, although the corresponding factorial interactions did not survive correction and replication is needed. The prediction of greater benefits for female students was most direct for role modeling, given that all featured role models were women and similarity is central to role-model theory (Morgenroth et al., 2015), yet role modeling showed no gender moderation, suggesting that exposure to female exemplars alone may not be sufficient to produce stronger benefits for female students, particularly if the models were not also seen as attainable and similar, conditions without which role models can backfire (Cheryan et al., 2011). Previous studies have mostly examined female role models in female or predominantly female samples (González-Pérez et al., 2020; Stout et al., 2011), leaving their comparative effectiveness across genders less well understood.

4.6. Practical Implications

For schools, a brief online goal-setting intervention is a promising, low-cost way to strengthen students' STEM confidence and interest within one scheduled class session. Where only one session is available, the full goal-setting sequence appears a better use of it than an abbreviated combination with role-model videos. Before wider use, completion under routine delivery and the persistence of gains beyond the immediate posttest need to be established, and perceived cost should be monitored, particularly when goal setting is combined with other components. If the rural findings replicate, goal setting and role-model videos may be most useful in schools where STEM pathways are less visible.

4.7. Limitations and Future Directions

First, although randomization was at the student level and school-level ICCs were small, the trial included only six schools, so the rural-urban differences need replication in more schools. Second, posttest retention differed across conditions and was lowest in the goal-settingonly condition, where a video fault stopped some students progressing. FIML assumes that missingness is ignorable given the model variables; the complete-case analysis reproduced the primary pattern with similar effect sizes, but selection on unobserved characteristics cannot be ruled out.

Third, outcomes were self-reported immediately after a single exposure, minutes after students had rated their goal confidence or the role-model careers, so demand characteristics and short-term reactivity cannot be ruled out. Immediate appraisals suit a brief intervention under SEVT, but whether they persist and shape later subject choices remains to be established.

Fourth, each condition was designed from prior work and tested as a package. The goalsetting sequence combined a relevance reflection, capability appraisal, and implementation planning, each of which has produced benefits on its own (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006; Hulleman et al., 2010), so the design cannot identify which element carried the effect, and a dismantling design is needed to isolate its active ingredients. Although manipulation checks and minimum viewing times were in place, students' engagement with the content could not be measured directly, so weaker effects, particularly for role modeling, may partly reflect limited engagement.

5. Conclusion

A brief, structured online goal-setting intervention raised adolescents' immediate STEM expectancy and intrinsic value, whereas brief role-model videos did not, and combining the two within a fixed session did no better than the average of its parts. Effects did not differ by gender on the main outcomes, although goal setting alone raised female students' intrinsic and utility value for chemistry in exploratory analyses. Exploratory rural benefits of goal setting for utility value and of role modeling for expectancy need replication, as does the persistence of these gains into senior subject choices.
