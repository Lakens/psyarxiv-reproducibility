Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: For young people experiencing loneliness or low community connection, physical activity may support social contact, enjoyment and belonging. Social prescribing offers a person-centred route to organised sport, exercise, active recreation and less structured movement, but identification or referral does not ensure usability. Little is known about the individual, relational, school and community conditions that make these opportunities meaningful to the young person, workable within their circumstances and acceptable in their social and emotional context.

Methods: A targeted qualitative content analysis with iterative framework development used data from INcreasing AdolesCent social and community supporT (INACT), a school-based social prescribing programme for pupils experiencing loneliness or low community connection. 38 interviews were screened (12 young people, eight parents/guardians, five school staff and 13 link workers); 37 contained physical-activity material. These were analysed alongside link-worker records for 163 young people. Material was charted by activity category, setting, pathway stage, barriers and inclusion or emotionalsafety considerations, then compared across pilot interviews, full-trial interviews and logs. Three researchers contributed to coding, checking and collective interpretation.

Results: Across interviews and routine records, physical-activity material encompassed organised sport, fitness and exercise, dance and creative movement, outdoor activity, informal movement or active recreation, and other community activities. Five overlapping areas of work were identified: understanding interests and personal fit; expanding activity options; supporting access and entry; working through practical feasibility and sustainability; and considering inclusion and emotional safety. These areas were not sequential and could overlap or be revisited. School settings interacted with family routines, peer cultures and community provision to shape usability. Logs more often documented named activities, practical actions and spending, whereas interviews provided fuller accounts of confidence, social fit and family constraints. Pathway stages from discussion and referral to attendance and continuation were not consistently distinguished.

Conclusion: For pupils experiencing loneliness or low community connection, access to a potentially connecting physical activity depended on more than availability or referral. The framework describes the practical, relational and contextual work involved in moving towards meaningful, workable and acceptable participation. It locates responsibility across families, schools, providers, services and local infrastructure while keeping young people's preferences and choices central. The study does not establish that physical activity reduced loneliness, but provides a basis for prospective evaluation of participation, social connection and loneliness outcomes.

Introduction

Physical activity is a broad umbrella term, encompassing organised sport and exercise as well as active recreation, play, dance and less structured movement (Caspersen et al., 1985; World Health Organization, 2024). Regular physical activity supports the physical health, mental health and development of children and young people, yet most adolescents do not meet recommended activity levels and opportunities to be active remain unequal (World Health Organization, 2024). In England, just 49.1% of children and young people aged 5-16 met the Chief Medical Officers' guideline of an average of at least 60 minutes of activity a day in 2024-25. Further, these averages are underpinned by inequalities. For example, there is a marked social gradient in participation rates, with 45% of children from the least affluent families engaging compared to 58% among those from the most affluent (Sport England, 2025).

These inequalities are underpinned by a range of factors including affordability and proximity intersecting with disability, gender, sexuality, ethnicity and access to supportive social networks (Dodd-Reynolds et al., 2024). For example, marginalised young people have described gendered discomfort in gyms, harassment, bullying, and spaces that were physically or socially inaccessible (Dodd-Reynolds et al., 2024). However, the very diversity of these factors underpinning inequalities provides a challenge when attempting to increase access to physical activity. Organised sport can support identity and belonging, but formal access does not guarantee meaningful participation when ability expectations, cultural capital or exclusionary practices reproduce disadvantage (Kingsley & Spencer-Cavaliere, 2015; Ríos et al., 2022). The activity itself also matters: one option may feel inviting, while another may feel evaluative, gendered or threatening (Dodd-Reynolds et al., 2024; Ríos et al., 2022). Further, some sport and exercise settings make performance visible to peers and invite comparisons by ability, particularly when participation is competitive. Comments about performance, bullying and gendered expectations may then affect whether a young person feels comfortable or safe taking part (Booth et al., 2024; Dodd-Reynolds et al., 2024; Ríos et al., 2022). Notably, programmes that have attempted to use standardised interventions to increase access have sometimes replicated some of the same inequalities. For example, the GoActive school-based trial provided similar physical-activity resources across schools, but pupils aged 13-14 from lower socioeconomic groups engaged less with parts of the intervention (Alliott et al., 2023). These patterns suggest that advice or programme availability alone may be insufficient when practical, social and emotional conditions shape whether young people can take part. Social prescribing offers one possible response to these challenges. It connects people with nonclinical community support through a co-produced social prescription (Muhl et al., 2023). Physical activity is one recognised area of social prescribing, alongside arts and culture, nature-based support, advice and other practical services, and work or volunteering (NHS England, 2023). In youth services, link workers can tailor support to what matters to the young person, build a supportive relationship and help them access community resources; this may also involve working with families and other services (Farina et al., 2024; Steffen et al., 2025). Young people have also identified shared decision-making and social support as important to engagement (Olsson et al., 2025). This person-centred, coordinating role may help address barriers that general advice or programme availability leave untouched, and allow a referral to be targeted to the needs and interests of an individual, but current evidence does not establish that it improves uptake or outcomes. Existing work on youth social prescribing reports substantial variation in populations, referral routes, linking roles, activities and outcomes, alongside a still-limited evidence base (Bertotti et al., 2022; Muhl et al., 2025; Mitchell et al., 2025), while recent implementation research has focused mainly on how services are configured (Berry et al., 2026).

What remains less clear is whether social prescribing helps to make a particular physical activity opportunity usable. Here, we define as "usability" as whether an intervention is appropriate or meaningful to an individual and valued by them, feasible in being something they can practically take up, and acceptable in that it is experienced as something that is comfortable, inclusive and emotionally safe (Proctor et al., 2011) (Sekhon et al., 2017). Moving towards usability in the context of youth referrals to physical activity via a social prescribing pathway may require coordination across the young person, family, school and community provider because permission, routines, confidence and unfamiliar settings can all shape access. School-based delivery may offer a familiar route into support, but an available option can still remain unusable for a particular young person even within this context (Hayes et al., 2025). Existing reviews and implementation studies of social prescribing have focused mainly on service configuration, service-level feasibility and acceptability, and broad outcomes, often treating individual community opportunities as one component of a wider pathway (Muhl et al., 2025; Mitchell et al., 2025; Berry et al., 2026). Less is therefore known about whether an individual opportunity fits a young person's interests and circumstances, or about the practical and relational work through which it becomes usable or stalls before that point. INACT is a co-produced, school-based social prescribing programme for pupils experiencing loneliness and low community connection. In both the pilot and full trial, link workers were based in or worked alongside participating schools, using conversations about 'what matters' to each young person to support connections with community resources (Hayes et al., 2025; Hayes et al., 2026). Schools therefore provided the route into support, while the opportunities discussed could take place in the wider community. Details of how loneliness and community connection were assessed for trial eligibility are reported in the respective protocols; these measures were not analysed as outcomes in the present study. For pupils allocated to the social prescribing arm, physical activity was one of several possible topics or community connections, rather than a required referral destination. We used a matrix-based qualitative content analysis informed by the Framework Method (Gale et al., 2013), combining descriptive classification of physical activity material with analysis of the work involved in making opportunities usable. Reporting followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (O' Brien et al., 2014).

Data came from the INACT pilot and full trial. The pilot randomised pupils in mainstream English primary and secondary schools to social prescribing, with a dedicated link worker, or signposting through a leaflet mapping the local area; this analysis included only pupils allocated to social prescribing, who received six one-to-one link worker sessions over eight weeks (Hayes et al., 2025). The full trial retained the school-based, person-centred pathway and expanded evaluation of effectiveness, costeffectiveness and implementation (Hayes et al., 2026).

Eligibility and extraction

The eligibility criteria were developed by NQB and RB during initial screening and refined through team discussion. Eligible material referred to organised sport, fitness/exercise, dance/creative movement, outdoor activity, informal movement/active recreation or another community activity with an explicit movement component. Material about barriers, inclusion or safety was included only when it related to a confirmed physical activity opportunity or movement-related choice. General 'activities' and non-movement clubs were excluded unless movement was explicit or the passage was needed to interpret movement-related choice.

The matrix recorded source, named activity, activity category, setting, delivery format, pathway stage, who raised it, barriers, inclusion/safety, an evidence note, coding confidence and queries. Each entry received one pragmatic primary category: organised sport (structured or rule-based club, team or coached activity, such as football or boxing); fitness/exercise (planned exercise, such as gym activity or running); dance/creative movement; informal movement/active recreation (casual, playful or selfdirected movement, such as trampoline play or bowling); outdoor activity (movement framed mainly as an outdoor or nature-based pursuit, such as horse riding or skate-park activity); or movement-related community activity (broader community opportunities with an explicit movement component). Movement with insufficient detail about form was coded as unclear. Setting and delivery format were coded separately; duration, intensity and health-enhancing thresholds were not inferred.

Analysis and quality assurance

For this analysis, usability was an organising concept rather than a measured outcome. An opportunity was treated as meaningful when the young person wanted or valued it; workable when practical conditions such as cost, transport, timing and family routines allowed participation; and acceptable when the activity and setting felt comfortable, inclusive and emotionally safe. These were interpretive categories rather than scored criteria or fixed thresholds. Inclusion and emotional safety referred to participants' accounts of comfort, welcome, judgement or exclusion; the analysis did not assess providers' formal safeguarding arrangements.

Analysis followed the Framework Method principles of structured charting, iterative framework development and systematic comparison within and across sources (Gale et al., 2013), adapted to this mixed-purpose dataset. Rows represented eligible physical activity entries rather than whole participant cases, and columns preserved source context and pathway features. Deductive fields (activity category, setting, pathway stage, barriers and inclusion or emotional safety) were combined with inductively developed areas of work. We first descriptively coded the material and compared recurrent actions, conditions and experiences within and across sources. We then moved from classifying activities to identifying the work involved in making an opportunity usable. Finally, we compared source patterns to distinguish findings from documentation effects. Candidate areas were combined where they overlapped and tested against declined, discontinued and formally available but exclusionary examples. Because elicitation differed, the final framework was checked separately against pilot interviews, fulltrial interviews and logs. The complete subdataset was analysed, with scope defined by the research questions rather than saturation.

Collaborative coding and critical-friend discussion were used to strengthen interpretation rather than to calculate statistical inter-rater reliability (Smith & McGannon, 2018). NQB led the initial extraction and development of the candidate framework under RB's supervision, and RB reviewed the developing coding and source material. Six interviews spanning the different participant types were independently double-coded, with each interview coded by two researchers and RB, NQB and SEC all contributing to this process. The three researchers discussed coding decisions, differences and candidate areas, returning to the matrix and original sources where needed. Reflective notes documented assumptions, category development, source effects and contradictory cases and were revisited while the five-area framework and Results were refined.

For the final checks, quotations were compared with the original transcripts, identifiers were removed and source labels were verified. The final subdataset contained 264 eligible coding entries. Of the 264 entries, 29 came from young-person interviews, 37 from parent/guardian interviews, 14 from school-staff interviews, 63 from link-worker interviews and 121 from link-worker logs. These were not unique participants, referrals or attendance episodes, and their frequencies were not interpreted as estimates of prevalence or importance. The team compared interpretations across researchers and sources, retained ambiguous and contradictory cases, and recorded decisions in the analysis matrix and reflective notes. Results

Context and source coverage

Physical activity material appeared across young-person, parent/guardian, school-staff and linkworker interviews and in routine link-worker records. It encompassed organised sport, fitness and exercise, dance and creative movement, outdoor activity, informal movement or active recreation, and other movement-related community activities. Illustrative examples of the range of activities are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Logs more often named providers, purchases and sessions, whereas interviews provided fuller accounts of confidence, social fit, family constraints and uncertainty. These patterns reflect what the sources documented rather than the prevalence or importance of particular activities or barriers.

Pathway stage was recorded inconsistently: discussion, suggestion and referral were often conflated, while first attendance, repeat attendance, valued participation and discontinuation were unevenly captured. Referral-to-attendance and continuation rates could therefore not be calculated. This documentation gap matters because the records could show that support was offered but not consistently whether acceptable and sustained participation followed.

Five overlapping areas of work around making an activity usable

The central distinction was between the activity itself, and the work needed around it. Football, dance or swimming described what was discussed, not what had to be considered or done to make it usable. The five overlapping areas were analytic areas, not stages, intervention components or a checklist (Table 2). Several could apply at once or be revisited as preferences, circumstances or experience changed. The framework did not classify opportunities as usable or unusable; it described work that might move them towards being meaningful, workable and acceptable. All five areas appeared across pilot interviews, full-trial interviews and logs, although interviews captured emotional and social experience more fully, while logs recorded practical actions and spending more consistently. Across the five areas, young people's interests and confidence interacted with family and peer support, school routines, link-worker practices, provider conditions and local infrastructure. The framework therefore describes a multilevel process rather than work located solely with the young person or link worker.

Understanding interests and personal fit

Physical activity entered the pathway through an existing interest, a valued identity, a previous experience or something the young person wanted to explore. Link workers described starting with what the young person said they wanted rather than offering a standard menu of activities. Interests gave link workers a practical starting point for discussing what mattered and finding a relevant opportunity.

"It was definitely getting their voice and seeing what they wanted to do and then going off and finding that activity in their area so it were easier to get to for them." (Full-trial link worker, LW-F04) A young person's account of dance showed that the link worker's contribution was to help turn an existing interest in dance into an accessible opportunity. "I was looking for a dance group before and I told [the link worker] and, yeah, he helped me to join … I just like dancing. It just makes me feel happy." (Pilot young person, CYP-P01) Existing participation did not necessarily indicate a need for another referral. Some logs described young people already attending several sports each week, for whom additional sessions would have conflicted with family schedules. Conversely, broad labels used by parents, school staff or link workers, such as 'sporty' or 'not sporty', obscured differences between competition, solitary exercise, playful movement and activity with trusted peers. Interest was therefore a starting point for discussion rather than proof of suitability.

Expanding activity options

Young people's accounts did not support a simple division between those who liked sport and those who did not. Some resisted formal exercise or competitive sport yet engaged readily in dance, trampoline play, walking, skating, bowling, pool, darts, climbing or movement-based games. The difference lay less in intensity than in the activity's meaning, social demands and sense of pressure. "She will do exercise if she doesn't realise it's exercise … ask her to go play on the trampoline for two hours and you've got to drag her in six hours later … ask her to dance for three hours and she'll do it anyway." (Full-trial parent/guardian, PG-F02) A link worker similarly described a polarised secondary-school culture in which pupils were positioned as either highly athletic or opposed to sport. For one young person, non-competitive swimming outside school was acceptable partly because it sat outside team hierarchies and familiar peer judgement. "It just feels like kids were either … really pro-or anti-sport … there's kind of a culture around sport and athleticism, particularly in secondary school, that can be a bit difficult." (Full-trial link worker, LW-F03) Lower-pressure activities offered a different experience of participation by reducing public comparison, providing a more comfortable social setting or making it easier to join in. One young person who did not generally like sport nevertheless found table tennis fun because it allowed them to socialise with friends and gradually meet other people. They also linked the confidence developed in this familiar setting with joining a non-physical after-school business club. In this account, confidence developed through table tennis appeared to support wider community participation, even though table tennis was not a link worker referral. "It's fun. I just get to socialise with people … I don't really like sports in general, but table tennis is, like, a pretty fun sport for me." (Full-trial young person, CYP-F04) Across these accounts, suitability depended on the relationship between the activity, its setting and the young person's experience, rather than on intensity alone. A lower-pressure option could still be physically demanding and socially meaningful. The table-tennis account also shows that a valued opportunity did not have to originate in social prescribing: the young person linked confidence gained in an existing community activity with joining another group.

Supporting access and entry

Link workers described practical and relational support around first access, including identifying provision, contacting providers, explaining what to expect, arranging transport or equipment, using flexible funding and involving a friend or family member. This could make an opportunity easier to approach, although the data could not show whether it led to attendance or continuation.

"What would make you feel more confident? … bringing a friend maybe … we can make that happen for you." (Full-trial link worker, LW-F04)

The young person who joined dance described moving from fear to familiarity after the link worker helped them understand their anxiety. The young person linked the link worker's support with the unfamiliar setting becoming predictable enough to try. "He just helped me understand why I was afraid of going … I'm fine because I know everyone now, so I feel comfortable." (Pilot young person, CYP-P01) Practical support also mattered. A parent described a grant-funded taxi enabling a skate-park visit, after which the family returned independently. The young person described the indoor setting as more beginner-friendly than an outdoor concrete park, peer encouragement when attempting a ramp and a new shared interest through which to approach other people.

"It boosts my confidence quite a bit more. Like, it's also made us more confident in making friends, because I can bond over something similar." (Full-trial young person, CYP-F05)

Logs also recorded equipment, transport and group vouchers used around first access. Across these examples, payment addressed only one barrier; familiarity, peer support and perceived fit also mattered.

Working through practical feasibility and sustainability

Movement from interest to attendance was not necessarily linear. Potential activities were filtered through cost, distance, timing, equipment, family routines, parental confidence, religious commitments and local availability. These conditions interacted. A free activity could remain inaccessible if it ended after the available collection time, was too far away, conflicted with other responsibilities or required a young person or parent to approach an unfamiliar club without support.

"Them being free or low cost, nearby and accessible … stuff being offered at school or within walking distance is really pretty helpful." (Full-trial link worker, LW-F03)

One football example had no obvious financial or travel barrier; the obstacle was the family's confidence in contacting a club.

"We never really got anywhere … a bit of nervousness from mum to actually approach clubs … there were no cost barriers, there were no travel barriers; it was just more confidence to approach." (Full-trial link worker, LW-F07) Provider reliability could prevent an otherwise willing young person from trying an activity. One young person woke early to attend a before-school fitness club and also tried an after-school track club, but both sessions were cancelled. They remained open to trying something similar later, indicating that willingness was present even though both available opportunities had fallen through.

"I did end up, like, waking up early and going to the fitness club. It got cancelled. And then the other club that I tried to go to also got cancelled." (Full-trial young person, CYP-F03) Attending once did not mean that participation could continue. Swimming passes were used for only a few months when families still lacked weekend transport; other activities clashed with existing commitments or family changes. Flexible funds helped, but could not secure transport, compatible schedules, reliable provision or sustained family capacity. As one school staff member noted, small discounts did not necessarily make costly activities affordable.

Considering inclusion and emotional safety

Physical activity settings were not inherently supportive. Participants described how competition, body-related judgement, bullying, limited playing time, school peer cultures and unfamiliar groups could leave young people feeling exposed. Previous harm could shape anticipation before a new session, making the choice to attend a judgement about whether an activity was worth the risk of public failure.

"She was bullied quite a bit for being bigger … She does a risk analysis on it: do I like this enough to do it in front of other people and possibly fail?" (Full-trial parent/guardian, PG-F02)

Being formally accepted into an activity did not necessarily mean being meaningfully included. A school staff member described organised sport as potentially 'brutal' and illustrated how being on a team could still involve very little time in meaningful participation.

"If you've only played like two minutes of a game, you're probably not going to want to go back again because you just think it's a bit pointless." (Full-trial school staff member, SC-F02) Context mattered even without an overtly harmful event. One young person spoke positively about a new girls-only football group and the people she had met there; another found mixed-gender physical education awkward. Similar activity labels can therefore conceal very different social experiences. These accounts indicate that gender and cultural fit cannot be inferred from the activity label alone. The parent's account of a young person weighing whether she liked an activity enough to risk failing in front of others shows why young people's choice mattered throughout the framework. The earlier link-worker account of starting with "their voice" and "what they wanted to do" provides another example of choice shaping the work around an opportunity. However, the options available were limited: one link worker described a "dearth of options" and the need to find the 'closest approximation' to what a young person wanted. Discussion

This study examined how physical activity opportunities became usable, or failed to do so, within school-based social prescribing for pupils experiencing loneliness or low community connection. Five overlapping areas of work were identified: understanding interests and personal fit; expanding activity options; supporting access and entry; working through practical feasibility and sustainability; and considering inclusion and emotional safety. Together, these areas show how individual preferences and confidence interacted with family and peer relationships, school routines, link-worker support, provider practices and local infrastructure. The findings do not establish that physical activity reduced loneliness. Instead, they explain how access to a potentially connecting community activity could be enabled or obstructed across these levels. Previous studies describe youth social prescribing as combining an understanding of a young person's needs with tailored support, respect for their agency, a trusting relationship and help to connect with community services (Farina et al., 2024; Steffen et al., 2025). The INACT findings build on this research by showing how these forms of support were applied to a specific physical activity opportunity: assessing whether it matched the young person's interests, helping them contact a provider or attend a session, addressing practical barriers, and considering whether the setting felt welcoming and emotionally safe.

INACT identified barriers at several points between initial interest and continued participation. Costs, transport and timing could prevent access; familiar settings, low confidence and attending alone could make attending for the first time difficult; and provider cancellations, family routines and limited local provision could disrupt attendance or continuation. Link workers responded by identifying and contacting providers, explaining what to expect, arranging transport or equipment, using flexible funds and involving a friend or family member. The findings help to explain a pattern observed in standardised programmes such as GoActive. Young people from lower socioeconomic backgrounds engaged less with some intervention components, even though provision was similar across schools (Alliott et al., 2023). In INACT, even a free activity could remain difficult to use when transport, timing, confidence or the setting made attendance hard. Providing individualised support to identify and overcome these barriers depended on relevant skills and ongoing support for link workers (Booth et al., 2026), as well as sufficient capacity, local knowledge, provider relationships and flexible resources (Bradbury et al., 2026). School-based delivery gave pupils a familiar route to link-worker support and enabled coordination with school staff, but school routines, parental permission processes and peer cultures also shaped what could be offered and how participation felt. Social prescribing therefore sat alongside existing physical activity provision, adding individual matching and coordination rather than simply providing another activity. However, the reach of social prescribing still depended on local options; when only the 'closest approximation' was available, it was still possible that the offer could not fully match what the young person wanted. Similar link-worker functions have been identified in youth social-prescribing services involving arts and other community activities, so these forms of support are not unique to physical activity (Farina et al., 2024; Steffen et al., 2025). Young people have similarly emphasised shared decisions, social support and the availability of suitable local opportunities (Olsson et al., 2025). Performance and peer judgement may also arise in other group activities. In INACT, however, the physical activity accounts linked these concerns to specific features of sport and exercise. A girls-only football group was described positively, whereas mixed-gender physical education felt awkward; being on a team did not feel inclusive when a young person received very little playing time; and previous weight-related bullying made the possibility of failing in front of others feel risky. These examples show that an activity could match a young person's interests but still feel unsuitable or unwelcoming because of how participation was organised and experienced, particularly after previous bullying or exclusion (Dodd-Reynolds et al., 2024; Ríos et al., 2022). Attention to these social experiences of participation was particularly relevant because INACT recruited pupils experiencing loneliness or low community connection. Reviews suggest that the relationship between physical activity and loneliness depends partly on the social experience of taking part (Pels & Kleinert, 2016; Ahn et al., 2024). A setting that reproduced judgement or exclusion could therefore work against the aim of supporting connection. By contrast, physical activity might support connection when it gave young people a shared focus for conversation and a sense of familiarity and encouragement. These contrasting experiences show why physical activity referrals required link workers to consider both the activity and how taking part was likely to feel for the young person. The fifth area of the framework, inclusion and emotional safety, captures this part of usability by focusing on whether a young person feels able and welcome to take part, rather than simply whether an activity has been offered or attended.

Strengths and limitations

The clearest strength of this study was the combination of narrated experience and contemporaneous delivery records. The analysis included 37 interviews with young people, parents/guardians, school staff and link workers, alongside routine logs for 163 young people, of whom 73 contributed confirmed physical activity material. Together, these sources captured established participation, new connections, rejected or disrupted opportunities and adverse experiences across varied forms of movement. Interpretation did not depend on one researcher's reading: three researchers contributed to coding, checking and collective development of the framework, including overlap coding of six transcripts. Reflective notes preserved how interpretations changed. The analysis retained uncertain and contradictory examples, including declined, discontinued and exclusionary opportunities, and examined differences between what interviews and logs captured. Using interviews from four participant groups alongside routine logs allowed the framework to be checked against narrated experiences, delivery records and unsuccessful referrals.

The principal limitations concern what can be inferred from the source material. The study used a mixed-purpose dataset: full-trial interviews asked directly about physical activity, whereas pilot interviews and routine logs did not. All five areas appeared across the three source groups, but richer interview detail may still have shaped how they were interpreted. Coded entries were not independent participants or events, and their frequencies describe extracted material rather than prevalence, importance or effect. The available data did not allow us to examine whether the opportunities or support documented differed by disability, neurodivergence, gender, ethnicity, deprivation or geography. As a result, the analysis cannot determine how often or intensely young people were active, causal effects, or whether particular forms of matching or support improved loneliness or other outcomes. Finally, INACT recruited pupils experiencing loneliness and low community connection. The five areas may also be relevant to young people referred for other reasons, but their content or relative importance may differ. Transferability beyond this population should therefore be tested rather than assumed. Prospective studies should record referral and attendance stages consistently, examine whether opportunities and support differ between groups, and test whether particular forms of support are associated with participation or loneliness outcomes.

Conclusion

Marked inequalities remain in young people's access to physical activity and community resources. For pupils experiencing loneliness or low community connection, a physical activity referral may provide a route towards social contact, enjoyment and belonging, but availability alone does not make an opportunity usable. The findings show how young people's interests and confidence interacted with family and peer support, school routines, link-worker practices, provider reliability and local infrastructure between referral and participation. Link workers could address some barriers through personalised exploration, practical support and supported introductions, but they could not compensate fully for inaccessible, unsuitable or limited local provision. The study does not show that physical activity reduced loneliness. Rather, it identifies multilevel conditions that may determine whether young people can access and sustain participation in potentially connecting community activities. Future research should test whether addressing these conditions improves participation, social connection and loneliness outcomes.
