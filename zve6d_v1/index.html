Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Mental health problems and low self-esteem are highly prevalent among college students and are associated with impaired academic functioning and poorer long-term well-being. Digital interventions are a promising and scalable approach to improve student mental health, but adherence is often limited. Gamification may improve engagement, though evidence remains scarce.

Objective: This study examines whether adding gamification elements to a guided digital CBT-based self-esteem intervention for students improves adherence, satisfaction, and mental health outcomes.

Methods: In a randomized controlled trial, 873 students were allocated to a gamified or non-gamified version of a guided self-esteem intervention. The primary outcome was adherence (number of modules completed), while secondary outcomes included satisfaction, self-esteem, depressive symptoms, anxiety, and quality of life. Assessments were conducted at baseline and at 4-week follow-up.

Results: No significant differences were found between conditions on adherence, satisfaction or clinical outcomes in the full sample. Among intervention starters (i.e. completed ≥1 module), the gamified version showed a small increase in adherence (d = .17). Across conditions, participants showed significant improvements in selfesteem, depression, anxiety, and quality of life from pre-to post-test (d = .44-.76).

Conclusions: Gamification may modestly increase adherence among intervention starters but does not improve satisfaction or clinical outcomes. Both intervention versions were associated with improvements over time; however, the lack of an untreated control condition limits causal interpretation. Future research should investigate mechanisms behind gamification components.

1. Introduction

Mental health concerns are highly prevalent among college students, with a recent meta-analysis estimating that 26.3% of students worldwide experience mental health conditions including depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders (Leow et al., 2024). College students may be particularly vulnerable to such complaints because they are typically in the developmental period of emerging adulthood which is characterized by major transitions in living arrangements, relationships, education, and career development (Arnett, 2007). These mental health conditions often persist throughout the university years and predict poorer psychological well-being in later developmental stages (Duffy et al., 2020; Geirdal et al., 2019).

One important factor related to mental health problems among college students is low self-esteem, defined as the cognitive-affective appraisal of one's self-worth and confidence in one's capabilities (Greenwald et al., 2002; Pelham & Swann, 1989; Shavelson et al., 1976). College students are susceptible to low self-esteem because identity exploration, a central feature of emerging adulthood, can give rise to uncertain and unstable self-perceptions (Luyckx et al., 2013). Low self-esteem is common among college students, affecting about one-third across countries (Azmi et al., 2022; Caring Universities Consortium, 2025; Ketata et al., 2021).

Low self-esteem negatively affects academic performance and overall well-being (Fairlamb, 2022). Students with low self-esteem experience greater academic pressure, are more likely to disengage from their studies, and tend to achieve lower academic outcomes (Acosta-Gonzaga, 2023). It is also associated with greater vulnerability to stress, depression and anxiety (Asici & Sari, 2022; Cuijpers, Smit, et al., 2021; Mofatteh, 2021; Zell & Johansson, 2025). If left unresolved during university years, low self-esteem may have lasting consequences for future mental health and employment outcomes (Krauss & Orth, 2022; Steiger et al., 2014).

Meta-analytic evidence indicates that psychological treatments can effectively address low self-esteem in adults, with CBT emerging as particularly effective (Kolubinski et al., 2018; Niveau et al., 2021). Among college students, interventions have also shown positive effects on self-esteem, mostly delivered in face-to-face group formats (Clore & Gaynor, 2006; Perveen et al., 2020; Qian et al., 2022).

Despite their effectiveness, access to such interventions is often limited by stigma, preference for self-reliance, and practical constraints such as cost, time, and geography (Russell et al., 2008; Vidourek et al., 2014). Digital interventions may help address these barriers due to their accessibility, anonymity, and scalability (Andersson & Titov, 2014; Ebert et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2023). While empirical evidence on digital self-esteem interventions for college students remains scarce, they show promising effects. One unguided mobile self-esteem intervention demonstrated small-to-moderate effects (Bruhns et al., 2021), and a therapist-supported CBT-based online intervention showed large effects (Berg et al., 2022). Although encouraging, these studies differ substantially in format (i.e. mobile versus web-based), adherence operationalization and level of guidance, limiting conclusions about usage and scalability. Moreover, therapist-guided interventions may be difficult to scale due to limited mental health workforce capacity (Ballout, 2025).

Despite the advantages of digital mental health interventions, one often-reported challenge is low adherence, which is the extent to which participants follow the therapy as prescribed (Religioni et al., 2025). Dropout rates in digital interventions are generally high, often exceeding 50% in real-world settings (Kelders et al., 2012; Nwosu et al., 2022), and are particularly elevated in unguided formats (Karyotaki et al., 2021; Opie et al., 2024). In digital mental health interventions, greater adherence is often associated with better treatment outcomes (Donkin et al., 2011). Low adherence rates may compromise the effectiveness of digital interventions and diminish their potential to produce significant health outcomes (Linardon & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz, 2020). Thus, preventing drop-out has been named a key priority (Linardon, 2026).

One possible approach to increase adherence in digital interventions is through gamification, which refers to the application of game design elements in non-game contexts to enhance engagement and promote desired mental health benefits (Castellano-Tejedor & Cencerrado, 2024; Deterding et al., 2011). Rather than transforming an intervention into a literal game, gamification entails integrating features from (digital) games into digital interventions. For example, participants may receive visual feedback on their progress, unlock bonus materials as a reward, or earn badges and certificates upon reaching milestones. (Aschentrup et al., 2024; Tondello et al., 2017). These elements may increase adherence through several psychological mechanisms, such as reinforcing behavioral change, stimulating curiosity and interest (V. W. S. Cheng et al., 2019), and nurturing intrinsic motivation and sustaining engagement over time (Ryan et al., 2006; Seaborn & Fels, 2015).

A growing body of research indicates the potential of gamification in digital interventions. One systematic review found that gamified interventions increased engagement (e.g. time spent, interaction with the intervention, visits) among adults compared to interventions without gamification (Looyestyn et al., 2017). Another systematic review suggests there may be a relationship between number of gamification elements and adherence: as one increases, so may the other (Brown et al., 2016). Beyond engagement, meta-analytic evidence suggests that gamified interventions may contribute to mental health outcomes, including improved general well-being, anxiety, and depression (C. Cheng & Ebrahimi, 2023).

Evidence in student populations specifically shows similar trends. One randomized controlled trial (RCT) found that a gamified mental health app had higher retention than a different, non-gamified app (Litvin et al., 2023). In another RCT, a gamified intervention performed comparably to a group CBT intervention, with both interventions resulting in greater reductions in anxiety and depression than a moodmonitoring control condition (Bantjes et al., 2024). A gamified CBT intervention showed promising effects in reducing dysfunctional negative emotions and irrational cognition among college students (Tomoiagă & David, 2022). Combined, these studies suggest that college students may benefit from gamified digital interventions in terms of both engagement and effectiveness.

However, the evidence on gamification of mental health interventions for the student population remains scarce and several important gaps persist. To our knowledge, no study has isolated and examined the effect of gamification on treatment adherence and effectiveness. Additionally, though low self-esteem poses a crucial developmental challenge for college students and is associated with a range of negative mental health outcomes, we found no trials on gamification within interventions targeting low self-esteem in this population.

For the present study, we developed a guided CBT-based digital intervention for low self-esteem tailored to college students in the Netherlands (EsteemUp). We conducted an RCT comparing two versions of the intervention: one with gamification elements and one without. The primary aim was to examine whether gamification improves adherence, operationalized as the number of completed modules. In addition, we examined whether gamification positively impacted participants' satisfaction, changes in self-esteem, depression, anxiety, and quality of life, as well as the relationship between adherence and clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that participants in the gamified condition would show greater adherence and more favorable outcomes than participants in the non-gamified condition.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study design

This study is a two-arm randomized trial comparing the effectiveness of two versions of a guided e-health intervention, EsteemUp, in enhancing students' self-esteem: a standard (non-gamified) version and a gamified version. Assessments were conducted at baseline (T0) and at post-intervention four weeks later (T1).

This study was conducted within the Caring Universities Consortium; a collaboration between -at the time of the trial --seven Dutch universities (VU Amsterdam, Leiden University, Maastricht University, Utrecht University, Erasmus University, University of Amsterdam and Inholland University of Applied Sciences). The Caring Universities Consortium is affiliated with the World Health Organization (WHO) World Mental Health International College Student Initiative (WMH-ICS) (Cuijpers et al., 2019). Ethical approval was obtained from the VU Scientific and Ethical Review Board (reference number 2020.088) and the trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID NCT05582954).

2.2 Participants and Enrollment

Participants in this study were undergraduate, graduate, or PhD students in the Netherlands. Eligible students could register for the EsteemUp program through multiple recruitment channels. First, through the annual WMH-ICS survey on student mental health, which is sent via email to all enrolled students. Second, Caring Universities conducted both online (e.g., social media) and offline (e.g., posters, flyers) marketing efforts to recruit participants. Finally, university staff members who work directly with students (e.g. student psychologists, study advisors, mentors, lecturers) were encouraged to refer students to the program. Participant recruitment and data collection took place from November 2022 to October 2025. Participants were not offered any compensation for filling out questionnaires.

2.3 Inclusion Criteria

The present study utilized the following inclusion criteria: participants had to 1) be aged 16 years or older, 2) be currently enrolled as a student or PhD candidate at a university within the Caring University Consortium, and 3) provide informed consent. There was no inclusion criterion with respect so selfesteem.

E-coaches provided asynchronous, text-based feedback via the platform after each module completed by a participant. This feedback was personalized based on the participant's answers to reflective questions and assignments. The goal was to increase motivation and engagement by showing empathy, offering affirmations, asking open-ended questions, and using reflective techniques, which is in line with motivational interviewing principles (Hall et al., 2012).

E-coaches received six hours of training as preparation and were required to attend weekly intervision sessions supervised by the research team. As additional quality assurance, the e-coaching messages were periodically examined on a sample basis by the research team.

2.5 Randomization

Participants were randomly assigned to either the regular or gamified condition using 1:1 block randomization via an automated system embedded within the Caring Universities platform. Randomization was stratified by gender and university, and conditions were allocated automatically upon completion of the baseline assessment. This automated process ensured allocation concealment and blinding.

2.6 Assessment measures

2.6.1 Sociodemographic information

The following sociodemographic information was obtained: age, gender, marital status, nationality, current university, faculty, education level (Bachelor, Master or PhD.), and current usage of psychotherapy (yes/no) and/or psychotropic medication (yes/no).

2.6.2 Primary outcome: Intervention adherence

Adherence was operationalized as the number of modules completed by each participant, with a total of four modules available in the intervention.

2.6.3 Secondary outcome measures

2.6.3.1 General satisfaction

General participant satisfaction with the intervention was assessed using the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8) (Larsen et al., 1979). The CSQ-8 consists of eight items rated on a 4-point Likert scale, yielding a total score ranging from 8 to 32, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction. Example items include 'In an overall, general sense, how satisfied are you with the service you have received?' and 'If a friend were in need of similar help, would you recommend our program to him or her?'. Though there are no formally established cut-off scores for different levels of satisfaction, previous research has proposed interpretative ranges. One article classified scores of 8-20 as low satisfaction, 21-26 as moderate satisfaction, and 27-32 as high satisfaction (Jilani et al., 2023), whereas another suggested that scores between 24 and 32 reflect high levels of satisfaction (Ozmen et al., 2023).

The instrument has demonstrated good model fit, high internal consistency, and strong construct validity in the context of online interventions (Boß et al., 2016). The Dutch adaptation demonstrated similar psychometric properties and was considered appropriate for use in the Netherlands. (de Brey, 1983; De Wilde & Hendriks, 2005).

2.6.3.2 Guidance satisfaction

Satisfaction with the e-coach was measured using the Working Alliance Inventory for guided internet interventions (WAI-I) (Gómez Penedo et al., 2020). The WAI-I consists of 12 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, producing a total score ranging from 12 to 60 with higher scores indicating a stronger working alliance and greater satisfaction with the e-coach. Example items include 'I believe the e-coach who supports me in the online program likes me' and 'The e-coach who supports me in the online program is really interested in my well-being'. Scores between 36 and 47 reflect sufficient satisfaction, while scores above 48 indicate high to very high satisfaction. The WAI-I has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties, including good reliability and validity in the context of guided internet-based interventions (Gómez Penedo et al., 2020).

2.6.3.3 Self-esteem

Self-esteem was assessed using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES) (Rosenberg, 1965), one of the most widely used instruments for measuring global self-esteem. The RSES consists of 10 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating higher self-esteem. Five items are positively worded (e.g., "On the whole, I am satisfied with myself") and five are negatively worded and reverse-scored (e.g., "At times I think I am no good at all."). The scale has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including good internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and construct validity (Sinclair et al., 2010).

2.6.3.4 Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) (Kroenke et al., 2001), a widely used self-report measure of depression severity. The PHQ-9 consists of nine items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 ("not at all") to 3 ("nearly every day"), yielding a total score between 0 and 27, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The instrument has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high sensitivity, specificity, test-retest reliability, and construct validity (Kroenke et al., 2001; Wittkampf et al., 2007)

2.6.3.5 Anxiety

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) (Spitzer et al., 2006), a brief self-report instrument designed to assess the severity of generalized anxiety symptoms. Each of the seven items is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 ("not at all") to 3 ("nearly every day"), resulting in a total score between 0 and 21, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity.

The GAD-7 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency, sensitivity, specificity, test-retest reliability, and construct validity (Löwe et al., 2008; Rutter & Brown, 2017; Spitzer et al., 2006).

2.6.3.6 Quality of life

Quality of life was assessed using the Mental Health Quality of Life questionnaire (MHQoL) (van Krugten et al., 2022), a brief instrument specifically developed to measure quality of life in individuals with mental health problems. The MHQoL consists of seven items rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21; higher scores indicate better perceived quality of life. The instrument has demonstrated good psychometric properties, including strong internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.85), test-retest reliability, and construct validity (van Krugten et al., 2022).

2.7 Sample size calculation

We could find no prior literature comparing gamified and non-gamified digital mental health interventions to inform the sample size estimation. Therefore we conducted a power calculation for a two-tailed difference between means with a small-tomoderate effect size of .3 (Cohen, 2013), which is still clinically meaningful. This indicated a total required sample size of n=580 (290 per study arm with complete data) (Faul et al., 2007).

2.8 Data analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 30 and Stata version 18. All randomized participants were included in the primary analyses following the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle.

To verify the success of the randomization, baseline differences between the two study arms ('regular' vs. 'gamified' condition) were examined using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent samples t-tests for continuous variables. The same tests were applied to assess potential differences between study completers and non-completers.

Adherence rates were compared between study arms for the total sample using an independent samples t-test. As an additional sensitivity analysis, we compared adherence rates between the two arms for participants who completed at least 1 module, as these participants are guaranteed to have been exposed to the gamification elements.Effect sizes (Cohen's d) were calculated manually, using the benchmarks of .2, .5, and .8 as small, moderate, and large effects respectively (Cohen, 2013).

As an exploratory analysis, we examined the association between adherence and post-intervention clinical outcomes using multiple linear regression models, controlling for baseline clinical scores. Adherence was evaluated as a predictor in the full sample and separately within each intervention arm. Missing outcome data were addressed using multiple imputation, with 70 datasets generated through Fully Conditional Specification (FCS). Parameter estimates were pooled according to Rubin's rules.

Group differences in satisfaction (CSQ-8, WAI-I) were analyzed using independent samples t-tests on the imputed data.

We explored changes in clinical outcomes of self-esteem (RSES), depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), and quality of life (MHQoL) with linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). The LMMs were conducted using the unimputed dataset, given prior evidence that LMMs applied to longitudinal data can appropriately address missingness without the need for multiple imputation (Twisk et al., 2013). As covariates we included baseline clinical severity, age, gender, marital status, nationality, university affiliation, educational level, and current psychotherapy and/or medication use (Ebert et al., 2013; Karyotaki et al., 2018; Reins et al., 2020). Model parameters were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), and estimated marginal means (EMMs) were computed to evaluate the interaction between time and condition, as well as the main effect of time. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) were calculated manually based on the EMMs.

As additional sensitivity analyses, we repeated all analyses for participants who had completed at least one module and were therefore guaranteed to have been exposed to the gamification elements.

Of these, 434 were allocated to the gamified intervention and 439 to the non-gamified intervention. In the gamified intervention, 280 started the intervention by completing at least one module ("intervention starters"), and 282 did in the non-gamified intervention. 3.2 Study attrition

Of the 873 participants included in study, 281 (32.2%) completed post-test (study completers) whereas 592 (67.8%) did not (study non-completers). 4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Principal findings

This randomized controlled trial (RCT) comparing two intervention conditions showed no significant difference in adherence for the total sample (1.85 of 4 modules in the gamified and 1.7 of 4 in the non-gamified intervention). However, when analyzing intervention starters (i.e. participants who completed at least one module and were therefore exposed to the gamification elements), the gamified version was associated with a small but statistically significant increase in modules completed compared to the non-gamified version (2.86 and 2.64 modules respectively, Cohen's d = .17).

No significant differences were found between the gamified and non-gamified intervention in satisfaction, satisfaction with e-coach guidance, or clinical outcomes. Participants in both groups reported moderate to high satisfaction, and showed significant improvements in self-esteem, depressive symptoms, anxiety, and quality of life, with moderate to large effect sizes (Cohen's d = .44-.76).

These findings suggest that gamification may have a small effect on adherence within intervention starters, though this does not appear to translate into higher satisfaction or improved clinical outcomes. Overall, both versions of the intervention were associated with meaningful improvements over time, suggesting that the core intervention, rather than the gamification elements, may be the primary driver of change.

4.2 Comparison to prior research

Our findings are in line with prior literature suggesting that gamification can enhance engagement and participation in digital interventions (Looyestyn et al., 2017; Seaborn & Fels, 2015). Indeed, meta-analytic evidence indicates that gamification tends to produce small to moderate improvements in engagement-related outcomes (C. Cheng & Ebrahimi, 2023), which was replicated in our study amongst intervention starters. However, contrary to our expectations, gamification had no significant effect on the satisfaction or clinical outcomes in this study. There are several possible explanations for this absence. The impact of gamification may depend on the type and quality of game elements (Hamari et al., 2014; Sailer et al., 2017). It is possible that the gamification elements in EsteemUp (i.e. progress bar, badges, unlockable content, and a certificate of completion) were not engaging or relevant enough for the students. Alternatively, the high baseline quality of the intervention and platform may have resulted in a ceiling effect. Given the relatively high satisfaction scores observed in both conditions, there may have been limited room for gamification to significantly improve engagement, satisfaction, or clinical outcomes.

Adherence in the total sample was on the lower side when compared to other digital mental health interventions for students (Harrer et al., 2018; Palacios et al., 2018; Rith-Najarian et al., 2024). This may be attributable to the real-world context of our study, which did not include financial incentives, as well as the relatively low level of mental health complaints in our sample. However, among intervention starters, nearly half completed it. This relatively high completion rate for a real-world intervention may indicate good acceptability among students and reflect a challenge in uptake rather than in adherence.

Regarding clinical outcomes, we found moderate to large improvements on all measures across intervention arms. Previous research on digital mental health interventions for students generally demonstrated small to moderate improvements in outcomes such as self-esteem, depression and anxiety (Bruhns et al., 2021; Clore & Gaynor, 2006; Cuijpers, Miguel, et al., 2021). Notably, we found two large effect sizes (on self-esteem and quality of life), which exceeded the small-to-moderate effects typically reported in previous research. Though the absence of an inactive control group prevents us from definitively attributing these changes to the intervention, our findings provide preliminary support for the intervention's potential to improve selfesteem and several related mental health outcomes.

Interestingly, intervention adherence was not associated with clinical outcomes in the present study. This is consistent with two previous studies on digital self-esteem interventions for students, which also found no relationship between adherence and clinical outcomes (Berg et al., 2022; Bruhns et al., 2021). This contrasts with evidence suggesting that higher adherence is generally linked to better treatment outcomes (Donkin et al., 2011; Linardon, 2026; Linardon & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz, 2020). One possible explanation is self-selection bias, which refers to systematic differences in those who choose to continue or discontinue an intervention. Indeed, attrition in digital interventions typically follows one of two patterns: participants may drop out due to lack of engagement or motivation, or they may drop out due to early improvement (Eysenbach, 2005). These opposing patterns may cancel each other out statistically, thereby weakening or obscuring conclusions that can be drawn on the relationship between adherence and treatment outcomes. Another possible explanation is that a higher intervention dose may not necessarily result in greater clinical improvement. This interpretation is consistent with literature demonstrating that meaningful improvements can occur after a single, well-designed therapeutic session (Schleider et al., 2025).

4.3 Limitations and strengths

The findings of the present study should be interpreted considering several limitations. Firstly, our study did not include process measures to understand and assess the mechanisms behind gamification, such as intrinsic motivation or user curiosity or interest (V. W. S. Cheng et al., 2019; Ryan et al., 2006; Seaborn & Fels, 2015). Including such measures would have allowed us to examine not only whether gamification had an impact, but also how and why in this specific context. As a result, the current study cannot provide insight into the mechanisms that underlie effective or ineffective gamification.

Another limitation is the absence of an inactive control group (e.g. waitlist). Although participants showed improvements in self-esteem, depressive and anxiety symptoms, and quality of life, these changes may also be due to alternative factors such as natural symptom fluctuation, regression to the mean, or external influences unrelated to the intervention (Barnett et al., 2005). Consequently, the observed improvements should be interpreted cautiously, and causal conclusions regarding intervention effectiveness cannot be drawn.

Lastly, our final sample was substantially smaller than required based on the a priori power calculation because of considerable attrition, resulting in lower statistical power than originally intended. In addition, the observed effect size was smaller than anticipated, meaning that a considerably larger sample would have been required to detect such effects. Consequently, the combination of the reduced sample size and smaller-than-expected effects likely limited our ability to detect statistically significant between-group differences.

The present study also demonstrates several strengths. First, it contributes to the growing body of literature on gamification in digital mental health. To our knowledge, no study has isolated the specific effect of gamification on adherence and effectiveness by directly comparing two versions of an otherwise identical intervention. Most RCTs on gamified digital mental health interventions compared a gamified intervention to an inactive control or waitlist (Aschentrup et al., 2024). There has been a call for "research that isolates the impacts of gamification to determine its effectiveness in the health and wellbeing domain" (Johnson et al., 2016), which is a gap addressed by the present study.

A final strength of this study is its real-world setting. EsteemUp was embedded into the service offering of several universities, which allowed us to examine how students engage with the intervention under natural conditions, as opposed to controlled research environments. As such, the present findings may offer valuable insight into the implementation of gamified mental health interventions in real-world higher education settings.

4.4 Directions for future research

A recommendation for future research pertains to the selection and evaluation of the gamification elements. As Cheng et al. (2019) stated in their systematic review: "researchers may treat gamification as a black box without consideration for its underlying mechanisms" (V. W. S. Cheng et al., 2019). Future research should therefore ensure that gamification features are appropriately tailored to the target population, ideally through early involvement of end users in the design and selection process. Due to time constraints, this form of preliminary user input was not feasible in the present study, although it may have improved the relevance and acceptability of the gamification elements. In addition, future research should include process measures such as motivation, interest, and engagement with the gamification elements. Together, these approaches would strengthen understanding of why, how, and under what conditions gamification contributes to intervention effectiveness.

Additionally, a natural continuation of this study would be to conduct a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to assess the clinical effectiveness of EsteemUp. We compared two active treatments in the present study, and though we did see improvements from pre-to post-test in self-esteem, depression, anxiety and quality of life, these cannot be directly attributed to the intervention. Therefore, future research should conduct an RCT that includes a waitlist or inactive control condition to more thoroughly assess the effectiveness of EsteemUp.

5. Conclusion

The addition of gamification elements to a digital self-esteem intervention produced a small significant increase in adherence amongst intervention starters but did not enhance satisfaction or clinical outcomes. Improvements in self-esteem, depression, anxiety, and quality of life were observed in both the gamified and non-gamified version of the intervention, highlighting the potential of digital self-esteem interventions to support students' mental health.

Future research should explore which gamification elements best fit the preferences of the target demographic, as well as assess the psychological mechanisms that underlie gamification. Additionally, a randomized controlled trial using an inactive control would allow for firmer conclusions regarding intervention effectiveness.
