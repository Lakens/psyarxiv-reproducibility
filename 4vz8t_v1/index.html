Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

In child language research and clinical speech language pathology settings, transcripts of speech are often used for investigations into language acquisition and development. The existing literature is heavily biased towards monolingual contexts, particularly from a small number of languages, meaning that there are few examples of theory-driven protocols for handling speech and deriving developmental metrics from contexts where multiple typologically diverse languages appear together. In the current report, we address limitations of existing approaches and describe a theorydriven protocol for transcription and morphemizing of multilingual translanguaged speech, as applied to large corpus of parent-child speech from multilingual Singapore. The protocol aims to be language-model neutral, treating all speech as valid input to the child, without excluding or separating by official language labels, and may be applicable to a variety of linguistic contexts. One key finding arising from this research was that applying the protocol created unique research challenges for the typological distinct languages used by parents in the local context (predominantly English, Mandarin Chinese, Malay and Tamil alongside unique lexical items from the local context, known as Red-Dot words), and that structural inequalities make those challenges more difficult to resolve for minoritized and under-resourced languages.

Introduction

In child language research and clinical speech language pathology settings, counts of words/morphemes are commonly used as a measure of speech quantity (e.g., Ezeizabarrena & Garcia Fernandez, 2018) and in computations of metrics for speech complexity (e.g., mean length of utterance, MLU; Eisenberg et al., 2001). As Kidd and Garcia (2022) have shown, the existing literature heavily favours monolingual contexts and linguistic data from a small number of languages. In monolingual samples, decisions about the unit of linguistic analysis tend to be straightforward, as each language's word-building rules keep the relationship between word-count and morpheme-count stable within a language. However, in multilingual contexts, people with linguistic knowledge in multiple languages may 'translanguage', or make use of all of their linguistic resources in a fluid and adaptive way (cf. Lewis et al., 2012 , Li & García, 2022; see Figure 1 below for examples). Such practices mean the relationship between word-count and morpheme-count can vary greatly even in a single sentence.

In this paper we present our protocols for processing, transcribing and quantifying child-directed speech in the multilingual translanguaging-heavy environment of Singapore. We describe the challenges faced in wanting to create language-inclusive protocols and the unequal research burdens uncovered as a result of structural inequalities and lack of resources for certain languages. This paper reports data from the first time point of the Talk Together Study (Woon et al., 2021), a micro-longitudinal study about parent-child language use in Singapore (child age: 8 to 40 months-ofage) in speech activities at home. 146 recordings of naturalistic parent-child interaction during a picturebook book-sharing session from the Talk Together Study were transcribed and analysed for this paper, of which translanguaging was observed for 80% of parents (Styles et al., 2022). To our knowledge, this is the first study to discuss language-inclusive protocols for computing MLU in a multilingual context with frequent translanguaging. As majority of previous research on this topic has been conducted in English, this investigation has specific implications for examining language use in a multilingual world, where at least half the global population speak more than one language (UNESCO, 2024).

Linguistic landscape of Singapore

Before diving into the details of our analyses, we lay out the unique linguistic landscape surrounding our study. First, our study was conducted in Singapore -a nation state located in Southeast Asia with a long history of diverse multilingualism. The bilingual policy in 1966 decreed that English would be used as a working language in schools and offices, while the so-called 'mother tongue' would be used for family and keeping community values and traditions alive (Stroud & Wee, 2010). Mandarin was designated the official 'mother tongue' for ethnically Chinese Singaporeans (other languages spoken: Hokkien, Teochew, Cantonese, Hakka, Hainanese etc.), Malay for ethnically Malay Singaporeans (other languages spoken: Arabic, Bahasa Indonesia, Javanese etc.) and Tamil for ethnically Indian Singaporeans (other languages spoken: Bengali, Gujarati, Hindi, Punjabi, Urdu etc.). All classes in schools are held in English and the three official mother tongues, although for Indian Singaporeans whose families speak a language other than Tamil, there are also language centres that offer classes outside of curriculum time (Ministry of Education, 2026).

The 2020 census (Singapore Department of Statistics, 2021a) shows alignment between the language policy and contemporary patterns of language use, with ethnically Chinese Singaporeans (74%) reporting that the main language spoken at home was English, Mandarin or another Chinese language (mainly for older Singaporeans), for ethnically Malay Singaporeans (14%) the main language reported was English or Malay, and ethnically Indian Singaporeans (9%), English, Tamil, Malay or another Indian language. The remaining 3% of Singaporeans reported speaking mainly English or another language not captured by the census. It is against this backdrop where bi/multilingualism is the norm in the society that the data for this study was collected. It is unsurprising then that our data of child-directed speech is largely bi/multilingual. However, it would an oversimplification to classify language input that Singaporean children receive as bi/multilingual because caregivers also use a special class of words that arose from extensive language contact in Singapore.

Language input to children growing up in Singapore

Multilingualism is highly prevalent in Singapore, with over 80% of Singaporeans aged 15-54 years old reporting literacy in more than one language (Singapore Department of Statistics, 2021c). In this vibrant language environment, there is extensive language contact among the spoken languages in Singapore, resulting in many local words that do not strictly belong to any of the commonly spoken language in Singapore (i.e., Singapore variety of English, Mandarin, Malay, and Tamil) even though the origin of many of these terms may be from one of the local languages. Singaporean speakers who use these terms are not deemed as code-switching, much like how English speakers are not deemed as having code-switched into Japanese when they use the word sushi in their conversations. Woon and Styles (2021) compiled 205 of these local words (termed 'Red-Dot' words by the authors) with the help of a team of multilingual Singaporean speakers and conducted a citizen science survey asking self-identified English-speaking Singaporeans (N = 309) to estimate the age at which children would likely acquire these local words. Results from the survey showed that adult Singaporeans deem most Red-Dot words being early acquired, with median estimates ranging from 6 months of age to 5.5 years of age. The very earliest acquired expressions were interjections (e.g., aiyo 'depending on tone, to show concern, frustration, dismay or surprise'), followed by verbs (pom-pom 'to shower') and names for people (ah gong 'grandfather') or foods (mam mam 'to eat' or referring to food), then onomatopoeia (shhh 'to hush someone') and names for things (chou chou 'comfort pillow'). In another report, the majority of parents report using these Red-Dot words with their children (61%; Ho, 2023), meaning that children growing up in Singapore will not only hear named languages that their bi/multilingual caregivers speak but also hear these unique Red-Dot terms frequently. Furthermore, translanguaging is a natural, but somewhat under-described phenomenon in child-directed speech. In one report of parent-child conversations, within-utterance translanguaging was observed for 80% of parents, with a median translanguaging rate of 5% (Styles et al., 2022; see Figure 1 for translanguaging examples).

Next, we present the challenges faced by child language researchers studying multilingual language environments like ours.

Child language quantification in multilingual language environments

Despite the fact that at least half the global population speak more than one language (UNESCO, 2024), many research/clinical practices have largely been tailored towards monolingual populations. In a review of over 40 MLU studies, Choo et al. (2026) highlighted that more than 85% of papers studied monolingual populations, while only 15% of the studies directly addressed multilingualism in their reports. One third of the studies with multilingual speech in their samples removed non-English speech before data analysis, thereby creating an artificially monolingual dataset. Excluding non-English speech may make a sample 'cleaner' for subsequent analyses, but this may underrepresent the natural linguistic mix of multilingual families, or over-estimate their monolingual features. For the two thirds of studies that reported multilingualism in their speech samples (10% of the original set of studies), one MLU measure was computed for each language. Calculating a separate MLU measure for each language effectively avoids the challenges of typological differences in the computation of metrics. However, this approach reinforces two assumptions that may not be true of multilingualism in-the-wild: first, that speech can be neatly separated into different named languages using word-or sentence-level evidence; second, that each language is cognitively represented separately in a multilingual person's mind, with active and intentional 'switching' between codes. These assumptions are challenged by the model of linguistic 'translanguaging'

A second issue with quantifying child language input is the reliance of counts based on written transcription of verbal speech. Word count and morpheme count are amongst the most common ways to quantify child-directed input and language output of children (see review by Choo et al., 2026). However, the status of a 'word' is somewhat tenuous in linguistics, and the definition of what constitutes a word is heavily influenced by the writing system (see Dixon & Aikhenvald, 2003 for how 'word' is defined across typologically diverse languages; see also recent commentary by Haspelmath, 2023), and different writing systems have different norms for denoting word boundaries. For example, for the languages spoken in Singapore, English, Malay and Tamil writing systems use spaces to denote word boundaries, but the logographic character-based Chinese writing system does not. As a result, most people assume a character in Chinese is a word, but that is not always true. When examining word count in child-directed speech in multilingual environments, researchers need to be fluent speakers and readers of said languages in order to determine what can be counted. Relatedly, for morphemes, a morpheme has been typically defined as the smallest unit of language that makes a meaningful difference in words (e.g., Aronoff & Fudeman, 2022). Across many languages, morphemes may combine together to form words, but counting morphemes is also difficult if the languages in the multilingual environment have different language typologies which would change the relationship between word count and morpheme count. For example, isolating languages are languages with words that typically stand on their own, and they have a ratio of words and morphemes that is close to one:one. In contrast, agglutinating languages are languages with words that may be made up of many morphemes, so they have more morphemes per word as to include rich grammatical information (e.g., gender of actor, number of actors and tense).

We provide some examples from the languages in our study. In English, a free morpheme may exist on its own (e.g., apple) while a bound morpheme attaches itself to other morphemes to change its meaning (e.g., the plural -s in apples indicates 'more than one apple'). In Chinese, the difference between morphemes and characters is not transparent, e.g., 葡萄 pu2tao2 is both a morpheme and word that means 'grape/grapes' (no distinction between singular and plural), but neither character can exist on its own as a morpheme or a word. In Malay, plurals may be indicated by reduplication (e.g., bola-bola 'balls'), but not all reduplication indicates plurality (e.g., semut means 'ant' but semut-semut means 'pins and needles'). Word formation rules in Tamil are such that a root word is often accompanied by many morphemes denoting various aspects of grammar. This is crucial in multilingual communities that translanguage often, as in the case of Singapore. We cannot count each language separately, or simply use English as the basis upon which other rules build.

Recent work on translanguaging has also shifted away from applying principles derived from English onto all other languages; rather, the emphasis of research work now should be to develop languageand/or community-specific norms (cf. We carefully considered the grammatical structure of each language in our dataset before constructing language-specific and languagegeneral rules to ensure equitable representation across all languages. Methods

The current report is an overview of the procedures and research burdens involved in creating transcripts from a corpus of parent-child speech, and processing the transcriptions for computation of word-and morpheme-level metrics such as mean length of utterance.

Principles guiding morphemising and our protocol

Morphemising is a process by which each word in a transcript is analysed to identify its constituent morphemes, thereby allowing automated counts of the number of morphemes in a span of speech. As the languages in our study have different typological structures, standard approaches would be to count words or morphemes separately for each language. We developed four morphemising principles to characterise multilingual translanguaged speech found in our corpus without artificially segmenting utterances at language boundaries. Based on extant work (Brown, 1973; Casby, 2011; Miller, 1981; Pavelko & Owens, 2016; Retherford, 2007), we codified our four overarching principles from the perspective of what may be learnable to a child rather than what may be parsed in an etymological form from a dictionary where word-building rules may be more abstract. The four principles are as follows: (1) Non-analysability; (2) Non-productivity; (3) Non-semantic transparency and (4) Cross-linguistic equivalence. We list examples and further elaboration of each principle in Table 1.

(1) Principle of non-analysability: a word should not be broken into smaller constituent morphemes if a child would not be able to identify the constituent parts (e.g., men = [man] + [plural], but the internal vowel change is non-obvious to a child). (2) Principle of non-productivity: a word should not be broken into smaller constituent morphemes if those morphemes are not used in active word building processes (e.g., in a child's understanding, the -let in bracelet would be unlikely to produce necklet as a synonym for necklace).

(3) Principle of non-semantic transparency: a word should not be broken into smaller morphemes if the word is not meaningfully composed of smaller constituent parts (e.g., red cannot be broken into smaller meaningful parts; a compound word such as rainbow does not obviously mean the combination of the rain and bow according to a child's understanding of the two words). (4) Principle of cross-linguistic equivalence: the logic of these three principles should apply across languages, even though they may not apply equally to individual translation equivalents. Note that this means that morpheme counts will be higher or lower for expressions with similar meanings in different languages. This is a natural feature of multilingualism and forms part of the parent's linguistic repertoire that they may deploy flexibly when translanguaging with their child. The four principles were developed through iterative design. First, two English/Chinese bilingual team members (first and third author) morphemised a random subset of 20 transcriptions (approximately 2% of the total recordings) -one manually, and the other using automated morphemisers to assess their suitability for our data. We first extracted all words in these 20 transcripts and applied the automatic morphemisers. This initial review showed that the suitability of automatic morphemisation varied considerably across languages (see Supplementary Table 1 for list of all resources used and consulted for all languages). For English, the initial review revealed considerable variation between the outputs of different morphemisers, with tools often differing in both the number of morphemes assigned to a word and the placement of morpheme boundaries. For Mandarin and Malay, the automatic segmentations were generally aligned with our segmentation principles, although some deviations were identified. In contrast, the automatic output for Tamil differed substantially from the segmentation expected by fluent Tamil speakers.

English.

There was a relatively wide range of available morphemisation resources for English, allowing us to evaluate several alternatives. The initial evaluation revealed that these tools often differed in how they segmented words into morphemes, both in the number of morphemes identified and in the placement of segmentation boundaries. We therefore manually reviewed words that showed substantial disagreement between the automatic tools and the humanannotated segmentations, as well as words for which the tools produced substantially different segmentation boundaries. For each case, we considered the alternative segmentations and made a final segmentation decision based on our four principles.

Mandarin.

We observed that the segmentations produced by Mandarin automatic resources were largely aligned with our segmentation principles. To further assess the applicability of the Mandarin morphemiser, we applied it to all words extracted from the dataset. In total, we identified 23 instances in which the automatic segmentation differed from our preferred segmentation. These differences primarily involved colour terms (e.g., 灰色 hui1se4 'grey colour': 2 morphemes by us, 1 morpheme by morphemiser) and question words (e.g., 怎样 zen3yang4 'how is it', 怎么 zen3me0 'how [to do something]', 什么 shen3me0 'what': 2 by us, 1 morpheme by morphemiser; 怎样办 zen3yang4ban4 'what to do now': 3 morphemes by us, 2 morphemes 怎样 and 办 by morphemiser). These cases were manually reviewed and the preferred segmentations were incorporated into the subsequent processing.

Malay.

The Malay segmentations produced by some automatic tools were aligned with our segmentation principles. To further assess its applicability, we applied it to all words extracted from the dataset. In total, we identified two instances in which the automatic segmentation differed from our preferred segmentation (orangutan which we treated as a single morpheme, and macam-mana 'how' which we treated as single morpheme). The preferred segmentations were incorporated into the subsequent processing.

Tamil. We tested a variety of tools and asked two transcribers (one full-time staff member and one intern) to independently verify their outputs. It was immediately clear to our fluent speakers that the automatic outputs from these tools that were developed from written Tamil did not adequately reflect the morpheme boundaries of spoken Tamil in our transcripts. We therefore proceeded with manual morpheme segmentation for all files containing Tamil words. The manual segmentation was conducted in two phases. First, each transcript was segmented by one of the two fluent speakers. The two fluent speakers consulted one another whenever they encountered difficulties in determining morpheme boundaries, with guidance from the first author. Given the limitations in manpower, each file was segmented by only one speaker. A word-morpheme dictionary was then created based on the segmentations completed by the two fluent speakers. This approach reduced the need for the fluent speakers to manually segment every transcript and helped distribute the workload more evenly.

In addition to comparing the outputs of the different morphemiser tools, we manually identified and flagged several types of English words and word sequences that required special treatment. These included transitive verbs, named entities, and connected word sequences. For transitive verbs (e.g., frightened, scared), we shortlisted words from our database and used contextual part-of-speech information (part-of-speech tags were labeled using Stanza NLP, Qi et al., 2020) to determine whether they should be segmented differently depending on their grammatical use. When a shortlisted transitive verb occurred in isolation, it was treated as a single morpheme. Named entities unique to the context were also identified and flagged to prevent their components from being incorrectly segmented as ordinary morphemes (e.g., Suzy Styles [author of the picturebook], Jurong [a location in Singapore]). Similarly, selected connected word sequences were treated as a single unit rather than as separate words (e.g., ice-cream, ice-pop).

Finally, we extracted Red-Dot and interjection words (e.g., 'oh!') from all transcripts and manually performed morpheme segmentation on each word. Red-Dot words were manually morphemised using lexicon lists from the whole corpus.

At the end of this process, we developed a semi-automated tool which allowed us to perform morpheme segmentation on the entire dataset of remaining transcripts following our principles and specified morpheme boundary decisions. To test our pipeline, we applied it to 14 transcripts (12 Mandarin Chinese-English-speaking families, 2 English-Malay-speaking family, 1 Tamil-Englishspeaking family). Each transcript was reviewed by at least two interns who were trained with the four principles. The interns examined the automatically generated morpheme boundaries and flagged words for which the segmentation differed from their own judgement.

As there are no current dictionaries documenting the local Singapore variety of the languages in the study, the iterative design of the transcription and morphemising protocols allowed us to develop linguistically informed approaches to handling individual speech tokens in our corpus. In some cases, as described above, challenging words were flagged for multiple rounds of group discussion where needed before deciding on spelling, language labelling, lexicalisation and/or morphemising. about morphemising. Automated morphemisers for each language were identified and tested for their suitability, adapted to align with the protocol if possible, and rejected if unsuitable (see Table 3 for illustrative examples).

Overall, at the end of the transcription process, we created 146 transcription files. We then morphemised each transcription and created a data table in which each utterance appears alongside metadata about the speaker, a list of its constituent morphemes, and a morpheme count. The data tables allowed us to calculate per-speaker measures of speech quantity and complexity such as the total number of morphemes spoken during the recording and the number of morphemes per utterance, regardless of whether utterances contain a single or multiple languages. This resulting morpheme counts are publicly accessible (https://doi.org/10.21979/N9/SZWBMG), and reported in detail in Choo et al. In the following section we evaluate the labour cost of creating and implementing transcription and morphemising protocols designed for translanguaging among typologically diverse languages.

Challenges and Issues

Throughout the process, it was clear that the labour cost needed to conduct language-related research, and develop and implement appropriate protocols in multilingual language environments is large. Specifically, for our study there was unbalanced cost of transcribing and tagging the speech across the languages of the corpus, with each language bringing its own unique complexities. We stress that the following challenges occurred because not only was our team transcribing a large amount of multilingual speech with a high percentage occurrence of translanguaging, we were also tagging each word/morpheme with language identity (i.e., English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil or Red-Dot for our dataset) so that accurate word counts and morpheme counts could be established. Furthermore, in order to be able to extract word and morpheme accounts accurately and efficiently, another goal we needed to ensure first was that the transcripts were machine-readable for automatic count processing to occur.

We note that the two largest languages in our corpus, English and Mandarin Chinese were also the languages with the most external language documentation and digital tools available (e.g., dictionaries, morphemisers). These were also the languages for which we had the largest group of transcribers and annotators. In the section that follows, we outline the specific challenges encountered for individual languages, the labour needed to solve those problems, the labour available to the project, and give an overview of the unequal burdens of research encountered for minority languages.

Labour cost of transcription

The iterative development of the transcription protocol meant that novel linguistic tokens were encountered throughout the transcription process, thus protocol refinements late in the process needed to be propagated retrospectively through completed transcriptions. 'Patching' was performed by the transcription team using a combination of automated flagging and specific lookup.

We used version control and changelogs to ensure that different versions of the transcription could be linked to different stages of protocol development.

The largest languages in the corpus were English and Chinese, so many transcription rules had to be created for challenging lexical items from these languages. However, since these languages also had the largest numbers of speakers in the team, it was generally easier to make decisions about lexical items in these languages than for languages supported by fewer team members (i.e., Malay and Tamil). Here, we describe the issues the transcription team had to resolve for each language in our dataset, beginning with the language that was easiest to transcribe. English words were straightforward to transcribe as the writing system is well documented, and transcribers merely needed to adhere to protocols for representing informal speech, as well as spelling unusual items consistently (e.g., already/oreddy mentioned above). For Tamil, the writing system is also systematic and allows transparent transcription of features of spoken language that do not typically appear in formal writing (i.e., epenthetic vowel இ 'i' is often inserted to aid in the pronunciation of words). Since spoken Tamil and formal written Tamil have different linguistic features, fluent Tamil-speaking transcribers needed to look out for these spoken features and transcribe them accordingly. We consider English to be the relatively easiest language in our dataset to transcribe, and Tamil, a little more complex due to the higher monitoring for informal speech features.

For Mandarin Chinese, transcribers manually added spaces between characters and manually marked out word boundaries. Doing so allowed us to maintain cross-linguistic equivalence across transcriptions in accordance with the fourth morphemising principle. This step required additional decisions about what counts as a singular word, as words do not always have a one-to-one correspondence with written characters. For this process, we consulted Mandarin Chinese dictionaries when deciding which characters should be grouped together as a word (e.g., reduplicated lexis 怕怕 pa4pa4 'scared scared', i.e., characters that are repeated but still represent one concept), or separated into two words (e.g., negated lexis 没 mei2 | 有 you3 'don't | have', i.e., concepts that represent a denial of occurrence). Mandarin Chinese was considered trickier than English to transcribe given that word boundaries needed to be checked.

The most difficult language to transcribe in our dataset was Malay because the writing system reflects a formal register that is rarely used in everyday spoken discourse (Abu Bakar & Buang, 2021; Jalil & Rickard Liow, 2008). Many features of the informal spoken register do not have standard spelling, resulting in high variability among individual transcribers. For example, there are variations in the spelling of the expression tidak ada 'do not have', which is commonly truncated in spoken Malay to tak ade or takde (this is akin to 'wanna' in spoken English). To handle spelling discrepancies, we spent several months working on a transcription protocol and spelling dictionary for transcribing Malay, with many rounds of patching and revision. Throughout the process, the lack of external resources on transcription of child-directed or informal Malay speech meant there was a limited evidence base for informed decision making by the team. Ultimately, consensus on spelling conventions was only achieved by conducting a study of spelling preferences for informal speech among an independent cohort of contemporary Malay speakers (binte Amran, in progress). We also had a smaller number of fluent Malay-speaking transcribers compared to Mandarin-speaking transcribers as a reflection of the language background of the population in Singapore. This meant that the smaller team had to work harder and for longer on the more complex language to transcribe.

As mentioned, Red-Dot terms are unique terms used with children that do not strictly belong to any one of the named languages in our dataset. Since our transcription team were made up of locals familiar with language use in Singapore, all members could spot Red-Dot terms in the child-directed speech and transcribe them. However, there were three main issues that the team had to resolve to create a standardised transcription corpus. First, the team had to define what constitutes a Red-Dot term, which can be tricky as some of these words have "equivalents" in one of the named languages. For example, in local speech, a commonly heard localised pronunciation of the Chinese term for 哥哥 'elder brother' is [kɒkɒ], with a slightly dipping tone on the first syllable and a rising tone on the secondfoot_0 . This is different from standard Mandarin pronunciation which is [kəkə] with short highlevel tones on each syllable. Mandarin-speakers in Singapore are likely familiar with both pronunciations and may use both interchangeably and/or choose either pronunciation based on formality of the speech exchange. Importantly, non-Mandarin local speakers are familiar with the word with its localised pronunciation given the language contact and high population density in Singapore (Woon & Styles, 2021). Our team therefore decided that when the localised pronunciation was heard in our dataset, it was deemed as Red-Dot. However, when the standard Mandarin pronunciation was heard from a Mandarin speaker, it was deemed as Mandarin. This decision was formalised in our BELA protocol.

Next, another issue arises from the use of terms like 'ah', 'oh' etc. These can be seen as fillers in spoken varieties of many languages spoken by speakers in our corpus e.g., in English (Brown, 1973). However, these terms also serve as local Singaporean discourse markers in clause-final positions, making them functional and meaningful in speech (Smakman & Wagenaar, 2013). We decided that when these terms serve as discourse markers, our transcribers would tag them as Red-Dot. While this decision was not difficult to make, adherence to this was challenging for the transcription team who had to listen to speech closely to decide if each occurrence of these terms were serving as discourse markers or not.

The third issue is dealing with spelling for Red-Dot terms. Because these terms largely occur in the spoken vernacular, there are no standardised or codified spellings. Our team had multiple rounds of discussions for handling these lexical items and decided on a standardised spelling for each term. An example of one of the discussions we had was deciding if certain Red-Dot terms which followed the linguistic pattern of reduplication were spelt with a hyphen or not e.g., mam-mam ('to eat'). This iterative process took several months and, in the end, we eventually established a dictionary for 205 words we deemed as Red-Dot (Woon & Styles, 2021). The dictionary was included in our BELA protocol, and all transcribers were asked to follow the protocol strictly.

Labour cost of morphemising

We made use of existing morphemisers where available and suitable to aid us in morphemising words into morphemes, following our four rules in the morphemising protocol.

As with the labour cost of transcription decisions, while we had a lot of English and Chinese data, we also had a lot of English and Chinese speakers. While we needed to create many rules for morphemising the large corpus of words in both languages, we had many speakers with which to split the decision process. Additionally, we could also use extant morphemisers to speed up the initial morphemising process, and the labour needed was to check words returned by standard morphemisers for exceptions (English), and to check all multi-character words and create a dictionary (Chinese).

The morphemising process for Malay was straightforward as the transparent word building in the language meant that we could make use of the extant morphemisers which were fairly accurate. Even though we had few speakers, the accuracy of the tools meant that there was not much manual checking needed.

The largest amount of labour and time needed for morphemising was for Tamil: we had to develop a dictionary of words from amongst the corpus, do manual morphemising for each of the words and do manual checks for each of the words. This was because we were unable to use any of the existing morphemisers that were geared towards written Tamil, which differs greatly from spoken Tamil (e.g., there are more morphemes in spoken Tamil as epenthetic vowels are included for ease of word production). The amount of work that our few Tamil speakers had to do was thus disproportionately more than speakers of the other languages who had extant morphemisers to reduce the initial amounts of manual workload. Even though we only had a small number of speakers and Tamil was the smallest subset in our corpus, we did not want to use systems that had not been curated for the purposes that we needed and end up with inaccuracies and misrepresentations of our smallest language.

For Red-Dot, there was also no morphemiser available so we had to create a new dictionary first before conducting manual morphemising. Nonetheless, we had a lot of Red-Dot speakers so the morphemising decision making process could be split amongst many people.

As a result, almost half the time was spent with Malay (especially for transcriptions) and Tamil (especially for morphemising) even though they took up the smallest portions of the data. We can understand why under-resourced labs may decide to exclude certain portions of the data to save on labour and time [see Choo et al.'s (2026) review of work that has subsampled from datasets, resulting in varying degrees of validity of the eventual measures].

Discussion

The rich collection of multilingual child-directed speech collected as part of the Talk Together Study (Woon et al., 2021) provides a unique opportunity to develop theory driven, linguistically informed approaches to multilingual translanguaged speech in development. In this paper, we documented details of our language-model-neutral approach to transcription and morphemising, and the challenges encountered when applying them to the corpus. While we report a comparison of different approaches to MLU-like metrics elsewhere (Choo et al., 2026), this report focuses on context-specific challenges and unequal burdens of labour we encountered in the process of creating and applying both protocols. First, generating protocols that treat all forms of speech across linguistic varieties with the same degree of fidelity was a wicked problem, due to typological and orthographic differences between languages, as well as definitional challenges about deciding which lexical items could and should 'belong' to which language categories (for example, Red-Dot words and interjections -see Chua et al., 2026 for further discussion). As discussed in detail in Choo et al., (2026), previous multilingual reports almost always elect to exclude minority languages or split their language metrics by language, approaches which can severely underestimate the true complexity of multilingual translanguaged speech. Given that translanguaged child-directed speech represents a parent's deployment of their full range of linguistic resources, the approach reported here attempts to capture the true richness of linguistic inputs regardless of language of origin -an approach that may have applicability in a variety of contexts. Second, application of our theory-driven languagemodel-neutral approach was challenging due to structural inequalities resulting in minoritised status for some languages and unequal availability of language resources resulting in different labour demands on transcribers. We describe each of these in turn.

Language-model-neutral, inclusive approach

Our approach in this project was to develop a dataset of transcribed speech that could be automatically processed in many different ways, including by researchers who subscribe to different models of bi-and multilingualism. For example, researchers who subscribe to the code-switching model of language-use conceive of named languages such as English and Mandarin Chinese as discrete linguistic systems that are represented separately in the mind (cf. Olson, 2024; Myers-Scotton, 2017), meaning that a bilingual would always know which language they are speaking, and all 'switches' from one code to another are intentional. According to this model, each sentence or clause is considered to have one 'matrix language' carrying most of the syntax for the clause along with 'embeddings' from other named languages (usually nouns, verbs and other content words integrated into the syntax of the matrix, cf. Myers-Scotton, 2002). Conversely, scholars of translanguaging (cf. Lewis et al., 2012) posit that a speaker has linguistic resources (e.g., words, morphemes, syntactic constructions) that may have their origins in one-or more-than-one language, meaning that a bilingual may traverse linguistic resources of two languages flexibly without necessitating awareness of which linguistic features items 'belong' to which code, or whether a mix of languages is intentional (Lewis et al., 2012; Li & García, 2022). This approach does not require one language to be considered the matrix language, and allows unique configurations of syntax arising out of language contact, and specific to a given speech community. We agree with scholars of the latter camp and our goal for the corpus was to produce word-and morpheme-level transcription data where every word was marked for language of origin, but where no matrix language is assumed, and an utterance can be comprised of any mix of words, including words which do not clearly belong more to one language than another (in our case, Red-Dot words). We refer to this as a 'language-model neutral' approach, as the data can be analysed from the perspective of multiple language-models.

Previous research on language complexity in parent-child speech tends to exclude non-majority languages, or artificially separate speech for processing named languages separately (for an overview of research including English-speaking parent-child conversations, see Choo et al., 2026). By doing so, researchers avoid direct comparisons of languages within their corpora as typologically distinct languages may have different strategies for incorporating complexity into speech. In many cases, this theoretical justification has the added benefit of reducing the burdens of working with minority languages. However, the approach is not 'language-model neutral'. First, it assumes that identifying which utterance belongs to which language is straightforward, and that all utterances containing words from multiple codes have a readily identifiable matrix language (see Auer & Muhamadova, 2005 for a discussion of how treating syntactic units as 'matrix language' and 'embedded language' may not always be clear-cut). Second, there may be an inherent assumption that the complexity of different linguistic resources is not under the active choice of the speakers. As mentioned, we chose a language-model neutral approach for our corpus. 80% of the parents in our corpus exhibit utterances containing multiple language codes, and given the linguistic landscape in Singapore, we did not want to assume that these utterances have specific 'matrix' vs 'embedded' languages. Indeed, some analyses of speech in Singapore have described a high prevalence of topiccomment syntax (Ng, 2024) like that found in Mandarin (e.g., Shi, 2000), in which individual phrases are not always coordinated into subject-predicate sentences, making it hard to evaluate which elements of speech would could be considered the 'matrix', and which 'embeddings' (see also Ong et al., 2014 for a case study of how matrix language theories may not apply in Singapore Mandarin-English mixing) Additionally, we posit that multilingual parents in our Singapore study who translanguage are actively choosing how to combine their linguistic resources in order to deploy complexity strategically when engaging with their children of different ages, or in different contexts. For example, when talking about a past event involving playing a game, a Chinese/English bilingual parent may select a tense-less Chinese verb for 'play' when speaking with a younger child to reduce complexity (e.g., 玩 wan2 -1 morpheme), and select a tensed English verb to increase complexity when speaking with an older child (e.g., played -2 morphemes); a Tamil/English bilingual parent may select a morphologically complex Tamil construction when their child is engaged and wellrested (e.g., வ�ைளயாடினார்கள் -4 morphemes), but select a lower complexity English construction when the same child is tired (e.g., played -2 morphemes). According to the translanguaging model, there is no reason to assume that parents are not active in their deployment of varied linguistic complexity across languages, rather than only within one language at a time. [For example, Purohit and Rahman (2021) highlighted how Indian parents actively translanguage in order to prepare their toddlers for English-medium preschools by using more words or longer phrases in English.] As such, our protocols allow investigation of this kind for the first time, where the language count does not have to conflict with language typology. Using this approach, Choo et al. (2026) reports linguistic complexity in child-directed speech for 146 parent-child dyads using both words and morphemes, with no separation of languages. Previous work that excluded the smaller languages or counted languages separately may have oversimplified and under-represented the amount of diversity and flexibility that bi-and multilingual parents deploy when translanguaging with their children.

Structural inequalities

Beyond the global prevalence of different languages, linguistic prestige in the Singapore context also impacted the project. Linguistic prestige refers to the social status attached to a particular language or dialect use in a community, which is often based on the social status of speakers of a linguistic variety (Schneider, 2025). Despite being the second most populous 'mother tongue' in Singapore, Anwar and Sim (2025) argued that Malay seems to be minoritised compared to the other official languages. From a language resource perspective, commentators have noted that a lot of Malaylanguage TV programming is sourced from other Malay speaking regions outside Singapore (Adnan, 2024), and the Malay Programmes Advisory Committee for the national broadcaster MediaCorp reports that "maintaining the standards of Malay language" is a key priority for programming (Infocomm Media Development Authority, 2012, p.4), rather than presenting naturalistic languageuse common in Singapore, such as slang expressions and translanguaging practices. Such policies contribute to a lack of institutional support for language resources such as dictionaries reflecting the local spoken variety, and translanguaging in general.

A second outcome of minoritisation arises from stereotypes associated with structural inequalities. For example, ethnically Malay Singaporeans are underrepresented in higher education (Singapore Department of Statistics, 2021c), making it harder to recruit student and graduate research assistants to support minoritised languages, as opposed to majority languages. Broader discourse in Singapore has also discussed an inaccurate but pervasive stereotype about the Malay community 'lagging behind' other groups with regards to education (cf. Suratnam, 2004). Consequently, at the data collection stage, we had more difficulties recruiting Malay-speaking families, some of whom were wary of taking part in a study that included Malay alongside majority languages, as they were uncomfortable about potentially being portrayed in a negative light about their language use. In our later projects we learned from this experience, and addressed this challenge with a more targeted focus on word-of-mouth and building bridges to trusted community organisations. However, in the current project, the minoritised status of Malay therefore gave us challenges for both recruiting staff and participants, as well as using external resources for high-fidelity language processing.

Unequal resources and labour demands

In the quantification of speech complexity in our sample of multilingual child-directed speech, our solution was a principled approach with iterative protocol design (Styles, 2020) for both transcription and morphemising. In our labour breakdowns, we show that for languages with larger numbers of speakers, we were able to distribute tasks and decision making efficiently among multiple team members, whereas for languages with smaller numbers of speakers, we devoted disproportionately more time to transcription (Malay) and morphemising (Tamil), in order to match the same data quality standards. In our descriptions of external tools and resources, we also show that for the larger languages (English, Mandarin Chinese) our decision making and automated processes could leverage external resources (e.g., dictionaries, morphemisers), while for the smaller/minoritised languages no such resources were available, meaning we had to create detailed protocols, dictionaries and datasets. This additional labour included running a separate study on spelling preferences for Malay, manually morphemising spoken Tamil and compiling a lexicon for Red-Dot words. Despite the fact that these languages contributed smaller proportions of the overall project dataset, the burden of the research work was greater per minute of speech for the minority languages, and harder to distribute among the smaller numbers of research staff.

Given that all the languages present in our corpus are supported by government bilingualism education policies, one might expect all languages to be supported equally by external resources such as dictionaries or reference collections of natural speech. However, that is not the case. There are no standardised dictionaries or official references for the Singapore varieties of any of the national languages. Our team looked into external resources and found that these external resources were more prevalent and easier to adapt for the larger global languages like English and Mandarin Chinese.

Can automation replace labour?

We have shown the systematic and iterative approach we took for both our transcription and morphemising protocols so that we could establish high fidelity transcriptions and metrics across our multilingual dataset. Resource costs are necessarily high for creating datasets with this kind of rich hand-curated approach to language data. This project was fortunate to be conducted in an Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) high income country (World Bank Group, 2026) where funding was possible to support work of this kind. Given the high cost of this kind of research, automation of processes is highly desirable for many researchers, especially those working in under-resourced contexts.

This means that complexity estimates created using automated transcription and morphemising tools will be only as good as the quality of the checking or localisation stage of the project. In recent years, automated speech processors have become increasingly available, developed using deep learning techniques to create text from speech using statistical probability models of language. In the field of transcription, many publicly available speech-to-text tools had been trained on corpora of legal, political and business speech (cf. McCowan et al., 2005), or corpora of closed-captioned content, such as news reports for television broadcast or audio books (cf. Panayotov et al., 2015). These kinds of professional transcripts typically exclude features that are common in child language contexts, such as fillers, repetitions and speech errors. As such, neither the type of speech nor the transcription style aligned well with the research goals of a project dedicated to understanding details about parent-child speech. At the time of project initiation in 2018, fewer automated processors were available, and the ones that emerged over the last few years lacked the desired fidelity for the current project (e.g., challenges with weak language identification in accented or code-switching speech, Chua et al., 2023; Kukk & Alumae, 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022; training bias in World Englishes, Chan et al., 2022; disagreements in parsing among different models, see Choo et al., in press). Indeed, our team have shown elsewhere that even when large speech processing models are fine-tuned on local translanguaged speech, language identification is less accurate for minority languages in the dataset, thereby compounding structural biases in the resulting data (Styles et al., 2023). In the current study, we used a fully manual transcription pipeline resulting in ground truth data for the word counts, and a semi-automated pipeline for the morphemisation process in three of the four languages (manual in the fourth), with high attention to the checking/localisation stage.

Figure 8C shows an alternative fully-automated pathway using pre-trained AI tools. In this pathway, automated tools trained on previously collected ground truth data are used to provide direct estimates of complexity (such as word count), without first producing a transcription. As with many AI tools, after the training process has concluded (usually before deployment of the tool) it is effectively a 'black box', where it is not possible to interrogate how the estimate was produced, what the accuracy of the tool is when used in a given context, and whether the tool has introduced error or amplified biases in the resulting data through general misalignment of the training data to the test data (e.g., tools trained on a typologically isolating language may severely underestimate the complexity of a typologically agglutinating language), or structured data loss (e.g., treating some languages as 'background noise' instead of speech). Published 'error rates' for automated models do not automatically transfer to new contexts, and error rates are unlikely to be disaggregated by individual or group, meaning it is not possible to check whether unequal errors introduce or enhance systemic bias in research data. Note that in this case, there is no opportunity to perform checking or localisation, unless the research team are actively involved in 'fine-tuning' existing tools to better align with their dataset. In the field of child language research, automated processors such as the LENA daylong recorder provide some automated estimates of speech complexity from the child's environment, for example, child vocalisation counts and adult word counts (LENA Foundation, 2015). However, these metrics are computed using a non-transparent, proprietary algorithm, trained on a wholly monolingual English-language dataset collected in the US. While some studies have shown that automated LENA word count estimates in other languages correlate with counts based on full transcripts (e.g., Gilkerson et al., 2015 on Mandarin Chinese), others have shown that the automated counts largely handle group-level norms but not individual differences (Cristia et al., 2021). Furthermore, none of the existing automated language count technologies have been trained or tested on multilingual data with notable translanguaging, meaning that further discrepancies may well be encountered. In theory, similar automated processing tools could be developed to create morpheme counts without reference to a transcript, but any such tool would be subject to the same lack of transparency and high risk of misalignment.

A final option that blends human data curation with automated processing is a cumulative subsampling approach, where human coders provide ground truth data on small snippets of data, and estimates are produced iteratively as more snippets are added to the sample, eventually converging on a stable estimate (Casillas et al., 2017). This approach is well suited to large datasets, such as daylong recordings, where the labour cost of manual annotation likely surpasses research funding even in well-resourced contexts.

Clearly, different research teams will need to consider their research goals when considering ways in which automated processes can be incorporated into their research. As algorithmic bias is an underappreciated problem in technology development (Birhane & Guest, 2021; Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; Criado Perez, 2020; Noble, 2018), research teams will also need to think critically about how they will account for errors or biases produced by automation in their resulting work. In low resource contexts, automation can be a powerful way to reduce the burdens of research, however, in many such contexts (such as undocumented or minoritised languages), the tools are simply not fit for purpose. Table 4 outlines the ways in which different kinds of automation could be useful in the Singapore context, and how they were applied in the project described here. Regardless of use of automated processors, there is still a need to hire research/clinical assistants and train them on study-specific transcription/morphemising protocols that catch uniquely childrelated speech features. The available processors have also not been tested in translanguaging contexts. For languages that are widely spoken, it is easier to hire proficient speakers to be research/clinical assistants and training them on the specific transcription/morphemising protocol, meaning the workload may be shared across many speakers. Where available and suitable, automated processors may be used as a first pass to transcribe/morphemise as much of the text as possible before a human combs through and edits the machine-produced transcription output. For under-represented languages, there are few sufficiently high-fidelity automated processors available, meaning many more manual review hours without the initial technological assistance. Under-represented speech communities also produce smaller numbers of proficient speakers who are available to be hired as research/clinical assistants so each person may have to take on a heavier workload than a speaker of a widely spoken language. Thus, under-represented speakers do disproportionately more amounts of the already-more manual work than larger speech communities. This burden of labour and time at multiple steps of the research/clinical process may mean that multilingual children, especially those from families who speak underrepresented languages, are inequitably represented in the types of norms available and/or may be potentially misdiagnosed in medical/educational settings.

Therefore, we showed how we created a computation protocol that equitably compares across four different language typologies (from isolating to agglutinating), and have shared the theory-driven principles, such that they could be applied in other research and clinical contexts. In addition to the different language typologies, we also showed how we could equitably represent the different languages/combination of languages that families may choose to use in their households. For example, our protocols could cater to families that use only one language at home, mostly one-language with occasional words from other languages, or have highly multilingual translanguaged speech. Each of these types of language combinations would be accurately represented and no one type of languaging is disadvantaged. We provide the full transcription protocol and morphemizing protocol as open-access materials. Elsewhere we have proposed a decision tree (https://doi.org/10.21979/N9/NPJQAS) that offers research/clinicians a systematic check on what kind of metric may best suit their data. These resources may serve as a useful starting point for approaching similar measurement challenges (e.g., turn-taking, partial repetitions, etc.) in other linguistically and typologically diverse and/or underrepresented communities.

In conclusion, our paper highlights the importance of taking a bottom-up approach to developmental language science, where developmental measures and metrics must be based on ground truth language data that can only be created with reference to the linguistic expertise of the local communities in the population of study. This means that 'standard' language tools may not apply to specific linguistic contexts, and different approaches may be needed for multilingual contexts where translanguaging is common. In our demonstration of working with multilingual data in four typologically diverse languages, we have also shown that underrepresented languages require disproportionately more manual labour as there are fewer linguistic resources to draw on (e.g., dictionaries), and that out-of-the-box automation is limited by the lack of tools trained on nuanced, linguistically informed ground-truth data from under-resourced languages, and underrepresented contexts. Put plainly, working with minoritised languages costs more money, requires human labour that cannot be replaced by out-of-the-box automation, meaning that progress in this field requires more resources for underrepresented contexts.

The four overriding principles are repeated here, as well as in the main paper. Table S2 acted as a reference and guide for application of the rules.

(1) Principle of non-analysability: a word should not be broken into smaller constituent morphemes if a child would not be able to identify the constituent parts (e.g., men = [man] + [plural], but the internal vowel change is non-obvious to a child). (2) Principle of non-productivity: a word should not be broken into smaller constituent morphemes if those morphemes are not used in active word building processes (e.g., in a child's understanding, the -let in bracelet would be unlikely to produce necklet as a synonym for necklace). (3) Principle of non-semantic transparency: a word should not be broken into smaller morphemes if the word is not meaningfully composed of smaller constituent parts (e.g., red cannot be broken into smaller meaningful parts; a compound word such as rainbow does not obviously mean the combination of the rain and bow according to a child's understanding of the two words).

(4) Principle of cross-linguistic equivalence: the logic of these three principles should apply across languages, even though they may not apply equally to individual translation equivalents. Note that this means that morpheme counts will be higher or lower for expressions with similar meanings in different languages. This is a natural feature of multilingualism and forms part of the parent's linguistic repertoire that they may deploy flexibly when translanguaging with their child.

Additional considerations

Set-Phrases / Lexicalised expressions. Some expressions required more consideration: a lot/a bit/a while vs. alot/abit/awhile. These are grammatical expressions which are more frequently occurring in everyday life than the other compound words, and frequent words are subject to variation at a much higher rate than infrequent words (cf. Bybee, 2002; Pierrehumbert, 2003). The lexicalized versions such as 'alot' are also frequently used in text messaging, the ubiquity of which has an influence on other forms of writing. As a result, allowed transcribers to use their interpretation as to whether they perceived these words as being said with no temporal pause between syllables (as in 'alot') or with a temporal pause between syllables (as in 'alot'). Instances of these were not very prevalent within these recordings hence any effect of a judgement of 1 or 2 morphemes for 'alot' vs. 'a lot' (and related words) was likely negligible. [NB: For expressions that may be spelled with an optional space in Singapore English (e.g., the examples listed above: alot vs. a lot, abit vs. a bit, awhile vs. a while), the space in the original transcript is used as a delimiter.]

Ellipses. The use of ellipses in BELA was to indicate 1) interrupted speech, i.e., partial words (e.g., the ele...) and 2) complete words that trailed off (e.g., the elephant...) or 3) an interruption that was later resolved in the next utterance. In the literature, partial words were usually excluded from the MLU computation (and would be scored as 0; e.g., Miller, 1981) whereas whole words were included (and scored as 1). In terms of automatic language processing, there was no available way to automatise the checking of the ellipses to see which condition it fell under. Thus, we made two counts: scoring all elided words as 0 or 1 to examine whether it would make a difference in MLU computation. There was a significant difference in the median of utterances when all elided words were considered 1 (i.e., all considered whole words even if they were actually partial words). In the interest of not excluding parents who often trailed words off in their speech, we factored into our model to count all elided words as 1.

Supplementary Material B: Protocols for processing data

Data cleaning. Following transcription, data pre-processing for metrics such as word-and morpheme-counts were developed by manually pre-processing a small number of files to establish a data-driven approach to processing, based on ground truth data and linguistic theory.

First, we selected 2% of the recordings (8 of 415) to be checked and segmented manually into words and morphemes (by the first author) and by extant automated morpheme processors (by the third author). The two authors did this independently of the other before coming together in the first discussion to determine a set of pre-processing rules. For a full list of automated morphemisers, see Table S1.

At the end of this first discussion, the following rules were set as ground truths: 1.

Include <Language>, Red-Dot and Languageless (interjections) words 2. Exclude Vocal Sounds (e.g., coughing) and non-Vocal Sounds (e.g., clapping) 3. Exclude utterances containing only non-/Vocal Sounds 4. Exclude 'administrative' utterances containing only language relevant to a page turn that do not address or include the child. Utterances containing 'next', 'next page' and related phrases embedded in them proved to be tricky, as it was sometimes unclear whether it was a directive to the researcher (e.g., next page please) or it was one but it was said as a sentence to the child (e.g., okay next please let's find out ).

Hence, we operationalised this as follows: a) Exclude any utterance containing only 'next' or 'next page' b) Include directives to child to say 'next page', e.g., can you say next please?, let's say next, next please BABYNAME said, let's go to the next page then, okay it's going to change next page okay change c) Include questions to child about story flow, e.g., what happens next?, want to see what's on the next page?, okay next please let's find out, okay let's find out next please, next page what did he... uh next page what did he do? d) Include utterances using 'next' for location and time, e.g., next time you can tell your teacher, what's next to the duck?, monkey go next okay e) Exclude clear administrative sentences, e.g., next (page/one/picture/slide) (please), turn (page) (please), (so/yes) (okay/alright/right) (then) next f) Check the addressee using prosody in mixed content with administrative sentences and keep content words, e.g., nice okay next, happy alright next, happy yes okay next page, got lightning again so next please

Thus, we followed a sequential order of pre-processing the recordings so that only text relevant to the narrative task was analysed for counts of words and morphemes.

Iterative morphemising protocol

During the development of theory-driven principles, manual coders were tasked with checking the outputs of automated morphemisers, and creating master-lists of textual exceptions, to be integrated into subsequent morphemising passes, which were again checked for consistency, until the automated morphemisers agreed with human coders' judgement. The procedure was straightforward for Mandarin Chinese and Malay, took a few rounds of iterative refinement for English, and was not possible for Tamil (due to inability of the automated morphemisers to handle the informal transcriptions of the local variety), resulting in fully manual morphemising for Tamil language text in the corpus.
