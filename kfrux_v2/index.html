Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Does the timing of artificial intelligence (AI) integration into creative work affect how creators are judged? While analysis of more than 70,000 creative requests to ChatGPT found that creators often integrate AI early, four main and eight supplemental experiments (N = 5,272) revealed that creators are perceived as less creative when they integrate AI recommendations early rather than late in their creative work. This occurs partly because creators are perceived as less effortful and less authentic when they integrate AI recommendations early. This penalty generalizes across creative domains, persists even when the total AI contribution is held constant, and holds regardless of the creator's expertise.

Communicating that the creator modified rather than directly implemented AI recommendations attenuates the penalty. Moving beyond existing research comparing judgments of whether creators use AI versus do not, our research demonstrates that when and how creators integrate AI into the creative process also shape judgments.

[div-01]

Imagine Jordan and Alex presenting advertising campaigns created using artificial intelligence (AI) tools. Jordan used AI early in the creative process, while Alex used AI late in the process. If both creators contributed an equal amount of creative input, would they be judged differently simply because they integrated AI early or late into their creative process?

This is important to consider because creative work often requires creators to bring in other individuals and tools to advance ideas toward implementation (Boussioux et al., 2024; Mahaney et al., 2025). AI is increasingly embedded in many creative works (Raisch & Fomina, 2025). Creators can use it to structure problems, ideate at scale, compare alternatives, and refine concepts (Boussioux et al. 2024; De Freitas et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024). In fact, a European Commission survey (N = 5,924) found that over one-third of EU respondents use generative AI tools for creative work (ESS ERIC, 2025). Since creators can substantially benefit from using AI, resisting their use would forgo productivity, efficiency, and output gains (Arora et al., 2025).

Yet creators face punitive social judgments when AI usage becomes visible (Benegal et al., 2026; Chamberlain et al., 2018; Reif et al., 2025). People favor AI-produced products that hold lower symbolic value (Granulo et al., 2021), and managers update trust and hiring decisions based on AI disclosure (Schilke & Reimann, 2025). Research on the timing of AI use has primarily focused on what creators produce, finding that independent ideation before AI integration boosts creators' originality and confidence (Qin et al., 2025). However, how people judge creators based on when AI entered their work remains unclear. Because people seldom observe creative processes directly and may instead infer the creator's contribution from observable cues, when AI enters the creative process can be such a cue that leads to biased evaluations.

throughout a creator's workflow and can even 'loop' recursively due to external feedback or other creator-initiated reasons (Akbar et al., 2018; Harrison & Nurmohamed, 2023; Perry-Smith & Mannucci, 2017). Accordingly, neither the early nor the late parts of the creative workflow should be treated as proxies for distinct creative modes or explicit differences in the type of creative work performed.

Because the creative process can change iteratively during a creator's workflow, the temporal dimension becomes a relevant manipulation (Tolkamp et al., 2025). Creators can view timing as a strategic choice of when to integrate AI tools in order to augment creative work (Bouschery et al., 2023; Boussioux et al., 2024; Chen & Chan, 2024). Indeed, AI can help with creative tasks at any point in the workflow, including functions that can change iteratively throughout the creative process (Huang et al., 2026). However, it is unclear whether people perceive creators differently based on when they integrate AI into their creative process.

Timing of AI Integration

The decision of when to integrate AI tools can be understood through the lens of entrainment, where the pacing and rhythm of processes are influenced by temporally structured events (Ancona et al., 2001; Omidvar et al., 2025). Actors therein interpret temporal cues and respond with actions, such as synchronizing with external constraints or deciding when to integrate help to deliver a creative product (Fisher et al., 2018). Within these temporally bound cycles, creators can decide to integrate AI tools, such as during the early or late phases of their creative work.

Importantly, then, choosing when to integrate AI reflects the creator's strategic choice rather than necessarily a creative deficiency (Zhou & Lee, 2024). Successful creative outcomes rely on both the creator's skill and AI's capability (Huang, 2025). Although the consequences of AI tool integration may vary across creative tasks and workflows, it is unclear whether evaluators respond differently depending on whether it enters early or late in the creative process. Nevertheless, people and decision-makers rarely observe creative processes directly. They infer from observable cues (Ambady & Rosenthal, 1992; Gilbert & Malone, 1995), such as when AI entered the workflow, without considering why the creator did so. Consequently, the timing of AI integration can cue assessments of the creator's creativity, regardless of their actual intent.

Perceived Creativity Based on AI Integration Timing

The directional effect of the timing of AI integration on perceived creativity, whether early or late AI integration is penalized more, is not obvious. Early AI integration might be perceived as strategic and efficient, reserving human cognition for high-value judgments later in the work (Jia et al., 2024), while late AI integration might signal underutilization of AI's potential. Further, research on the 'late action effect' shows that actions closer to outcomes are seen as more causally influential because of heightened counterfactual thinking and reversibility (Ziano & Pandelaere, 2022). Relatedly, receiving help late in a task undermines well-being as compared to early help (Koo et al., 2023). Peak-end effects research also shows that people disproportionately judge the overall agency, authorship, and impact of a sequence based on the endpoints rather than the initial efforts (Alaybek et al., 2022). Therefore, AI integration at the end of a creative process may increase both evaluator scrutiny of the creator's independent role and doubts about human creative contribution, leading to less favorable reactions than AI integration early in the process.

Although an increased penalty for late AI integration is plausible, we propose that early AI integration reduces perceived creativity more than late integration does. That is, while integrating AI in creative work generally faces a penalty in the evaluator's perceptions (Benegal et al., 2026; Reif et al., 2025; Schilke & Reimann, 2025), we suggest the penalty is higher when a creator integrates AI early rather than late in their creative work. Because people tend to attribute creativity and original thinking more to humans than to AI tools (Horton Jr et al., 2023), AI's position in the temporal sequence of work might shape evaluators' judgments. When AI is integrated early, evaluators may infer that the creator relied on algorithmic assistance to accomplish the part of the work that typically signals originality (Epstein et al., 2023; Friedman, 2008). In contrast, integrating AI later can preserve the creator's originality because the AI assists with work the creator has already developed. As a result, evaluators may be less likely to discount the creator's creativity.

Therefore, we hypothesize: H1: Creators are perceived as less creative when they integrate AI recommendations early rather than late in their creative work.

We do not assume that timing changes who de facto owns the creative work, who is responsible for the final product, or the total contribution. Instead, we examine whether similar general assistance from an AI tool receives different evaluations depending on whether it enters the creative workflow earlier or later.

Perceived Effort and Authenticity as Parallel Mechanisms

We further suggest that early AI integration reduces perceived creativity more than late integration does because it also diminishes perceived effort and authenticity to a greater extent. We theorize that perceived effort and authenticity operate in parallel.

Perceived Effort

We propose that the early AI integration penalty in creativity judgments partially stems from creators being perceived as exerting less effort when using AI earlier than later in their creative process. Effort has traditionally been a crucial metric for assessing individuals, which prompts people to publicly signal effort (Inzlicht et al., 2018). People often use effort as a heuristic in judgments of quality and value (Kruger et al., 2004; Norton et al., 2012).

Generally, using AI might not be seen as effortful because AI assistance in work is generally seen as effort-saving (Bechwati & Xia, 2003), reducing time on tasks (Fieldhouse, 2025), which can increase concerns that creators might become overly reliant on AI tools (Lira et al., 2025). Taking AI assistance also creates ambiguity regarding how much credit belongs to the creator versus the help they received (Kay et al., 2018).

Consequently, creators may be expected to invest more time and effort early rather than late in their process. Early AI integration may signal that the creator is taking a shortcut in the phase where effort is presumed to matter most because it was used before creator involvement was substantially observable. In contrast, late AI integration gives evaluators more opportunities to infer that a creator engaged substantially in the creative task before receiving assistance from an AI tool. Indeed, individuals using AI are perceived as lazier and less capable than those who use non-AI technologies (Reif et al., 2025). The inference of effort here is precisely regarding the amount of creator involvement that is visible before any external tools are integrated.

Since effort has traditionally been the standard for assessing output (Buell & Norton, 2011; Fuchs et al., 2015; Garcia-Rada et al., 2022; Kruger et al., 2004), and evaluators value work that carries visible human labor (Inzlicht et al., 2018; Judge et al., 2020), reduced perceived effort in early versus late AI integration may result in diminished creativity judgments. Therefore, we hypothesize that: H2: Perceived effort mediates the effect of AI integration timing on perceived creativity.

Perceived Authenticity

We further propose that the early AI integration penalty also stems from reduced perceptions of authenticity: Creators who integrate AI early in the process are seen as less authentic than those who use it later, and reduced authenticity reduces perceptions of creativity.

Authenticity involves expressing ideas originating from oneself and acting consistently with one's unique identity (Amabile & Pratt, 2016; Goncalo & Katz, 2020).

Authenticity assessment relies on how well the outcome truly reflects the creator's vision, and technological interventions that might be seen as corrupting (Moulard et al., 2014; Sun & Pham, 2025). Conversely, taking credit for creative work that is not one's own reduces perceived authenticity (Ellis, 2022), presumably because the greater the creator's creative control over their work, the more authentic they are perceived to be (Valsesia et al., 2016).

Generally, using AI tools in creative work may raise authenticity concerns. For instance, AI-designed fashion products and marketing communications are seen as less authentic than human-created ones (Kirk & Givi, 2025; Lee & Kim, 2024). In addition, disclosing that creative writing was generated or assisted by AI tools is systematically perceived as less authentic, real, and meaningful (Raj et al., 2026).

Building on this logic, we theorize that the timing of AI integration can serve as a salient cue for evaluating perceived authenticity. Early integration of AI may signal that the creator failed to exert creative control, and the resulting outcome reflects AI outputs more than the creator's authentic voice. Conversely, in late AI integration, the creator maintains more creative control over the process, and the outcome may align more closely with their vision, thereby preserving the perceived authenticity more effectively.

Since authenticity is emblematic of genuine individual perspectives and personal essence (Cheng et al., 2008) it prompts the impression of originality, which is a marker of creative merit (Nunes et al., 2021). Early integration of AI tools may therefore lead evaluators to view the creator's engagement with the work as less personal expression. On the other hand, late AI integration may make the creator's prior engagement and expression more visible, supporting stronger inferences that the creator was genuinely involved. Therefore, we hypothesize that: H3: Perceived authenticity mediates the effect of AI integration timing on perceived creativity.

Overview of Studies

Field data from more than 70,000 creative requests to ChatGPT suggest that most creators use AI earlier, rather than later, in the creative work. Yet across four main and eight supplementary experiments (all preregistered; total N = 5,272; including U.S. American, British, and French participants), this research reveals a robust temporal asymmetry in how creators' creativity in AI-assisted work is evaluated. Study 1 demonstrates that creators who integrate AI late are perceived as more creative than those who integrate early. Study 2A reveals that decreased perceived effort and authenticity mediate the negative effect of early AI integration on creativity judgments. Study 2B finds that emphasizing that the creator elaborated on AI suggestions rather than directly implementing them significantly attenuated the early AI integration penalty. Finally, Study 3 revealed that managers allocate higher bonuses and are more likely to implement and recommend outcomes from late AI integrators.

In supplementary studies, we rule out expertise of the creator as a boundary condition for the early AI integration penalty (Study S1), find that the penalty is not reduced when AI is framed as trained on the creator's own style (Study S2) and that it is stronger for hand-sculpted than for 3D-printed work (Study S3), generalize the effect across six professional contexts (Study S4A) and to a French sample (Study S4B), replicate it in a different artistic context with a marketplace consequence (Study S4C), replicate the moderating role of usage beliefs (Study S5), and extend the effect from AI to human assistance (Study S6). We operationalized AI integration timing in two ways. In Studies 1, 2A, 2B, and 3 (and Studies S1, S4A, S4C, and S5), we specified whether the AI recommendations supported activities early (e.g., generating initial sketches) or late (e.g., finalizing design layouts) in the creative process. Because these descriptions vary in both the AI's contribution and the timing of its use, Studies S2, S3, S4B, and S6 provide a conservative test. In these studies, the AI recommendations were used only during the first 25% or the last 25% of the task, and no activities were described. Timing was therefore the only feature that differed between the early-AI and late-AI conditions, and the penalty held in these studies as well (meta-analytic g = -0.40; see Supplementary Material). Field Evidence: When Do Creators Integrate AI?

Before examining how evaluators judge the timing of AI integration, we examined when creators actually integrate AI into their creative work. We analyzed WildChat-4.8M (W. Zhao et al., 2024), a public corpus of ChatGPT conversations that users consented to share, collected between April 2023 and July 2025 across models from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4o and o1.

Method

Of 1,658,803 English conversations, we identified creative requests from keywords in the opening message (full keyword lists in the Supplementary Material). A broad filter identified 801,629 conversations that opened with a creative request (e.g., a story, poem, song, slogan, design, or marketing content). Within these, we flagged two overlapping subsets: requests in the creative domains that informed our experimental scenarios (fashion, music, advertising and marketing, product development, research proposals, public communication, training, and sculpture and art), and requests in professional contexts with organizational stakes (advertising, marketing, branding, product development, innovation, communication, training, and research proposals). We then coded each request's timing as "early-AI" if it asked the AI to produce the work (e.g., "write a," "create a," "come up with") and as "late-AI" if it asked the AI to work on the creator's existing draft (e.g., "improve," "proofread," "here is my draft"). Requests matching neither or both patterns were left uncoded. Because some prompts were submitted thousands of times, often by automated bots, we counted each distinct request once and estimated 95% confidence intervals by resampling users.

Results

Of 70,451 coded requests from 31,233 users, 85.9% (95% CI [84.6, 87.4]) integrated AI early, roughly six for every request that brought AI in late (see Figure 2). Early-AI requests also predominated in the creative domains that informed our experimental scenarios later (84.5%, 95% CI [83.2, 85.8]) and in professional contexts (71.5%, 95% CI [69.8, 73.2]).

The early AI integration pattern held for one request per user (85.6% early AI); when requests matching both keyword lists were included in the analysis, regardless of whether all were counted as late-AI (78.0%) or early-AI (87.2%). The pattern also held across all ChatGPT models (72.5% to 89.2%), in each year from 2023 to 2025, among requests of similar length (84.7%), and in a multilevel model controlling for user, country, message length, model, and year (87.7%; Supplementary Material). Few early-AI conversations asked the AI to revise its output (1.1%).

Discussion

In everyday use, creators tend to integrate AI early in their creative work. This data analysis has limits: an opening request marks the start of an exchange with AI, not necessarily the start of the creative process. Overall, it indicates that early AI integration is common. The following experiments examine how evaluators judge it.

Study 1: Early Versus Late Integration of AI

This preregistered study (https://aspredicted.org/8mhs-r2fh.pdf) tests our primary hypothesis that creators are perceived as less creative when they integrate AI early rather than late in the creative process. We also included a control condition with no timing information to determine whether the evaluator's default assumption is closer to early or late AI integration.

Method

Sample and Design

After applying the pre-registered exclusion criteria (240 recruited, 0 excluded), the sample included complete responses from 240 U.S. American participants recruited through the Connect Cloud Research platform (120 males, 119 females, 1 preferred not to disclose gender; Mage = 40.91, SD = 9.55). The study used a between-subjects design with three conditions: early-AI, late-AI, and control (no timing information). This sample size allows us to detect effect sizes of Cohen's d = 0.45 and larger with 80% power and 5% alpha (twotailed) for pairwise comparisons.

Procedure

Participants in the "early-AI" condition read the following scenario: "Alex Chen is a fashion designer at a premium clothing brand. He has extensive experience designing clothing lines. While developing a new line of designer shirts, Alex decided to integrate recommendations from an AI-powered tool at the beginning of the fashion development process. Alex used the AI's recommendations to generate initial sketches, propose different color palettes, and suggest different fabric patterns." Participants in the "late-AI" condition read the following scenario: "Alex Chen is a fashion designer at a premium clothing brand. He has extensive experience designing clothing lines. While developing a new line of designer shirts, Alex decided to integrate recommendations from an AI-powered tool at the end of the fashion development process. Alex used the AI's recommendations to finalize the design layouts, refine the color effects, and optimize the fabric specifications."

In the "no timing" condition, participants read that Alex integrated recommendations from an AI-powered tool into the fashion development process, but the text provided no timing information or details about the nature of the AI's use. Next, participants rated Alex's creativity ("How would you rate the level of creativity in Alex's approach to fashion designing?") on a 7-point scale anchored at 1 (not at all creative) and 7 (very creative).

Consistent with prior creativity literature, we measure creative perceptions of an individual within a given creative process (Amabile, 1983). In this paper, we use "early" and "in the beginning" interchangeably, as well as "late" and "in the end." Except when describing the experimental scenarios, we use "early" and "late" for parsimony.

Discussion

These results support our primary hypothesis that evaluators perceive creators who integrate AI early in the creative process, rather than late, as less creative. Importantly, if early AI integration is common in real-world creator workflows, this finding suggests that most creative AI use falls into the category evaluators penalize most. Having established this basic effect, we next examine why it occurs.

Study 2A: Perceived Effort and Authenticity Explanations

This preregistered study (https://aspredicted.org/8xwg-7ph3.pdf) examines whether the effect of AI integration timing on perceived creativity can be explained by the perceptions of effort the creator exerts and the creator's perceived authenticity. We predict that integrating AI early rather than late in the creative process leads to lower perceived effort and authenticity, which, in turn, is associated with lower creativity judgments.

Method

Sample and Design

After applying the pre-registered exclusion criteria (300 recruited, 0 excluded), the sample included complete responses from 300 U.S. American participants recruited through the Connect Cloud Research platform (148 males, 150 females, 2 preferred not to disclose gender; Mage = 40.55, SD = 9.10). This study had a between-subjects design with two conditions: early-AI or late-AI. This sample size allows us to detect effect sizes of Cohen's d = 0.32 or larger with 80% power and 5% alpha (two-tailed) in independent-samples t-tests.

Procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the between-subjects conditions.

Participants in the "early-AI" condition read the following scenario: "Jordan Parker is a hip-hop producer in the recording industry. He has extensive experience producing songs in a broad range of musical styles. While working on a new single for an album, Jordan decided to integrate recommendations from an AIpowered music tool at the beginning of the music production process. He utilized the AI's recommendations to help him generate ideas for a novel rhythm and produce a unique beat for the song." Participants in the "late-AI" condition read the following scenario: "Jordan Parker is a hip-hop producer in the recording industry. He has extensive experience producing songs in a broad range of musical styles. While working on a new single for an album, Jordan decided to integrate recommendations from an AIpowered music tool at the end of the music production process. He utilized the AI's recommendations to help him finalize the song, polish the rhythm, and tighten up the beat."

Following this, participants rated Jordan's creativity ("How would you rate the level of creativity in Jordan's approach to music production?") on a 7-point scale anchored at 1 (not at all creative) and 7 (very creative). To measure perceived effort, participants responded to "How much effort do you think Jordan has put into producing the song?" on a 7-point scale from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much). To measure perceived authenticity, participants completed two items: "How authentic do you think Jordan's approach was in designing the song?" and "To what extent do you think Jordan was genuine in his work process", both on 7point scales from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much so). These items were averaged to create an authenticity index (Cronbach's α = .967).

Results and Discussion

Perceived Creativity

Replicating previous findings, participants perceived the creator as less creative in the early-AI condition (M = 3.56, SD = 1.62) than in the late-AI condition (M = 4.68, SD = 1.48), t(298) = -6.20, p < .001, d = -0.72, 95% CI [-0.95, -0.48].

Perceived Effort

Participants perceived the creator as less effortful in the early-AI condition (M = 3.88, SD = 1.61) than in the late-AI condition (M = 4.98, SD = 1.45), t(298) = -6.21, p < .001, d = -0.72, 95% CI [-0.95, -0.48].

Perceived Authenticity

Participants perceived the creator as less authentic in the early-AI condition (M = 3.60, SD = 1.67) than in the late-AI condition (M = 4.72, SD = 1.56, t(298) = -5.95, p < .001, d = -0.69, 95% CI [-0.92, -0.45].

Mediation Analysis

A mediation analysis (Model 4 of the PROCESS macro with 10,000 bootstraps; Hayes, 2013) with timing of AI integration (0 = late-AI,1 = early-AI) as the independent variable, perceived effort and authenticity as the parallel mediators, and perceived creativity as dependent variable, revealed a significant indirect effect through perceived effort (ab = -0.42, SE = 0.09, 95% CI [-0.65, -0.25]), and perceived authenticity (ab = -0.56, SE = 0.11, 95% CI [-0.82, -0.35]). Discussion

This study uncovers two key explanations of the effect of AI integration timing on creativity judgment: perceived effort and authenticity. When creators integrate AI early in their work rather than late, evaluators perceive them as both less effortful and less authentic, leading to lower creativity ratings. Next, we examine whether our proposed mechanism, through perceived effort and authenticity, has process-consistent evidence.

This study revealed that informing evaluators that the creator elaborated on AI recommendations attenuated the early integration of AI penalties related to perceived creativity, effort, and authenticity. This indicates that when evaluators learn that creators elaborated on AI suggestions as part of their creative process, it diminishes the timing effect by restoring perceptions of effort and authenticity. We next examine whether the penalty carries over to managers' consequential decisions.

Study 3: Professional Estimates and Consequences

Moving beyond evaluators' reactions, this preregistered study (https://aspredicted.org/2qjp-9vsk.pdf) investigates whether professional managers differ in decisions about bonus allocation, project execution, and recommendations to higher management for employees who integrate AI early versus late in their workflows.

Specifically, we predict that early AI integration would be perceived as less creative and elicit less favorable managerial reactions than late AI integration.

Method

Sample and Design

After applying the pre-registered exclusion criteria (301 recruited, 0 excluded), the sample included complete responses from 301 U.S. American managers recruited through the Connect Cloud Research platform's panel of verified managers (148 males, 152 females, 1 preferred not to disclose gender; Mage = 39.86, SD = 9.08). The sample included 32% in senior management roles, 28% as assistants or associates, 17% as C-suite executives or directors, 16% as analysts, and 7% as consultants across fields such as marketing, product management, and customer experience. The study used a between-subjects design with two conditions: early-AI or late-AI. This sample size allows us to detect effect sizes of Cohen's d = 0.32 and larger with 80% power and 5% alpha (two-tailed) for pairwise comparisons.

Procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two between-subjects conditions.

Participants were asked to imagine themselves as a senior marketing manager at an audio technology firm. Next, they learned that their company had partnered with an advertising agency tasked with delivering a campaign for its new line of headphones. As managers, they are responsible for managing the campaign's development and helping guide strategic decisions for this advertising work. Following this, participants were presented with a scenario describing Sarah Martinez, the advertising agency's creative director. In the early-AI condition, participants read the following scenario: "Sarah Martinez is the creative director at the advertising agency. She has extensive experience developing advertising campaigns. While developing the campaign, Sarah decided to integrate recommendations from an AI-powered tool at the beginning of the advertising campaign development process. She utilized the AI's recommendations to help her generate campaign message concepts, illustrate mood boards, and uncover customer insights."

Participants in the late-AI condition read the following scenario: "Sarah Martinez is the creative director at the advertising agency. She has extensive experience developing advertising campaigns. While developing a new campaign for an upcoming product, she uses AI-powered design tools at the end of the advertising campaign development process. She utilized the AI's recommendations to help her polish the campaign messaging, finalize advertising layout, and optimize audience targeting parameters." Next, to measure perceived creativity and effort, participants provided their opinions on the questions "As a marketing manager responsible for the advertising campaign, how would you rate the level of creativity in Sarah's approach to advertising campaign development?" and "How much effort do you think Sarah has put into designing this advertising campaign?" on a scale of 1(not at all creative/not at all) to 7(very creative/very much). Following this, participants made three responses intended to mimic typical workplace decisions: (1) willingness to implement the campaign without changes by responding to the question "As a marketing manager responsible for the advertising campaign, how willing would you be to implement Sarah's advertising campaign without any further changes?" on a scale of 1(not at all) to 7(very much), (2) likelihood of recommending the campaign to senior leadership by responding to the question "As a marketing manager responsible for the advertising campaign, how likely are you to recommend that your company's senior leadership implement Sarah's advertising campaign without any changes?" on a scale of 1(not at all likely) to 7(very likely), and (3) bonus allocation. For the bonus allocation measure, participants were informed that their company allocated a discretionary bonus pool ($12,500) based on creative work, which amounts to 5% of the total campaign budget of $250,000. Next, participants reported, in an open-ended response, the amount in USD they would be willing to allocate to Sarah based on their assessment of Sarah's creative work.

Results and Discussion

Perceived Creativity

Managers perceived the creator as less creative in the early-AI condition (M = 3.89, SD = 1.67) than in the late-AI condition (M = 4.98, SD = 1.29), t(283.74) = -6.35, p < .001, d = -0.73, 95% CI [-0.96, -0.50].

Perceived Effort

Managers perceived the creator as less effortful in the early-AI condition (M = 4.03, SD = 1.64) than in the late-AI condition (M = 5.43, SD = 1.19), t(275.51) = -8.48, p < .001, d = -0.98, 95% CI [-1.21, -0.74].

Willingness to Implement

Managers were less willing to implement the campaign in the early-AI condition (M = 3.80, SD = 1.66) than in the late-AI condition (M = 4.88, SD = 1.46), t(299) = -5.97, p < .001, d = -0.69, 95% CI [-0.92, -0.45].

Likelihood of Recommendation

Managers were less likely to recommend the campaign to senior management in the early-AI condition (M = 3.61, SD = 1.77) than in the late-AI condition (M = 4.68, SD = 1.53), t(294.49) = -5.60, p < .001, d = -0.64, 95% CI [-0.88, -0.41].

Bonus Allocation

Managers allocated a lower performance bonus (log-transformed) in the early-AI condition (M = 5.68, SD = 3.59) than in the late-AI condition (M = 7.46, SD = 2.69), t(280.16) = -4.87, p < .001, d = -0.56, 95% CI [-0.79, -0.33]. Discussion

This study revealed that professional managers not only perceive creators who integrate AI early as less creative than those who integrate it late, but also translate these perceptions into tangible penalties: lower bonuses, a lower likelihood of project implementation, and a lower likelihood of project recommendations to upper management. These suggest that disclosure of when AI is used could significantly influence career outcomes in creative industries.

General Discussion

Across 12 preregistered experiments (total N = 5,272), we demonstrate a robust temporal asymmetry: creators are perceived as less creative when they integrate AI early rather than late in their creative process (meta-analytic effect size, g = -0.61, 95% CI [-0.73, -0.49]). This early AI integration penalty generalized across several creative domains (Studies 1, 2A, 2B, S4A, and S4C), held when AI assistance was confined to an equal share of the work (Studies S2, S3, S4B, and S6), and did not depend on the creator's expertise (Study S1) or on whether the AI was trained on the creator's own data (Study S2). Mediation evidence suggested that parallel reductions in perceived effort and perceived authenticity partly explained it (Studies 2A and 2B). The penalty was attenuated when evaluators learned that the creator elaborated on, rather than directly implemented, the AI recommendations (Studies 2B and S5). Contrary to our prediction, the early-AI integration penalty was attenuated when the work was 3D-printed rather than hand-sculpted (Study S3). Finally, people were willing to pay less for products made with early AI integration (Study 2B), and managers gave early AI integrators smaller bonuses and were less willing to implement or recommend their work (Study 3).

Sample and Design

After applying the pre-registered exclusion criteria (400 recruited, 0 excluded), the sample included complete responses from 400 U.S. American participants recruited through the Connect Cloud Research platform (201 males, 196 females, 2 other, 1 preferred not to disclose gender; Mage = 38.19, SD = 9.77). Participants were randomly assigned to one of six conditions in a 3 (usage: control vs. direct implementation vs. elaborate and modify) × 2 (timing: early-AI vs. late-AI) full factorial between-subjects design. This sample size allows us to detect effect sizes of Cohen's f = 0.16 or larger for the interaction between usage and timing with 80% power and a 5% alpha (two-tailed).

Procedure

All participants read about Alex Chen, a fashion designer at a premium clothing brand with extensive experience designing clothing lines and developing a new line of designer shirts. We manipulated the timing of AI integration by stating, "Alex decided to integrate recommendations from an AI-powered tool at the beginning (end) of the fashion development process." This was similar to the manipulations we used in Study 1.

Importantly, to highlight the nature of usage of AI suggestions by the creator, participants in the "direct implementation" condition read, "Alex reviewed the AI's recommendations and directly implemented them, using them exactly as they were suggested by the AI". While participants in the "elaborate and modify" condition read, "Alex reviewed the AI's recommendations and elaborated on them, modifying them from how they were suggested by the AI".

Following this, participants rated perceived creativity, effort, and authenticity (two items: Cronbach's α = .959) using the same measures as in Study 2A. As an exploratory measure, we assessed a downstream consequence by asking participants to report, in an open-ended response, the amount in U.S. dollars they would be willing to pay for a designer shirt made by Alex.

Decomposing the interaction in Tukey-corrected post hoc comparisons, we found that the penalty in perceived effort in the early-AI (vs. late-AI) condition was largest when the creator directly implemented the AI recommendation (Mearly-AI = 2.71, SD = 1.35; Mlate-AI = 4.50, SD = 1.71), t(394) = -6.78, p < .001, d = -1.18, 95% CI [-1.53, -0.83]. Perceived Authenticity

A 3 (usage: control vs. direct implementation vs. elaborate and modify) × 2 (timing: early-AI vs. late-AI) between-subjects ANOVA with perceived authenticity as the dependent variable revealed a significant interaction of the usage and timing conditions, F(2, 394) = 5.37, p = .005, ηp² = .03.

Decomposing the interaction in Tukey-corrected post hoc comparisons, we found that the penalty in perceived authenticity in the early-AI (vs. late-AI) condition was largest when the creator directly implemented the AI recommendation (Mearly-AI = 2.60, SD = 1.47; Mlate-AI = 4.30, SD = 1.71), t(394) = -5.92, p < .001, d = -1.03, 95% CI [-1.38, -0.68]. Theoretical Contributions

This research makes three theoretical contributions. First, we identify the timing of AI integration as a consequential dimension of AI disclosure in creative work. Prior research (Benegal et al., 2026; Kirk & Givi, 2025; Raj et al., 2026; Reif et al., 2025; Schilke & Reimann, 2025) has primarily examined the consequences of AI disclosure on workplace trust, social evaluations, and perceptions of AI-assisted products and outputs. We extend this literature by shifting the unit of analysis from output to creators and from whether AI was used to when it was used. Moreover, withholding timing information appears to offer little protection because evaluators who learned only that AI was used rated the creator closer to the early-AI than to the late-AI creator (Study 1). This "guilty until proven innocent" pattern suggests that creators who remain silent about the timing of AI integration may face a penalty similar to that of creators who disclose early integration. Second, we identify two parallel process inferences: perceived effort and perceived authenticity. The findings extend research on creativity heuristics like visible effort (Buell & Norton, 2011; Kruger et al., 2004) and creative control (Valsesia et al., 2016) by demonstrating that the timing of AI integration shifts both perceptions simultaneously. People tend to see creative work as an expression of the self (Goncalo & Katz, 2020). AI assistance used early therefore may diminish perceptions of authenticity at the very stage where such personal imprint is expected to emerge. The same two pathways emerged when early assistance came from a human colleague rather than AI (Study S6), suggesting that the penalty may reflect lay beliefs about where creativity resides, not just concerns specific to AI.

Third, we contribute to research on human-AI collaboration (Arora et al. 2025; Huang 2025) by identifying a key boundary condition: evaluators respond less negatively to early AI integration when the creator's role is described as elaborating on and modifying the AI recommendations rather than implementing them directly. Prior work has examined how the timing and mode of AI use shape what creators produce (Chen & Chan, 2024; Qin et al., 2025). Our findings suggest that they also shape how creators are judged.

Practical Implications

Our findings also offer several practical implications. First, strategically disclosing when an AI tool is used can immunize creators from credibility erosion. Creators who use AI at the end of the creative process benefit from transparent disclosure, as evaluators reward late integration with higher creativity attributions. When integration occurs early, emphasizing that the creator adapted and modified AI recommendations rather than implementing them directly may attenuate the early-integration penalty (Study 2B). Notably, framing AI as trained on one's own creative style does not provide similar protection (Study S2), suggesting creators should focus disclosure on active modification of AI output.

Second, because field evidence suggests most creators use AI early, organizations should recognize that current evaluation practices may systematically disadvantage employees who openly disclose early AI use. In Study 3, managers gave smaller bonuses, were less willing to implement the campaign, and were less likely to recommend it to senior leadership when the creator disclosed early rather than late AI integration. Consequently, employees may hide beneficial AI usage to avoid evaluation penalties, reducing sharing and organizational learning about effective human-AI workflows. Organizations may reduce this penalty by separating evaluation of the final output and its impact from interpretive assumptions about when AI help was used, and by establishing norms that treat early AI integration as a strategic choice rather than a signal of low creativity.

Third, product marketing can leverage timing cues to preserve perceived value. When accurate, product descriptions and marketing campaigns could describe AI as an "assistant" used late in the process, highlighting the creator's initial effort and authentic vision.

Marketing communications that restore perceptions of effort and authenticity could also mitigate penalties associated with AI disclosure.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of our research offer directions for future research. First, our studies used single-creator scenarios, although many creative endeavors involve teams (Kay et al., 2018). The timing of AI integration and its effects on evaluations of team creativity remain unexplored. While we expect the temporal asymmetry to hold, the effect's intensity may vary by team composition and other factors.

Second, because we compared AI integration at the early and late stages of the creative process, whether integration in the middle, or at several points, is judged more like early or late integration remains open for future research (Acharya et al., 2025).

Third, our samples consisted of participants based in the US, UK, and France. Given the variations in how creativity is conceptualized across cultures (Leung & Morris, 2011), the early integration penalty might vary across cultures. Cultures higher in individualism might penalize early AI integration more severely than cultures higher in collectivism.

Fourth, while we identified effort and authenticity as parallel explanations, other psychological processes likely contribute as well. Perceived autonomy (Breaugh, 1985) may matter, such that early AI integration could signal reduced self-determination over creative direction. Future research should examine other candidate mechanisms, potentially with alternative mediators included simultaneously.

Fifth, our scenarios explicitly describe timing information, which may increase attention to this cue beyond naturalistic settings. Nonetheless, as AI disclosure becomes common through transparency norms, platform watermarking, or regulation, timing information will become more accessible. Future research should examine how effects manifest when timing must be inferred.
