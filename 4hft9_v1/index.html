Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Eating disorders are serious psychological concerns associated with high levels of physical and psychological comorbidity and elevated premature mortality rates. Despite the severity of these disorders, gaps in clinical care exist and most individuals with eating disorders either do not receive or do not benefit from existing evidence-based treatments. There has been a growing movement to incorporate neuroscience-informed targets into eating disorder treatment; however, to date psychoeducation for eating disorders remains narrowly focused on eating-and weight-related behaviors and less directed towards their underlying mechanisms. Yet, neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE) can provide vital insights into diagnostic distinctions, patterns of psychiatric comorbidity, risk and maintenance mechanisms, brain-body relations, prognostic course, and therapeutic targets for eating disorders. Further, knowledge of neuroscience can assist in designing the delivery of psychoeducation with the neurobiology of eating disorders in mind by tailoring content to consider the impact of personality and illness stage on the learning process. In this chapter, we highlight the promise of NIPE for eating disorders, provide a case example of NIPE for eating disorders delivered in a clinical setting, and discuss future directions for research in this area. Ultimately, NIPE can be used to bridge the gap between research knowledge and clinical care in eating disorders, thereby enhancing patient selfefficacy, dispelling misconceptions about eating disorders, and enhancing the accessibility of the science for individuals with these serious, yet undertreated disorders. NIPE for Eating Disorders 2

Neuroscience-informed Psychoeducation for Eating Disorders

Eating disorders are serious psychiatric conditions characterized by persistent disturbances in eating or weight control behaviors that lead to impairments in physical health and/or psychosocial functioning (Klump et al., 2009). The most common eating disorders formally recognized by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) are anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), binge-eating disorder (BED), and avoidantrestrictive food-intake disorder (ARFID; American Psychiatric Association, 2013). However, many disordered eating experiences do not neatly match one of these diagnostic labels, with 20-30% of eating disorder diagnoses being classified within the "other" or "unspecified" eating disorder category (Mancuso et al., 2015). Eating disorders are associated with a variety of serious medical and psychological risks, frequently co-occurring with other psychiatric conditions like mood and anxiety disorders (Ulfvebrand et al., 2015), and carrying some of the highest mortality rates of any mental illness (Chesney et al., 2014). With global lifetime prevalence estimates for eating disorder as high as 26.7%, these disorders impact individuals across genders, ages, and sociocultural backgrounds (Silén & Keski-Rahkonen, 2022). As such, these serious conditions are more common and widespread than frequently acknowledged (Haynos et al., 2024a).

Several evidence-based treatments have been developed to address eating disorders.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and family-based treatment (FBT) for adolescents are the most widely evaluated and disseminated evidence-based treatments for eating disorders (Dalle Grave & Pike, 2023), but other approaches such as interpersonal psychotherapy and dialectical behavior therapy also have empirical support (Grilo, 2024). Psychoeducation (PE) plays a key role in many of these treatments (Raykos & Andony, 2024), with much of the content focused on NIPE for Eating Disorders 3 correcting misconceptions about weight and eating and highlighting the intrapersonal and interpersonal consequences of maladaptive eating disorder behaviors (Murphy et al., 2010). Such PE, delivered to patients or their caregivers, has been shown to be effective in increasing treatment engagement and self-efficacy, thereby, fostering symptom improvement (Balestrieri et al., 2013; Prema & Shanmugamprema, 2026; Vander Steen et al., 2025). Yet, there are also limitations to the existing empirically-based treatments for eating disorders. First, there remains a well-documented research-practice gap, with many patients not receiving evidence-based care (Cooper & Bailey-Straebler, 2015). Therefore, there may be value in ensuring that patients can receive effective PE outside the confines of individual psychotherapy and within settings in which routine care is provided (e.g., primary care). Second, even the treatments with the strongest evidence base do not work for many individuals, with up to two-thirds of patients not achieving remission following gold standard treatments like CBT (Grilo, 2024). These outcomes may result in part from the limited theoretical models informing these treatments, which focus narrowly on eating and weight related targets (Murphy et al., 2010). As such, there have been increasing calls to develop novel intervention approaches that draw from the growing literature on the neurobiological influences that promote eating disorders (Bryson et al., 2024).

Despite growing evidence for the influence of brain-based processes in the risk and maintenance of eating disorders (Frank, 2019), the way PE is taught to individuals with these disorders in practice has changed little over time. While the current PE integrated into treatment helps patients, families, and clinicians understand the medical and psychological consequences of disordered eating, the lack of widespread neuroscience-based PE leaves many patients unaware of how brain processes contribute to their behaviors. Most of this knowledge remains in academic journals instead of being translated into tools that connect neuroscience with lived NIPE for Eating Disorders 4 experience. Neuroscience-informed PE (NIPE) offers a way to close that gap. As the field increasingly recognizes eating disorders as neurobiologically-rooted conditions (Steinglass et al., 2019), NIPE provides a structure for bringing that understanding into treatment. Although elements of NIPE have been incorporated into broader neurobiologically-informed treatments for eating disorders (Haynos et al., 2024; Steinglass et al., 2022), relatively little research has examined NIPE for eating disorders directly, further emphasizing the gap between translational neuroscience and patient care. NIPE can ground PE in a transdiagnostic framework that highlights shared and distinct neurobiological mechanisms that contribute to the development and maintenance of different eating disorders and mechanisms that make recovery possible. This chapter outlines a proposed structured model of NIPE for eating disorders in order to translate the science of the brain into the language of recovery.

Neurocognitive Approach to PE: Content

The goal of integrating NIPE into eating disorder treatment is to offer patients a clearer picture of how brain processes contribute to the behaviors they engage in and how shifting brain function may also lead to symptom change. Sharing this information can reduce self-blame (Lee et al., 2016), highlighting the role that underlying and typically invisible neural processes have in shaping emotions and behaviors that emerge over the course of the illness. However, at the same time, to avoid solely deterministic biological explanations that imply permeance, clinicians can emphasize that eating disorders are multifaceted, with neurobiology constituting only one contributor to eating pathology, and that even biological processes are malleable (Perricone & Ahn, 2024). NIPE content may also help patients understand that an eating disorder diagnosis does not reflect a single neurobiological profile, but rather a heterogeneous combination of neurobiological processes. Below, we outline key concepts that can be incorporated into NIPE NIPE for Eating Disorders 5 materials for eating disorders. As research continues to explore links between brain and behavior, the development of NIPE approaches may also illuminate critical gaps in knowledge, ultimately generating new research questions to advance the field. 1. Diagnostic similarities and differences across eating disorder diagnoses: Certain patterns of brain activity have been demonstrated to be common across diagnoses. For instance, there is evidence of hyper-sensitivity to threat and punishment that manifests across eating disorders (Harrison et al., 2010). Further, while neurobiological indices alone cannot be used to establish clinical diagnoses, in some cases, specific eating disorders may demonstrate distinct neural patterns. For example, restrictive eating disorders, such as AN, have been frequently linked to increased activity in self-control regions, such as the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and anterior cingulate cortex (Bronleigh et al., 2022), while binge-eating disorders (e.g., BN, BED) have often shown increased engagement of brain areas tied to impulsivity and reward (Bronleigh et al., 2022; Celeghin et al., 2023; Simon et al., 2016). Such NIPE can help patients and clinicians understand why some behaviors may manifest across eating concerns (e.g., negative affectivity), but others may differ (e.g., food approach versus avoidance).

2. Diagnostic similarities and differences between eating disorders and other mental health

concerns: It is well documented that eating disorders also share neural patterns with other mental health disorders. For instance, individuals with restrictive eating disorders often demonstrate deficits in reward processing that mimic those with mood and anxiety disorders (Haynos et al., 2021) and neural patterns of habitual and automatic decisionmaking that overlap with obsessive-compulsive disorder (Steinglass & Walsh, 2006).

There are also many parallels between how individuals with binge-eating disorders NIPE for Eating Disorders 6 respond to food and body image cues on a neurobiological level that mimic brain responses to drug or alcohol cues seen in substance use disorders (Smith & Robbins, 2013). At the same time, individuals with eating disorders tend to exhibit amplified responses to eating disorder-specific cues, such as food, that are not seen among individuals with other disorders (Cowdrey et al., 2011; García-García et al., 2013). This information may help individuals understand why they simultaneously experience other psychological concerns alongside their eating disorder symptoms and why some may resolve alongside eating pathology, yet others may continue to persist even following normalization of eating patterns (Eielsen et al., 2021). It may also help patients recognize that neurobiological processes contribute to a broad range of psychological experiences and behaviors, potentially reducing the perception that these processes are solely shaped or disrupted by their eating disorder.

3. Threat and punishment responding as mechanisms of eating disorders: Disturbances in the negative valence system, which encompasses neurobiological responses to aversive experiences such as threat, loss, and frustrative non-reward, may promote engagement in eating disorder symptoms (Vannucci et al., 2015; Wong et al., 2025). Maladaptive emotion regulation strategies such as avoidance, rumination, and suppression have been associated with eating disorder psychopathology across diagnoses (Aldao et al., 2010; Leppanen et al., 2022; Wong et al., 2025). Such concerns manifest in patterns of neurobiological reactivity as well. For instance, heightened activation of regions involved in threat processing (i.e., insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala) have been observed among individuals with BN to negative social interactions (Luo et al., 2022), among those with AN in response to anxiety-inducing food and body image stimuli (Seiger et al., 2023), and among individuals with ARFID in response to threatening stimuli (Thomas et al., 2025). Translating this information into NIPE may help patients to understand why they experience heightened emotional distress to the experience of threat and, thereby may turn to disordered eating to manage this distress (Wong et al., 2025). It may also help explain why many individuals transition between eating disorder diagnoses over time (Eddy et al., 2008). These findings may also provide scientific rationale for interventions that seek to reduce disordered eating by enhancing alternative emotion regulation strategies.

4. Reward processes as mechanisms of eating disorders: Dysfunctional reward processing is consistently implicated in the development and maintenance of eating pathology (Frank, 2013), though patterns vary across different diagnoses. Individuals with BN and BED generally show greater reward-related impulsivity and heightened reward circuit responsivity to palatable foods, which may promote an excess approach drive towards food (Wonderlich et al., 2021). Conversely, individuals with AN, especially the restricting subtype, commonly under-respond in reward circuitry to cues that are considered positive to the average person (e.g., palatable food, social interaction) and over-respond in reward circuitry to disorder-relevant cues (e.g., restrictive eating, thin bodies) (Haynos et al., 2020), which may promote prioritization of weight loss over other positive experiences. NIPE about reward processing can highlight why patients may feel so strongly compelled towards their eating disorder symptoms even when, on some level, they might not want to engage in these behaviors. The concept of neuroplasticity can also be introduced to explain that, while these reward pathways may have kept the patient stuck in their eating disorder, they are modifiable.

5. Executive functioning and cognitive control as mechanisms of eating disorders:

Executive functioning, or cognitive control, is often disrupted among patients with eating disorders (Wonderlich et al., 2021). Individuals with BN and BED have shown decreased cognitive control and associated reductions in activation in control-related brain regions to a range of cues, including food, which may contribute to the rash behaviors often seen among individuals with these presentations (Steward et al., 2018; Wonderlich et al., 2021). Alternatively, individuals with AN show heightened activation in brain regions involved in self-control and inhibition, such as the prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortex, especially in response to food cues (Bronleigh et al., 2022), and reduced cognitive flexibility (Zastrow et al., 2009; Hirst et al., 2017; Tchanturia et al., 2004 Tchanturia et al., , 2012)), providing a neurobiological basis for the rigidity, perfectionism, and need for control commonly observed among these patients. While executive functioning in ARFID remains understudied, emerging research shows that ARFID symptoms are linked to deficits in cognitive flexibility, impulse inhibition, and effortful control, sharing potential mechanisms with both AN and binge-spectrum disorders and potentially explaining why these patients may struggle with self-regulation (Donnelly et al., 2026). Impairments in executive functioning have also been associated broadly with more severe eating disorder symptoms (Diaz-Marsa et al., 2023; Roberts et al., 2010). NIPE explaining the influence of executive functioning on eating disorder symptoms can help individuals understand why they may struggle to organize their behavior towards recovery-oriented goals. As a wide range of interventions exist that target executive functioning (Juarascio et al., 2015), such information can also provide hope that these impaired cognitive processes can be targeted to produce eating disorder symptom change. 6. Disturbed interoception as a mechanism of eating disorders: Research also suggests that individuals with eating pathology experience disturbances in interoception (Datta et al., 2025; Pollatos et al., 2008), or difficulty perceiving internal bodily states such as hunger, satiety, and heartbeat (Craig, 2002). Neuroimaging studies have found that individuals across the eating disorder spectrum demonstrate altered or abnormal brain activity in regions associated with interoceptive awareness, such as the insula, and lessened activation on tasks associated with hunger and satiety (Datta et al., 2025). Disturbances in interoception may represent a transdiagnostic mechanism contributing to the development and maintenance of eating disorders, as there could be a reliance upon emotion or body image to guide eating behavior, instead of stimulation from hunger or satiation (Klabunde et al., 2013). Providing patients with resources on disturbed interoception in eating disorders can help them understand how miscommunication between the body and brain may reinforce key maintaining behaviors. This knowledge can also provide a rationale for why, in early stages of treatment, individuals with eating disorders may not be able to rely on internal cues to determine when they should eat, and may need to temporarily rely on more structured eating patterns (e.g., eating every 3-4 hours, independent of hunger and satiety; Fairburn, 2008). 7. Potential shifts in neurobiological maintenance mechanisms over time: Emerging work suggests that the neurobiological and cognitive processes that maintain eating disorder symptoms in earlier stages of the disorder may differ from those that occur later in illness. For instance, there are theories suggesting that binge eating behaviors in BN and BED and weight-loss behaviors in AN are goal-directed in early stages of illness, driven by activation of reward-circuitry (e.g. ventral striatum) upon receipt of food consumption or weight loss (and associated positive social feedback), respectively (Bodell & Racine, 2023; Steinglass & Walsh, 2006). However, over time, binge eating and/or weight-loss behaviors may become overtrained, automatic, or driven by craving, despite no longer being associated with pleasurable outcomes. At this stage, these behaviors are presumed to come under the control of the dorsal striatum and regions associated with motor control. Although much of this literature remains theoretical, there is some initial evidence to support the hypothesis that neural mechanisms vary as a function of illness duration or severity (Davis et al., 2020; Lloyd et al., 2025) Such NIPE can be helpful to explaining to patients why they may continue to engage in behaviors, even once they no longer derive pleasure from them. Additionally, this information can highlight why it may be useful to tailor neuroscience-informed treatments on the basis of illness stage or duration.

8. Effects of nutritional state on brain function: Not only do brain processes impact eating

behavior, but eating behavior can also have profound effects on brain functioning.

Prolonged restriction can reduce gray and white matter volumes (Seitz et al., 2013), disrupt neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine (Monteleone et al., 2018), and create measurable disruptions in brain structure and connectivity (Collantoni et al., 2021).

Many of these neural alterations have negative cognitive implications (Wierenga et al., 2015), though have been shown to reverse with sustained nutritional rehabilitation (Hemmingsen et al., 2021). While malnutrition is a hallmark of AN, ample evidence shows that individuals across the eating disorder spectrum engage in severe caloric restriction (Elran-Barak et al., 2015). Even among individuals with eating disorders who are at a healthy or higher weights, transient episodes of restrictive eating impair cognitive NIPE for Eating Disorders 11 functioning (Berner et al., 2023; Kaye et al., 2020). Although the impacts of binge eating on cognition are less well understood, binge eating may impact concentration and information processing through increasing fatigue, physical discomfort, or negative affectivity (Kjelsås et al., 2004). This information can highlight why restoring adequate and balanced nutrition is an essential target in order to restore optimal cognitive functioning, promote engagement in cognitive therapeutic techniques, and reduce ongoing eating disorder symptoms (American Psychiatric Association, 2023).

9. Neurobiological improvements with nutritional rehabilitation: Although nutritional

deficits negatively impact cognition, nutritional rehabilitation can significantly correct any starvation-related impairments in cognitive functioning. Weight restoration is a strong predictor of long-term outcomes in AN (Frostad et al., 2022; Maurel et al., 2024) and promotes adaptive neural changes, normalizing activity in brain regions involved in threat detection, emotion regulation, and executive functioning (Seidel et al., 2022; Steward et al., 2022). Weight recovery also influences disorder-specific neural activity and brain representations (Boehm et al., 2021). Additionally, stabilization of eating patterns has been associated with normalization of neurobiological networks involved in attention, salience, and sensorimotor processing in BN (Jia et al., 2026) and affect and decision-making in BED (Fleck et al., 2019). Educating patients about how nutritional rehabilitation impacts threat, reward, and self-regulation brain systems may increase their willingness to normalize eating patterns and, for those requiring weight restoration, to gain weight, in spite of the momentary associated distress. Often, patients wish for other symptoms, such as mood disturbances, to shift before making eating-related changes.

However, NIPE can help to highlight why treatment often needs to work "outside in" by NIPE for Eating Disorders 12 first stabilizing eating in order to promote downstream neurobiological effects that positively influence other psychological experiences. Emphasizing the potential reversibility of neural alterations associated with disorder maintenance may also challenge patients' perceptions of being "stuck" in their disorder, particularly among those with a longer illness duration.

10. Neurobiological risk and resiliency factors:

Emerging neurocognitive research has identified various executive functions as critical risk factors for the initial development of eating disorders. Difficulties in set-shifting, or the ability to think flexibly and easily switch between tasks, present as a transdiagnostic risk factors for the development of an eating disorder (Roberts et al., 2010). Weak central coherence, often described as "struggling to see the forest through the trees", is another risk factor for developing the binge-eating and loss-of-control eating behaviors characteristic of BN and BED (Chong & Martinelli, 2024), as well as the perfectionistic and obsessive-compulsive traits commonly seen in AN (Lang, Lopez, et al., 2014). Although neurobiological resilience factors are not well understood, early animal studies suggest that an inhibitory neuron in the hippocampus may play a role in downregulating core eating disorder behaviors such as driven exercise and restrictive eating (Chowdhury et al., 2019) 11. Neurodevelopmental factors in promoting eating disorders: Eating disorders typically onset in adolescence, often corresponding to pubertal changes, especially in females (Solmi et al., 2022). Adolescence is also a key period for neurocognitive development (Sydnor et al., 2021). As such, neurodevelopmental factors during this rapid period of brain restructuring may heighten the risk of eating pathology. For instance, the general tendencies toward reward-seeking and exploration seen in adolescence may serve as a catalyst for developing binge eating patterns among vulnerable individuals (Hagan et al., 2024). At other times, the failure to make expected developmental shifts in neurocognitive processing (i.e., lessening cognitive control circuit engagement in the face of competing demands) may increase risk of eating pathology (e.g., binge eating; Cyr et al., 2017). Finally, eating disorder symptoms may play a role in fundamentally disrupting neurodevelopmental processes. For example, starvation in adolescents with AN disrupts reward-related brain regions that continue to mature into emerging adulthood, often prolonging the disorder (Hagan et al., 2025; Posner & Steinglass, 2024). Similarly, bingeeating and purging in adolescents with BN have been associated with persistent alterations in neural circuits responsible for inhibitory control and self-regulation even following partial or full remission, suggesting that these dysfunctions may contribute to persistence of BN symptoms over time (Cyr et al., 2017). On the other hand, the enhanced neuroplasticity during adolescence may make teens with eating disorders more resilient to neurobiological consequences. For instance, adolescents show greater NIPE for Eating Disorders 14 reductions in grey and white matter (Seitz et al., 2016), but less pronounced executive functioning deficits compared to adults (Lang, Stahl, et al., 2014). Framing the emergence of eating disorder symptoms in the context of neurodevelopment can help patients understand how symptoms may have developed as extensions or deviations of expected brain changes. Further, NIPE focused on adolescent neurodevelopment can highlight the critical task of intervening early on eating disorders symptoms to capitalize upon plasticity and avoid more lasting disturbances on neurodevelopment.

12. Neurocognitive predictors of long-term outcomes: While the most robust predictor of treatment outcome in eating disorders is early symptom response to treatment (Nazar et al., 2017; Vall & Wade, 2015), emerging research is beginning to identify neurobiological and neurocognitive factors that may predict long-term outcomes. Certain neural circuits, like those in the cerebellum or involved in reward processing, have been linked to treatment outcome and adherence (Balodis et al., 2014; Milos et al., 2021; Seitz et al., 2015). Neurocognitive factors linked to poor weight outcomes in AN include impaired decision-making (Lucas et al., 2021), weak central coherence (Schilder et al., 2025), extremes in inhibitory control (Schilder et al., 2025), and difficulties with detailfocused processing and set-shifting (Oldershaw et al., 2018). Similarly, executive functioning deficits have been associated with higher frequency of self-induced vomiting in BN (Singh et al., 2026). Although the research on neurocognitive predictors of outcomes is still emerging, referring to these existing studies can help patients and providers to understand the importance of correcting maladaptive neurocognitive processes in treatment.

NIPE for Eating Disorders 15 13. Emerging psychotherapy interventions for neurobiological targets: Although there is limited research about neurocognitive predictors of response to eating disorder treatment the emerging evidence linking neurocognitive processes to eating disorder treatment outcomes highlights the need for interventions to target such processes. In recent years, a number of psychological and behavioral treatments for eating disorders, such as Relapse Prevention and Changing Habits (REACH+) (Steinglass et al., 2022), Temperament Based Treatment (Kaye et al., 2015), and Positive Affect Treatment (Haynos et al., 2024a), have been developed to target neurobiological mechanisms that may encourage or maintain eating pathology. Trials examining the impact of these treatments on eating disorder-related neurobiological processes are still ongoing; however, initial trials suggest the utility of such approaches for altering key neurocognitive processes and reducing eating disorder symptoms (Haynos et al., 2024a; Stedal et al., 2023; Steinglass et al., 2018). Highlighting this information can instill hope that disruptions in neural processing may be modifiable, thereby enhancing motivation for treatment engagement, and can provide insight into future neuroscience-based directions in eating disorder treatment research.

14. Pharmacological and neurobiological interventions:

It may also be useful to explain how medication may intervene on neurobiological targets. Lisdexamphetamine dimesylate and fluoxetine are FDA-approved for BED and BN, respectively (Muratore & Attia, 2022).

Lisdexamphetamine dimesylate may decrease binge eating by altering food-related reward processing (Schneider et al., 2021), while fluoxetine is presumed to decrease BN symptoms by improving satiety (Goldstein et al., 1999). There is some evidence that olanzapine, an atypical antipsychotic that decreases dopamine activity, may support weight restoration in AN, but its neural mechanisms are understudied (Çöpür & Çöpür, 2020). Other emerging neurobiologically-informed treatments for eating disorders include transcranial magnetic stimulation, transcranial direct current stimulation, and deep brain stimulation. While there is evidence that these treatments may be effective in reducing eating pathology in treatment-resistant individuals, more research is needed (Bryson et al., 2024). Incorporating information on varied biological interventions targeting neurobiological processes within NIPE could expand an awareness of novel avenues for eating disorder treatment and highlight the ability to manipulate neural disturbances perpetuating symptoms, further enhancing hope for pathways to recovery. 15. Balancing neurobiological explanations with personal agency: While neurobiological explanations for eating disorder behaviors may help reduce self-blame, NIPE should be presented in a way that emphasizes malleability rather than inevitability or permanence.

Pairing NIPE with information about neuroplasticity and reinforcing personal agency may help emphasize that neurobiological processes can change over the course of recovery and that individuals retain an active role in this process. Together, these messages may promote optimism, self-efficacy, and hope for recovery (Farrell et al., 2015). However, discussions of neuroplasticity should be carefully framed to avoid implying that recovery depends on the complete normalization of a particular neural circuit or that such normalization is necessary for meaningful improvement. Overall, neurobiological concepts should complement broader messages of agency, flexibility, and hope, allowing clinicians to incorporate neuroscience into care without overwhelming patients with an overly biologically-focused understanding of their disorder.

Neurocognitive Approach to PE: Structure

It is important to carefully consider how NIPE content is delivered to patients with eating disorders to ensure they gain the maximum benefit. Given the medical and psychological sequelae in these disorders, a patient's current medical and psychiatric state should be taken into account before presenting this information. Factors such as nutritional or illness status, temperament, mood, developmental stage, and past treatment experiences are especially important to consider. Additionally, sociocultural factors should be considered when delivering NIPE, as eating disorders can present and be experienced differently across cultural and demographic groups. Failure to adapt NIPE to these factors could make it harder for patients to meaningfully engage with and fully understand NIPE material.

1. Consideration of nutritional impairments and/or illness state: As mentioned, there are well documented impacts of acute and prolonged starvation on a range of cognitive abilities, including attention, executive functioning, and memory (Stedal et al., 2021), and their related brain systems (Koithan et al., 2024). Therefore, careful consideration of nutritional state is needed when delivering NIPE. When possible, it is important to encourage clients to come to treatment sessions appropriately nourished in order to better comprehend and retain the information provided. NIPE content on the effects of nutritional status on brain function may reinforce the importance of adequate nourishment while providing an explanation for any cognitive slowing and fatigue patients may be experiencing. It also may be helpful for clinicians to have snack foods available for individuals who come to session under-nourished. Further, given the impact of eating disorder behaviors on higher-order cognitive processes, it is essential to tailor NIPE to be easy to comprehend and retain. Brief, simple, and clear language suitable for a lay audience is preferrable, whereas highly detailed or technical descriptions of neuroscience-grounded concepts are discouraged. Concrete prompts or reminders (e.g., worksheets, web-based materials) may support individuals with eating disorders in remembering the specifics of NIPE messaging in their daily lives. It may also be useful to share NIPE with close others (e.g., parents, partners) who are supporting the person with an eating disorder in order to provide another source of consistent PE messaging. Further, careful consideration is needed to determine what NIPE is appropriate for which stage of illness. For instance, it is suggested that most active cognitive interventions for underweight or otherwise malnourished individuals are best suited for after they have undergone nutritional rehabilitation and experience the cognitive benefits of adequate caloric intake (American Psychiatric Association, 2023). Therefore, it is possible that more complex NIPE concepts may be best suited to later stages of the treatment or recovery process, or may warrant consistent repetition across stages of recovery.

2. Consideration of temperament:

There is a wealth of information demonstrating that certain biologically-based personality traits present commonly among individuals with eating disorders and emerging data suggest that it may be helpful to tailor NIPE to temperamental characteristics (Knatz Peck et al., 2021; Stedal et al., 2023).

Transdiagnostically, common traits in individuals with eating disorders include sensitivity to punishment and criticism and high achievement orientation (Hill, 2024).

Thus, encouraging patients to approach NIPE information from a place of curiosity and openness may decrease potential pressure to "learn perfectly," inhibiting self-criticism that may interfere with learning. Some individuals with eating disorders, especially those with restrictive eating disorders, experience high levels of perfectionism, harm avoidance, and emotional over-control (Atiye et al., 2015; Lampard et al., 2012). For such individuals, NIPE content explaining the neurobiological mechanisms underlying their behaviors may be helpful in reducing anxiety by providing a sense of clarity regarding their symptoms, treatment course, and long-term outcomes. It may also be helpful when working with such individuals to deliver NIPE in a manner that is structured, predictable, and concrete. If information is presented in a vague or abstract way, the ambiguity may be experienced as distressing, distracting from the main objectives of the content. In contrast, other individuals with eating disorders, especially those with binge eating disorders, show a tendency towards emotional under-control, novelty-seeking, and impulsivity (Atiye et al., 2015; Hill, 2024). For such individuals, it may be important for material to be novel and attention-grabbing. For instance, engaging online platforms or interactive groups in which patients can share their lived experience of NIPE may increase and maintain interest in its content. NIPE explaining reward processes underlying impulsivity may help patients understand how the way information is presented can influence their processing. This may clarify the structure of NIPE while helping patients tailor their environment and communication to support learning and understanding throughout recovery.

3. Consideration of mood state: Depression and anxiety commonly co-occur across the eating disorder spectrum (Hambleton et al., 2022), which may influence how some patients process, retain, and engage with new PE content. Common depressive features such as slowed processing (LeMoult & Gotlib, 2019), reduced motivation (Treadway et al., 2012), and difficulties with sustained attention (Keller et al., 2019) can make relevant or interesting material feel overwhelming or unimportant. This may translate to patients seeming unengaged with NIPE content even if this is inconsistent with their internal beliefs. Similarly, individuals with anxiety often experience executive functioning deficits (Nyberg et al., 2021), emotional dysregulation (Mennin et al., 2005), and repetitive negative thinking (Petwal et al., 2023) which may lead to heightened emotional reactivity to potentially threatening material such as learning about their neurobiological risk factors. For this reason, it is important for clinicians to thoroughly assess a patient's current mental state before introducing NIPE to ensure they can engage effectively and that receiving novel information will not be emotionally overwhelming. Presenting NIPE material during extreme mood disturbances should be avoided, as this may reduce overall comprehension and retention. For patients with psychiatric comorbidities, NIPE content addressing the neurobiological similarities between their eating disorder and their cooccurring condition can help patients understand how comorbidity influences symptom presentation and treatment engagement. This may be particularly valuable when symptoms or illness-related cognitions conflict with their internal motivation to recover.

Clinicians may find that using simplified explanations, brief handouts, or more interactive approaches, or taking brief breaks, may make dense information seem more manageable for patients that are experiencing cognitive and emotional overload. 4. Consideration of developmental stage: NIPE for adolescents with eating disorders should be designed and presented to align with their developmental needs. Because adolescents often have a shorter illness duration (Miskovic-Wheatley et al., 2023), they may be less familiar with the mechanisms and factors underlying the development and maintenance of their disorder. Adolescents are also experiencing ongoing development of higher-order cognitive functions and, thus, executive functioning skills may not be fully developed (Sydnor et al., 2021). As a result, NIPE may need to be presented in a simplified, but detailed manner, with key concepts broken down into smaller, more comprehensible subconcepts. Accompanying NIPE with visual aids (e.g., model of the brain), interactive exercises, or peer discussions may increase understanding and engagement by leveraging the increased sensitive to novelty and social experiences characteristic of this time period (Crone & Dahl, 2012). Presenting complex material in an age-appropriate and accessible way may help adolescents understand challenging topics without feeling disinterested or overwhelmed. It is also important to deliver NIPE to families alongside adolescents to ensure everyone is educated about the neural consequences of the disorder in the same way. In fact, research has demonstrated that PE delivered to families can reduce family burden and foster more positive interactions between adolescents and their families (Uehara et al., 2001). NIPE content emphasizing heightened neuroplasticity during adolescence may further instill hope among adolescents and their loved ones while motivating adolescents to make meaningful changes that can support lasting recovery into adulthood. Emerging evidence also suggests that NIPE can foster parental understanding of both their child's symptoms and overall temperamental patterns in a way that recontextualizes and destigmatizes behaviors that are often distressing to family members (Stedal et al., 2023).

5. Consideration of past treatments: Eating disorders can be enduring conditions with high rates of relapse (Richard et al., 2005). As a result, many individuals often cycle in and out of treatment, which may affect the way that they interact with NIPE material. This offers an opportunity to deliver more specialized NIPE to individuals with a chronic course of illness who are already familiar with many foundational concepts from past treatment.

For these individuals, novel material that offers new neuroscience-based insights that reframe their previous knowledge, or correct misconceptions about the implicated neural systems, is crucial. Yet, prior research as demonstrated that biological explanations for eating disorders can reduce optimism for the future unless the malleability of these factors is emphasized (Farrell et al., 2015). For individuals with longer courses of illness in particular it may be important to emphasize NIPE as offering novel pathways to recovery. In contrast, for patients new to treatment, NIPE that assumes prior knowledge can be overwhelming and lead to disengagement or frustration, especially for individuals with heightened threat sensitivity (Harrison et al., 2010). For these reasons, clinicians may opt to align specific NIPE content with a patient's current stage in recovery.

6. Consideration of cultural, socioeconomic, and demographic background: Eating disorders are often misconceived as conditions that primarily affect affluent, White, cisgender women. However, growing evidence demonstrates that eating disorders affect individuals across diverse socioeconomic, gender, body-size, and racial and ethnic backgrounds (Burke et al., 2023; Gorrell & Murray, 2019; López-Gil et al., 2023; Parker & Harriger, 2020). For patients with marginalized backgrounds, NIPE should reflect the experiences of minoritized populations, as explanations that do not resonate with a patient's lived experience may perpetuate harmful stereotypes and misconceptions about who can develop and experience an eating disorder. Although the core content of NIPE may remain largely unchanged, tailoring its delivery to explicitly challenge stereotypical presentations of eating disorders and recognize cultural and socioeconomic barriers to nutrition and treatment may enhance its relevance and help reduce stigma (Husain et al., 2017; Shin, 2004). NIPE for such populations may also benefit by emphasizing the noted NIPE for Eating Disorders 23 impact of stigma and discrimination on the functioning of many of the neural circuits implicated in eating pathology, highlighting ways in which external influences may impact neurobiological processes (Berger & Sarnyai, 2015). Additionally, NIPE should account for the broad spectrum of eating disorder presentations and be adapted to the distinct experiences and needs of diverse patient populations. For example, NIPE addressing disturbed interoception as a mechanism maintaining an eating disorder should be tailored differently for an adult with BED in a larger body, a male with AN, and an adolescent with sensory-based ARFID, with neuroscientific explanations adapted to reflect the distinct experiences associated with each presentation.

NIPE Case Example

Below, we provide a case example demonstrating the implementation of NIPE into medical visits and psychological treatment.

At presentation to outpatient treatment, Lorraine was a 34-year-old White, bisexual, cisgender woman who lived with her partner in the Midwest and worked full-time as an elementary school teacher. Lorraine shared that she had struggled with her eating patterns and body image since she was 16 years old. She stated that she had a long-standing history of depressive symptoms that started in childhood, but escalated during adolescence. During that time, she had incidentally attempted a popular diet plan alongside some friends and had been pleasantly surprised by how the resulting weight loss helped to alleviate her momentary feelings of low mood. She found that, although her friends ultimately abandoned the diet fad, she felt increasingly "addicted" to weight loss, pursuing it until she reached an underweight state. Since then, her symptoms had fluctuated over time, such that she experienced some periods of heightened restriction accompanied by low weight, as well as other periods in which she was able to eat relatively normally and maintain a healthier weight. More recently, eating disorder symptoms had escalated following a number of stressors, including a job loss and ongoing conflict with her partner. However, she was not aware of the severity of her recent eating concerns until she was referred to a partial hospitalization program (PHP) by her primary care physician. At admission to the PHP, her symptoms were consistent with AN, restricting subtype, with a BMI of 16.4 kg/m 2 , severe food restriction, excessive exercise patterns, strict dietary rules, and shape and weight concerns. Lorraine was discharged from the PHP at a BMI of 20.5 kg/m 2.

Although her overall caloric intake increased following PHP, she continued to endorse strict dietary rules, as well as heightened concerns about shape and weight after discharge. She often experienced symptoms of depression and anxiety related to her eating disorder symptoms and worried she would not be able to achieve full recovery. She described feeling shame that she continued to struggle with eating disorder symptoms after so many years, especially because she knew that these behaviors put a strain on her relationship with her partner. NIPE was incorporated into the outpatient treatment for Lorraine to help her better understand how her symptoms had developed and been maintained over time in a way that could reduce her self-blame related to her ongoing struggles. Further, NIPE was used to highlight directions for intervention and to enhance a sense of hope that the symptoms that she was experiencing could be reduced by "retraining" her brain. Given Lorraine's tendency towards a concrete and detail-oriented cognitive style, the therapist made sure to break NIPE concepts into small, manageable bits of information, supported by concrete examples and written materials.

Additionally, the therapist took into consideration Lorraine's nutritional state and mood when presenting NIPE material, with more novel and complex information being presented at times when Lorraine arrived more adequately nourished and less acutely distressed. The therapist also NIPE for Eating Disorders 25 encouraged Lorraine to share the NIPE discussed in treatment with her partner in order to support their understanding of the factors leading to Lorraine's struggles with eating.

At the beginning of treatment, the therapist began by describing how Lorraine's eating disorder symptoms may have originally developed through a combination of her neurocognitive tendencies interacting with the unique brain development processes occurring in adolescence.

Specifically, the therapist highlighted that adolescence is a period in which reward circuitry is sensitized and executive functioning skills and their underlying circuits are still being developed.

Therefore, Lorraine may have been especially sensitive to the reward of weight loss, especially when it was paired with social connection and praise, and also developmentally limited in her ability to comprehend the long-term consequences of her eating disorder symptoms and to shift her behavior accordingly. This process may have interacted with Lorraine's general tendency towards cognitive rigidity and perseverance, which may have explained why she had persisted in restrictive eating even once her friends had stopped. This information was helpful in combating the messaging that Lorraine had received from friends and family members when she was younger that had indicated that her behaviors were "selfish" or "manipulative." By highlighting that these behaviors were direct results of normative brain processes gone awry, and that her mental tendencies contributing to symptom maintenance (e.g., cognitive persistence) were not inherently bad and had been helpful in other contexts (e.g., academic success), Lorraine was able to experience diminished self-blame.

When introducing NIPE into Lorraine's understanding of the maintenance of her disorder, the therapist highlighted that individuals with restrictive eating disorders demonstrate neurobiological patterns characterized by: 1) increased threat detection in response to eating-and body-related stimuli; 2) heightened reward responsivity to weight-loss cues; 3) diminished NIPE for Eating Disorders 26 reward responsivity to other pleasant stimuli; 4) over-engagement of cognitive control circuitry; and 5) limited ability to understand and respond to interoceptive signals. As such, her brain was primed to look to eating disorder symptoms as a solution to reduce her pervasive experience of aversive reactivity and anhedonia. Additionally, Lorraine's neurobiological tendencies may have made it easier for her to engage her cognitive control circuitry than to understand her bodily experiences and needs. Lorraine reported this explanation aligned with her experience, such that she often found that her eating disorder symptoms became the "solution" to any of her problems, whether it be a distressing interaction with her partner or feeling of being out of control of her work situation. She also reported continuing to struggle with interpreting bodily cues for hunger following weight restoration, regularly doubting her internal sensations and opting to suppress these feelings instead. The therapist also introduced the concept that, over time, the way in which her eating disorder symptoms interacted with her brain may have shifted, such that her behavior came under the control of more automatic areas of the brain associated with wanting or striving, rather than acute pleasure. Lorraine indicated that this was one of the most frustrating aspects of her experience -she desperately wanted to change her eating disorder behaviors, but found it had become increasingly difficult to do so.

Once Lorraine had learned about how neural processes contributed to her disorder, her therapist shifted towards utilizing NIPE technique to motivate ongoing recovery actions. First, the therapist highlighted that the process of nutritional rehabilitation and weight restoration alone had already gone a long way towards correcting some of the neurobiological disturbances that had kept her stuck. Lorraine acknowledged that, since weight restoration, she had been less emotionally reactive, more positively engaged in her life, and less preoccupied with eating disorder thoughts. The therapist emphasized the importance of Lorraine continuing to maintain NIPE for Eating Disorders 27 weight and a regular pattern of eating in order to foster longer-term restoration of neurocognitive functioning. Second, the therapist introduced the concept of neuroplasticity, explaining that, although Lorraine's brain had developed certain patterns of responding to eating disorder cues and perceived threats and rewards more generally, these could be altered through targeted interventions. The end result would be the development of new neural pathways to support more effective patterns of behavior. The therapist stated that targeting established neurocognitive eating disorder mechanisms had the potential to not only ease Lorraine's eating disorder symptoms, but also many of her accompanying affective symptoms. Lastly, Lorraine's therapist described the existing and emerging psychological and biological treatments designed with these targets in mind. This discussion provided Lorraine with a newfound sense of hope that her biological-rooted drive towards restrictive eating and weight loss was modifiable and that options existed for targeting these patterns in treatment.

This case example briefly illustrates how NIPE can be incorporated into everyday conversations with patients who struggle to understand the factors driving their behaviors or who frequently engage in self-blame, holding themselves responsible for their disorder. Additionally, the emphasis of neural plasticity and the reversibility of these neural dysfunctions can help patients recognize that recovery is achievable and that small, gradual steps can foster lasting neural rehabilitation. Although this example specifically focuses on the neural mechanisms underlying restrictive eating in AN, the aim is to demonstrate how clinicians can translate complex neuroscience into a digestible and personalized format that patients can understand and share with others.

Lorraine's case can be adapted to reflect the specific eating disorder diagnosis she experiences. For example, if Lorraine instead had a diagnosis of BED and presented with NIPE for Eating Disorders 28 emotion dysregulation and impulsivity, the therapist might instead work to ensure NIPE was highly engaging through heightened use of interactive exercises and real-world practice and place greater emphasis on reward circuitry and the reinforcing effects of binge eating, rather than on weight loss. NIPE could focus on how binge eating frequently develops during a developmental period where adolescent brains are primed to respond to primary rewards, such as food, and that, over time, binge eating becomes an overlearned strategy for regulating negative affect during stressful situations or a craving-driven process. Alternatively, if Lorraine had ARFID and presented with strong aversions to particular food textures, tastes, or smells, the therapist might place less emphasis on neurobiological mechanisms underlying weight or shape.

Instead, NIPE could be tailored to explain how differences in sensory processing and interoceptive awareness can contribute to food avoidance, emphasizing that this avoidance reflects underlying neurobiological processes rather than intentional refusal or lack of motivation to eat. Thus, while the core principles of NIPE can be applied transdiagnostically, the content and emphasis of NIPE can be adapted to the distinct maintaining mechanisms and symptom presentations associated with each disorder. Future directions for NIPE in eating disorders

NIPE occupies a growing intersection between research, clinical practice, and lived experience and offers a shared language for understanding recovery in ways that emphasized the role of relevant neurobiological processes. However, systematic effort is required to integrate NIPE for Eating Disorders 29 NIPE in a meaningful and practical way. The next phase of work should focus on testing, refining, and expanding NIPE to ensure it remains empirically grounded, clinically useful, and responsive to the people and systems it aims to serve. We have outlined below several priorities for advancing this work. 1. Strengthening translation between neuroscience and clinical practice: Intentional translation of neuroscience into clinical practice is a vital in bridging the researchpractice gap (Hildebrandt & Goldschmidt, 2024). Clinicians need accessible ways to interpret and apply neuroscientific findings, while researchers need feedback from those working directly with patients to ensure their work reflects real world needs. Thus, stronger efforts are needed to bridge the bench-to-bedside divide by considering the clinical consequences of neuroscience research, as well as the clinical findings that may shape an understanding of neurobiological targets.

2. Co-developing NIPE with people with lived experience and front-line practitioners:

There is growing recognition of the need to involve individuals with lived experiences of mental illness, including eating disorders, into research (Schleider, 2023). Thus, NIPE should be shaped collaboratively with individuals who have experienced eating disorders, their families, and the practitioners (e.g., therapists, dieticians, medical providers) that work closest to them. Co-designing materials helps ensure that neuroscientific concepts are accurately tailored to real-life experiences (Downs, 2024) and explained in ways that are understandable, relevant, and relatable. Their input can also guide how information is framed and delivered so it fits the realities of different recovery paths and care settings.

3. Evaluating the impact of NIPE directly:

To move NIPE from concept to practice, its impact should be tested in eating disorder settings. Directly testing NIPE will be critical to establishing its efficacy and advancing the integration of evidence-based neuroscience into eating disorder care. Research must examine not only whether NIPE works, but when, for whom, and under what conditions. These studies should account for factors such as illness stage, cognitive flexibility, and readiness for recovery while recognizing that PE may influence people differently across these contexts. Evaluation should also include all intended audiences -patients, families, and clinicians -since each engages with NIPE in distinct ways that shape understanding and outcomes. In addition to evaluating the benefits of NIPE, it will be important to evaluate potential pitfalls of such approaches (Angermeyer et al., 2013), as well as ways to mitigate any unintended harms of such explanations. 4. Understanding how to best teach NIPE to clients, support systems and practitioners:

Teaching neuroscience effectively is central to the impact of NIPE. Future work should determine how to present complex brain concepts in ways that match people's cognitive and emotional capacities during recovery. Consistent with the NIPE framework presented by Rezapour and colleagues (2026), it will be important to determine how to frame NIPE for families and clinicians in order to balance structure with empathy that can help turn neuroscience into care that builds understanding and connection.

5. Integrating emerging technologies to expand NIPE's reach and relevance: Emerging technologies offer promising ways to bring NIPE to life. Interactive tools, virtual environments, and adaptive learning platforms can help visualize brain processes, tailor content to individual learning needs, and provide feedback that deepens understanding (Schleisman et al., 2018). Used intentionally, these innovations can make complex brain-behavior concepts feel tangible and empowering, extending NIPE outside formal treatment settings and into the broader recovery landscape.

Conclusion

NIPE for eating disorders may be integrated into traditional or emerging treatment approaches to help patients understand how their brain and its processes influence their thoughts and behaviors. Treatment for eating disorders often involves encouraging patients to adjust maladaptive cognitive and behavioral patterns. Providing neuroscientific insight into the origins of these thought and behavior patterns and the potential neural gains associated with change may support sustained motivation in recovery. Despite the growth of neuroscientific research on eating disorders (Steinglass et al., 2019), there remains a lack of consideration in the field on how to effectively educate patients about these findings. NIPE can be used to bridge the gap between research knowledge and clinical care in order to bolster patient self-efficacy, dispel common misconceptions about eating disorders, and enhance the accessibility of the science behind behavior change for individuals with eating disorders.
