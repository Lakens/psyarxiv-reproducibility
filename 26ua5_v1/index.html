Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. Abstract

Longitudinal research links participants' responses across waves, yet sensitive topics often require anonymity. Self-generated identification codes (SGICs) reconcile both, but participants may sometimes mistype, skip, or swap code parts. Most studies handle such mismatches with ad hoc rules of unknown accuracy. We present a simulation-based workflow and introduce the web app wavelink for choosing a customized matching procedure. To link waves, researchers must describe their design, SGICs, and expected errors; wavelink then simulates studies with known true links, compares exact, rule-based (tiered), and probabilistic procedures, recommends the procedure that misses the fewest links within a set false-link limit, and exports an R script for linking the researcher's study data. To illustrate, we report a three-wave school panel of adolescents, for which error-tolerant procedures found 91-92% of true links versus 73% for exact matching. Simulations across conditions show that code length limits accuracy and that auxiliary variables compensate for short codes.

Studies rank SGICs as a beneficial method among anonymous linkage options because they avoid direct personal identifiers (Audette et al., 2020).

SGICs are particularly helpful in school-based research with children and adolescents. In schools, tracking minors across multiple waves is critical for evaluating the impact of educational interventions, policies, and environments (Agley et al., 2021; Brändle & Pläschke, 2024). At the same time, research with children and adolescents, especially on sensitive, stigmatized, or illegal behaviors, often requires anonymous data collection to obtain honest responses (Brändle & Pläschke, 2024; COMPASS Study), and adolescents tend to answer sensitive questions more sincerely when they feel that their identity is protected (Ripper et al., 2017). Moreover, anonymity is a regular ethical prerequisite for institutional review boards to waive active parental consent in low-risk studies. Such waivers matter because requiring active parental consent can introduce selection bias at recruitment, reduce sample sizes, and discourage participation from high-risk youth (Agley et al., 2021).

Anonymity also has value after data collection, as the data can be shared openly, for example on repositories such as the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/) or Zenodo (https://zenodo.org), and be reused for other analyses without additional consent procedures, in line with the FAIR and FAIRRREST principles (Smith et al., 2026; Wilkinson et al., 2016).

Many funders require project data to be accessibly shared (e.g., Data Management Plan (DMP), Swiss National Science Foundation). Hence, researchers must balance the analytical necessity of accurately linking participant data across multiple time points with the ethical requirement of protecting the identities of vulnerable participants, weighing potential imperfect linkage rates against the benefits of anonymity or pseudonymity and data accuracy (Battista et al., 2019).

Despite decades of use, reported SGIC match rates vary considerably (e.g., over 90% in some samples; Ripper et al., 2017). One reason is that participants do not enter their code identically in every wave: they make typos, click the wrong option, misremember a part, or skip it. Such errors may be more frequent among minor participants, as they tend to favor speed over accuracy more than adults (De Jong et al., 2018; Starns & Ratcliff, 2010). Reported error rates differ widely between code parts: For example, one study found that 1.5% of birth months but 18.8% of mother's initials were erroneous or blank on average (Audette et al., 2020). For whole codes, 9% to 12% of participants in a German school panel who reported having taken part in the previous wave had no matching code (Lipp, 2021). Schnell & Weiand (2023) simulated identifier error rates of 0.1% to 1% in a student register, and Ariel et al. (2014) assumed error levels of 10% to 30% in health data. Although such error rates may differ across studies, much of the variability in matched participant rates likely stems from how studies handle imperfect codes, typically through informal, study-specific tolerance rules rather than a validated matching procedure (Yurek et al., 2008). McKibben et al. (2026) recently highlighted this gap, observing that the methods used to develop and evaluate SGICs are still under-researched.

Evaluating matching procedures with real data is challenging because it is often unknown which codes truly belong to the same individual. To address this, we combine a simulationwhere the true links are known, allowing us to identify both missed and false links -with an application to real data. Specifically, this study proposes a step-by-step workflow that assists researchers in determining which matching procedure is best suited for their study design. The app is written in R (R Core Team, 2025) with the shiny package (Chang et al., 2026). The linkage uses the R packages reclin2 (Van Der Laan, 2022) and stringdist (Van Der Loo, 2013). The source code and installation instructions for wavelink are available on https://github.com/felixdidi/wavelink. Besides a hosted version (click https://mediapsych.uni-mainz.de/wavelink/ for a live preview), researchers can install wavelink as an R package (in R: remotes::install_github("felixdidi/wavelink")) and run the app on their own computer with wavelink::run_app().

To determine the best matching procedure, wavelink opens with a short overview of the workflow and three ways to begin. Researchers can start from an empty guide and enter their study themselves, load an example study that fills in every step with a complete setup (design and variables of that study, with the default error and missing rates), or load a configuration they saved in an earlier session. All entries can be changed afterward, and a decision tree in the sidebar shows which steps have been completed and what was entered. No participant data are uploaded at any point: the app simulates data that imitate the study from the numbers entered, and the R code generated at the end runs locally on the researcher's own computer. The template of the [blinded] example study lets readers follow the example below in the app.

Step 2: Study Design

When attempting to match participants across waves, the study design is crucial. For example, the more participants drop out or join at later waves, the more records there are that a procedure can link wrongly. Wave size matters for the same reason: with more records, more pairs of different persons share a code by chance. Researchers can typically determine these numbers from their field records; retention can be estimated from response counts per class or school, or from an exact-code linkage, which underestimates it slightly. In wavelink, both retention and new participants must be set for each wave, because for more than two waves, every later wave is compared with all persons seen so far, using each person's best-matching earlier record. Records that cannot be linked join the list as new persons, so participants who skip a wave can still be found in the next. To illustrate, the [blinded] example study had three waves with 1,581 participants in the first wave. We roughly estimated participation from the data and set retention to 65% (Wave 1 to 2) and 59% (Wave 2 to 3). Of the participants who missed a wave, 21% returned, and new participants accounted for 16% and 7% of the first wave.

Step 3: The Self-Generated Identification Code

The SGIC determines how well participants can be matched. Each part of the SGIC is specified by type: letters (e.g., an initial), days, months, generic numbers (e.g., a digit of the house number) or categories (e.g., twin status). The usefulness of each part depends on its distribution (e.g., how common each initial is), its error rate (how often a participant enters a value in a wave that differs from their true one) and its missing rate. Furthermore, some errors affect several parts at once: a participant may not answer the code at all, enter a completely different code, or swap two parts. Errors also cluster in careless participants: in surveys with little at stake, about 12% of participants answer carelessly (Ward & Meade, 2023, p. 581).

However, few researchers know these rates before data are collected. The web app therefore uses a literature-based default for every rate, which researchers can use as a starting point and/or replace with their own estimates. Because adolescents and adults may differ in their error rates, each default is set at an average of adolescents and adults. For the [blinded] example study, the six SGIC parts (see Table 1) were entered as three letters, a day, a month, and twin status as a category with three values. The error and missing rates were left at the wavelink defaults, except for the mother's initial, which we set to 5% per wave because studies report considerably higher error rates for this part than for other initials (Audette et al., 2020; Schnell et al., 2010; Yurek et al., 2008).

Step 4: Other Variables

Besides the SGIC, many surveys ask for other variables that remain stable (e.g., gender, country of birth) or change in a predictable way (e.g., age rises by one per year, grade by one per school year). Such variables can help in two ways: First, when two records differ in only a few code parts, agreement on these variables makes it more likely that the records belong to the same person; second, they can be used for blocking, which means that only records with the same value (e.g., the same school) are compared. In wavelink, each variable is described by its type (category, number, or free text), its distribution, and its error and missing rates. Two features are specific to panel data. First, numbers such as age or grade level may change between waves, so researchers specify by how much (e.g., by 0 to +1 per wave). Second, free texts such as a school name may be typed differently in different waves ("Goethe-Gymnasium", "Goethe Gym", "Gg") and are therefore compared by their similarity (Jaro-Winkler; Winkler, 1990): two texts agree if their similarity exceeds a cutoff.

For the [blinded] example study, we added gender (four categories), age (range 12-19, rising by 0 to +1 between waves), grade level (range 5-11, likewise), country of birth (four categories), and the school's name as free text (20 schools) at the default error rates (Table 2, Panel B).

Step 5: Procedures to Compare

A matching procedure is a sequence of steps. Each step can link records that are still unlinked, but it may also add false links. We distinguish two kinds of steps. Rule-based steps link a pair of records if their codes are identical (exact), differ in at most k parts, agree after swapping two parts, or agree on chosen parts; each rule can additionally require agreement on other variables (e.g., gender). A rule step accepts a link only if it is unique for both records, so that ambiguous records are left for later steps. In contrast, a score step, which can only be the last step, weighs agreement on all variables and links the pairs with the highest scores, one-to-one, above a threshold. Two scores are available in wavelink: a weighted score, in which every agreement and disagreement counts with a weight derived from the error rates and distributions entered in Steps 3 and 4 (the log-likelihood ratio of Fellegi & Sunter, 1969; on dependence between linking variables, see Tromp et al., 2008), and probabilistic linkage, in which these weights are estimated from the data with the EM algorithm (Bauman, 2006). Because other variables are often related among different persons (e.g., age and grade), wavelink estimates the EM in two stages: first from code parts alone, which identifies the likely matches, and then the weights of other variables given these matches. Finally, each procedure can block on a variable.

Which procedures to compare is the researcher's choice. We recommend always including exact matching as the baseline and a score procedure whenever other variables are available. For the exact codes first, then codes that differ in one part, then codes with swapped day and month or swapped initials; and (C) probabilistic linkage (EM) on all code parts and other variables.

Step 6: Priorities

Each of these procedures can make two kinds of mistakes: First, a missed link may split one participant into two, reducing statistical power. Second, a false link may merge two participants into one, biasing estimates in data analysis (Zhu et al., 2015). Missed links show up as attrition, whereas false links cannot be recognized in the linked data afterward. Wavelink therefore asks for a limit on the share of false links among all links (by default 0.1%, that is, one false link per 1,000 links) and recommends the procedure that misses the fewest links within this limit. If no procedure stays within the limit, the app recommends the one with the fewest false links and says so. Researchers who prefer to weigh both mistakes can instead state how many times worse they consider a false link to be compared to a missed one; across typical designs, a ratio of 20 gives about the same results as the 0.1% limit. Finally, researchers can state how many uncertain links they can check by hand; the score step then gets a review band, and the app exports these links for manual checking. For the [blinded] example, we kept the limit of 0.1% and did not plan manual reviews.

Step 7: Data Preparation

The simulation assumes clean data, so researchers need to prepare their data before the recommended procedure is applied. The app lists the necessary steps, and the generated script repeats them as comments: set codes for "don't know" and "no answer" (e.g., -9) to missing; set fake codes (e.g., the same letter in every part) to missing; remove questionnaires submitted twice within a wave; use the same format in every wave (letters as letters, days 1-31, months 1-12, the same category codes); and use the same column names in every wave. The script also warns about data that look uncleaned. For the [blinded] example data, we applied these cleaning steps before linking (Step 9), which resulted in the wave sizes reported above.

Step 8: Simulation and Evaluation

For the simulation, wavelink generates artificial studies based on the design, variables and errors entered in Steps 2 to 4. In each simulated study, a population of participants with true values is drawn; in each wave, every participant enters every variable anew, with errors and missing values as specified. All procedures are then applied to the observed waves exactly as they would be applied to real data, that is, observed records are compared with observed records.

Because the true links are known within the simulation, the app counts the correct, false and missed links for every procedure and threshold. For waves of more than about 1,000 records, only pairs that agree on at least half of the code parts are compared to increase efficiency.

Thresholds of score steps are chosen on one half of the simulated studies, and all reported numbers are derived from the other half.

We recommend that researchers report at least three measures from the simulation: (1) the share of true links found (recall), (2) the share of false links among all links, and, for more than two waves, (3) the number of persons found in all waves. Wavelink additionally reports how robust the results are to error rates that differ from the assumed ones and how many links each step contributes. For the [blinded] example, we simulated 40 studies (Table 3). Exact matching (A) made almost no false links (0.5 per study, 0.02%) but missed 685 of about 2,519 true links (recall 72.8%). The rule-based procedure (B) found 91.0% of the true links, but 1.00% of its links were false, about ten times the default limit; most of them came from the step that allows one code part to differ. Probabilistic linkage (C, with a tuned threshold of a posterior probability of .90) found the highest number of true links (92.1%) and made 1.2 false links per study, a share of 0.05%. This is within the default limit of 0.1%, so wavelink recommended probabilistic linkage.

The example thus shows the trade-off that the priorities of Step 6 decide: compared with exact matching, probabilistic linkage found 485 more true links per study at the cost of less than one more false link (1.2 vs. 0.5). It would also have been recommended with a cost ratio of 20, and under a limit of 1% with a lower threshold (recall 94.7%, 0.86% false links). The robustness check points the same way: with error rates half as high as assumed, probabilistic linkage made 0.02% false links, and with twice the rates, exact matching found only 58.7% of the true links, while probabilistic linkage still found 87.0% and stayed within the limit (0.08% false links). Still, its share of false links lies close to the limit: when the simulation was repeated with other random numbers, it ranged from 0.05% to 0.10%, and in one of four repetitions it slightly exceeded the limit, so that exact matching would have been recommended. In such a close case, researchers should decide on the limit on substantive grounds before looking at the results; we return to this point in the Discussion.

Step 9: Applying the Procedure to Real Data

Once a procedure is chosen, wavelink provides a short R script that can be applied to the real data and produces the same links the simulation would produce. It returns the links with the step that made them, a file of uncertain links for manual review (if planned), and the waves with a person identifier. The app also provides a methods paragraph that describes the procedure and its expected accuracy, which can be reported directly inside a manuscript.

Simulation-Based Rules of Thumb

Beyond the study-specific guidance of wavelink, its underlying simulation approach can show which procedures work under which conditions in general. We therefore simulated 80 conditions with 40 studies each (Figures 2 and 3). Each study had two waves (retention 80%, 5% new participants) and a code of three to six parts: the initial of the first name and the day and month of birth (three parts), plus the surname initial (four), the mother's first-name initial (five), and a digit such as that of the house number (six). Error rates were half, one, two, or four times the literature defaults. We varied the wave size (300, 1,500, or 5,000 participants, with gender and age as other variables) and, at 1,500 participants, the other variables (none; gender and age; gender, age, and school). We compared exact matching, the rule-based tiers, the weighted score, and probabilistic linkage under the limit of 0.1% false links.

The results demonstrate that code length sets the limit: with three parts no procedure holds false links below 0.1%, and with four parts only a few conditions allow it. Procedures that tolerate typing errors recover far more true links than exact matching. At six parts and the literature-default error rate they find 93-95% of true links, exact matching 77%; at four times the default error rate, exact matching falls to about 50% (Figure 2). Larger waves make the limit harder to meet, because more candidate pairs create more chances for false links: at 5,000 participants per wave and four times the default error rate, only exact matching still meets the limit at six parts. However, other variables can compensate for a short code (Figure 3). For fivepart codes at the default error rate, adding gender and age raises the share of true links found from about 82% to 94%; adding school makes four-part codes usable across all error rates.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the workflow was illustrated with a single example data set, a school-based online panel of adolescents in three European countries comprising three waves of data collection. Designs that differ from this example (e.g., more waves, longer intervals between waves, other age groups, different cultural backgrounds) may yield different results, and the rules of thumb should therefore be applied with caution outside these simulated conditions. Second, the validity of the simulation depends on the accuracy of its input parameters. The default error and missing rates are derived from a small number of studies, conducted predominantly with school-aged or adult samples in different contexts, and studyspecific rates are rarely known prior to data collection. Although the robustness analysis quantifies the consequences of error rates that are half or twice as high as assumed, it cannot account for error structures that are not represented in the data-generating model.

Third, except for careless responding, errors are modeled as independent of participant characteristics, and the other variables are drawn independently of each other. In empirical data, however, errors may be concentrated in specific subgroups (e.g., younger participants, participants whose first language differs from the language of the survey, or marginalized groups), and other variables are related among different persons (e.g., age and grade level).

Under such conditions, linkage error is no longer random and may introduce bias beyond that implied by the simulated error rates. In the example data, a single EM over all variables converged to groups of pupils from the same school cohort instead of matches; wavelink therefore estimates the EM in two stages (Step 5), but other dependencies may remain that the simulation cannot reveal.

Fourth, the simulation assumes fully cleaned data and, where manual review is specified, error-free reviewer decisions; the reported performance should therefore be interpreted as an upper bound. Finally, the inclusion of auxiliary variables improves linkage accuracy but simultaneously increases the distinctiveness of individual records. This potential increase in re-identification risk needs to be weighed against the anonymity that motivates the use of SGICs, particularly when data and code are shared openly.

Discussion

Linking anonymous survey waves through self-generated identification codes typically relies on ad hoc tolerance rules with unknown error rates. This paper replaces such rules with a simulation-based workflow: researchers describe their study, compare matching procedures in simulated studies in which the true links are known, and apply the recommended procedure to their own data with an R script that produces the same links as the simulation. It also introduces the web app wavelink, which makes this workflow available without programming, and the example study shows that its predictions hold up on real data: the false links estimated for the rule-based procedure closely matched the simulation, and error-tolerant procedures recovered substantially more participants than exact matching.

Moreover, simulated rules of thumb suggest that no matching procedure is universally effective: the best choice depends on code length, wave size, the error rates of the code parts, and the auxiliary variables available beyond the SGIC. Furthermore, the simulation does not determine how much each type of linkage error costs in a substantive analysis. False links pose the greater challenge because they cannot be identified systematically, whereas missed links bias estimates only when whatever breaks the link also influences the association between predictor and outcome (Breen & Joo, 2026; Doidge & Harron, 2019; Rubin, 1976). In linked hospital records, bias from false matches grew when few records had a true counterpart (38% at a 10% match rate, 8% at 70%), and errors that differed between hospitals or related to the outcome distorted estimates most (Harron et al., 2014).

Yet, extant research still mostly lacks validated thresholds, or at least target ranges, for how many false links a study may tolerate. The example study shows why this aspect matters: probabilistic linkage was recommended with 1.2 false links per study, against about 2.3 allowed, in exchange for 485 additional true links, but in repeated simulations its share of false links came close to the limit or just above it, which would have reversed the recommendation. We therefore report the conditions under which procedures perform well and leave the choice of tolerance to researchers, because it depends on their study design and on the specific analyses they plan to perform with the linked data. For researchers who link waves with SGICs, we recommend five steps.

First, expect linkage error and plan for it. Linkage error can occur in every study, and it is even more likely with codes generated by adolescents. Linkage belongs to the measurement model, and its error structure is part of the data-generating process (Doidge & Harron, 2019). It can therefore affect every downstream analysis, not only the sample size. When planning a longitudinal study and deciding on the components of the SGIC, researchers should therefore also include auxiliary variables that support the linkage. The most common are stable demographic variables such as gender or country of birth, but variables of other types can help as well, such as the number of siblings (a number) or the name of the school (free text).

Second, identify the conditions that drive linkage error. These are the expected attrition and retention between waves, because a low share of true matches raises the risk of false links (Harron et al., 2014), and the auxiliary variables, such as gender, age, or school, that can support matching beyond the code (Figure 3). Once the waves are linked, comparing linked and unlinked participants may indicate whom the procedure loses (Harron et al., 2017).

Third, choose the procedure by simulation. Entering the design, the SGIC, the expected error rates, and the available auxiliary variables into wavelink returns the recall and the share of false links of each procedure, and thus the procedure that best suits the study.

Fourth, run sensitivity analyses. Estimate the main model under the chosen procedure, repeat it under at least one alternative, such as a stricter or a looser threshold, and compare the estimates. Stable estimates are robust to the linkage decision; estimates that change show that the decision taken on the linkage procedure matters to result interpretation (Gilbert et al., 2018).

Fifth, report the linkage transparently. State the procedure, its expected recall and share of false links, and the outcome of the sensitivity analyses (Gilbert et al., 2018).

Conclusion

SGICs allow researchers to track individuals across waves in longitudinal research without collecting identifying information. Linking them, however, involves a trade-off between missed and false links, and methodological guidance on matching procedures that protect data quality is scarce. Because the true links in real data are unknown, this trade-off has so far been managed with study-specific, ad hoc tolerance rules. To address these gaps, we developed the web app wavelink, which replaces such ad hoc choices among matching rules with insights from a simulation of the researcher's own study. It compares matching procedures in simulated studies that match the study design, code, and expected error rates. It recommends the procedure that misses the fewest links within the researchers' false-link limit and exports R code to apply to the real data, along with a methods paragraph for reporting.

The findings indicate that no single procedure is universally optimal. Code length sets the limit on accuracy; sample size per wave and error rates further decide which procedure performs best, and the availability of auxiliary variables compensates for short SGICs. We therefore strongly encourage researchers to treat linkage as part of the study design and the measurement model. This includes planning the SGIC and auxiliary variables with matching in mind, selecting and justifying a procedure through simulation, and reporting its expected accuracy alongside the substantive results.
