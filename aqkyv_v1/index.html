How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Psychologists often rely on observational studies for causal evidence, which raises the question of how to strengthen the design of observational studies for causal inference. One widely used approach in medical research is target trial emulation (TTE). In this tutorial, we introduce TTE to psychological research, with particular emphasis on its cornerstone concept that is routinely ignored in practice: time zero. Following a brief primer on TTE, we foreground time zero and highlight its critical role in improving valid causal inferences from observational data. Using examples drawn from psychological research, we systematically enumerate all possible permutations of the time zero components to show how their misalignment can introduce systematic biases, including selection bias, post-treatment confounding, treatment-dependent (in)eligibility, and immortal time bias, even when all baseline confounders are measured. To address this, we develop a decision framework for integrating time zero into research practices and offer practical recommendations. Through a running example of workplace AI use and employee burnout, we demonstrate how clearly specifying time zero clarifies causal questions, prevents many design-based biases, and strengthens internal validity. We further emphasize that time zero alignment is only one design feature of a strong observational study, and we delineate the boundaries of TTE, cautioning against the misconception that it is a magic fix that automatically delivers causal conclusions. We conclude that explicitly justifying time zero is a necessary step toward improving the rigor and transparency of causal research in psychology.

It's about time: Specifying time zero for target trial emulation in psychological research

Psychologists are often interested in answering causal questions. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or experiments are generally considered the preferred design for causal inference (Rubin, 2007; Shadish et al., 2002). In practice, however, RCTs are not always ethical or feasible (West et al., 2008). Compared with observational studies, they may impose stricter exclusion criteria and have lower external validity (Diener et al., 2022). Researchers therefore frequently turn to observational study designs that are less vulnerable to these limitations (Bailey et al., 2024; Grosz et al., 2020; Hammerton & Munafò, 2021), such as administrative or archival data, panel studies, and longitudinal surveys.

Although RCTs are not free of methodological problems (e.g., post-treatment loss to follow-up or partial compliance), causal inference from observational data presents additional challenges (Bailey et al., 2024; Poppe et al., 2025; Shadish et al., 2002). How can researchers strengthen the design of observational studies for causal inference? One well-established approach is target trial emulation (TTE). TTE is an organizing framework for guiding the design and analysis of observational studies and has been widely applied in fields such as medical, clinical, and epidemiological research (Hernán et al., 2025; Hernán & Robins, 2016; Hubbard et al., 2024). In this tutorial, we introduce TTE to psychological researchers with particular emphasis on its cornerstone concept --time zero. This article has three intended contributions. First, we raise awareness of design-based biases in observational studies. In current research practices, one well-recognized challenge of causal inference using observational data is confounding (Shadish et al., 2002; Steiner et al., 2010; Thoemmes & Kim, 2011; West et al., 2014): because treatment (or exposure) is not randomized, its association with covariates (which are also associated with the outcome) can introduce bias (Rubin, 2007). However, confounding due to nonrandomized treatment is only one of several threats to causal inference in observational studies. Compared to RCTs, observational studies are vulnerable to additional biases due to design flaws. One often-overlooked design flaw is the failure to specify or correctly align time zero, which can introduce a range of biases, including those familiar to psychologists, such as selection bias and missing data, as well as lesser-known biases such as post-treatment confounding, immortal time bias, and prevalent-user bias (Hernán et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2022). Adopting TTE can help prevent these design-based biases.

Second, we adapt the TTE framework for psychological research. Although the literature on TTE is extensive, it has focused predominantly on medical (vaccines or surgical) and pharmacological (drug) treatments using electronic medical records, administrative, and claims databases (Y. Ren et al., 2026; Simon-Tillaux et al., 2024). Here, we embed TTE and time zero firmly within psychological science, which presents its own set of challenges. To illustrate, we draw on research questions from the broad literatures in psychological science, including artificial intelligence (AI) use in the workplace, intimate partner violence, tenure and promotion decisions in academia, and child development. Through these examples grounded in psychological research, we direct readers' attention to the pitfalls of leaving time zero unspecified in study designs that are common in psychological science, such as prevalent user bias. Note that we draw on these literatures solely for illustrative purposes. Our aim is not to critique any particular study or literature, but to make the methodological issues concrete.

Finally, we provide practical recommendations for implementing TTE with particular attention to time zero. Although time zero is central to TTE (Fu et al., 2026; Hernán et al., 2016), it is often poorly implemented even in studies that claim to emulate a target trial (McIntyre et al., 2025; Simon-Tillaux et al., 2024). As we will discuss in the next section, many elements of TTE are already familiar to applied researchers in psychology, including designing RCTs, prespecifying eligibility criteria, and committing to analysis plans before data collection or access. By contrast, time zero has received little attention in psychology and often goes unnoticed in research practices. We therefore explain how to specify time zero and integrate it into existing research practices when designing observational studies.

The remainder of the article proceeds as follows. We first introduce TTE and explain the role of time zero within the framework. Next, we explain what time zero is and why it is an important design feature of an observational study. To illustrate its importance, we take a systematic approach to explaining why the three components of time zero must be aligned at the same time point by enumerating all possible permutations of the time zero components and explicating the repercussions of their misalignment. In the third section, we provide practical guidance for selecting time zero. We present a visual decision flowchart, recommendations for more complex designs, and a worked example of AI use and burnout.

Using the example, we demonstrate how TTE can be integrated with established best research practices such as preregistration and registered reports. Finally, we embed time zero and TTE within broader considerations of causal inference, emphasizing that specifying time zero is necessary yet insufficient, and that TTE is neither the gold standard nor a magic fix for making causal claims.

To ensure accessibility, we minimize technical detail throughout and instead use plain language, simple visualizations, and intuitive examples familiar to a broad audience. Do-operator

An idealized hypothetical intervention that conceptually sets a variable to a fixed value (Pearl, 2009).

Immortal time bias

"Immortal time" refers to a period after follow-up during which, by design, the outcome event cannot occur. For instance, the interval a participant must survive, or must remain in a particular state, before treatment is initiated. When this event-free interval is misattributed to the treated condition, the treatment may appear spuriously protective (Hernán et al., 2016; Suissa, 2008).

Negative control outcomes

These are variables that are justifiably unaffected by treatment but share the same confounders as the focal outcomes (Lipsitch et al., 2010; Loh, 2025). They may be used to either test for, or to identify the causal effect in, the presence of unmeasured confounding.foot_0

Nonparametrically point-identified

A causal estimand is nonparametrically point identified if, under a set of qualitative (i.e., nonparametric) causal assumptions, it can be recovered exactly from an infinite amount of data, without any parametric restrictions on functional forms or distributions of the observed variables.

Partial identification;

A causal estimand is nonparametrically partially identified if, under a set of causal assumptions, only a range of values can be determined from the observed data. The deterministic bounds deterministic bounds are the upper and lower endpoints of that range, and they will not generally collapse to zero even in infinite data (Kaiser et al., 2026; Manski, 1990).

Post-treatment confounder

A variable that is simultaneously affected by an earlier treatment and a confounder of a later treatment and outcome. Either adjusting or not adjusting for the variable induces bias. It is also termed a time-varying or treatment-dependent confounder.

Various methods have been developed to address the challenge of post-treatment confounding when assessing the sustained causal effects of time-varying treatments.

Introductions of these methods in the psychological and behavioral research literature include marginal structural models using inverse probability weighting (Kennedy et al., 2023; Thoemmes & Ong, 2016), parametric g-formula (Loh, 2026b; Loh et al., 2024), and structural nested mean models using g-estimation (Loh, 2026a; Loh & Ren, 2023a).

Potential outcomes

The outcome values for an individual under each treatment condition, including conditions the individual did not in fact receive. For example, each individual has two potential outcomes for a binary treatment, with exactly one outcome factually observable. Causal effects can be defined as contrasts between potential outcomes.

Prevalent user bias

This bias arises when a study enrolls participants who already initiated treatment prior to the start of the study (prevalent or current users). Conditioning on current users selects a non-representative subset of those who either tolerated or benefited from the treatment; in addition, measurements of baseline covariates may not be possible. A standard fix is new-user designs, restricting to participants who start the treatment (new, or "incident," users) at the start of the study (Ray, 2003).

Routine analysis

This refers to the default comparison of treated versus untreated conditions exactly as they happen to be recorded, without first verifying that eligibility, treatment initiation, and the start of follow-up are aligned at a time zero. The absence of verification is precisely the practice we caution against. We use this term to flag the design flaw of time zero misalignment.

Stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA)

The assumption (Rubin, 1986) that: (i) an individual's potential outcomes do not depend on the treatments of other individuals (no treatment interference; Hudgens & Halloran, 2008; Loh & Ren, 2022); and (ii) each treatment level corresponds to a single, well-defined version of the treatment (no multiple versions of treatment; Cole & Frangakis, 2009).

Structural counterfactuals

Counterfactual quantities defined by the system of equations that describe a structural causal model. The value a variable would counterfactually take is derived from the model by externally manipulating a causally upstream variable using the do-operator (Pearl, 2009).

A brief primer on Target Trial Emulation

We first orient readers to the TTE framework (Hernán et al., 2025; Hernán & Robins, 2016) before turning to time zero specifically.

What is TTE? TTE is an organizing framework for improving the quality of observational research for causal inference (Hernán & Robins, 2016). TTE guides researchers seeking causal inferences using observational data to conceptualize a hypothetical randomized experiment for answering the same research question (Hernán et al., 2025). Specifying this target trial helps researchers navigate adhering to the rigorous design principles of an RCT (e.g., a clearly specified time zero), strengthen the design and analysis of observational data (e.g., confounding adjustment), reduce the risks of design-based biases, and enhance the validity of research conclusions (Hernán et al., 2025; Hubbard et al., 2024; Matthews et al., 2022). TTE has been widely applied in medical and clinical research. For example, TTE has been applied to estimate the effect of antibiotic initiation in pregnancy on preterm delivery (Caniglia et al., 2023). For systematic reviews of its applications in medical and clinical journals, please see Y. Ren et al. (2026) and Simon-Tillaux et al. (2024). The adoption of TTE in psychological or behavioral sciences remains rare. In a recent exception, scholars applied TTE to estimate the impact of prescribing psychologists (compared to psychiatrists or primary care physicians) on patient outcomes (Hughes et al., 2025).

A target trial emulation proceeds in two iterative steps. First, one explicitly specifies the protocol of a hypothetical trial ("specification"): its eligibility criteria, the treatment strategies being compared, the assignment procedure, the outcomes, the follow-up period, the causal contrast of interest, and the analysis plan (Table 2). Second, one mimics the target trial's structure, by emulating each component as closely as the observational data allow ("emulation") (Hernán & Robins, 2016). TTE provides a unifying framework that integrates existing recommendations and established best practices into a streamlined, user-friendly guide (Hernán et al., 2025). It is intended to support applied researchers in navigating the estimand-to-estimate pathway and in translating causal questions into appropriate study designs and analyses. Researchers already familiar with the causal inference literature may not find TTE novel. However, TTE is designed for a broader audience: applied researchers across diverse fields who may be less familiar with modern design principles and analytical methods for drawing causal inferences from observational data. For example, researchers may be unfamiliar with formalizing a research question within the potential outcomes framework. TTE addresses this by guiding researchers to conceptualize a hypothetical target trial, specify its concrete components, and define the specific contrast of potential outcomes the study aims to estimate. In this way, TTE helps researchers translate vague research questions into a clear causal estimand, and determine whether their research question can be answered with the data at hand. The importance of well-defined causal estimands has been increasingly recognized in psychological and social sciences (Junker et al., 2025; Lundberg et al., 2021; D. Ren et al., 2025).

TTE can be seamlessly integrated into the best research practices in psychological science. TTE builds on psychology researchers' established foundation and deep knowledge of designing randomized studies (Diener et al., 2022; Shadish et al., 2002). Importantly, TTE aligns well with existing best practices in psychological science, such as preregistration and registered reports (Chambers, 2013; Shadish et al., 2002). Many elements of a target trial protocol, such as specifying treatments, outcomes, and analysis plans, are already regularly preregistered in practice.

However, a crucial component of TTE remains overlooked in psychology and absent from current research practices: time zero alignment. Time zero alignment requires that eligibility, treatment assignment, and the start of follow-up be aligned to the same time point. In the next sections, we will focus on time zero: we define it, show how its misspecification introduces biases, and provide practical guidance for specifying it as part of a target trial protocol.

Time zero: What is it and why does it matter?

Let us start with an illustrative example. Suppose an organization aims to reduce employee burnout by providing AI tools to its employees. At the beginning of a new year, the organization introduced AIpowered virtual assistants for employees to use in their daily work (Ranganathan & Ye, 2026). Their question is: does using AI at work (X) reduce or worsen employee burnout at the end of the year (Y)? Answering this question accurately is important to help the organization decide whether to make AI tools available at work (Ranganathan & Ye, 2026). To answer this question, one intuitive and widely adopted study design is as follows: We will survey employees about whether they use AI tools at work (X; yes or no) after a period following roll-out, then measure their burnout levels at the end of the year (Y). This design allows us to compare the outcome of interest (burnout) between employees who reported using AI at work and those who did not.

Does this design enable valid causal inferences?

Although this design may seem intuitive, several important methodological issues remain unaddressed for valid causal inference. Here and throughout, to foreground the notion of time zero, we set aside pretreatment baseline confounding, and assume that all potential confounders, such as baseline burnout, are precisely recorded for adjustment. We also set aside for the moment other important issues that could arise in both RCTs and observational studies, such as post-treatment nonadherence and loss to follow up, and return to these later.

Even setting aside these other methodological considerations, this design is flawed because it overlooks specifying a core element: time zero. To understand time zero, let us envision how an ideal randomized experiment or target trial could have been implemented. Ideally, when the organization is ready to introduce AI tools in the workplace (e.g., the first month of the year), employees would be randomly assigned to one of two groups: (A) a treatment group, whose members are randomized to use AI tools at work, and (B) a control group, whose members are randomized to not use AI tools at work. Following this randomized assignment, we would measure the outcome of interest-in this case, employee burnout at the end of the year. Under this experimental setup, three distinct conditions are (implicitly) met at the same moment: 1) Eligibility is determined: being an employee at the organization when AI tools are introduced.

2) Treatment is assigned: employees are randomized to either use AI tools or not.

3) Follow-up begins: researchers start observing or measuring possible outcomes such as burnout.

Time zero is defined as the moment when all three conditions are met concurrently: eligibility is determined, treatment is assigned, and follow-up of outcomes begins (Fu et al., 2026; Hernán & Robins, 2016). In other words, time zero marks the starting point of the causal process we are investigating: when the treatment is initiated among eligible individuals and any possible effects on the outcome can begin to emerge.

So why is time zero important for valid causal inferences? When time zero is specified inappropriately, so that eligibility, treatment assignment, and follow-up are misaligned, biases can arise, leading to incorrect conclusions. While time zero may be determined by design in RCTs, it must be carefully and justifiably specified in observational designs. To illustrate this, consider again the observational study design examining AI tools at work and employee burnout in our motivating example. At the start of the year (the first month), the AI tools are first introduced. At this moment, participants (employees) become eligible for the study, and any possible influence of their treatment on the outcome, as well as their subsequent treatment status, starts. However, AI use is surveyed (i.e., "assigned") only later (e.g., after six months). This misalignment introduces bias.

To formalize the problems, we used a causal diagram representing the possible data-generating mechanisms. To further empirically demonstrate biases resulting from the inappropriate time zero in the study design, we conducted a Monte Carlo simulation study. The causal diagram, the simulation results, full R script, including details of the data-generating processes, are available online for interested readers (https://osf.io/mqjwx/). Here, for clarity and readability, we will unpack the possible problems using hypothetical examples and discuss each in turn.

Problem 1: Selection affected by treatment

Consider an employee, Rob. Rob started using the AI tool upon its rollout. Using AI, however, undermined his subsequent productivity and caused burnout symptoms which made him resign from his position at the organization. Crucially, because his turnover occurred before the survey of AI use, his information on treatment (and outcome) was not recorded in the study. Failing to capture observations such as Rob introduces outcome-associated selection bias (Lu et al., 2022 (Lu et al., , 2024;; D. Ren & Loh, 2024) and could lead to an underestimation of the impacts of introducing AI at work.

Problem 2: Selection unaffected by treatment

We can also consider another employee, Emma. While Emma also experienced burnout symptoms, the symptoms were not caused by using AI. Nonetheless, she also left the organization before her data (treatment assignment and outcome) could be recorded. This type of dropout represents another form of outcome-associated selection bias (Lu et al., 2022 (Lu et al., , 2024)), leading to incorrect conclusions.

Problem 3: Post-treatment confounding

Even when dropout does not occur, the inappropriately specified time zero remains problematic. We illustrate this with a different employee, Mark. Mark used the AI tools at work and felt that AI undermined his productivity and caused burnout symptoms. He subsequently stopped using AI before the survey of AI use. Hence, his treatment assignment was recorded in the survey as "non-user", potentially leading to an underestimation of the negative impacts of introducing AI at work. This exemplifies how only prevalent users who continued using AI up to the time of the survey are recorded as "users." This problem arises due to post-treatment confounding (see Table 1): burnout at time t is simultaneously affected by earlier AI use at time t-1, associated with burnout at other times due to a shared underlying process, and a confounder of later AI use at t+1 and burnout at t+2 (Loh & Ren, 2023b).

As illustrated using the above examples, the proposed study design -while intuitive and common, has an inappropriate time zero. This design flaw alone could lead to wrong results. Note that the problems described above are not mutually exclusive and may co-exist in real-world data. Moreover, they do not represent an exhaustive list of potential biases that could arise from the proposed study design. The core issue we have focused on in the study design stems from the misalignment of the three conditions that must be met at time zero: although eligibility began in the first month, treatment assignment was only recorded after a delay. As a result, the "users" (X=1, employee exposed to AI) in the survey dataset captured only current or prevalent users (Hernán & Robins, 2016; Matthews et al., 2022), who have consistently used AI leading up to the survey because they were unaffected by, or benefited from, using AI. Such prevalent user designs (the treatment initiation took place before the study began) are common in some survey-based psychological research: treatments of interest are often recorded via surveys about participants' past experiences, events, or behaviors. In such designs, the treatment already initiated before the study began.

Misaligned components: Considering all possible permutations

So far, we have used an AI and burnout example to introduce the concept of time zero and illustrate the problems of misaligned components. This example featured a specific misalignment: eligibility is determined before treatment initiation, then follow-up begins after a delay. In addition to this specific misalignment, other misalignments are possible. What are these scenarios? Are any of them "benign" and not prone to biases? We will address these questions next.

Scenario B

In Scenario B, follow-up is delayed after treatment initiation, which can introduce selection bias (e.g., dropout may be affected or unaffected by treatment) and post-treatment confounding. Unlike scenario A, this scenario introduces an additional complication and source of bias. Because eligibility is assessed after treatment initiation, treatment itself may influence whether participants meet or fail to meet eligibility criteria -a problem we term treatment-dependent (in)eligibility.

To contextualize this misalignment, we draw on the psychological literature examining the well-being benefits of pet ownership (Allen, 2003; Herzog, 2011; Kretzler et al., 2022). Consider a hypothetical study examining the effect of living with a dog on life satisfaction among current college students without pre-existing mental health conditions. Students meeting that latter criterion enroll in the study, report whether they currently live with a dog, and complete a life satisfaction measure. A routine analysis would then compare those living with a dog and those without one, and examine their life satisfaction outcomes. But this design is susceptible to three sources of bias due to an unclear time zero. First, students who previously enrolled in college but may have subsequently dropped out would thus be absent from the study sample (selection bias). Second, students who were formerly dog owners but found dog ownership unmanageable during college, due to reasons such as busy schedules or restrictive residential situations, may have rehomed the dog before data collection. These students would then be considered non-owners at the time of assessment (post-treatment confounding). As a result, the dogowner group is likely disproportionately composed of those who received support or accommodations for dog ownership and could afford to maintain dog ownership during college. Third, and most crucially for this scenario, living with a dog may have altered students' eligibility status (treatment-dependent eligibility): if dog ownership confers mental health benefits, a student who would otherwise not have met the eligibility criterion (i.e., pre-existing mental health condition at the time of study) might be rendered eligible precisely because they own a dog.

Scenario C

Scenario C, like Scenario B, is susceptible to selection bias, post-treatment confounding, and treatmentinduced (in)eligibility. Here, we use a different research area for illustration: leadership science (Hopp & Pruschak, 2023; Tackett et al., 2023). Consider a study examining whether holding a leadership position in high school predicts income 50 years later. Researchers draw on a longitudinal panel dataset, recruiting participants who are currently employed and asking them to retrospectively report any leadership roles held during high school. These self-reports are then linked to current income records available in the panel. A routine analysis would compare those who reported being a leader in high school and those who were not, and examine their income levels. This design is also susceptible to three sources of bias. Participants may have become unavailable for the current survey (e.g., moving to a different state or country, or death, after high school) and are therefore absent from the sample (selection bias). Students who held leadership positions in their early years in high school but could not maintain their roles may have stepped down (in)voluntarily and considered themselves non-leaders instead (post-treatment confounding). Holding a leadership position in high school may have shaped educational opportunities, which in turn influence long-term career trajectories -including the likelihood of remaining employed at the time of the study (treatment-dependent eligibility). Because current employment is an eligibility criterion, treatment (high school leadership experience) may have systematically affected who was eligible to participate.

Scenario D

In scenarios A-C, follow-up was delayed after treatment initiation, a common occurrence in psychological research when participants are surveyed about their past experiences, behaviors, or events (i.e., treatment). However, follow-up may conversely precede treatment initiation. This problem, as shown in scenarios D-F, is well recognized and extensively discussed in medical research. For example, a patient receives a diagnosis (follow-up begins) based on administrative data or medical records, but treatment is considered initiated only after a waiting period. This misalignment introduces two distinct problems: patients with more severe pre-existing conditions may not survive long enough to initiate treatment (selection bias); and because the waiting period is incorrectly attributed to the treatment condition, it precludes the occurrence of time-to-event outcomes for individuals who subsequently initiate treatment, potentially making the treatment appear more effective than it actually is (immortal time bias).

Similar scenarios can arise in psychological research. We will draw on the literature on intimate partner violence (IPV) (Arriaga et al., 2026; Babcock et al., 2004; Bell & Naugle, 2008; Birkley & Eckhardt, 2015; Farrell et al., 2023). Suppose researchers are interested in examining the effect of IPV on relationship longevity (operationalized as time to separation) among married couples. Newlyweds are recruited and monitored for subsequent IPV experiences. A routine analysis would then compare those who reported IPV with those who did not and examine the time to separation. This design is susceptible to selection bias: couples may separate and drop out of the study before any IPV occurs, thereby systematically underrepresenting less committed relationships. It is also susceptible to immortal time bias: the period between getting married and reporting the occurrence of IPV may be misattributed to the IPV-exposed condition because separation could not have occurred during this period, artificially extending the apparent duration of relationships in that group and obscuring the true harmful effects of IPV on relationship longevity.

Scenario E

This is another possible scenario that introduces the risk of selection bias and immortal time bias. Here we draw on the literature on tenure and promotion decisions in academic careers (Ambrose & Cropanzano, 2003; Azmitia et al., 2025; Masters-Waage et al., 2024; Treviño et al., 2018). Consider a study examining whether winning an NSF CAREER award accelerates promotion to full professor among tenure-track faculty. Researchers construct a dataset that records the date of PhD conferral (initiating follow-up), the start of the first tenure-track position (establishing eligibility), whether each individual received an NSF CAREER award, and the date of promotion to full professor. A routine analysis would then compare those who won the award with those who did not, and examine the number of years required to become a full professor. This design is susceptible to selection bias: tenure-track faculty who face barriers in academia such as underrepresented minority scholars may leave before they have the opportunity to win the award, meaning that those who remain (and thus appear in the sample) are systematically different from those who left. It is also susceptible to immortal time bias: faculty must remain on the tenure track and cannot become tenured as a full professor prior to winning the award by definition, and misattributing this pre-award period to the awarded condition artificially lengthens the time-to-promotion for award recipients, undermining the potential benefits of winning the award.

Scenario F

Scenario F is susceptible not only to selection bias and immortal time bias, but also to treatmentdependent (in)eligibility. Here, we draw on research from developmental science on unstructured free play for children (Dankiw et al., 2020; Pellegrini & Smith, 1998; Pellis & Pellis, 2007). Consider a study examining whether participation in an unstructured play training program improves children's motor skill development. Researchers enroll a cohort of kindergarten students at the start of the year. The training program on unstructured play comprises 10 sessions over three months. Children who attend at least 8 of the 10 sessions receive a completion certificate. A routine analysis would then compare motor skill outcomes of children who received the certificate against those who did not. This design is susceptible to selection bias: children whose caregivers face barriers such as financial hardship or lack of familial support may not even be enrolled, systematically excluding the most at-risk families from the study sample. It is also susceptible to immortal time bias: between study enrollment and receiving the certificate, the child must remain enrolled and continue attending sessions in order to meet the attendance requirement. As a result, children who persist long enough to qualify for the certificate tend to be those who were already developing well, which can artificially exaggerate the benefits of the training program. Finally, it is susceptible to treatment-induced (in)eligibility: participation in a training program may improve parents' perceived support from the kindergarten, thereby affecting whether they decide to continue enrolling their children and ultimately meet the eligibility criterion.

Guidance for integrating time zero into research practices in psychological science

We have so far illustrated how misaligned time zero components, regardless of the specific orderings, lead to wrong conclusions. So how can we specify an appropriate time zero with all components aligned as part of the TTE protocol?

To facilitate specifying time zero, we propose that at the start of the research process -before collecting primary data or analyzing secondary data, researchers could work with a decision framework as shown in Figure 2 to specify time zero, and include it as part of their (preregistered) research protocol. As shown in Figure 2, specifying time zero is an iterative process, as selecting a time zero requires aligning all three components while taking into account data availability, through either accessing existing data or collecting new data. In what follows, we elaborate on the decision framework and offer practical recommendations for specifying time zero in psychological science.

Recommendation 1: Redefine the causal effect of interest when necessary

The conceptualized target trial is not intended to represent an idealized trial. For example, a blinded target trial in which participants are unaware of their assignment cannot be emulated using observational data. Rather, the conceptualized target trial is a pragmatic trial grounded in data currently available or that can feasibly be collected. When specifying time zero, relevant data at the time that follow-up starts must either already exist or be realistically obtainable. Otherwise, the causal effect of interest needs to be revised (Figure 2).

When a treatment is novel (e.g., the rollout of new AI tools in an organization), it is possible to design an observational study, just as in an experiment, in which follow-up begins at the very first time the treatment was introduced. However, psychologists are often interested in treatments that may already have been initiated or implemented, so that some individuals may have been exposed to or treated before the start of the study or follow-up. This complicates the specification of time zero, particularly when historical data are unavailable. Nonetheless, as we will illustrate through the example below, time zero need not be the singular moment when a treatment first became available. Researchers could redefine the target trial and causal effect of interest to accommodate data availability (Bennett et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026; Hernán & Robins, 2016).

Suppose researchers are interested in examining whether a norm-based recycling information campaign (informing residents that most others in their community already recycle) increases individual recycling behavior in a nation. The campaign was adopted on a small scale before 2020; and in 2020, the government officially encouraged its adoption nationwide. Ideally, researchers would have access to archival records documenting the first time each individual was exposed to the campaign. However, such data may not be available. A practical solution is to redefine the eligibility criteria to include only individuals who had not previously been exposed to the campaign (i.e., excluding those exposed before 2020). In this design, the year the nationwide campaign started (2020) can serve as time zero because it is the point at which eligibility is determined (individuals who had not been exposed to the campaign), treatment is assigned (receiving the recycling information or not), and follow-up begins (recycling behavior).

This example requires pinpointing the moment of treatment initiation, which may not always be feasible or known with certainty. For example, when participants are instructed to recall the onset of a treatment, they may not know or accurately remember when it began; in intervention studies, the onset of a multi-session or gradually adopted treatment can depend on the time frame (Lawes et al., 2025); or the causal process under study may already be underway before the first recorded observation (Browne & Zhang, 2007; Gollob & Reichardt, 1987). When onset is uncertain, the chosen definition of time zero should be stated explicitly and, ideally, examined in a sensitivity analysis. In the next recommendation, we explicitly encourage researchers to consider multiple time zeros.

Recommendation 2: Consider multiple defensible time zeros

In practice, the availability of rich longitudinal data may make it plausible that multiple defensible time zeros exist. Importantly, each candidate time zero corresponds to a different target trial and causal estimand. Consider gender inequality in academia as an example. Suppose researchers seek to examine whether women scholars are less likely than men to succeed in academia. One challenge is determining when scholars first become at risk of experiencing gender inequality. Gender inequality may arise at different stages of an academic career and in various contexts, including graduate admissions, supervisor support, access to tenure-track positions, promotion and tenure decisions, journal review processes, and research funding applications. Each stage and context implies a different time zero and target trial. Researchers should first specify the question they seek to answer and the corresponding estimand, as these decisions determine the appropriate time zero and ultimately, the research results.

As an illustration, consider hiring decisions for tenure track positions. One study may define time zero as the moment an application is submitted and then track applicants through the hiring process. Another study might instead define time zero as the year of PhD completion and follow individuals to assess whether they secure a tenure-track position. Although related, these studies address distinct causal questions. The first examines gender differences in the probability of being hired during the hiring process, whereas the second examines gender differences in the probability of securing a tenure track position following completion of PhD training. Because these are different questions, they warrant different time zeros and different target trials, and thus may yield different conclusions. Researchers may find no evidence of gender bias during the hiring process alone, yet still observe gender disparities in obtaining tenure-track positions over a longer time frame. The latter disparities may arise from earlier stages in the pipeline-for example, if women with PhDs are less likely to apply for tenure-track positions because of lower levels of mentor support. Ultimately, researchers must decide which question they seek to answer and then select the time zero that aligns with that question when designing their target trial. This process can be repeated when researchers seek to address multiple research questions.

In some cases, however, a single research question may have multiple defensible time zeros. In such cases, researchers may emulate a sequence of target trials. Consider graduate schools offering mentoring programs in which graduate students are paired with mentors (outside their PhD supervision team) for academic guidance and support. Suppose students become eligible to enroll at the start of each academic year, with some enrolling during their first year, others enrolling later, and some never enrolling. To assess whether mentoring programs improve academic performance, researchers can conceptualize a sequence of target trials in which students become eligible at the beginning of each academic year. At the start of each year, researchers identify eligible students (those who have not previously enrolled), assign treatment (participate or not participate in the mentoring program) and follow them forward in time. This design effectively creates a sequence of target trials, each aligned to a different time zero.

When should multiple time zeros be considered? One scenario is when the treatment can be initiated at different times and treatment initiation affects participants' eligibility, as in the mentoring program example above and the workplace AI use example (which we return to later, in the section titled "A worked example"). Multiple time zeros may also be useful when systematically comparing candidate time zeros as a sensitivity analysis for a treatment onset that cannot be pinpointed precisely. In contrast, multiple time zeros are undesirable when alternative time zeros correspond to different estimands or populations that are not of substantive interest. Researchers should also avoid blindly exploring a set of time zeros without theoretical guidance. In particular, we advise against selectively choosing time zeros based on whether they yield findings deemed more interesting or publishable (e.g., statistically significant results). We therefore recommend specifying and justifying the time zero(s) before data analysis through preregistration (Figure 2).

Recommendation 3. Specifying time zero for time varying treatments.

In psychological research, treatments may not occur only once at a single point in time but instead recur over the course of follow-up. In such longitudinal settings, designs with repeated measurements of both treatment and outcome can strengthen causal inference relative to a single post-treatment assessment. However, valid estimation of sustained treatment effects over time requires strong, explicitly stated causal assumptions and additional design considerations. When a treatment is time-varying, substantive interest often lies in assessing the causal effects from comparing different treatment regimes or strategies. These may be either static (e.g., using AI throughout the study vs. not using AI throughout the study) or dynamic (e.g., adaptively starting, stopping, and restarting the use of AI tools depending on interim behaviors or responses). The concept of time zero applies directly to the initial treatment instance. The target trial being emulated can then be considered a multi-arm randomized study that compares distinct strategies in which the treatment, starting at time zero, varies over time through the end of follow-up, with each strategy as a different experimental condition (McGee et al., 2025). In these settings, causal effect estimation using the observed data becomes more complex not only because both confounders and outcomes may change over time, but also because they may be influenced by earlier treatments (Loh & Ren, 2023b). Addressing such complex scenarios often requires specialized causal inference methods that can handle post-treatment confounding for recurring treatments (Hernán et al., 2025; Hernán & Robins, 2016; Loh & Jorgensen, 2025; also see Table 1). Readers seeking a fuller treatment of time-zero specification and confounding adjustment for time-varying treatments should consult the relevant chapters in Hernán and Robins (2020).

A worked example

To concretely illustrate how to apply TTE and specify time zero in practice, we will continue with our first example of introducing AI at work to address employee burnout. Recall that the question is: does using AI at work influence employee burnout? In this example, employees may start using AI at work at different times. That is, at any given time, only some employees may begin using AI tools at work, corresponding to multiple defensible time zeros. This can be resolved by adopting the sequence-oftarget-trials design (Caniglia et al., 2023; Hernán & Robins, 2016). Using calendar month as the time interval, we can emulate a sequence of monthly target trials, one for each month over the rollout window (say, months 1 through 12). The causal estimand for the trial in month m is defined as the effect of initiating AI use at work in month m, versus not initiating in that month, on burnout assessed the following month. Pooling across the monthly trials targets the effect of initiating AI use at any point during the rollout window.

Following TTE, we first specify a protocol for the target trial. In this target trial, the organization randomizes the use of AI tools to eligible new users: employees who have not yet used AI tools prior to month m. Then, we would measure employees' burnout levels in the subsequent month. In each monthly trial, month m is time zero: the time point when eligibility is met (being an employee & not yet previously used AI at work), treatment (AI use) is initiated, and follow-up begins.

Next, we emulate this target trial using an observational study design. In medical settings, the date of vaccine administration, prescription or dispensing of a drug, or a surgical procedure, serves as an indicator of treatment initiation (Hernán et al., 2025; Hernán & Robins, 2016; Y. Ren et al., 2026; Simon-Tillaux et al., 2024). In our running example, because AI use was not assigned externally or controlled in practice, employees' naturally occurring use of AI is simply recorded (Paul & Horwitz, 2026) as the indicator of treatment initiation. Same as in the target trial, we then measure employees' burnout in the subsequent month.

We emphasize two features of this design. First, the causal estimand is trial-specific. The trial beginning in month m is restricted to employees who had not used AI at work before month m; its estimand is the effect of initiating AI use in month m among employees who remained previously untreated. The design therefore yields a series of different estimands, each generalizing to a different population. Second, follow-up in this design is deliberately short: the burnout outcome is assessed in the subsequent month m + 1. Longer follow-up times may require methods that are designed to handle post-treatment confounding (see Recommendation 3).

Embedding time zero within broader considerations of causal inference

In this article, we drew on examples of research questions from various areas in psychological science to explain what time zero is, why it matters, and how to specify it in practice guided by a TTE framework. Through this nontechnical presentation, we aimed to raise awareness of the importance of time zero in observational studies for causal inference in psychological science. We encourage researchers to use TTE to carefully justify how time zero is specified by emulating the design principles of RCTs familiar to many psychology researchers. This approach is useful for designing new studies prior to data collection and for analyzing existing datasets (Hernán et al., 2025). As we illustrated in previous sections, carefully and explicitly specifying time zero helps researchers articulate precise causal questions, prevent common design-based biases, and improve the likelihood of obtaining meaningful and valid answers (Fu et al., 2026; Hernán & Robins, 2016).

Time zero is broadly relevant to psychological science as researchers increasingly turn to observational data to answer causal questions across various domains. We offer a few selected examples here. Largescale longitudinal surveys have been used to examine meat consumption as a potential causal factor in depression (N. P.-J. Tan et al., 2026). Consumer complaints submitted to the US consumer Financial Protection Bureau have been analyzed to assess how large language models affect the probability of obtaining relief (Shin et al., 2026). TED talks have been studied to investigate the relationship between speakers' accents and online engagement (Zewail et al., 2025). Faculty members' promotion and tenure decisions at US universities have been used to demonstrate how underrepresented minority status reduces the chances of receiving positive outcomes (Masters-Waage et al., 2024). Together, these examples highlight researchers' interest in using real-world, observational data for causal inference in psychological research. The flexibility and rigor of TTE make it an invaluable framework for specifying a research protocol, particularly the often-overlooked component of time zero. Specifying time zero supports generating more robust evidence to inform theory, intervention, and decision-making across a wide range of psychological research areas.

TTE is useful for guiding research practices in psychology. But it is just as important to clarify the boundaries of TTE to establish a more accurate understanding of what it is and what it is not. In this section, we discuss three perennial questions about causal inference that may arise when using TTE.

Although some of these discussions concern long-standing debates and are not specific to TTE or time zero, we include them here because these issues may not be well known to our broad audience of psychological researchers.

What kind of causal questions is TTE most applicable to?

There are broadly two kinds of causal questions (Dawid & Musio, 2022; Holland & Rubin, 1988; Pearl, 2009). An "effect of a cause" (EoC) question fixes the cause and looks forward to the putative effect or outcome; for example, if a person were to begin using AI tools at work, what would their burnout be? In contrast, a "cause of an effect" (CoE) question starts from an observed outcome or effect and looks backward for a putative cause; for example, if a person experienced burnout after using AI tools to improve work productivity, was using AI the cause of burnout? The distinction may be delineated in terms of different study designs: EoC questions can be investigated using randomized or prospective observational designs, whereas CoE questions can be investigated using case-control or retrospective designs that select on the outcome. The latter designs, which select on the outcome, are structurally prone to the kinds of selection biases we discussed. While the former designs enroll participants before outcomes occur, they can nonetheless also be subject to other forms of selection biases, as we have explained. However, we argue that both types of causal questions are fundamentally assessing, counterfactually, whether the effect would have occurred absent the cause; hence, they can be formalized in terms of potential outcomes using the structural semantics of counterfactuals (Pearl, 2015). As Pearl states: "Consequently, going from effects to causes does not require extra mathematical machinery beyond that used in going from causes to effects." (Pearl, 2015, p. 151).

We acknowledge that a key challenge for CoE causal estimands is that they generally cannot be nonparametrically point-identified due to selection on the outcome, thus requiring either parametric assumptions (Pearl, 2009) or permitting only partial identification to yield deterministic bounds (Pearl, 2015). We briefly introduce a putative CoE causal estimand, explain why it selects on the outcome, and the counterfactual quantities required for identification. Let 𝑌 ! denote an individual's potential outcome 𝑌 had they, possibly counterfactually, received treatment 𝑋 = 𝑥. A CoE causal estimand (Pearl, 2015) is then: 𝑃𝑟'𝑌 ! ! = 𝑦 " |𝑌 = 𝑦, 𝑋 = 𝑥+. In words, this is the probability that, among those whose observed outcome and treatment were 𝑌 = 𝑦 and 𝑋 = 𝑥, respectively, their potential outcome under the unobserved (and different) treatment level 𝑥 " would have taken a different value (𝑌 ! ! = 𝑦 " ). Continuing our motivating example, among those who experienced burnout (𝑌 = 1) after using AI tools (𝑋 = 1), what is the probability that they would not have experienced burnout (𝑌 # = 0) had they counterfactually not used AI tools (𝑋 = 0)? This is the CoE causal estimand: it describes the probability that the treatment was necessary for causing the outcome, in the sense that a different outcome would have been more likely to occur had the treatment been counterfactually different (Pearl, 2015). Hence, the causal estimand selects on the outcome because the probability is conditional on those whose outcomes in reality took a specific value (D. Ren & Loh, 2024). Therefore, unlike conventional average causal effects, it is not a contrast between two counterfactual scenarios because one of those scenarios is already factual. Identification of this causal estimand then requires estimating the counterfactual probabilities 𝑃𝑟'𝑌 ! ! = 𝑦+ and 𝑃𝑟(𝑌 ! = 𝑦). When using data from an observational study to estimate these probabilities, the analysis can then follow TTE principles. We refer interested readers to Dawid et al. (2014) and Pearl (2015) for a more thorough discussion of CoE effects, including details on the partial identification strategy used to determine the bounds. It is also worth noting that this distinction builds on a longstanding criticism of the counterfactual approach (Dawid, 2000), a debate that is beyond the scope of TTE.

Here, we argue that TTE and the notion of time zero apply to any causal estimand that can be clearly and meaningfully defined within the potential outcomes or structural counterfactuals framework. For the EoC framing, which is consistent with how most psychological research is naturally posed, time zero clarifies when eligibility, assignment, and the start of follow-up should occur; crucially, these need not coincide with when a prospective study commences or when participants are surveyed. For the CoE framing, when an experiment could not be conducted due to practical or ethical limitations, TTE explicates the conceptualization as a hypothetical thought experiment. The cause in the control condition is externally set to a fixed value x' that is different from the treatment condition value x. The outcome observed under the treatment value is then compared with the counterfactual outcome that would have been observed under the control value. We emphasize that TTE is not restricted to emulating "ideal" randomized studies, and researchers are free to design any number of pragmatic trials to be emulated to answer the research question, including case-control or instrumental variable designs (Dickerman et al., 2020; Didelez et al., 2024; Swanson, 2017). TTE offers an organizing framework to explicitly formalize a hypothetical trial for a well-defined and meaningful causal estimand, including design decisions that follow best research practices to strengthen a causal claim. Investigating an EoC causal question with a prospective observational design does not automatically allow for the use of TTE, just as investigating a CoE causal question with a retrospective case-control study does not automatically dismiss its use. Some of our illustrative scenarios use retrospectively gathered survey data (e.g., participants reporting their AI use at work in the past year; PwC, 2025), which reflects the common practices of survey studies in psychological research which may result in participants only consisting of prevalent users or current users. But the same time-zero logic applies whenever a study is interpreted as estimating the effect of initiating a treatment.

Must the treatment be manipulable?

TTE requires researchers to conceptualize a hypothetical RCT, possibly as a thought experiment, in which the treatment of interest can be externally manipulated. Although this is relatively straightforward for medical and pharmacological interventions, which are often based on treatment strategies from real-world RCTs, it poses distinctive challenges for psychological science, where treatments can span a wide range of phenomena, including psychotherapies, behavioral interventions, life events, social experiences, attitudes, and beliefs. These challenges have sparked a long-standing philosophical debate between Miguel Hernán and Judea Pearl about the scope of causal inference.

Hernán's target trial framework is based on an interventionist view of causality: a well-defined causal question requires a well-defined exposure that must be manipulable by a clearly specified (hypothetical or realized) intervention (Hernán, 2016; Hernán & Taubman, 2008). In contrast, Pearl argues that causal effects can be well-defined even for variables that do not correspond to an intervention, using counterfactuals derived from structural equations within the structural causal model (SCM) framework (Pearl, 2009). This debate is directly relevant to psychological exposures, which we organize into two broad categories.

The first category encompasses exposures that may be unethical or infeasible to manipulate experimentally but remain manipulable conceptually. Examples include intimate partner violence (IPV), childhood adversity, and social discrimination. From Hernán's interventionist perspective, such exposures are amenable to the quantitative potential outcomes framework and can still qualify as valid for applying TTE, because the conceptual exercise or thought experiment does not require a realistic trial. One can conceive of external hypothetical interventions that stipulate distinct, well-defined counterfactual scenarios in which the exposure is enforced or eliminated. Provided these conditions can be conceptually defensible (i.e., the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption or SUTVA can be justified), the target trial serves as a useful scaffold for the observational analysis to emulate in addressing a defensible causal question. Here, Hernán's framework and Pearl's SCM approach largely align: both permit counterfactual reasoning so long as the treatment conditions are sufficiently clearly defined.

The second category is more contentious and at the core of the Hernán-Pearl debate: stable individual differences such as race, gender, and immigration background -what causal inference scholars (Greiner & Rubin, 2011; Holland, 1986) termed "immutable characteristics" that are unsuitable for defining potential outcomes. These are substantively important causes studied throughout psychology, yet they cannot be conceptually manipulated by a (hypothetical or realized) intervention. Hernán has argued that causal questions about such attributes are meaningful only in the context of a manipulable mechanism or downstream process -for example, shifting from "what is the effect of race?" to "what is the effect of the racialized treatment a person receives?" (VanderWeele & Hernán, 2012). Pearl's SCM framework is more permissive, allowing causes to be hypothetically fixed at specific levels using the "dooperator," even for non-manipulable causes, so that counterfactuals can be well-defined (Pearl, 2018). Pearl further argues that restricting causal inquiry to manipulable causes can "inhibit causal thoughts, formal definitions, and mathematical analyses of the mechanisms that propel the phenomena under investigation." (Pearl, 2009, p. 361).

For psychological researchers, this debate has important practical implications. Many causes of interest in psychological research are individual differences that are inherently complex, multifaceted, and stable enough to be prima facie deemed "non-manipulable" and fall into the second category. Yet, depending on the context in which they are framed, specific components can nonetheless lead to meaningful, welldefined counterfactual interpretations and implications (Pearl, 2018; Sen & Wasow, 2016), and be admissible for use in TTE. For example, perceived gender, race, or social class are well-defined and experimentally manipulable causes (Combs et al., 2023; National Research Council, 2004; Quillian & Midtbøen, 2021; J. J. X. Tan & Tai, 2026). Crucially, restricting causes to only those that fall neatly within the first category, or have been realistically manipulated, privileges the quantitative potential outcomes framework for driving substantive research questions, delimits the kinds of causal inquiries that are theorized (Krieger & Davey Smith, 2016), and dismisses established psychological scholarship on fundamentally causal inquiries. The quantitative potential outcomes framework merely reveals the conceptual and practical difficulties and complexities of the causal questions researchers pose. A counterfactual is vague only to the extent that researchers cannot precisely specify the hypothetical intervention that would manipulate the cause. Potential outcomes, therefore, encourage researchers to explicate the scientifically important features in achieving these hypothetical counterfactual scenarios. To illustrate this issue, consider a study examining whether faculty members from racial minority backgrounds are less likely to receive tenure compared to white colleagues (Azmitia et al., 2025; Masters-Waage et al., 2024). Minority background is not manipulable, even conceptually. Nevertheless, the counterfactual contrast is meaningful: would this individual have received tenure had they been perceived by the committee as white? Following Hernán, researchers should be explicit about which aspect of the causal process is under investigation: whether it is structural bias in evaluation criteria, differential access to mentorship, or discriminatory decision-making. Then, researchers should specify time zero accordingly (e.g., the date of tenure dossier submission, the start date of employment as a tenure track faculty). Following Pearl, the structural equation linking minority background to promotion can be used to define the counterfactual formally, even without specifying a manipulation. In either case, TTE remains applicable.

We retain this discussion because it addresses a fundamental prerequisite for any putative TTE application: whether the treatment of interest admits a well-defined, possibly hypothetical intervention, and therefore whether a target trial can be conceived at all. The interventionist requirement stems from the mantra of "no causation without manipulation" (Holland, 1986; Rubin, 2010; West & Thoemmes, 2010), which mandates that the intervention be sufficiently well-defined, even if hypothetical. The "bundle of sticks" perspective (Sen & Wasow, 2016) offers a constructive approach: a putatively immutable characteristic can be decomposed into manipulable components, such as perceived race or gender, as indicated by information on a CV or visual cues from an interaction partner. Specifying which aspect or component is being intervened upon both defines the estimand (Pearl, 2018) and fixes the corresponding time zero (for example, the moment a CV is screened, or the start of a social interaction). We argue that explicating the manipulable components and making the hypothetical interventions precise are fundamental, unavoidable difficulties in formulating a meaningful causal question. TTE, and more generally the quantitative potential outcomes framework, brings these difficulties into the open.

Does TTE guarantee valid causal inference?

No. TTE is not a magic fix that automatically delivers causal conclusions and should not be viewed or used as such (Didelez et al., 2024; Hernán et al., 2025). As we illustrated above, TTE offers several benefits. It guides the design of observational studies to prevent many design-based biases, such as prevalent user bias arising from time zero misalignment. Following TTE helps researchers confront the limitations of their data or the possibilities of collecting data that can answer their questions. In this process, it helps researchers to make vague questions more precise in pursuit of a clear causal estimand. Doing so may help explain and reconcile seemingly inconsistent findings in the literature. Even when researchers are interested in similar research questions, they may be implicitly emulating different target trials with different time zeros (Hernán et al., 2025; Hernán & Robins, 2016). Different time zeros correspond to different causal questions (Figure 2), and it is therefore expected that they may yield different conclusions (Caniglia et al., 2023). Following TTE also provides a basis for collaborations between causal inference experts and substantive researchers by facilitating and encouraging crosscutting communication of substantive questions and causal analyses.

While TTE is useful, we emphasize that following TTE alone (including specifying an appropriate time zero) is insufficient for valid causal inference using observational data. TTE does not address confounding bias arising from nonrandomized treatments. We maintain the recommendation to record a comprehensive set of pre-treatment covariates for confounding adjustment (Steiner et al., 2010), and to justify their inclusion using causal diagrams. Because the threat of unknown or unmeasured confounding bias can rarely be fully eliminated, we suggest conducting sensitivity analyses (Bonvini & Kennedy, 2022) to assess how much unmeasured confounding might influence the conclusions, or using negative control outcomes (Lipsitch et al., 2010; Loh, 2025) to test for its presence or recover the average causal effect.

In addition, TTE is not designed to address problems that can occur in both RCTs and observational studies. It is important to note that emulating a target trial can align time zero but, in and of itself, does not eliminate complications that arise after time zero. As in a randomized study, an observational study can still suffer from post-treatment attrition or loss to follow-up (Akl et al., 2012; Dumville et al., 2006), partial compliance or noncompliance with the assigned treatment (Aronow et al., 2019; Sagarin et al., 2014; Shadish et al., 2002). The intention-to-treat estimand in our worked example avoids the latter by focusing only on the assigned treatment status at time zero; however, the resulting effect, while welldefined and the default causal estimand in randomized studies, may not be the effect of substantive interest (Sheiner & Rubin, 1995). An alternative estimand is the so-called per-protocol effect (Hernán & Robins, 2017), which focuses instead on the treatment actually selected or received. Because this treatment is no longer randomized, it requires additional identification assumptions or more complex estimation methods for handling confounding (for example, instrumental variables or negative control outcomes for alternative identification strategies, or inverse probability of treatment weighting or propensity score matching for estimation). We make this point explicit because several of our motivating examples permit post-treatment attrition or noncompliance in the original design: aligning time zero is necessary to prevent the design-based biases we emphasize, but it is not sufficient to eliminate the threats posed by post-treatment challenges to which both RCTs and observational studies are susceptible.

Applying TTE also requires researchers to explicate decisions and choices that are easy to conceal and leave implicit, most notably the discretization of time: the granularity (days, months, study waves) at which eligibility, treatment initiation, and follow-up are defined. This decision can itself affect estimates and should be reported and justified. Additionally, TTE does not address other time-related biases. For example, in both RCTs and observational studies, the estimated effect can depend on when the outcome is assessed relative to time zero (Gollob & Reichardt, 1987), so the timing of follow-up is part of the estimand and should be justified rather than left to convenience.

Finally, we reiterate that TTE, and more generally the quantitative potential outcomes framework, requires that the treatment be manipulable by a sufficiently precise and well-defined intervention (either realized or hypothetical) for a counterfactual contrast of the outcomes to have a meaningful causal interpretation. We acknowledge that this requirement is more readily met for some psychological causes than for others, as discussed above. Notwithstanding the importance of these issues, they are beyond the scope of this work, which focuses on time zero within the TTE framework for psychological research. A key takeaway is, therefore, that time zero is necessary but insufficient for causal inference. Time zero should be treated as one ingredient within a broader design-based strategy for causal inference. The quasi-experimental tradition offers complementary identification tools that a wellemulated target trial does not provide on its own, including nonequivalent comparison groups chosen to share selection processes with the treated group, multiple baseline (pretest) measurements, the addition of nonequivalent dependent variables, and triangulation across designs whose assumptions fail in different ways (Shadish et al., 2002; Shadish & Cook, 1999). We encourage readers interested in drawing causal conclusions to engage with the established methodological literature in psychology and other disciplines, including many other references that we cannot list here exhaustively. Causal claims, like any other scientific claims, are most rigorously evaluated using diverse types of evidence (e.g., different designs, identification strategies, analytic methods). We recommend that researchers combine an explicit time-zero specification with one or more of these design elements rather than relying on alignment alone.

Conclusion

As psychological science increasingly relies on observational data to address causal questions, careful attention to design-based biases is essential for valid inferences. In this nontechnical tutorial, we emphasize that, beyond confounding -which many psychologists are familiar with -misalignment among eligibility, treatment assignment, and follow-up can introduce substantial and often overlooked biases. We argue that clearly specifying an appropriate time zero as part of a target trial emulation protocol is a critical step in designing observational studies. Integrating this step into routine research practices can ultimately enhance the validity, transparency, and reproducibility of causal inferences in psychology.
