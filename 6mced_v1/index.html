Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction

Business ethics education is widely recognised as an essential component of preparing future business leaders in the fast-developing era of AI technology, in which ethical competence is increasingly recognised as a crucial competence for human flourishing (OECD, 2025). Evidently, the importance and relevance of business ethics and of fostering students' ethical competence are thus being acknowledged by both faculty and students (Beauvais et al., 2022). However, there is often a perceived gap between what has been acknowledged at a theoretical level and how it has been implemented and practised in reality (Mukherjee & Ghosh, 2022; Macfarlane & Ottewill, 2004). One such discrepancy can be inferred from research into business students, who have frequently been compared with students from other schools or majors, not least in terms of their moral competence (Ghoshal, 2005; Mitroff, 2004).

This research has elicited an ongoing debate about the relative moral standing of business students versus non-business students. On the one hand, some studies project an 'immoral' or 'unethical' image of business students: that they are more likely to practise deception for personal gain and apply egoism in ethical decision-making (Shank, 2018); they have a higher tendency for moral disengagement (Baker et al., 2006); they are more tolerant of unethical business practices compared with non-business students (Yu & Zhang, 2006); and they often engage in cheating behaviours (Parks-Leduc et al., 2022). On the other hand, other research findings indicate that business students are not that different from non-business students in terms of their personal moral philosophies (Neubaum et al., 2009), attitude towards professional ethics (Lumsden & Fatoki, 2013; Jamil et al., 2019), cheating behaviours (Simkin & McLeod, 2010), and moral judgement (Hummel et al., 2018). This mixed bag of research results places business students under the moral spotlight, as reflected in debates about whether business school education effectively shapes students' values to be self-interest driven, or if business schools attract students with certain prior character traits (e.g., greed, dishonesty) (Kristjánsson et al., 2017). In our view, it is still open to debate whether those students are, indeed, somehow ethically compromised. When previous studies are examined closely, it appears that most focus on a single character trait (e.g., a specific virtue/vice) or one aspect of moral development (e.g., Parks-Leduc et al., 2022; Shank, 2018; Wang et al., 2011). The problem with focusing on a single trait or multiple specific traits is that it reduces students' perceived moral competence to a discrete box or boxes, while neglecting the fact that they are developing as holistic human persons. As significant as individual character traits and virtues are, only a small subset of those cannot reflect the overall moral status of students. Thus, there has been insufficient holistic exploration of students' moral excellence at a meta-virtue level, even within studies motivated by Aristotelian virtue ethics. Such virtue ethics is gradually becoming the theory of choice within large segments of business ethics (Alzola et al., 2020; Sison & Redin, 2023), and we draw upon it as our theoretical foundation in this paper.

Because Aristotelian virtue ethics does not posit any single moral virtue as a prioritising master virtue (such as justice in Plato or ren in Confucianism), but contextualises all moral decision-making as being integrated through the intellectual meta-virtue of phronesis, any Aristotelian measure of moral competence will need to focus on the metacognitive, decision-making capacities of phronesis.

Yet despite the presumed significance within Aristotelian virtue ethics of phronesis (practical wisdom) in predicting ethical motivation and behaviour (Darnell et al., 2019; 2022; McLoughlin et al., 2025), few studies have examined this issue in the context of business education. Therefore, we deemed it important to carry out research that compares business students and non-business students through a phronesis lens, as phronesis mastery is associated with moral excellence across domains of individual virtues (Kristjánsson & Fowers, 2024). Although business students may appear to be demonstrating certain questionable characteristics, such as being excessively money-driven or greedy, this does not necessarily mean that they possess less well-cultivated phronesis compared with other groups of students. Perhaps they may be greedier when facing temptations of a certain commercial kind, while remaining comparable to non-business students in overall moral functioning, for instance.

The present study aims to advance the ongoing debate about business students' degree of moral development by utilising a newly validated measure for multifaceted phronesis, the Short Phronesis Measure (SPM: McLoughlin et al., 2025). Our study aims to respond to the ongoing debate on whether business students are morally less developed (or not) than their counterparts. Our study also responds indirectly to the important question of whether business education is corrupting by nature, dragging down the moral level of those students who are studying at a business school. We employ the SPM in order to treat moral functioning in an integrative manner, instead of other measures focusing on a single character or virtue or one aspect of morality. Therefore, our work contributes to business ethics education research via the application of a more integrated moral-functioning lens, in a field so far largely dominated by a trait-by-trait measurement lens.

We deem the choice of a measure to be crucial here because our aim is to ameliorate gaps in the current literature on the relative moral competence of business versus nonbusiness students: literature that has so far been too selective and trait-specific. Obviously, there are other measures of overall moral competence available, and our preference for the SPM is not only motivated by its rootedness in Aristotelian theorising, but also by the fact that business ethics is becoming increasingly driven by virtue ethics. More specifically, we consider the SPM is the most suitable one for at least five reasons: 1) It is the only available short measure of phronesis; 2) standard Kohlbergian and neo-Kohlbergian measures focus exclusively on the rational element in decision-making and motivation, rather than the overall metacognitive aspects of moral functioning; 3) the widely used Values-in-Action (VIA) measure from positive psychology lacks an integrative higher-order virtue (e.g., practical wisdom), which is critically important for the integration of virtue, especially in cases of virtue conflicts; 3) the SPM is preferable to the currently fashionable Moral Foundations Theory measure because the SPM has been found to predict (inversely) the traits of the Dark Tetrad with more precision than Moral Foundation Theory; 5) the SPM predicts components of overall human flourishing; 6) business students' ethics is arguably even more important in the age of AI, and phronesis predicts positive usage of AI.

An elaboration of those six reasons can be found in McLoughlin et al. (2025 McLoughlin et al. ( , 2026)).

However, we mention at the end of the Discussion section one area in which the SPM may seem to depart from orthodox Aristotelian virtue ethics. The main body of the present paper starts with a literature review of the issue in question, followed by methods, data analysis, results, and discussion, and finally arrives at a conclusion.

Literature Review

Are business students ethically compromised?

Business ethics education studies have developed for over 40 years and witnessed some key shifts in themes, from integration of business ethics into business school curricula to the pedagogical approach and tools used to teach business ethics, which later also incorporated sustainability-related concepts (Jaganjac et al., 2024). This includes accounting ethics education in business schools, with a more refined focus on accounting students (Nguyen & Dellaportas, 2020), which emphasises the benefits of education in virtues to improve students' ethical decision-making and behaviours (Nguyen et al., 2024). This emphasis not only shares a similar purpose as business ethics education but also resonates highly with the overall goal of character education, such as neo-Aristotelian character education, in particular. Its main selling point above other moral education approaches is making sense of phronesis as excellence in ethical decision-making that can constructively guide behaviour even when there are serious virtue clashes at stake (Kristjánsson, 2015; Jubilee Centre, 2022). Phronesis offers hope, more so than any other characterological construct, that the proverbial gap between moral knowledge and moral action can be bridged (Darnell et al., 2019; DeTienne, 2021).

Business ethics, including accounting ethics education, matters on its own, as does sustainability education, but the concerns of those discrete subjects are ultimately parasitic on the overall concern that students' ethical and moral development matters (Montiel et al., 2020). All these concerns can be observed from various business schools' curricular efforts and the UN's 17 Sustainable Development Goals. Yet, the increasing interest in business and accounting ethics per se does not necessarily guarantee successful ethics interventions that advance business students' moral development. For example, business students have shown higher psychopathy scores than students of other academic backgrounds (Wilson & McCarthy, 2011). Wang et al. (2011) also reported a positive correlation between studying economics and levels of greed, which was further elaborated by how greed appears in marketing students' consumption (Jaskari et al., 2018). Frank and Schulze (2000), through investigating determinants of corruptibility, found that business students were more corrupt than other students, which they ascribed to self-selection rather than indoctrination. McCabe et al. (2006) found that graduate business students cheated more frequently than their non-business-student peers, based on a somewhat dated survey measuring academic dishonesty (McCabe & Trevino, 1993).

Although some studies (e.g., Ford & Richardson, 1994; Borkowski & Ugras, 1998) have elicited contrasting findings to the above perception of business students, suggesting that the tendency for ethical behaviour does not significantly differ between business and non-business students (e.g., Beltramini et al.,1984), these 'more positive' studies tend to be fairly dated, and there seems to be a lack of recent research providing an update about business versus non-business students' moral development. This is odd, given various recent efforts to enhance business ethics education, either through discrete taught curricula or embedded or 'caught' interventions. It still remains something of a 'black box' whether business students have made any progress in terms of moral and ethical development of late, when compared with non-business students, especially given the fact that business ethics education seems to have acquired a higher profile in recent years, in the time of AI, than various other sub-disciplines of professional ethics.

To sum up, some previous research suggests an 'unhappy reading' when examining business students' moral development, especially when viewed through a virtue ethics, characterological lens. Character-oriented studies tend to focus on one or a few select character traits in business students and then compare them with non-business students.

The limitations of this existing research were mentioned in the Introduction. Other studies, employing non-virtue-ethical moral frameworks, have concentrated upon espoused moral attitudes or observable behaviour outcomes. However, a lacuna in all those research traditions, within and outside of virtue ethics, is the failure to look more holistically at students' moral metacognitions or ability to balance competing moral demands. This is even more remarkable given how many of the recent studies are motivated by Aristotelian virtue ethics; for according to such virtue ethics, the ultimate criterion of moral functioning in adults lies in the intellectual meta-virtue of phronesis (as already indicated). This lacuna has inspired our phronesis-based research lens, as explained in the following sub-sections.

Virtue ethics and the relevance of phronesis (practical wisdom)

Virtue ethics is gradually superseding, or at least complementing, deontology and utilitarianism as the theory of choice within professional fields, not least business ethics.

Modern virtue ethics typically takes its inspiration from Aristotelian notions of character and virtue, although in the East, Confucian virtue ethics is also making a comeback (Alzola et al., 2020). Several concepts central to the way Aristotle understood ethics are important components of modern virtue ethics theory. These concepts are outlined by Aristotle in his Nicomachean Ethics (1985) and include eudaimonia (flourishing), arete (excellence or virtue), and phronesis (practical wisdom).

According to virtue ethics, an action is right if it exemplifies virtue, with such exemplification being conducive to or constitutive of human flourishing. Thus, an action is right not because it is required as one's duty in accordance with a formalistic principle (as in deontology) or because it has desirable overall consequences (as in utilitarianism) but because it exhibits good character, which, in turn, promotes human flourishing. In contrast to other moral theories, the concept of flourishing (and good character insofar as it promotes flourishing) is thus what is foundational in virtue ethics, rather than the concepts of duties or consequences; and what defines acting well is derivative or a matter of what is consistent with good character. Within business ethics, in particular, what attracts theorists is virtue ethics' focus on how exhibiting the virtues is both conducive to and constitutive of good business practices and, more generally, promotes the ideal of virtuous professionalism in business. When making this connection, many neo-Aristotelian business ethicists read Aristotle through a MacIntyrean lens (e.g., Sison & Redín, 2023).

Neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics is unique in positing that the most problematic choices facing ordinary people, most notably adolescents, young adults, and professionals, are not choices between vice and virtue but between different virtues when those seem to clash in the same situation (e.g., loyalty to colleagues versus honesty to your boss in potential whistleblowing scenarios). Therefore, one of the intellectual virtues, namely phronesis or practical wisdom, acquires a unique meta-role (as it did in Aristotle's original virtue theory) as a coordinator and arbitrator: the conductor of the whole virtue orchestra, as it is sometimes put.

Phronesis is meant to oversee the moral values and virtues and provide them with the necessary checks and balances to secure overall ethically wise decisions. This proposed meta-virtue can thus be defined as excellence in ethical decision-making. It is concerned both with finding the correct 'golden mean' (i.e., the medial state between deficiency and excess) of a single virtue, when applied in complex contexts, and the proper interactivity, or balancing, of different virtues where those seem to call for different responses in the same situation. For instance, is it possible to reconcile the commonly clashing demands of honesty and kindness by being kindly honest or honestly kind, or in a particular situation, may it call for the abandonment of one of the two virtues? Whatever the eventual decision will be, it is the faculty of phronesis that is meant to do the adjudicative heavy lifting. At the same time, it turns simple dispositions to be good along various independent paths into a coherent way of life, a comprehensive moral journey, guided by right reason, albeit a journey that is heavily contextualised and individualised (Kristjánsson & Fowers, 2024).

Conceptualising and measuring phronesis

Phronesis has been undergoing a significant academic revival of late. What we could call the new 'phronesis bandwagon' was originally driven by philosophers (e.g., Annas, 2011; Curzer, 2012; Russell, 2009), but this interest has also percolated to psychologists (Schwartz & Sharpe, 2011; Wright et al., 2020; Fowers et al., 2024) and theorists working within various branches of professional ethics, such as business (Alzola et al., 2020), medicine (Kaldjian, 2014) and nursing (Armstrong, 2007). Various interdisciplinary studies are also emerging (e.g., Kristjánsson & Fowers, 2024). Despite this growing interest, prior to work in the Jubilee Centre at the University of Birmingham, no psychologically credible conceptualisation of phronesis existed, nor was there an instrument to measure it (Darnell et al., 2019; 2022; McLoughlin et al., 2025).

The Aristotelian Phronesis Model (APM: Darnell et al., 2019) is a four-component framework distilled from Aristotle's writings on phronesis, or practical wisdom. It comprises Moral Perception, Moral Identity, Moral Emotion, and Moral Adjudication, each representing a critical facet of how moral understanding is developed and enacted in practice. Darnell et al. (2022) provided a valuable initial empirical examination of this model, offering preliminary support for its structural integrity. Their study was notable for employing confirmatory factor analysis within a structural equation modelling framework, using existing psychological instruments that were theoretically aligned with the APM's four domains. While this approach lent initial empirical credibility to the model, it bypassed the exploratory phase typically used to assess how the structure might emerge from data organically, instead relying deferentially on Aristotelian theory. Moreover, because the measures were adapted from unrelated domains and the item pool was neither designed specifically for phronesis nor made publicly available with scoring instructions, the study raised important concerns regarding how others might replicate the confirmatory model or use the measure in practice. The lengthy nature of the instrument (45 minutes approx.) also constrained its utility for applied or large-scale research.

In response to these limitations, the Short Phronesis Measure (SPM) was invented by McLoughlin et al. (2025), based on the APM. Upon its development, the measure was validated with a large-scale representative (census-matching) dataset collected from adult participants recruited in the USA and UK. The SPM enables researchers to examine the multifaceted nature of phronesis in a feasible way due to its brevity. It has also been found to predict various outcomes of interest (directly or inversely), including dark personality traits, moral disengagement, flourishing, and critical and ethical stances on the use of AI and emerging technologies (see McLoughlin et al., 2026). Further methodological details about the SPM will be listed in the Methods section.

Phronesis and moral excellence

Interest in measuring moral excellence has proliferated across many academic disciplines, since Kohlberg (1981) founded the sub-discipline of moral psychology. Typically, in recent research, such measurements focus on a series of moral virtues and utilise self-report instruments, such as the Values-in-Action (VIA) measure from positive psychology (Peterson & Seligman, 2004). However, from an Aristotelian perspective, such measures are doubly deficient, at least when the participants are of adolescent age or older. First, while children can be considered adequately virtuous if they exhibit individual moral virtues such as honesty and compassion, adolescents and adults can only be considered to possess moral excellence if they are adept at resolving moral quandaries: i.e., dealing with situations that involve the apparent conflict of virtues. Notably, the widely used VIA measure does not contain any discrete meta-virtue like phronesis. Second, as we are not transparent to ourselves, measures of moral excellence should ideally not rely solely on self-reports. This is why neo-Kohlbergians (see, e.g., Rest et al., 2000) suggested providing participants with moral dilemma scenarios for evaluation, for which answers could be deemed appropriate or not by comparing them with the verdict of an expert panel. The approach of avoiding a pure self-report measurement strategy has filtered down from the neo-Kohlbergians into current phronesis research (Darnell et al., 2022; McLoughlin et al., 2025).

The most genuinely Aristotelian approach to measuring moral excellence in adults (including aspiring and practising professionals) is by homing in on their phronesis levels.

In the Aristotelian model, it is impossible to be practically wise without moral excellence (although it is possible to be amorally competent in calculating outcomes: what Aristotle referred to as the sub-virtue of deinotes or instrumental cleverness). This is because the moral virtues provide phronesis with both substantive content and psychological motivation. Conversely, it is impossible for adults to excel morally without phronesis, as in default of this vital intellectual virtue, they will flounder when confronted with the complex and often ambivalent rough and tumble of adult personal and professional life.

This explains further why we consider the recent Short Phronesis Measure, described above, the most suitable instrument to measure the moral excellence under scrutiny in this paper.

Current study

In this study, instead of comparing single or multiple character traits or certain dimensions of moral excellence (e.g., ethical attitudes), we compare the overall moral excellence of business and non-business students by using the recently validated SPM instrument, which synergises insights from both virtue ethics and moral psychology. Numerous previous studies have attempted to uncover the 'myths' about business students versus non-business students from different angles or perspectives, yet none of them has employed a comprehensive approach with a meta-intellectual virtue lens to understand moral excellence and character functioning between those students. For example, as already noted, most previous studies have either focused on one aspect of moral functioning, e.g., moral judgement and reasoning, or specific character traits, while failing to capture a holistic or orchestra-like view of moral excellence. Hence, the implications of their findings might be limited or partial.

From a business ethics education perspective, over the past two decades, the importance of integrating ethical values into curricula has become a critical component in business school education (Issa & Pick, 2018), driven by the need to equip future entrepreneurs and managers with ethical excellence and a sustainable developmental mindset. What was once a matter of individual school choice is now embedded in the expectations of major accreditation bodies such as AACSB, EQUIS, and AMBA. This shift positions universities' efforts within a broader global movement in business education rather than as an isolated institutional practice. However, the efforts and effectiveness of this integration may vary across institutions. For example, some institutions engage with ethics, sustainability, and responsible management education genuinely; whereas in some cases, these elements may be introduced mainly to satisfy external expectations or accreditation requirements, without deeper reflection on how such values are actually developed, transferred, and assessed through the student learning experience. The university under investigation in the present study has made deliberate efforts to integrate ethical values into its ethos, practices, and teaching, going beyond mandatory and optional courses by embedding ethics, ESG, sustainability, and responsible management across its curricula, assessment methods, and continuous quality improvement mechanisms -rather than merely documenting them in the accreditation process. However, this does not represent the general status of other business schools in China, as the university under investigation is distinct with its internal features, such as a high percentage of international staff and exclusively English-speaking courses.

Our study explores differences in moral excellence, as defined by the SPM, which embraces cognitive, affective, perceptual, and motivational aspects. Because this is believed to be the first study to employ the theoretical model in question and the resulting measure in a business ethics context, and the existing previous studies in the field have reported contradicting results, we employ a data-driven approach based on a Bayesian perspective rather than hypothesis-driven testing based on a frequentist perspective (Han, 2024a). So instead of setting and testing theory-driven a priori hypotheses (e.g., business students would show higher scores in SPM components than non-business students or vice versa), we use Bayesian regression analysis and equivalence testing to investigate whether the collected data support the existence of statistically significant differences (Kruschke, 2018; Wagernmakers et al., 2018). We expect that this data-driven exploration will show researchers in the field a holistic picture of the integrative understanding of moral and character functioning among business students (in comparison with non-business students), for further hypothesis-driven examinations and educational practice in business schools.

Methods

Participants

The participants in the study were students from a joint-venture university between a Chinese University and a UK university (English is the official teaching and learning language), located in southern China, with a predominantly local student body, though a small but growing international student body. This university is distinct and does not represent the general status of Chinese higher education. The reason that we chose this study context is that English is the official language, and teaching and learning activities are also in English. This made it possible to administer a survey in English. The study was approved by the first author's university Ethics Committee in December 2023. A survey platform called Wenjuanxing (问卷星) was adopted to collect data, and the survey was advertised via school emails, student groups, and module leaders. The participant information sheet and consent form were attached to the advertisements. It took about 20-25 minutes to complete the survey. As compensation for students' time and effort to participate in the study, a small number of lucky-draw e-coupons (about 30RMB or $4USD per/student) were provided. The duration of the data collection was from February to July 2024. By default, Wenjuanxing blocks duplicate submissions from the same logged-in user upon questionnaire publication.

The sample frame consisted of 1056 responses. Among these, 403 were identified as invalid and excluded from further analysis for the sake of data reliability, mainly due to duplicated answers from the same IP address but with different user names. Consequently, the response rate was 62%, and the final valid sample size was 653 (male:164 = 11%; female:489 = 89%) from business students (n = 289) and students from other schools (n = 364), for example, schools of humanities and social science, mathematics, and education.

Measure

The survey includes two major parts: the first part was demographic information asking about students' gender, major/school and level of study, and the second part utilised the newly validated SPM (McLoughlin et al., 2025), and also an additional short measure called Moral Growth Mindset (MGM) (Han et al., 2020), in view of its correlation with the SPM as reported in a separate paper by Han et al (under review). The present paper focuses on analysing and reporting students' phronesis, or practical wisdom levels only; hence, we will concentrate on the SPM measure and leave the MGM out.

The SPM (McLoughlin et al., 2025) was developed as a purpose-built instrument grounded directly in the theoretical components of the APM. Crucially, it approached the measurement of phronesis inductively in the first instance (drawing on 4,000 representative adult US and UK respondents), using exploratory factor analysis to allow the data to shape the structure rather than imposing a theorised structure a priori. This led to the identification of ten interrelated factors, constituting a refined framework referred to as the neo-APM. Three of these dimensions relate logically to the idea of Moral Perception:

Moral Relevance (accurately recognising when an action reflects on one's character), Situational Moral Irrelevance (identifying when it does not), and Virtue Identification (discernment of which virtues apply in a given context). Moral Emotion fractionates into Emotional Regulation (managing feelings in morally charged moments), Positive Moral Emotion (feeling good when doing good), and Negative Moral Emotion (feeling bad when acting immorally). Two components relate to Moral Identity: Moral Self-Relevance (seeing morality as personally, not just socially, important) and Aspired Moral Identity (having an ideal moral self and striving toward it). Finally, Moral Adjudication fractionates empirically into two dimensions known as Moral Deliberation (actively seeking relevant information before making a moral choice) and Moral Integration (coherently weighing all other components in the decision process).

This tenfactor structure preserves the spirit of the APM while extending it empirically, offering a more fine-grained and applicable understanding of phronesis, suitable for both research and practice. Confirmatory analyses revealed that this model was a good fit to the data in the large representative samples of both the USA and the UK. It was also shown to have good test-retest reliability and excellent criterion validity for predicting a wide range of personality, flourishing, and moral outcome variables.

Data Analysis

Measurement invariance test

First, we performed the measurement invariance test for the SPM via multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to examine whether the ten-factor measurement model was equally valid across business and non-business students. It is necessary to establish measurement invariance to be able to compare variable scores of interest between different groups without any seriously problematic bias possibly emerging from the non-invariance in the measurement model across the groups (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). In general, four different levels of invariance are tested: configural, metric, scalar, and residual invariance (see Table 2 for further information) (Han, 2024b; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). According to methodological guidelines, at least the scalar invariance should be established to compare mean variable scores (Han, 2024b).

Bayesian Equivalence Test

Second, once we confirmed the measurement invariance (scalar at the minimum), we conducted Bayesian equivalence tests for ten component scores to compare the scores between the two groups (Makowski et al., 2019). Before performing the actual comparisons, we calculated latent factor scores, which consider measurement errors, instead of mere composite or mean scores for the ten components for further analyses (Han & Dawson, 2025; McNeish & Wolf, 2020). We decided to employ the latent factor (versus composite or mean) scores to minimise any possible biases during the analysis processes due to measurement-related errors or biases.

After calculating latent factor scores, we first conducted Bayesian model regression analysis to examine whether the alternative model, including students' major as a categorical variable, was significantly better supported than the null model without the categorical predictor (Ntontis et al., 2023). The regression analysis was performed with the brms package while employing the default Cauchy priors (Bürkner, 2017; Rouder & Morey, 2012). The prior distribution was adjusted for multiple comparisons with BayesFactorFMRI to minimise possible false positives, as ten components were compared (Han, 2020; 2021). The method intended to apply an adjusted Cauchy prior distribution based on the number of tests to be performed simultaneously, ten in our study, so that the inference could be done conservatively to reduce the likelihood of Type I error. It is conceptually similar to the methods for multiple tests in the frequentist approach, which adjust the α level for family-wise error correction (e.g., Bonferroni correction) (De Jong, 2017).

We examined whether the model Bayes Factor (BF) indicated the extent to which the alternative model was more strongly supported by data than the null model for ten components (Wagenmakers et al., 2018). When 2log(BF) exceeded 2.00, we concluded that the data positively supported the alternative model versus the null model; thus, the following equivalence test would be meritorious for the component of interest (Kass & Raftery, 1995).

For the phronesis component reporting the responsive model 2log(BF) exceeding 2.00, we performed a Bayesian equivalence test to examine whether the difference in the component scores between two groups was practically significant (see Han [2024a] for methodological details). We first estimated the posterior distribution of the effect size of interest, i.e., the mean difference in a phronesis component of interest between the two groups, via Bayesian regression. Then, we examined the extent to which the 89% highest density interval (HDI) of the posterior distribution overlaps with the region of practical equivalence (ROPE, Kruschke, 2018), which indicates the region practically equivalent to the null effect in the case of our study (Blackburn et al., 2024). We used Cohen's d = ±.10 as the threshold for the ROPE, indicating trivial effects following Funder and Ozer (2019).

According to them, the effect size corresponding to Cohen's d = .10 "indicates an effect that is very small for the explanation of single events but potentially consequential in the not-very-long run" (p. 156). Thus, it would be reasonable to assume that the regions within the d = .10 cutoff may represent the absence of practically significant effects. If the calculated ratio of the HDI overlapping with the ROPE was non-zero, we concluded that the evidence would not decisively support that the effect size of interest would be practically equivalent to zero. The Bayesian equivalency tests were performed with bayestestR (Makowski et al., 2019).

We expected that we would be able to see the big picture of the statistical and practical differences in phronesis components between the two groups via the BF and the Bayesian equivalence test. First, the BF estimated from regression analysis could inform the extent to which an alternative hypothesis or a null hypothesis was more strongly supported by evidence compared to the other; that said, the resultant BF would become an indicator of statistical significance (Han, 2024a). Second, the result from the Bayesian equivalence test would provide us with insights into whether the estimated difference was practically significant. Even in the case where a statistically significant non-zero effect exists (e.g., 2log(BF) ≥ 2.00 or p < .05), the effect might still be practically trivial if the effect size is very small (e.g., Cohen's d smaller than .05, etc.; Funder & Ozer, 2019). In such a case, a Bayesian equivalence test would quantitatively inform us that the effect of interest approximates zero, so the null conclusion assuming the presence of a null effect may not be rejected from a practical perspective despite the statistical significance (Han & Graham, 2026; Kiers & Tendeiro, 2025).

Discussion

In the current study, we explored the differences in ten subcomponents of phronesis assessed by the SPM across business and non-business students. Our Bayesian regression analyses, used to explore the differences in a data-driven manner, showed that the null model, suggesting the non-significance of the group (business vs non-business) as a categorical predictor, could not be rejected, for any subcomponent.

In general, these findings offer novel insights into the debates regarding whether moral development among business students is significantly different from that among non-business students. Previous studies have reported contradictory results. For example, to reiterate, in some previous studies, business students are perceived as having more lenient attitudes towards cheating than non-business students (Simha et al., 2012; Smyth & Davis, 2004; Klein et al., 2007); and multiple studies suggest that business students cheat more frequently than non-business students (Simha et al., 2012; Smyth & Davis, 2004; Parks-Leduc et al., 2022; McCabe et al., 2006). In contrast, some research suggests that non-business students also engage in cheating at comparable or even higher rates (Simkin & McLeod, 2010; Iyer & Eastman, 2006). The issue here may be that the conventional conceptual and methodological approaches employed in the previous studies reporting the contradicting outcomes did not consider the holistic and multifaceted nature of moral motivation and behaviour.

The majority of previous studies and debates pertaining to business students' moral qualities tended to concentrate upon several 'cliché' character traits, such as greed or dishonesty. They did not treat and examine the students as moral agents with moral excellence in a holistic manner. Hence, given the results from the current study, we may need to re-evaluate the validity of the stereotypes about business students as being morally inferior to their counterparts who are not studying business. Subsequently, we need to carefully examine the philosophical foundations and educational approaches to promoting their ethical and positive development effectively for their successful career as well as for their flourishing as human beings .

The phronesis model and its new measure, the SPM, inherited from Aristotle's theorising, will help us better navigate what should be pursued in business ethics education by providing us with novel theoretical and methodological insights.

For instance, it will allow researchers and educators to examine and evaluate the effectiveness of business ethics education in a holistic manner by addressing multifaceted moral functioning, which embraces cognitive, affective, and motivational aspects of human morality. The research outcomes obtained with the comprehensive measure, the SPM, provide us with better insight into understanding and promoting ethical behaviour within the context of ethics education, compared with conventional measures focusing on certain aspects of moral functioning, such as the Defining Issues Test focusing on moral reasoning only (Choi et al., 2020). The lesson for business ethics educators -and indeed other professional ethics instructors -seems to be to put even more emphasis on phronesis development. During the recent resurgence of interest in moral formation at universitiesoften following a broad neo-Aristotelian script -we remain puzzled as to why so much of it focuses on the cultivation of standalone virtues such as empathy, compassion, and gratitude (see e.g., Brant et al., 2022). According to Aristotelian virtue ethics, those virtues should already be in place from the students' earlier habituation phase. If students have not acquired a character trait of compassion by the age of 18, there is probably not much that can be done about that in university classes of professional ethics. However, phronesis is developed through late adolescence and early adulthood, so the time is ripe to foreground such development at the university level.

Although it was beyond the scope of our original study, following an anonymous reviewer's suggestion, we performed additional exploratory analyses to examine the relationship between gender, age, education level, and phronesis. First, research relevant to business ethics has reported that women showed significantly higher scores in both moral reasoning and emotional capacities compared with men (Elm et al., 2001; Ward & King, 2018). Thus, female participants would be expected to outperform in virtue identification, which is closely tied to ethical judgment and decision-making, as well as in negative moral emotion, dealing with moral sensitivity to detect potential moral problems and concern for others' welfare (Han, 2026). Second, we may refer to developmental psychology research reporting that moral reasoning and emotional capacities develop continuously among young adults, the same age group as the participants in our study, to interpret the regression analysis results with respect to age (Eisenberg et al., 2005). These previous research findings may explain the positive association between age, moral deliberation, moral self-relevance, situational moral irrelevance, and emotional regulation that we found. Finally, the positive associations between education level and all phronesis components are consistent with previous research on higher education and moral development. It has been shown that experience in higher education is significantly associated with sophisticated moral reasoning and related moral cognitive skills (for review, see King & Mayhew, 2002). Future studies may be necessary to examine further details on why the significant results were not reported across all subscales in analyses involving participants' gender and age.

To recap, business school students showed no significant difference from nonbusiness students across all SPM subscales. We do not consider this finding to stand in need of a specific explanation; if not because of some contradictory findings in the past, this is probably what most people would have expected anyway. As Miller (1999) argued, business students do not come into business schools with a tendency or intention to be unethical or greedy, but rather they may be influenced by the principle of rational selfinterest values absorbed from finance and economics courses. Colby et al. (2011) highlighted that main business courses (e.g., marketing, management, finance, and accounting) are dominated by market-based analytic frameworks, and this leads to business students' uncritical application of these frameworks. As a result, business students are often associated with certain specific negative character traits. However, those traits may not negatively impact moral functioning across the board, as we have demonstrated.

These findings point to an educational implication worth further consideration.

While the study does not test whether specific curricular or co-curricular interventions directly cause ethical outcomes or develop virtue literacy, the results suggest that deliberate institutional efforts -such as the integration of ethical values through mandatory and optional modules -may play a supportive role in enriching students' virtue literacy (Jubilee Centre, 2022), an important precursor to ethical action. This raises the possibility that business school educators could benefit from incorporating ethical values genuinely into their teaching and learning activities, such as curricula, through multiple channels, including curricular, co-curricular, and extracurricular activities, and that universities might consider policies and resources to support such integration across business schools and beyond. Rather than viewing business education solely in terms of profit-making, one might also consider an educational ethos that aligns profit with societal development, mutual flourishing, and the common good. This could include attention to professional development, interactive pedagogies, an explicit focus on character and ethics, training in social-emotional competence, modelling of character, classroom and behavioural management strategies, as well as community service or service learning (Berkowitz & Bier, 2007). These remain open questions for future research and curriculum design rather than being directly implied by conclusions already demonstrated by the present study.

Limitations of the study

The present study was administered in English at an English-taught university, as the students were all capable of using English as a learning and communication language and met the language standard (e.g. IELTS, TOEFL). However, for the majority of participants, English is not their first language; future studies may consider translating SPM into Chinese and other languages. Going beyond the language level, since ethical excellence is often bound to context and culture, future studies should not only consider validating the SPM by simply translating the instrument but also giving attention to its cultural fit. Furthermore, for better comparison in the domains of situational moral irrelevance and virtue identification, which demonstrated 2log(BF) = .33 and -.76, respectively, collecting more data would be beneficial. In all other domains, the absolute values of the resultant 2log(BF) values were above 2.00, indicating the presence of evidence significantly supporting either the null or alternative hypothesis compared to its counterpart.

Another limitation regards the disciplinary focus of this paper; this is a paper using tools of moral psychology in a business educational context. However, this is not an educational paper, and hence we elide questions about the effects that business ethics education classes might have had on the business students. Mountains of research in professional ethics have shown that professional ethical competence is mostly caught from the university environment, and later work environment and organisational culture, through 'osmosis' (Kristjánsson, 2024). Phronesis will not be caught from a learning environment that is inimical to virtue development in general. As Russell notes (2021, p. 17), the difficulties in learning phronesis are not only caused by its 'high intrinsic load' (the need to synthesise cognitively different values and virtues) but also by a 'high extrinsic load' (environments often full of misleading feedback and bad practices). Conversely, a virtueconducive environment can facilitate the development of phronesis. We have not considered the possibility here that the kind of business ethics education that the students in our study have received has influenced their phronesis scores. It could well be that they do better than their counterparts in many older studies because of the high quality of their business ethics education.

We finally wish to acknowledge a possible theoretical and contextual limitation.

Our approach and outcome might seem to be undermotivated with respect to Aristotelian virtue ethics, in the sense that they do not presuppose Aristotle's famous 'reciprocity thesis', according to which the moral virtues require phronesis and phronesis requires the moral virtues. Rather, we assume that business students could score high on overall phronesis while lacking some discrete moral virtues. It must be noticed here that the reciprocity thesis is about the phronimos (the ideal person with full phronesis). We, on the other hand, treat phronesis as a scalar concept (Fowers et al., 2024) and attempt to measure where participants are on the scale with respect to the 10 identified sub-factors.

A non-phronimos could presumably, in the Aristotelian picture, have a decent standing on the phronesis scale but still lack some specific moral virtues by being, for example, too greedy. That said, we acknowledge that future studies should ideally study phronesis in conjunction with specific moral virtues to figure out which particular moral virtues predict or do not predict a higher phronesis score with respect to specific phronesis components. This might also, helpfully, lead to the identification of different 'phronesis profiles' of individuals with different combinations of strengths or weaknesses, when their different phronesis components and mastery of different moral virtues are explored collectively.

One possible contextual limitation in the differences between business and nonbusiness students relates to the nature of major choice in Chinese higher education.

Unlike the assumption of autonomous self-selection in some Western studies, students' majors in China are often shaped by parental expectations, admission scores, perceived employability, institutional allocation, and family views of prestige; consequently, 'business student' may be a weaker proxy for a distinctive self-selected moral or value orientation than the literature sometimes suggests, which could add nuance to the debate over selection versus socialisation.

Conclusion

All in all, if we follow Aristotle in considering phronesis as a proxy for moral functioning, we conclude that business students seem to have the same potential to be morally virtuous as other students, based on our study findings, despite the literature being filled with labels or descriptions about their moral deficiencies. For example, when facing a morally challenging situation involving a choice between competing moral alternatives, our findings indicate that they would be at least as adept as other students in choosing the best course of action. Thus, the stereotypes and negative labels applied to business students should be abandoned, acknowledging that business students can be educated equally towards virtuous conduct and a flourishing life as any other students.

We firmly believe that business students' moral potential can be enhanced further through business ethics education that incorporates character education in general and phronesis cultivation in particular at the university and school level, not least within the discipline of business ethics. American character educationist Marvin Berkowitz often remarks that all good education should be good character education. We would like to rephrase it here as: all good business education should be good business ethics education.

The university and business school should intentionally create a culture and design programmes that promote ethical competence and good conduct, promoting individual and societal flourishing.

There is an old saying in Chinese that there are no students who cannot be taught, only unskilled teachers. Returning to existing debates about whether business degree courses effectively shape students' values to be money-driven or whether business schools attract students with certain character traits (e.g., greed, dishonesty) (Kristjánsson et al., 2017), our study has not directly offered any conclusive evidence one way or another. What it has shown, however, is that worries about business students' inability to exhibit apt moral functioning may be vastly exaggerated.

For business schools, however, a more substantive approach would require clearer curriculum mapping, greater coherence across modules and school ethos, and a more holistic view of how students progressively acquire these ethical and responsible management capabilities. The compulsory or elective distinction is therefore perhaps better understood as a technical curriculum design choice, whereas the more fundamental question is whether the integration is compliance-driven or genuinely educational in purpose. This will require business school leaders, faculty, and programme designers to deliberately develop a strategy to advance business ethics education and to incorporate it thoughtfully into the school-wide ecosystem, identifying new avenues to better support their students' moral development that advance individual and collective flourishing.
