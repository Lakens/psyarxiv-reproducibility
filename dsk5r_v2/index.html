Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Phenomenological characteristics are central to theoretical distinctions among modern memory constructs. For instance, reexperiencing is often considered diagnostic of episodic recall, with hippocampal reinstatement likely underpinning this characteristic at the neural level. However, most phenomenology research relies on instruments with content and scoring schemes that embed theoretical assumptions into their measurements. Here, we explored whether phenomenological characteristics emerge from self-reported memory experiences, as anticipated by episodic memory theory. We solicited memories from a large online sample, following each response with a novel questionnaire designed to evaluate the phenomenology of individual memories. We used folded bottom-up factor analyses to examine and validate the global latent factor structure of participants' memory experiences. Consistent with Tulving's declarative memory framework, our analyses produced five stable dimensions: imagery, autonoetic consciousness, mental time travel, relational memory, and semantic memory. In order, these dimensions reflect multimodal perceptual reexperiencing, personal meaning, sense of reliving, detailed spatiotemporal context, and conscious use of semantic knowledge during recall. The semantic memory dimension has no clear predecessor, suggesting the strategic use of general knowledge during recall is a distinct and previously uncharacterized aspect of remembering. This factor structure generalized across memories elicited by recent and remote episodic prompts. However, distinct versions of mental time travel and relational memory emerged in response to the semantic prompt, suggesting these dimensions of memory are context-sensitive rather than fixed. While our findings validate the link between episodic memory and its theorized phenomenological elements, they also invite broader investigation of how phenomenology may vary across different classes of memory.

The Emergent Structure of Memory Phenomenology Depends on Whether Memories Are More Episodic or Semantic

What does it feel like to remember? The subjective experience of recall (phenomenology) is central to current definitions of episodic memory, with phenomenology so tightly coupled to these definitions that it is often used as a primary screen for categorizing memories as episodic or nonepisodic (Tulving, 2002). While research has offered insight regarding the experience of remembering (for a review, see Chiorri & Vannucci, 2024), instruments assessing characteristics of memory phenomenology (qualia) are commonly developed using top-down methods (e.g., confirmatory factor analysis) to capture theoretical dimensions of episodic memory without testing their underlying premises. Here, we tested whether episodic qualia emerge naturally from reports of memory phenomenology, or whether they are synthetic products of theory.

1.1. Qualia Structure and Episodic Memory Phenomenology

Episodic qualia were initially described in Endel Tulving's Organization of Memory (1972) in which he proposed working definitions of episodic and semantic memory. He argued that memory processes information using two specialized systems that interact during recall: episodic memory processes information about personal experiences and temporally locates them in reference to other experiences, and semantic memory organizes information about the meaning and interrelatedness of concepts. While qualia are commonly considered distinguishing features of episodic memory, Tulving (1985) proposed that the only phenomenological feature that is unique to episodic memory is conscious awareness, or the subjective awareness that a memory is a past personal experience.

Studies using instruments that assess constructs of episodic memory phenomenology (e.g., imagery, emotional connection, reliving) have provided valuable insights (for a review, see Chiorri & Vannucci, 2024). However, we are only aware of three instruments that interrogate the underlying structure of memory phenomenology (Boyacioglu & Akfirat, 2015; Fitzgerald & Broadbridge, 2013; Sutin & Robins, 2007). Yet even these studies limited their explorations to hypothesis-driven approaches that constrain the detection of emergent dimensions. While existing instruments largely organize memory qualia into distinct categories according to episodic theory, memories reported as strongly relived have been correlated with the presence of details, vividness, emotional connection, and continuous imagery (Irish et al., 2008 (Irish et al., , 2011)). This finding suggests that characteristics like reliving, details, and emotional connection may be better represented by dimensions that blend qualia across categories, rather than conforming to those discrete categories. The current study assumes existing instruments reliably measure the dimensions they were designed to measure, questioning only how well they represent the underlying structure of memory phenomenology.

Beyond the analytic method, the narrow range of memory classes used to study memory phenomenology may further limit theoretical development. Few studies have sampled experiences of memories that cannot be labeled as episodic (for a review, see Chiorri & Vannucci, 2024). While we were able to locate instruments assessing general semantic ability (for example, see Dritschel et al., 1992; Palombo et al., 2013), we found none that assess phenomenological qualities of semantic recall. This gap is likely a result of the expectation that semantic recall only produces an awareness of acquired knowledge (Tulving, 1985), but this neglects the ability to use semantic knowledge to build multimodal models (Tulving, 2002) and construct scenes (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007), such as a cat napping in a warm sunbeam or beach scene complete with accompanying sensations. If conscious awareness is the only quale that is unique to episodic memory (Tulving, 1985) and we only use instruments that quantify memory phenomenology to study episodic memories, then we may be conflating general properties of declarative memory with qualia that are specific to episodic memory.

1.2. Qualia Structure

To further explore this idea, we turned to strength and component-based accounts of memory to establish competing predictions concerning the relationship among qualia. Drawing on a memory strength account (e.g., Wixted, 2007; Yonelinas, 2002), we imagined that qualia may be rigidly bound, such that the strength of a memory and its qualia rise and fall together (the "iceberg" model). For example, all episodic memories may involve reexperiencing and mental time travel, with stronger reexperiencing predicting greater mental time travel. By contrast, component processes accounts propose that episodic memories recruit separable components that group to meet moment-to-moment retrieval demands (Moscovitch, 1992; Moscovitch et al., 2016). For example, scene construction may begin with generation and maintenance of a coherent spatial context (Dalton et al., 2018; Hassabis & Maguire, 2007) followed by recollection of a specific action seen in that location. In this case, hippocampal, spatial and visual brain areas may contribute spatial orientation and visual imagery qualia.

Yet in memories focused on the sequences of sounds, temporal and auditory qualia may be more prominently observed. We characterized the dynamic combination of sets of qualia as the "ad hoc" model.

1.3. The Current Study

We sampled a variety of memories and their characteristics using a purpose-built instrument that probed experiences associated with individual memories. We developed prompts to sample three memories with different memory characteristics, including memories that are widely considered episodic and semantic. Items were adapted from existing instruments and extended with novel content from relevant theoretical concepts, enabling our instrument to assess the breadth of Tulving's (2002) declarative memory framework. We then ran a family of exploratory factor analyses (EFA) to test and validate the emergent structure of declarative memory phenomenology from the natural variation of participants' reports. This cross-validation approach was designed to investigate whether the emergent structure of declarative memory phenomenology better aligns with theoretical models than other possible constructs, and whether traditionally recognized qualia are recovered from participants' reports of phenomenology when experiences are free to organize according to their natural covariance structure.

Using our novel instrument, participants evoked three different classes of memory and rated the phenomenology of each reported memory. We then tested our research question using a two-part analytic strategy: whether there is a global latent factor structure of phenomenology, and whether this structure depends on how memories are evoked. To test for a global factor structure that is stable across prompts, we divided the data into three mixed-prompt folds and conducted EFAs to identify and validate the global structure of phenomenology. We expected to identify and replicate a factor structure that broadly aligns with Tulving's theoretical framework. Since these analyses mixed classes of memories that are expected to produce different memory characteristics, this finding would suggest that phenomenological structure is a stable global feature of memory. Finally, we surveyed our results for possible evidence of a strength-or component-inspired qualia structure. If the structure has promptdependent (component-inspired) rather than global (strength-inspired) structural properties, this would be revealed by prompt-specific EFAs. To test whether prompt-dependent structure exists, we ran three prompt-specific EFAs. Given the theoretical distinction between episodic and semantic memories, we did not expect the global structure to fully generalize to each of the prompt-specific analyses and we expected factors to look different in retrievals that were episodic versus semantic.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Participants

We recruited 721 English-speaking participants over the age of 18 for an online study using Prolific (n = 531) and Reddit (n = 190). Participants who were not fluent in English, reported having a psychological diagnosis or epilepsy, reported being intoxicated, or failed data quality checks for all recall prompts (description empty or unrelated to prompt; Reddit only) were omitted from analysis (Prolific = 134, Reddit = 174). The final sample included 413 participants (200 women) between the ages of 18 and 77 (M = 41.01 years, SD = 14.59). Participants who completed the study on Prolific were paid £3.45 and participants who completed the study on Reddit were not compensated. This study received ethical approval from the Queen's General Research and Ethics Board.

2.2. Measures

>Measures 2.2.1.1.

2.2.1. Inventory of Autobiographical Experiences (IAE)

Computer-Guided Recall. We adapted the Autobiographical Interview (Levine et al., 2002) instructions to an online Qualtrics survey that prompted the recall of three different autobiographical memories. The survey started with a screen showing the general instructions, before proceeding to the specific recall prompts. Each prompt included the target life period for the memory and a short description of the type of information expected, including examples (See Supplementary Materials S1 for the complete script). For the two prompts that targeted specific experiences, participants were shown the standard instructions with embedded follow-up probes. For the prompt that targeted general experiences, instructions and probes were adapted to encourage participants to think about their overall experiences. Specifically, we prompted retrieval of a recent event (a specific experience from last week), a remote event (a specific experience from childhood), and a period summary (general experiences from childhood). The first prompt called for content widely regarded as episodic, since it has had little time for consolidation while the second called for content that may remain episodic, albeit possibly supplemented by semantic scripts. In contrast, the last prompt called for content widely regarded as semantic since general autobiographical knowledge lacks spatiotemporal specificity (Conway & Pleydell Pearce, 2000; Levine et al., 2002).

2.2.1.2. Questionnaire.

We developed a 53-item self-report questionnaire that was administered following each computer-guided recall prompt to quantify the expression of declarative memory phenomenology. We adapted existing items assessing episodic memory phenomenology (see Supplementary Materials S2 for schedule of influences) and created novel items assessing conscious use of general knowledge, concepts, and ideas. This questionnaire included items identifying traditional elements of episodic memory content (e.g., spatiotemporal specificity, mental imagery, conscious awareness), semantic memory content (e.g., conceptual information), and features associated with memory quality (e.g., elements of memory construction). See Supplementary Items S3 for the complete list.

All items were refined into simple and concise sentences, using similar sentence structure to assist comprehension and speed of completion. Wherever reasonable, items were phrased to focus on the experience of a specific recall. All items used a 5-point scale (generally,1 = strongly disagree to5 = strongly agree, although 18 items used custom anchors; see Supplementary Materials S3 for details).

Item order was randomized within four blocks that were themselves triaged to prioritize sampling information that was expected to fade the fastest, ensuring that the most time-sensitive content was captured before the integrity of the memory degraded. Items in the first two blocks probed the experience of recall (e.g., sense of immersion and mental imagery), items in the third block largely probed memory construction (e.g., ease of recall and use of conceptual information), and items in the fourth block probed fixed features of the memory (e.g., when and where it occurred).

2.3. Procedure

Participants completed the study online using a survey hosted on Qualtrics. Following collection of informed consent, they completed a demographic questionnaire and proceeded to the computerguided recall portion of the IAE. Participants were given a prompt corresponding to one of three conditions. After spending 1 min writing a brief description, they completed the IAE questionnaire about their memory. This sequence was repeated until all three randomly ordered prompts were complete.

The expected time to complete the study was approximately 25 min. Upon completion, participants were automatically thanked and debriefed.

2.4. Data Cleaning

To address ceiling and floor effects, IAE items with an extreme value as the median were removed from further analysis (I14, I15, I26, I41, I42). Seven additional items were omitted because they assessed attention (I30), were focused on the written narrative (I16, I17), were subject to demand characteristics (I40), had categorical responses that varied on multiple dimensions (I1), or created an artifactual dimension (opposing vantage points) that pulled on phenomenologically relevant items and obscured the construct of interest (I4, I5). The final factor analysis included 41 IAE items. Any memory with a description that was insufficient to determine compliance (<15 characters or unrelated to prompt; n = 4), with a failed attention check (n = 16), or with a missing response (n = 84) was omitted from analysis. To account for bots and low-effort responding, we calculated response variability across items for each memory and removed outliers (n = 1; Meade & Craig, 2012).

3.1. Identifying the Global Structure of Declarative Memory Phenomenology

To identify the global latent factor structure of declarative memory phenomenology, we randomly divided the data into three folds, with each exceeding Fabrigar and Wegener's (2012) minimum recommended sample size for stable EFA solutions. Each fold contained a maximum of one memory per participant, and a similar number of memories from each prompt (recent event, remote event, period summary). Given that our primary goal was to identify a global structure of phenomenology, and that each prompt sampled a class of memory expected to produce different memory characteristics, we used mixed-prompt folds to capture this global structure (see Table 1 for the specific composition of each fold). Our questionnaire contained ordinal and categorical participant responses so we used polychoric correlations and minimum residual factor analysis, which are robust to violations of the assumptions of normality (Flora et al., 2012; Flora & Curran, 2004). Capturing 12% of the variance and consisting of eight items, we named the fourth factor relational memory. These items reflected vivid and detailed recollections that were supported by contextual and structured information about time and space. Drawing inspiration from the role of semantic memory in recall (Irish & Piguet, 2013), we labeled the fifth factor semantic memory because it had items related to the constructive aspects of remembering. Capturing 11% of the variance and consisting of 10 items, this factor described the presence of generic imagery and reliance on general knowledge. 3.2. Replicating the Global Structure Across Folds

Next, we investigated whether there was evidence of configural invariance across all folds of the data (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). To this end, we used the same method outlined for fold 1 to determine the latent factor structure of the remaining two folds. We first confirmed the suitability of folds 2 and 3 for factor analysis (BTS: χ 2 (820) = 9890.12 and 10345.51, respectively, p < .001 for both; KMO = 0.89 for both). Since the parallel analysis and scree plot supported different models (six versus one to three factors, respectively), we explored three models per fold (four to six factors) for evidence of configural invariance. Although fit indices are not reliable determinants of model fit for EFA (Montoya & Edwards, 2021), we found that the RMSEA pointed towards solutions with at least five factors for fold 2 (See Supplementary Materials S4 for details). We again preferred the five-factor model in both folds because it continued to offer the most parsimonious interpretable structure with at least three substantially loaded items per factor.

As with the analysis for fold 1, all factors demonstrated good internal consistency (ω = .84 to .93) and the overall reliability was excellent (ω Tot = .97, .96) with substantial shared variance across factors (ω h = .74, .72; ECV = .49, .47) for folds 2 and 3, respectively. An inspection of the items loading on each factor revealed few differences in folds 2 and 3 compared to fold 1 (See Supplementary Materials S5 for solutions). While these items did not meet the criteria for primary or cross-loading status on relational memory, they were within .10 of these thresholds. 4. Discussion

Here, we investigated the emergent structure of memory phenomenology. Using an empirical approach that mixed classes of memories expected to produce different memory characteristics, we distinguished and replicated five qualia of declarative memory across three folds of our data: imagery, autonoetic consciousness, MTT, relational memory, and semantic memory. While these factors emerged naturally from participants' reports of memory phenomenology, they showed varying degrees of alignment with established instruments. Notably, the semantic memory factor has no clear predecessor in the literature. We then explored whether this factor structure depends on how memories are evoked by testing the stability of this structure in each prompt from which it was derived. As expected, we found that the structure fully generalized to both event prompts but not the period summary, which suggests memory phenomenology has prompt-dependent rather than global structural properties.

These findings support and extend Tulving's (2002) framework, and they are more consistent with a component-inspired "ad hoc" structure than a strength-inspired "iceberg" qualia structure.

4.1. Characterizations of Episodic Memory Dimensions

We found evidence that coherent factors emerge naturally from participants' reports, such that there was a cluster of four moderately to strongly intercorrelated episodic memory dimensions. This cluster of episodic dimensions included two factors representing facets of conscious awareness (autonoetic consciousness and MTT), the only quale that Tulving (1985) identified as uniquely episodic.

An item assessing participants' intimate connection with the memory cross-loaded on both of these factors, supporting Tulving's (2002) notion that autonoetic consciousness and MTT are closely related facets of a larger construct. The episodic cluster also included imagery and relational memory factors, both of which measure facets that Tulving (2002) postulated to be correlates of recall quality. Notably, the relational memory factor includes the assessment of features commonly used to determine the episodic richness (see Levine et al., 2002 for an example using detail counts). Consistent with this practice, the relational memory factor may represent a distinct and necessary dimension of episodic phenomenology that distinguishes between specific experiences and general knowledge of events.

Broadly construed, the structure we observed provides bottom-up support for core premises of episodic memory theory.

When compared to studies using hypothesis-driven approaches, our exploratory methodology revealed alternative characterizations of memory constructs. Aspects of memory detail and vividness tend to be investigated as a single dimension (Boyacioglu & Akfirat, 2015; Sutin & Robins, 2007). Our analysis, however, placed these in the larger construct of relational memory, which includes spatiotemporal and chronological features of recall. Although it may seem more logical for elements like detail and vividness to be independent or combine with mental imagery, we found evidence that they are closely related to spatiotemporal and relational elements of memory. That is, memories that are highly detailed and vivid tend to be high in spatiotemporal features, but not necessarily in imagery.

Consistent with scene construction theory and research showing the importance of spatial context in memory representations (e.g., Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Robin et al., 2018), this finding suggests the importance of spatiotemporal features in the reconstruction of memories. Interpreted through the lens of Conway and Pleydell-Pearce's (2000) hierarchical model of autobiographical memory, spatiotemporal representation of an event may act as a scaffold that allows access to the event. This could create a high degree of correspondence between ratings of details, vividness, and spatiotemporal features. While the strong correlation between relational memory and imagery factors suggests perceptual content often accompanies relational memory, their separability indicates that richly detailed memories are anchored in spatiotemporal structure rather than perceptual imagery.

The cross-loading of thoughts and emotions on autonoetic consciousness and imagery is yet another pattern that was only revealed through the use of bottom-up analysis. Metacognitions like thoughts and emotions reflect a person's state of mind and affect at a given moment in time, constituting both self-referential and perceptual experiences. Therefore, these elements should be expected to bridge dimensions of autonoetic consciousness and imagery. This notion is consistent with research showing that emotional connection during recall is correlated with experiences of imagery and reliving (Irish et al., 2008 (Irish et al., , 2011)).

4.2. Novel Characterization of Semantic Processes in Recall

Our last factor, semantic memory, has no clear predecessor in the literature on memory phenomenology. To the best of our knowledge, no questionnaires exist that probe for a potential phenomenological profile of semantic recall (for a review, see Chiorri & Vannucci, 2024). The closest analog we found is the Survey of Autobiographical Memory (Palombo et al., 2013). This tool, however, only measures perceived general ability to recall facts rather than the use of general knowledge during recall. The semantic memory factor, by contrast, assesses the conscious experience of using semantic knowledge during individual recollections, and it does not include any characteristics that are traditionally considered episodic. This factor can be interpreted as having two thematic subgroups: one describing the use of general knowledge to construct or supplement individual memories, and a second evaluating difficulty remembering and the absence of personal connection. These groups measure the use of active strategic processes and the absence of episodic memory during recall, respectively.

Considered on its own, the second subgroup could be interpreted as reflecting memory failure.

This subgroup-based interpretation, however, evaluates the semantic memory factor in isolation, ignoring its relationship with other factors. If the semantic memory factor actually represented memory failure, then we would expect to see a strong negative relationship between it and the other four factors. Instead, weak interfactor correlations with semantic memory were observed, suggesting it is largely independent. Since the semantic memory factor was robustly detected in all three classes of autobiographical memory recall, and it shared limited variance with the other factors, it may represent a distinct mode of retrieval that supports the recall of different classes of declarative memory. In effect, when we have an absence of episodic memory during recall (e.g., fewer details), we may increase our reliance on active strategic processes to fill in the blanks and smooth out our memory. This notion is consistent with theoretical accounts of semantic memory (Irish & Piguet, 2013; Tulving, 2002).

4.3. Prompt Based Findings

We found evidence that the global structure of declarative memory phenomenology was stable across memories elicited by event prompts but not period summary prompts. Imagery, autonoetic consciousness, and semantic memory factors were consistently found to have similar items loading onto them. However, when period summaries were prompted, the MTT and relational memory factors were distinctive. For the global structure, MTT was associated with general recreations of the original experience, but for period summary prompts, vivid dynamic imagery also loaded onto the factor. For the global structure, relational memory included items that contextualized spatiotemporal and metacognitive features of memory, but these were not associated in the period summary case, leaving the factor only with items associated with temporal sequencing. Despite these differences, the period summary prompt was still associated with five differently organized EFA factors that were each interpretable and internally consistent, and its overall model fit approximated that of the global structure. We interpreted this as an illustration of how the relevant phenomenological dimensions of recall may shift as a function of the type of memory under interrogation. This differentiation is not consistent with sampling variability: the core global structure was stable across both event prompts, while the period summary prompt showed distinct organization in only two factors.

The period summary instructions were designed to elicit personal memories that were not grounded in a specific time and place. These memories should be semantic in nature according to traditional definitions, which implies they should only produce an awareness of acquired knowledge (Tulving, 1985). By this account, it would not be surprising to detect the semantic memory factor. The alignment of imagery with MTT in this prompt, though unexpected, can be similarly explained by Tulving's (2002) supposition that imagery is a correlate of memory quality rather than a defining feature of episodic memory. Since Tulving (1985) considered conscious awareness to be the defining feature of episodic memory, the continued detection of factors assessing autonoetic consciousness and MTT in a prompt type that does not meet the definitional criteria for episodic memory is puzzling. An explanation for this counterintuitive result can be found in Conway and Pleydell-Pearce's (2000) model of autobiographical memory. Suggesting that autobiographical memory has a hierarchical organization, this model implies that semantic information about the self retains access to the source information from which it was derived. If this produces contextual tags that connect personal semantic information to its original episodic traces, then recalling period summaries might coactivate networks of episodic content and associated qualia. This is consistent with neural evidence showing recruitment of episodic memory systems during semantic retrieval tasks (Sheldon & Moscovitch, 2012). While our blind rating procedure provided evidence of general compliance with the prompt-level instructions, a review of participant responses revealed several memory descriptions that blended episodic content into period summaries.

Regardless of the mechanism, the detection of autonoetic consciousness and MTT in a prompt that is expected to produce semantic recollections calls into question Tulving's proposition that autonoetic consciousness and MTT are defining features of episodic memory.

4.4. Strength-Versus Component-Inspired Qualia Structure

Our strength-inspired "iceberg" model predicts covariance of qualia, whereas the componentinspired "ad hoc" model predicts separaration. Consistent with the former prediction, each of our hierarchical omega analyses showed support for a common general factor, and we observed moderately strong interfactor correlations among four of the five identified factors (with the fifth factor showing weak correlation with the others). However, we did still observe a partially separable multidimensional structure, and at least 70% of the variance was unique to each factor. The iceberg model also predicts stability in qualia structure across memory classes (event prompts versus period summary prompt) whereas the "ad hoc" account anticipates variation. A five-factor structure was detected across analyses, but MTT and relational memory factors reorganized when prompts were non-episodic.

Accordingly, while the qualia analysis offers some positive support for both models, the iceberg model is not easily reconciled with the observed variation in structure across classes of memory.

To further explore the relationship between observed factors and component process accounts (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Moscovitch et al., 2016), we compared our results with the network of component processes identified by Hassabis and Maguire (2007; see Box 2). Five of the nine identified processes map loosely onto factors in the current study: the visual imagery process resembles a facet of the imagery factor; the processes of autonoetic consciousness and self are both related to the autonoetic consciousness factor; the subjective time process resembles the MTT factor; and the narrative process is related to facets of the relational memory factor. The remaining four processes have no clear counterparts in the current study, including scene construction, which was most closely represented by two spatial-context proxies (spatial immersion and clarity) that aligned with different factors in the event prompt (MTT and relational memory, respectively). The spatial context items were assigned to the same factor in the period summary prompt, wherein a group of richness items (spatial clarity, vividness, details) diverged from items detailing temporal relationships between event elements, and aligned with the sensation of spatial immersion while reliving a memory. The joint-factor relationship between ratings of richness and reliving in memory retrievals that span extended periods of time may be indicative of increased abstraction of memory content across multiple experiences, leading people to integrate experiences across the period as a whole such that values of richness and reliving covary. Since event and period summary prompts elicit different task demands (describe single event versus synthesize extended time), changes in qualia structure in the period summary prompt are broadly consistent with the theory that memory recruits different component processes to meet task demands (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Moscovitch et al., 2016). This pattern is broadly consistent with the proposal that memory recruits different component processes to meet task demands (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Moscovitch et al., 2016). The current results may in this way be compatible with a broadly networked, hippocampally based retrieval process that recruits distinct regions associated with individual qualia-based components as needed (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Moscovitch et al., 2016; Poppenk et al., 2013), wherein factor scores may offer valuable insights into individual versus scaled recruitment of qualia-based components and their related brain regions.

4.5. Limitations and Future Directions

As with all survey-based research, the emergent factor structure observed in the current study may reflect covariance driven by surface features (e.g., item wording), rather than by memory content.

However, the majority of the IAE items were adapted from validated instruments and we reviewed response patterns to determine the suitability of each item for analysis, removing any that might destabilize the factor structure. We then replicated this structure across three independent subsamples, providing further evidence that the observed structure represented stable covariance in the data instead of sample-or item-specific artifacts. Next, the IAE does not include an item that assesses recall success. We were, however, able to review memory descriptions to confirm general compliance. Still, an item assessing recall success would be helpful for distinguishing between semantic recall and memory failure. Finally, our analyses were constrained by the assumptions of EFA, which requires all responses to be independent. While we were able to investigate natural variance in responses across subsamples of mixed or isolated prompt types, our analyses only ever contain one response per participant. As a result, we were unable to account for any variance attributable to individual differences. That said, an exploratory analysis that included all available responses per participant (up to three, across all conditions) again showed the same factor structure. The reintroduction of non-independence suggests that individual differences did not substantially distort the observed covariance among items. Still, future work should attempt to disentangle these contributions.

There is growing evidence that declarative memory cannot be cleanly divided into categories that are purely episodic and semantic (Bontkes et al., 2025; Renoult et al., 2012; Tanguay et al., 2023).

To date, there has been little research on the phenomenology of memories that do not meet strict episodic criteria, let alone qualia indicative of the use of semantic memory during recall (for a review, see Chiorri & Vannucci, 2024). In the current study, we examined recollections that were expected to be more semantic than episodic, but this is only an introductory investigation. Real-world memory use commonly requires summarizing or synthesizing information across memories until they no longer adhere to the strict spatiotemporal boundaries of episodic memory (e.g., high-level summaries of single experiences and descriptions of repeated events; Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000; D'Argembeau, 2020), meaning there are other types of declarative memory that may not adhere to the global structure we identified in this study. Moving forward, it will be important to examine whether there are other classes of memory that produce distinct phenomenological profiles.

4.6. Conclusion

Moving beyond hypothesis-driven approaches to memory phenomenology, we showed that qualia are discoverable using bottom-up analyses of subjective experiences across probed classes of memory. Consistent with Tulving's (2002) framework and validating the link between episodic memory and its theorized phenomenological dimensions, the observed five-factor structure included traditional dimensions of episodic memory and a novel factor querying the strategic use of semantic memory during recall. While all five dimensions were consistently detected in mixed and specific subgroup analyses, our analysis of the period summary prompt produced distinct versions of MTT and relational memory factors. That is, MTT absorbed items related to vivid re-experiencing while relational memory lost items that contextualized the memory. This divergence was not consistent with sampling variability, hinting that memory phenomenology is prompt dependent such that it shifts in response to the class of memory being recalled as predicted by a component-inspired "ad hoc" account of qualia structure. This finding encourages further investigation of prompt-dependent effects, as well as caution when applying traditional one-size-fits-all methods to the study of memory phenomenology.
