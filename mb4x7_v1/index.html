Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 24 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

design_dependent_variables On each trial of the short Kent Face Matching Test (KFMT: Fysh & Bindemann, 2018), participants will be asked to decide whether two simultaneously presented faces show the same person. Participants will respond to the question “Do these photographs show the same person, or two different people?” along a 6AFC scale, which contains an identification decision (same, different) and an associated level of confidence (definitely, probably, guess). These 6AFC responses will be used to calculate all possible measures of performance on the face matching task. We will report overall accuracy [((Hits + Correct Rejections) / Total Trials)*100)] in the main text, to ensure that these data are easily understood by all readers. However, we can also use these responses to calculate measures from signal detection theory, including Area Under the Curve (AUC), sensitivity, or criterion (Macmillan & Creelman, 2004; Stanislaw & Todorov, 1999). If reported at all, these other measures of performance would likely be placed in the supplementary materials.

Our other key measure will be that of reliance. Here we define reliance as instances in which the participant changes their final response to be more alike that of the AFRS. We anticipate measuring reliance in steps of confidence change. The identification responses are made on a 6-point scale from “Definitely Same” to “Definitely Different” (1-6). However, we will create a measure of reliance in which the participant’s initial decision becomes point 0, and the final decision is calculated in units of confidence change, with positive values indicating the decision becoming more alike the AFRS and negative units demonstrating “rejection” (in which the final decision moves away from the decision of the AFRS). An alternative measure of reliance is that of binary identification change (i.e., did the participant change from a “same” identification decision to a “different” identification). However, this measure does not capture the more nuanced changes in confidence that can still be attributed to AFRS reliance (i.e., moving from “guess same” to “probably same”). Reliance on the AFRS is not possible when participants make an initial decision that matches that of the AFRS with “definite” confidence (i.e., the participant’s initial decision is “Definitely same”, and the AFRS also shows “same”). We will remove these trials from the calculation of reliance measures, and report them separately (since it is still possible, though unusual, for the participant to show “rejection” of the system’s decision in these cases, which would occur if their final decision moved away from that of the system after it confirmed their initial decision).

Trust in the AFRS will be measured using Lee and Moray’s (1994) two item relative trust in automation measure. As in their original study, we will subtract self-confidence ratings from trust in automation ratings, to leave a change score in which positive values indicate higher relative trust in the automated system and negative values greater self-confidence. In addition to Lee and Moray’s established measure, we will also ask participants whether they trust the AFRS to help them in the task (binary “yes” or “no”), and to give estimates (0-100%) of their individual accuracy, the accuracy of the AFRS, and their accuracy when assisted by the AFRS. Any exploratory analyses will be clearly identified as such in the resulting text.

additional_comments Method
Kent Face Matching Test (KFMT): All participants will complete the short version of the KFMT (Fysh & Bindemann, 2018). The short KFMT consists of 40 unfamiliar face pairs (20 identity matches, 20 identity mismatches). The stimuli will be presented in colour, and the presentation order of all trials will be random.

Face Matching Trial Procedure: Two faces will be presented onscreen simultaneously. The question shown on each trial will be “Do these photographs show the same person, or two different people?”. Participants will give a 6AFC response which consists of an identification decision (“same” or “different”) and a confidence (“definitely”, “probably”, “guess”). Participants will first give their unassisted independent decision to each trial. Once the initial response has been made, two new sentences will appear below the original question prompt. The first will show participants their initial response (as a reminder). Below that will be the sentence “Facial Recognition System Says:…” with the possible responses being “SAME” and “DIFFERENT”. For the purpose of calculating reliance, these decisions will be treated as “Definite” from the AFRS. Participants will then be asked to make their final judgment, again using the 6AFC response options.

Attention Check: As in our previous work (Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Carragher et al., 2022), we will insert an attention check trial into the main face matching task. The attention check trial will be a mismatched pairs of famous faces that, even if not familiar to the participant, can be correctly resolved by differences in ethnicity. Data from participants who fail to correctly respond “different” (at any level of confidence) to the attention check trial will be discarded from all analyses. The AFRS will not show a decision for the attention check shown – rather, the system will report that it is “OFFLINE”.

AFRS: As in Carragher and Hancock (2023), the decisions made by the simulated AFRS in the experiment will be based on the performance of the real Deep Convolutional Neural Network (DCNN). In the current study, this was a research DCNN from Imperial College London shared with PJBH. This DCNN computes a similarity score between the two faces, which it evaluates against a threshold value to make a final identity decision (see Carragher & Hancock, 2020, 2023).
The real DCNN correctly resolved 40/40 trials in the short KFMT (overall accuracy 100%). To achieve our desired level of performance for the simulated AFRS (see below), we selected two face pairs that the simulated AFRS would be seen to err on in the experiment (one match pair, one mismatch pair). The selected trials were those that received the similarity values closest to the real system’s decision threshold (i.e., those that were closest to being errors).
In this study, the simulated AFRS will err on the same two pairs (one match trial, and one mismatch trial) for all participants, which gives the AFRS an overall accuracy of 95% (sensitivity of d´ = 3.29). However, participants will only be told that the facial recognition system is “highly accurate” and “state-of-the-art” (unlike our previous experiments, in which participants were told the accuracy of the system directly).
Since the average performance of humans on the short KFMT is 66% (Fysh & Bindemann, 2018), an AFRS with an overall accuracy of 95% is likely to benefit most participants.

As in the original study, we will subtract self-confidence ratings from trust in automation ratings, to leave a change score in which positive values indicate higher trust in the automated system and negative values greater self-confidence. In addition to Lee and Moray’s established measure, we will also ask participants whether they trust the AFRS to help them in the task (binary “yes” or “no”), and to give estimates (0-100%) of their individual accuracy, the accuracy of the AFRS, and their accuracy when assisted by the AFRS (see Additional Documentation).

We will conduct our planned analyses based on participant’s responses to the post-training relative trust measure, since this represents the intention of the participants to trust the system immediately prior to the main face matching task. However, we anticipate conducting exploratory analyses to investigate how trust in the AFRS might change after using the system as a decision aid (i.e., from post-training to post-KFMT).

Training Trials (Feedback & Experience Manipulation): Before starting the main face matching task (KFMT), all participants will complete 20 training trials (simply presented to participants as the “first face matching task”). Those in the feedback condition will interact with the AFRS and see the correct answer after each trial. This feedback can be used by the participants to see whether they or the AFRS provided the correct answer. Participants in the experience condition will complete the training trials with the AFRS, but will not receive feedback. Participants in the control condition will complete the training trials, but without the AFRS and without feedback. The 20 training trials will come from the unfamiliar faces of the Stirling Famous Face Matching Test (Carragher & Hancock, 2020). The AFRS will be seen to make 1 error over the 20 trials, giving it an accuracy of 95% (just like the main experiment). The error trial will be selected in the same way as for the main experiment (i.e., that with the similarity rating closes to the real DCNNs decision threshold). The trial procedure for the training block will be identical to that in the main experiment (i.e., an initial decision, then an aided final decision), the only exceptions being that participants in the Feedback condition will be shown the correct answer after each trial, and that the “control” condition will not use the AFRS at all during the training block.

To select 20 trials from the larger unfamiliar SFFMT set for training, we selected the 5 male and 5 female match trials with DCNN similarity ratings that were closest to the system’s decision threshold – and then did the same for the mismatch trials. Cases where the ICL system returned the same similarity values to multiple face pairs were then sorted by lowest human accuracy on the task, taken from the 53 control participants in Carragher and Hancock (2020). The ICL system correctly resolved all unfamiliar face pairs in the SFFMT. The error trial for the training block was a male mismatch trial, selected using the same procedure as in the main experiment. During the training block, the AFRS made “SAME” or “DIFFERENT” decisions. The feedback given to participants showed the correct answer to the trial, and then indicated that the facial recognition system was “Correct” or “WRONG!”. Participants then responded to the question “was your final decision correct or incorrect?” to reinforce the feedback.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Identity verification often involves deciding whether two images show the same person or two different people. Automated Facial Recognition (AFR) systems can improve unfamiliar face matching accuracy when used as decision aids, yet humans consistently fail to use them optimally, with average automation-assisted performance regularly below that of the system alone. This suboptimal performance may reflect participants' difficulty in appropriately weighing their trust in the automation with self-confidence in their own ability. We investigated whether a brief training block with either trial-by-trial feedback, or simple exposure to the AFR system, would improve automation-assisted unfamiliar face matching performance in a subsequent task. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three training conditions: feedback (AFR system, trial-by-trial feedback), experience (AFR system, no feedback), or control (no AFR system, no feedback). While we found all conditions improved with assistance from the AFR system in the subsequent face matching task, the feedback condition improved significantly more than the experience and control conditions.

Feedback specifically increased trust in automation, which led to greater reliance on the AFR system. However, greater reliance also led to greater acceptance of rare AFR system errors in the feedback condition. Our results show that a brief period of trial-by-trial feedback, but not mere exposure to the AFR system, can improve automation-assisted unfamiliar face matching performance, offering a promising direction for improving identification outcomes.

[div-01]

Automated Facial Recognition (AFR) systems compare two images to make a prediction about whether they show the same person or two different people (Howard et al., 2023; Noyes & Hill, 2021). Identity verification involves comparing a probe image to a specific reference image (1:1), while face recognition typically compares the probe against an entire gallery or watchlist (1:n). Here we are interested in identity verification, which is known as face matching (or face comparison) in the psychological literature (Fysh & Bindemann, 2017). AFR systems are a relatively recent development in the history of face recognition (Towler et al., 2021), tied to technical progress in computer science and computer vision. Many modern AFR systems that use Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNN) achieve remarkable levels of verification accuracy on high-quality images (Grother et al., 2021), though their performance has been reported to differ for different demographic groups (Cook et al., 2019). Even though they still make errors, top AFR systems now outperform all but the very best human observers (Phillips et al., 2018; Towler et al., 2023). As these systems improve, they are being integrated into many real-world settings where the face is used for identity verification.

Before AFR systems, humans performed identity verification, often by comparing a live individual to their photo ID document (e.g., a driver's licence or passport). Given the prevalence of this task, it is easy to assume that we must be highly skilled at face comparison.

That assumption is generally true when the individual is known (familiar) to us (Jenkins et al., 2011; Johnston & Edmonds, 2009). Familiar face matching is very easy for most observers, even under highly challenging conditions (Bruce et al., 2001; Noyes & Jenkins, 2019). But performance changes dramatically when the individual is unfamiliar, as they mostly are in applied real-world settings (Fysh & Bindemann, 2017; Hancock et al., 2000).

Laboratory studies consistently show that the average human observer makes errors on 10-30% of decisions when comparing two unfamiliar face images (Burton et al., 2010; Fysh & Bindemann, 2018b; White et al., 2015). While there are significant individual differences in ability (Bobak et al., 2016; Burton et al., 2010; Russell et al., 2009), and some professionals (Wirth & Carbon, 2017) including highly skilled forensic face examiners can perform exceptionally well (Phillips et al., 2018; Towler et al., 2023; White et al., 2015), professional experience alone does not automatically improve unfamiliar face matching performance (Burton et al., 2026; Weatherford et al., 2021; White et al., 2014).

AFR systems are typically used with the involvement of a human operator.

Depending on the applied setting, human involvement can range from providing oversight to actively using the system as a decision aid. One aim of human involvement is for the human to prevent any errors from the AFR system from being enacted. Even though the AFR system and the human operator can both make errors, it is possible for two imperfect agents to achieve a level of collaborative performance that exceeds either alone (Bahrami et al., 2010), even if such collaborative accuracy gains are rarely found (Bartlett & McCarley, 2017; Boskemper et al., 2021). While statistically fusing independent identification decisions from humans and algorithms has consistently led to collaborative accuracy gains (O'Toole et al., 2007; Phillips et al., 2018; Towler et al., 2023; White et al., 2015), independent fusion does not represent a scenario in which the human operator is aware of the output of the AFR system before deciding on their final course of action. An emerging stream of research has consistently shown that novice participants struggle to use AFR systems effectively as decision aids, when they are aware of the system's prediction before giving their own (Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Carragher et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2026; Kokje, Lermer, Donkin, & Gaube, 2026; Kokje, Lermer, Kleine, & Gaube, 2026; Mueller et al., 2026).

Early studies showed that the human's decisions were biased toward those of the AFR system, regardless of whether they were correct or incorrect (Fysh & Bindemann, 2018a; Howard et al., 2020). The strength of this bias toward the system's predictions appears to increase as the task becomes harder, including when the face pairs are particularly challenging (Carragher & Hancock, 2023), covered by masks (Barragan et al., 2022), or if the participant is placed under time pressure (Hua et al., 2026). When the AFR system is more accurate than the average participant, this bias means that most participants significantly improve their own performance when using the system as a decision aid (Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Hua et al., 2026; Mueller et al., 2026). Crucially, however, automationassisted performance has consistently failed to reach the level of accuracy the AFR system offered alone (Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Carragher et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2026; Kokje, Lermer, Donkin, & Gaube, 2026; Kokje, Lermer, Kleine, & Gaube, 2026; Mueller et al., 2026). Participants do not accept every correct prediction from the AFR system, while also often failing to correct the rare errors it makes (Hua et al., 2026; Kokje, Lermer, Donkin, & Gaube, 2026). This overall suboptimal assisted performance (Bartlett et al., 2024) is consistent with automation disuse; a reluctance to rely on an automated aid that is highly reliable (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997).

There are many factors that contribute to an operator's decision to use an automated decision aid (NASEM, 2022), but two key factors are the operator's trust in the automation and their self-confidence in their own ability to perform the same task (Dzindolet et al., 2003; Hoff & Bashir, 2015; Lee & Moray, 1994; Lee & See, 2004). When trust exceeds selfconfidence, the operator is likely to use the aid. When self-confidence exceeds trust, the operator is unlikely to use the aid (Madhavan et al., 2006). One approach has been to measure "relative trust in automation" (RTIA), in which ratings of self-confidence are subtracted from ratings of trust in the automation (Lee & Moray, 1994). Using this approach, Carragher et al. (2024) found that greater RTIA was associated with larger performance gains when using the AFR system as a decision aid in an unfamiliar face matching task. However, using a different questionnaire, Mueller et al. (2026) did not find a relationship between trust in their AFR system and assisted performance. Optimal automation use requires appropriate calibration between the level of confidence that the human operator has in their own abilities, and the level of trust they have in the automated aid (Lee & Moray, 1994; Lee & See, 2004).

In these previous experiments, the simulated AFR systems have generally been given accuracy above 90%, far better than most participants on the same tasks (Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Carragher et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2026). Consequently, most participants would benefit from relying on the AFR system more often. Despite telling participants the exact accuracy of the simulated AFR system, these studies still report an overall pattern of disuse (Carragher & Hancock, 2023), even among those with high trust in automation (Carragher et al., 2024). One possibility is that this disuse may be due to inappropriately high self-confidence or inappropriately low trust in automation. Research has consistently shown that participants have little insight into their global unfamiliar face matching ability, with estimates of overall task accuracy bearing little relation to observed task performance (Bindemann et al., 2014; Bobak et al., 2019; Carragher et al., 2024; Palermo et al., 2017).

Less is known about perceptions of AFR systems in these tasks, although public opinion about facial recognition technologies varies widely (Ritchie et al., 2021). Howard et al. (2020) reported participants were more inclined to trust themselves to make face matching decisions, compared to algorithms or other people. While Carragher et al. (2024) reported that the average participant showed greater trust in the AFR system than their own ability, it is possible that the extent of the AFR system's performance advantage was underappreciated.

With past research suggesting automation reliance is influenced by both self-confidence and trust in automation (Lee & Moray, 1994), interventions that influence these factors could be effective at improving the use of the AFR system as a decision aid.

The aim of the current study was to investigate whether we could improve automation-assisted unfamiliar face matching performance. We tested whether providing trial-by-trial feedback during an initial "training block" would increase reliance on the AFR system in a subsequent automation-assisted unfamiliar face matching task. We expected that trial-by-trial feedback would assist participants in gauging the performance of the AFR system (i.e., that it is highly accurate), and of their own ability (i.e., that they are probably less accurate than the AFR system). These conditions should lead to increases in RTIA, which in turn, should lead to greater reliance on the AFR system in the subsequent task. A second "experience" condition completed the training block with the AFR system, but without receiving trial-by-trial feedback. The experience condition was included to test whether explicit feedback was necessary for improved automation-assisted performance or whether participants could make inferences about levels of performance simply from completing the initial task with the AFR system (i.e., Carragher & Hancock, 2023).

Therefore, we included a third control condition in which participants completed the training block without feedback or the AFR system, which allowed us to isolate the contribution of AFR system experience from mere exposure to an unfamiliar face matching task. Based on the literature reviewed above, we preregistered the following hypotheses 1.

H1:

The aided performance of all three training conditions will fail to reach the level of performance achieved by the AFR system alone.

H2: Aided performance on the main face matching task will differ as a function of training condition, with the feedback condition showing greater performance than the experience condition, and the experience condition showing greater performance than the control condition.

Design

The experiment consisted of two unfamiliar face matching tasks. However, the first task served solely to deliver the key feedback and experience manipulation (referred to as the "training block" in this manuscript, but not to participants). The second "main" task was where we investigated whether the feedback or experience manipulation affected AFR system use. All analyses reported below come from performance on this second task.

The main experiment had a mixed measures design. Training Condition (feedback, experience, control) was a between-participants factor. Decision Phase (initial, assisted) was a within-participants factor. All participants made an initial unassisted identification decision to each trial, before seeing the prediction of the simulated AFR system, and then making a final assisted decision.

Kent Face Matching Test

The main task used the short version of the Kent Face Matching Test (KFMT: Fysh & Bindemann, 2018b), which consists of 40 pairs of unfamiliar faces (20 identity matches, 20 identity mismatches). The short KFMT is a challenging unfamiliar face matching task, with normed average overall accuracy of 66% (Fysh & Bindemann, 2018b). The stimuli were presented in the original test format: in colour, and with one image smaller than the other (as it was from a student ID card). The 40 trials were presented in random order, within a single trial block.

Face Matching Trial Procedure

The two faces were presented onscreen simultaneously, with the question "Do these photographs show the same person, or two different people?" shown below. Responses were made on a 6-point scale, consisting of an identification decision ("same" or "different") and a level of confidence ("definitely", "probably", "guess"). First, participants made their unassisted initial decision on the trial. Once the initial response was made, two new lines appeared below the original question prompt. The first reminded participants of their initial response, while the second said "Facial Recognition System Says:…" with the possible responses being "SAME" and "DIFFERENT". Participants then made their final assisted response to the trial, using the same response options.

Automated Facial Recognition System

The predictions of the simulated AFR system in the experiment were based on the performance of a real DCNN on the same face matching taskfoot_0 . However, the real DCNN correctly resolved all 40 trials of the short KFMT. As in previous work (Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Carragher et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2026), we introduced errors into the performance of the simulated AFR system in the experiment (hence why it is described as "simulated"), so participants could intervene on system errors. We selected two face pairs to become errors (one match, one mismatch), bringing system accuracy to 95%, far exceeding average human performance on the task (Fysh & Bindemann, 2018b). The trials selected as errors had similarity values closest to the real system's decision threshold (i.e., they were closest to being errors). Participants were told that the facial recognition system in the experiment was "highly accurate" and "state-of-the-art" but were not explicitly told the accuracy of the system.

Attention Check

We added a mismatched pair of famous faces that could be correctly resolved by differences in ethnicity as an attention check to the KFMT (e.g., Carragher & Hancock, 2023). An incorrect initial decision (any confidence) led to exclusion from all analyses.

Feedback & Experience Manipulation (Training Condition)

Before the main KFMT, all participants completed the first face matching task, which consisted of 20 trials from the unfamiliar section of the Stirling Famous Face Matching Test (SFFMT: Carragher & Hancock, 2020). To select 20 trials from the original set of 40, we selected the 5 male and 5 female match/mismatch trials with similarity ratings closest to the real DCNN's decision threshold (using lowest human accuracy to resolve ties). One error was added to the performance of the simulated AFR system in the training block using the same procedure described above, giving 95% accuracy (consistent with the main experiment).

The Feedback condition completed the training block with an AFR system, following the Face Matching Task Procedure outlined above. But after submitting their final assisted decision, these participants saw the correct answer to the trial and a statement that the facial recognition system was "Correct" or "WRONG!". They also responded to the question "Was your final decision correct or incorrect?" to reinforce the feedback before the next trial started. The Experience condition completed the training block with assistance from the AFR system, following the Face Matching Task Procedure outlined above. They did not receive feedback. Participants in the Control condition completed the training block without the AFR system, and without feedback. Control participants only made a single unassisted identification decision for each trial during this portion of the experiment.

Trust Questionnaire

We constructed a brief questionnaire for the current experiment based on previous work (Carragher et al., 2024). First, participants reported their self-confidence in their own face matching ability. Second, they indicated their trust in the facial recognition system to accurately judge whether two photographs showed the same person. Both ratings were made on a 10-point scale, anchored with "Extremely Low" and "Extremely High". In addition to these established measures (Carragher et al., 2024; Lee & Moray, 1994), we also asked participants several exploratory questions: whether they trusted the AFR system to help them in the task (binary "yes" or "no" response), and to estimate (0-100%) their individual unassisted accuracy, the accuracy of the AFR system, and their accuracy when assisted by the AFR system. We did not preregister analyses for these exploratory questions and report them here for transparency. Participants completed the trust questionnaire three times; before the training block (pre-training), after the training block but before the main task (post-training), and then again after the main experiment (post-KFMT).

Procedure

The experiment was run online via Qualtrics. Participants could not access the experiment on a mobile device. Participants were told that they would be completing two face matching tasks, and that they would be using a simulated AFR system at times in the experiment. They were shown examples of trial displays with the AFR system, including an explanation of how to understand the system's output. As a reminder, participants were told that the simulated AFR system was "state-of-the-art" and would be "highly accurate" but not exactly how accurate it would be. Participants then completed the pre-training trust questionnaire before going on to do the training block, which contained the key feedback and experience manipulation. Participants then completed the post-training trust questionnaire.

All participants then progressed to the main experiment (the KFMT with the AFR system), before completing the final trust questionnaire (post-KFMT). The median time taken to complete the experiment was 16.51 minutes (M = 18.98, SD = 9.20) and participants received 2.00GBP for their time. This project was approved by the Human Research Ethics Subcommittee in the School of Psychology at the University of Adelaide (H-2023-01).

Analysis

Face Matching Performance

Overall Accuracy. We calculated overall accuracy according to the formula ((Hits +

Correct Rejections) / Total Trials)*100, where hits were correct "same" responses to match trials and correct rejections were correct "different" responses to mismatch trials. Our primary interest was whether the training conditions differed in their subsequent use of the AFR system. As such, significant interactions in ANOVAs are often interpreted by comparing the change in performance achieved by each condition (final accuracy -initial accuracy), which also removed any baseline differences in initial accuracy between the conditions. Overall accuracy does not include decision confidence.

Area Under the Curve. We calculated Area Under the Curve (AUC) to characterise participants' ability to discriminate between identity match trials and mismatch trials at different response thresholds (Macmillan & Creelman, 2004; Stanislaw & Todorov, 1999).

AUC is calculated by plotting a participant's hit rate against their false alarm rate (incorrectly responding "same" to a mismatch trial) at each level of the response scale to produce a Receiver Operator Characteristic (ROC) curve. The measure AUC has a maximum value of 1.0, a minimum of 0.0, and 0.5 indicates chance responding.

Automation Use

Our preregistration described analysing automation "reliance", which we define as occurring when participants change their initial decision to become more like that of the AFR system. However, it is also possible, albeit very rare (see exploratory results), for participants to "reject" the AFR system's prediction. Because we have included all trials in our analysis of decision change (deviating from our preregistration), rather than focusing only on reliance, we now use "automation use" as the superordinate term for decision changes following the AFR system's prediction (even though most decision changes are examples of reliance). We report two measures of automation use: steps of confidence scale change, and changes in binary identification decisions.

Confidence Change.

All identification responses were made on a 6-point scale from "Definitely Same" to "Definitely Different" (numeric values 1-6). AFR system outputs were treated as being of "definite" confidence. To calculate confidence change, the participant's initial decision became point 0, and their final decision was transformed into units of confidence scale change, where positive values show "reliance" as the decision moved toward that of the AFR system (e.g., an initial "guess same" response that changes to "probably same" after the AFR system predicts "same" is +1 units), and negative units show "rejection" (the final decision moved away from the AFR system). These analyses use average scale change across all trials (including those where the final response did not differ from the initial response).

Identification Decision Change.

We also report binary identification decision change, which occurred only when the underlying identification decision changed from initial to final response (changing from "same" to "different", or vice versa).

Trust Questionnaire

Participants completed the trust questionnaire three times (pre-training, post-training, post-KFMT). Our primary measure from this questionnaire was RTIA, which was calculated by subtracting self-confidence ratings from trust in automation ratings (Carragher et al., 2024; Lee & Moray, 1994). Positive scores indicated greater trust in the system; negative scores indicated greater self-confidence. To test H3, we compared RTIA pre-training and posttraining. To investigate whether RTIA was related to overall accuracy and automation use (H4, H5, H7), we used the post-training measure, with the rationale being that this measure reflected participants' intentions toward the AFR system immediately before the main task.

H5: RTIA and Automation Reliance

As predicted, post-training RTIA was also significantly and positively correlated with use of the AFR system, whether measured as average confidence change, r(202) = .44, p < .001, 95%CI [0.32, 0.54], or the frequency of binary identification decision change, r(202) = .37, p < .001, 95%CI [0.24, 0.48]. As expected, average automation-assisted accuracy was positively correlated with use of the AFR system, whether use was measured as confidence change, r(202) = .67, p < .001, 95%CI [0.58, 0.74], or binary identification decision change, r(202) = .60, p < .001, 95%CI [0.50, 0.68].

Those who changed their decisions in response to the AFR system's predictions more often achieved greater levels of automation-assisted accuracy.

H7: Mediation Analysis

A mediation analysis (PROCESS macro, Model 4) with Training Condition as the predictor variable, post-training RTIA as the mediator variable, and aided performance as the outcome, was used to test H7. There was no significant difference in direct effect, B = 1.42, p = .457, 95%CI [-2.34, 5.18], or indirect effect, B = -0.72, 95%CI [-1.96, 0.43] for the Experience condition, compared to the Control condition.

There was no significant difference in indirect effect, B = 0.95, 95%CI [-0.27, 2.41], for the Feedback condition, compared to the Control condition. However, the Feedback condition resulted in a greater direct increase in aided performance, compared to the Control condition, B = 5.92, p = .001, 95%CI [2.41, 9.38]. The difference in aided performance for the Feedback condition relative to the Experience condition was partially mediated by post-training RTIA, with a statistically significant difference in both the direct effect, B = 4.48, p = .009, 95%CI [1.14, 7.82], and indirect effect, B = 1.70 95%CI [0.61, 3.00]. However, greater accuracy in the Feedback condition compared to the Control condition was not mediated by post-training RTIA. Further, contrary to H7, neither the direct nor the indirect effect differed between the Experience and Control conditions.

Our planned analyses examined use of the AFR system in several correlations. Here we investigate whether average confidence shift or the frequency of binary identification decision change differed between the three training conditions.

Confidence Change. The one-way ANOVA was significant, F(2, 201) = 7.61, p < .001, ηp² = .07, with the Feedback condition showing greater average shifts in confidence toward the AFR system's predictions (M = +1.00, SD = 0.53), than participants in either the Experience (M = +0.72, SD = 0.48), t(201) = 3.06, p = .008, or Control conditions (M = +0.67, SD = 0.56), t(201) = 3.63, p = .001. The Experience and Control conditions did not differ from each other, t(201) = 0.57, p = 1.00.

Binary Identification Decision Change.

For context regarding the frequency of decision changes, there were 8160 trials in this experiment (204 participants x 40 trials).

Participants changed their identification decisions on 1680 trials (20.6%). Of those decision changes, 1645 were reliance (97.9%) and 35 were rejections (2.1%). Therefore, while we analyse all instances of decision change together throughout this paper (rather than filtering only for reliance), it is clear that nearly all changes are reliance, made to endorse the prediction of the AFR system.

A one-way ANOVA, F(2, 201) = 10.37, p < .001, ηp² = .09, showed that participants in the Feedback condition made more binary identification decision changes (M = 10.65, SD = 5.21) than participants in the Experience (M = 6.93, SD = 5.10), t(201) = 4.05, p < .001, and Control conditions (M = 7.13, SD = 5.74), t(201) = 3.83, p < .001. Decision change did not differ between the Experience and Control conditions, t(201) = 0.22, p = 1.00.

AFR System Errors

Finally, we investigated performance on the 2 trials that the AFR system gave the wrong prediction (acknowledging that an individual's accuracy can only be 0%, 50%, or 100%). A mixed measures ANOVA returned a significant main effect of Decision Phase, F(1, 201) = 133.95, p < .001, ηp² = .40, in which accuracy decreased after seeing the incorrect prediction from the AFR system (see Figure 7). The main effect of Training Condition was non-significant, F(2, 201) = 2.86, p = .060, ηp² = .03. The interaction between the two factors was significant, F(2, 201) = 3.24, p = .041, ηp² = .03. The Feedback condition (M = -32.35, SD = 26.99) suffered a greater decrease in accuracy than the Experience condition (M = -19.12, SD = 29.96), t(201) = -2.50, p = .040, but not the Control condition (M = -23.53, SD = 35.06), t(201) = -1.67, p = .291. The Experience and Control conditions did not differ, t(201) = 0.83, p = 1.00. These results suggest that the increase in overall accuracy in the Feedback condition may have come at the cost of accepting more errors from the AFR system, compared to the Experience and Control conditions.

Discussion

We investigated whether the provision of trial-by-trial feedback, or simple experience using the simulated AFR system, during an initial "training block" could improve automation-assisted performance in a subsequent unfamiliar face matching task. Across all three conditions, the AFR system proved to be a useful decision aid, such that assisted performance was significantly better than the accuracy participants achieved on their own.

Despite the significant improvements, all three conditions used the decision aid suboptimally, failing to achieve the level of accuracy the AFR system offered alone. Both results replicate past findings (Bartlett et al., 2024; Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Carragher et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2026; Mueller et al., 2026). With that overarching constraint noted, we found participants who received trial-by-trial feedback in the training block recorded a larger increase in automation-assisted performance compared to participants in the experience and control conditions. Importantly, the feedback was delivered on a different set of faces, and at a different point in time (prior to the main task), speaking to the generalised nature of the improvement offered by the intervention. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first results to show that the use of an automated decision aid can be improved in an unfamiliar face matching task.

We had expected that the provision of feedback during the training block would help participants to gauge both their own unfamiliar face matching ability, and the capabilities of the AFR system, which in turn would lead to greater reliance on the AFR system in the main task and increased automation-assisted performance. Our results were consistent with this mechanism. The feedback condition showed a large increase in relative trust in automation (RTIA) following the training block. Separate analysis of self-confidence and trust in automation, the two ratings that contribute to RTIA, showed that their increase in RTIA was due to a significant increase in trust in automation. There was a small decrease in their selfconfidence, which was consistent across all conditions. Our exploratory analyses showed that this increase in trust in automation, and RTIA, was maintained through the experiment.

Building on previous research (Carragher et al., 2024), which showed a positive relationship between RTIA and automation-assisted performance, here we show that an intervention that increases trust in the AFR system can also improve automation-assisted performance.

Our mediation analysis offered partial support for RTIA as the mechanism underlying the feedback condition's advantage. Their larger increase in aided performance compared to the experience condition was partly explained by their higher post-training RTIA, consistent with the account above. However, their advantage over the control condition was not mediated by RTIA. This asymmetry is interpretable in light of our exploratory analyses: the control condition's RTIA also increased (non-significantly), but because of a decline in self-confidence as opposed to an increase in trust in automation. Because RTIA is a change score, it offers an incomplete description of the factor feedback influenced. What distinguished the increase in RTIA for the feedback condition was specifically an increase in trust in automation, and it is that component, rather than the composite change score measure, that appears to drive improved automation use.

Our predictions that the experience condition would benefit more from the training block the control condition, which we expected to manifest as increased automation-assisted performance, were unsupported. Although previous research reported evidence consistent with participants gauging the reliability of the AFR system through exposure (Carragher & Hancock, 2023), other lines of unfamiliar face matching research show that individual performance does not improve through repeated exposure to the task (Bindemann et al., 2012; Carragher & Hancock, 2025), including for professionals (White et al., 2014).

Crucially, this null result is also consistent with a recent study, showing that automationassisted unfamiliar face matching performance does not improve with time over the course of a task (Carragher et al., Under Review). Taken together, these results suggest that explicit feedback is needed to improve automation-assisted performance, rather than experience alone.

Across all participants, we found that RTIA was positively correlated with automation-assisted performance (regardless of performance measure). Those who had greater trust in automation relative to their self-confidence in their own abilities improved more when using the AFR as a decision aid. It is important to remember that at 95% accuracy, the simulated AFR system far exceeded the performance of the average participant on the KFMT (Fysh & Bindemann, 2018b), so greater trust in automation was generally appropriate. All three conditions did have a positive RTIA value pre-training, though of approximately just 0.5 units of the 10-point scale, which underestimated the 30% advantage of the AFR system compared the initial response accuracy. These findings replicate previous research, with similar medium-large effect sizes (Carragher et al., 2024), further highlighting RTIA as an important factor in understanding the use of decision aids (Dzindolet et al., 2003; Lee & Moray, 1994; Lee & See, 2004), including in the context of unfamiliar face matching performance.

We asked participants to make an initial unassisted and final assisted decision on every trial (Hua et al., 2026; Mueller et al., 2026), which allowed us to measure direct instances of AFR system reliance, unlike past research on face matching and trust in automation (Carragher et al., 2024). With this new method, we found that RTIA was positively correlated with greater use of the AFR system, whether use was measured as the magnitude of response change toward the system's output, or as the frequency of binary identification decisions changes. We also showed greater use of the AFR system was associated with greater automation-assisted performance. While these later findings are not particularly surprising (i.e., that using the highly accurate system more often would lead to better assisted performance), they offer a sensible grounding of our results. Though our planned analyses showed these relationships were evident in correlations across the entire sample, our exploratory analyses showed that the feedback condition also displayed greater use of the AFR system compared to the experience or control conditions, consistent with their increased RTIA and overall improved automation-assisted accuracy. While we refer to automation use throughout this project, our exploratory analyses showed that 97.9% of identification decision changes were examples of reliance on the automation.

However, our data show that this increase in automation-assisted performance was not without cost to the feedback condition. While rare AFR system errors were detrimental to all conditions, the decrease in accuracy was greater for the feedback condition than the experience or control conditions. Thus, increasing reliance on the AFR system was effective overall for the feedback condition, since the accuracy of the AFR system was greater than the average operator, and following the advice led to endorsing many more correct answers. But following the system more often, perhaps uncritically, appears to have led the feedback condition to endorse more errors that the other two conditions. This pattern of results was previously hinted at in the data of Hua et al. (2026), with increased acceptance of algorithm errors under conditions that promoted greater reliance on the algorithm (increased time pressure). Similarly, Carragher et al. (2024) previously reported that participants with explicit trust in the AFR system appeared to accept algorithm errors more often. One of the reasons given for human involvement in the deployment of AFR systems is for detecting and correcting algorithm errors. Our current findings highlight the inherent difficulty of this task.

Among our novice participants, most would benefit from following the AFR system more often than they already do (since they show suboptimal performance, H1). But always following the system means that rare errors are less likely to be corrected.

As in past research, our participants were untrained novices, probably with little to no experience of performing face matching comparisons or using AFR systems (Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Carragher et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2026; Mueller et al., 2026). Further research is still needed with experienced users of AFR systems to ensure the generalisability of these findings. But our findings also show that simply getting experience using the AFR system in the training block did not lead to any additional improvement in performance beyond the control condition (see also Carragher et al. Under Review). We also acknowledge that while our findings show feedback improved automation-assisted performance, it was to a level of performance below that of the AFR system alone, just like the experience and control conditions. Nonetheless, this increase in automation-assisted occurred after only 20 trials of feedback in the training block. An obvious direction for future research is to investigate whether a longer training block with more opportunities for feedback could lead to more substantial improvements in automation-assisted performance. Despite these limitations, our findings offer promising directions for future research, having shown that it is possible to improve human use of the AFR system in an unfamiliar face matching task.

As facial recognition technologies improve, AFR systems will become more prevalent in applied settings. But these systems are typically used by, or under the supervision of, a human operator, so advances in technological capability will not automatically translate into improved outcomes; outcomes will depend on how effectively human operators use these systems (Noyes & Hill, 2021). Previous research has shown that novice participants struggle to use AFR systems effectively as decision aids (Bartlett et al., 2024; Carragher & Hancock, 2023; Carragher et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2026; Mueller et al., 2026). Here we show, for the first time, that this use can be improved. A brief training block of trial-by-trial feedback (20 trials, on a different set of faces, before the task of interest) increased trust in the AFR system, increased reliance on it, and improved automation-assisted accuracy in a challenging unfamiliar face matching task. That such a light-touch and transferable intervention produced a measurable benefit is an encouraging sign for the development of future approaches to operator training. Effective use is not the same as uncritical reliance, however, and our results are a reminder that interventions which increase reliance may also reduce the detection of the rare system errors that human oversight is meant to catch. Calibrating the trade-off between greater reliance on the AFR system when it is correct, but greater rejection of the system when it is incorrect, is the central challenge for future work in the field of automation-assisted unfamiliar face matching.
