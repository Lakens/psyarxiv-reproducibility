Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). Abstract

People with low vision (PLVs) experience difficulties in spatial navigation associated with their low vision (LV) and are inadequately supported by status quo information solutions. The LV case, in which visual input is present but functionally unreliable, also falls out of scope of existing taxonomies of spatial navigation derived from mostly normally sighted subjects. We propose three perturbations through which LV adds attentional demands to navigation: we review evidence that it de-automates the task of avoiding obstacles and safely walking (vigilant locomotion), hypothesize that it strains visual search by creating uncertainty about information sources' presence, placement, form, and accessibility (naive search), and conjecture that it lowers confidence for wayfinding decisions, encouraging backtracking and frequent confirmation (fragile decisions). Empirical evidence supports that vigilant locomotion under LV interferes with concurrent cognitive processes, especially spatial learning. We hypothesize that this interference extends to wayfinding and that difficulties in information sampling (naive search) and low metacognitive confidence (fragile decisions) additionally strain attention. Accordingly, we introduce three design aims which can help evaluate whether current and prospective wayfinding information solutions support lowvision navigation by addressing these perturbations; information should be continuous at step-level, easy to sample, and confirmatory via paired feedback. Future research should directly test these predictions in ecological contexts and investigate the possibility that acute stressors can mimic the LV-related perturbations in the general population.

1. Introduction

As my RER B train pulls up I confirm that, yes, I had taken the correct path. Frustrated and confused, I follow my original path, this time searching more methodically for directions to the 14. I spot a sign on the wall which points me down another hallway and make my way there to take the moving walkway to a second section of the station.

When I lose my way again, another passenger offers her help, guiding me to a final set of stairs up to the platform. The train status display reads "14: Saint-Denis -Pleyel," the correct direction. I have finally reached my platform and can take a seat until the next train arrives.

This trip, an autobiographical snapshot about navigating public transit with low vision (LV), exemplifies three LV-related perturbations to navigation that are key to explaining and intervening in the difficulties people with low vision (PLVs) experience daily while traveling in urban hubs. PLVs' spatial navigation performance is generally worse than that of people with good vision: they make more errors and take longer to complete navigational tasks (Daga et al., 2017). Contrast sensitivity and visual field deficits appear to have a greater negative impact on navigation performance than do visual acuity deficits (Leat & Lovie-Kitchin, 2008; Marron & Bailey, 1982). PLVs also self-report difficulties in orienting in variable lighting and avoiding peripheral obstacles (Latham et al., 2017). Poorly located and illegible signs also cause frustration in public transit wayfinding (Boadi-Kusi et al., 2024). Performance deficits exist for blind people as well, though evidence differs as to whether these deficits are persistent, convergent, or cumulative (see Schinazi et al., 2016, for a review).

LV is a uniquely informative case for understanding spatial navigation: it occupies a sui generis cognitive regime wherein visual information is present (in contrast with "total blindness") but is functionally unreliable (in contrast with "normal vision"). We propose that LV reveals the attentional competition of processes which accounts of normally sighted navigation treat as automatic and which accounts of blind navigation describe but to which they do not attribute a cognitive cost. The narrative provides anecdotal evidence of this attentional competition. Firstly, the chaotic scene navigating from the platform edge to the escalator exemplifies how ensuring one's safe locomotion and mobility with LV is a demanding process which detracts from one's ability to perform concurrent tasks; we term this vigilant locomotion. Secondly, the repeated misses of important wayfinding signs illustrate how finding information about one's destination under high uncertainty about this information's existence, form, and accessibility complicates the search task beyond simply locating a target; we term this naive search. Thirdly, the instance of backtracking to the original source of information about line 14 shows how low confidence can make one prone to seeking confirmatory information and second-guessing one's decisions; we term this fragile decisions. These three LV-related perturbations co-occurred throughout this single trip, causing confusion, backtracking, and wayfinding failures.

Sections 2.1-2.3 address the three perturbations in turn and detail experiments to empirically test them when applicable, and Section 3 presents the three design aims we derive from them.

2. LV-Related Perturbations to Navigation

>LV-Related Perturbations to Navigation

2.1 Vigilant Locomotion

LV stresses locomotion, which appears to be automatic under normal vision, at the expense of other controlled processes. Two prominent taxonomies for spatial navigation's constituent processes presuppose locomotion and sign detection and explicitly place these processes outside their scope. Montello (2005) and Wiener and colleagues (2009) emphasize the distinction between locomotion (local movement guidance, short-horizon) and wayfinding (goal-directed destination-seeking, long-horizon) and formalize which knowledge different wayfinding tasks require. Montello (2005) describes locomotion as typically cognitively impenetrable and less demanding of working memory than wayfinding tasks. Wiener and colleagues (2009) state that following a known path "requires little attentional resources, almost no reasoning and runs automatically." These taxonomies from normally sighted navigators take simple navigational tasks to be efficient processes-that is, ones that run with little or no attentional or cognitive capacity (Moors & De Houwer, 2006) and give little attention to locomotion.

LV locomotion, however, appears to be inefficient. In our opening narrative, avoiding other passengers and obstacles was an attentionally demanding task that led to missed directions and wayfinding breakdowns: locomotion interfered with wayfinding. Tasks compete most when they share the same cognitive stage, sensory modality, code, and visual channel (Wickens, 2002). Locomotion and wayfinding under LV are two such tasks, as they are both response-based (same stage), visually guided (same sensory modality), and spatial (same code); especially in the case of peripheral visual field deficits, the two tasks also both rely on focal visual streams (same visual channel). The degree to which two tasks interfere (e.g., as measured by reaction time or accuracy) indexes the extent to which they compete for attentional resources, allowing for the empirical validation of competition in spatial navigation processes.

Experimental work provides evidence that LV causes heightened attention to locomotion which increases cognitive load and impairs concurrent tasks. Normally sighted subjects wearing blurred goggles which reduced their acuity to 20/562 (logMAR 1.45) displayed impaired spatial learning and lowered accuracy on a concurrent auditory attention task compared to baseline (Rand et al., 2015). Subjects with artificially restricted visual fields of 4 and 10 degrees showed slowed auditory reaction times, but only 4-degree restriction and total occlusion impaired spatial learning (Barhorst-Cates et al., 2016; see Creem-Regehr et al., 2021, for a review). In a complex museum environment, spatial learning and reaction times were both degraded with a 10-degree field of view (Barhorst-Cates et al., 2019). Dual-task evidence from PLVs is congruent: people with retinitis pigmentosa showed longer reaction times to tones than normally sighted walkers on a complex route, although not on a simple one (Turano et al., 1998), and glaucoma patients' reaction times while walking are correlated with the severity of their visual field loss (Geruschat & Turano, 2007).

De-loading locomotion also facilitates spatial learning in people with artificially degraded vision. Guiding older adults with simulated LV through indoor spaces allowed them to learn these spaces better than when they were unguided (Barhorst-Cates et al., 2017). Subjects also responded more quickly to concurrent auditory attention tasks when being guided through indoor spaces (Rand et al., 2015). This evidence suggests that lightening the load of locomotion liberates attentional resources shared with other controlled processes.

Locomotion's relationship with concurrent tasks (increased load impairs them and decreased load restores them) suggests that locomotion, a seemingly efficient process for normally sighted navigators, is instead inefficient and attentionally demanding under simulated and actual LV. Creem-Regehr and colleagues (2021) document LV locomotion's interference with spatial learning; we extend this logic by hypothesizing that these demands also interfere with wayfinding. Locating and reading signs, and making and executing route decisions, two key components of wayfinding in public transit hubs, are controlled visuospatial tasks which both rely heavily on focal vision under LV, a profile that multiple-resource models predict to interfere substantially with visually loaded locomotion (Wickens, 2002).

2.2 Naive Search

We propose that the LV-imposed naive search for information is a second process which competes with other controlled processes for attentional resources. The LV case again falls out of scope of wayfinding models that commonly consider information search to be undemanding "as long as signs are put up reliably at every decision point" (Wiener et al., 2009). This is also the case in visual search experiments where the target presence may be uncertain but its location is bounded, and its appearance known. Gottlieb (2018) makes the simplifying assumption of conventional decision paradigms explicit: "the relevant information is given," and "the identity of the relevant sources is rarely in question." Under these conditions, humans search nearly optimally (Najemnik & Geisler, 2005). During everyday activities with normal vision, gaze is serial and task-driven, with over 95% of fixations serving the ongoing task (Hayhoe, 2017; Land & Hayhoe, 2001).

Understanding and supporting LV navigation in environments such as public transit hubs requires relaxing these assumptions. In our opening narrative, the uncertainty concerns the information sources themselves, rather than their locations alone: is there signage for line 14? Where? In what form? Will it be readable from where I stand? LV plays a role in creating such uncertainty about information sources. In conditions of a restricted visual field, anything outside the residual field is invisible until fixated. When central vision is degraded, informative cues may not be able to be distinguished from their surroundings.

Accordingly, we hypothesize that under LV, sampling an information source becomes a costly step in its own right.

The multiple-resource account of attention predicts that information sampling will interfere with locomotion in LV (Section 2.1). In normal vision conditions, "when we successfully walk down a corridor while reading a book, we are exploiting the parallel processing or multiple resource capabilities of focal and ambient vision" (Wickens, 2002). LV navigators often cannot make use of this parallel processing, as LV brings locomotor guidance into the focal stream. Therefore, information sampling and locomotion should maximally interfere in the case of peripheral vision loss, while central vision loss should interfere with information sampling mostly because of the single-task difficulty of locating and reading signs.

Evidence suggests that PLVs perform visual search less efficiently than people with good vision and that this search interferes with other tasks. Patients with severe LV searched 50% more slowly than agematched controls in simple feature search; five days of training reduced the gap without eliminating it (Liu et al., 2007). Patients with central vision impairment needed four times longer than controls to find targets in natural images, and found only 62% of them (Luo et al., 2012). Dual-task visual search under LV produces similar deficits. When walking through an indoor obstacle course while performing a letterrecognition task, subjects wearing blurred goggles missed 21% of targets and PLVs missed 31% of targets, compared with only 3% in the normal vision condition (Freedman et al., 2019). One reason for the decreased dual-task search performance may be that PLVs direct their gaze toward the ground more when walking; subjects with retinitis pigmentosa spent more time looking down while walking (26% during obstacle crossing, 28% during level-walking) compared to sighted controls (Timmis et al., 2017). Visual search and general dual-task load also seem to interfere with locomotion performance. During an obstacle avoidance task, subjects with glaucoma contacted significantly more obstacles than controls, and all subjects contacted more obstacles when performing a simultaneous shape search task; however, the group-by-condition interaction did not reach significance (Lajoie et al., 2018). These observations suggest that visual search under LV interferes with locomotion, but whether it interferes with navigation as a whole remains to be directly tested.

A plausible objection to naive search requiring more attention under LV is that people may adapt to LV, and adaptation might absorb the additional cost. However, evidence about gaze behavior suggests that individuals adapt behaviorally to LV, using attention to compensate for visual impairment. Patients with peripheral visual field loss make up to two saccades per second into regions outside their residual field (Luo et al., 2008). These compensatory eye movements cannot be stimulus-driven, since their targets were invisible until fixated, meaning they are adaptive at the behavioral level. Such compensatory gaze strategies also appear during real urban navigation. PLVs made more fixations and had longer travel times as a function of visual impairment severity despite achieving lower spatial fixation coverage than normally sighted participants (Feng et al., 2026). Shifting one's gaze draws on perceptual attention (Kowler et al., 1995), and Turano and colleagues (1998) proposed that repeated fixation redirections for people with restricted visual fields are associated with greater mental effort during navigation. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that these compensatory gaze adaptations come with their own attentional cost.

We have presented evidence that LV strains visual search and that adaptive mechanisms available to PLVs tend to increase attentional load rather than decreasing it. What is lacking from the literature, however, is an experiment isolating the specific cognitive cost of information sampling under LV navigation. A relevant study design would vary the existence, placement, and form of directional signage while participants navigate a route, under normal viewing, simulated LV, and actual LV. It would measure the time and cognitive load cost of finding each sign, and its interference with concurrent route decisions, including obstacle avoidance. Until such a study is conducted, naive search under LV's interference with navigation as a whole remains a hypothesis.

2.3 Fragile Decisions

Here, we conjecture that PLV navigators' metacognitive confidence while wayfinding further strains the navigational process by prompting costly behavioral adaptation and imposing a moment-to-moment attentional cost. Metacognitive confidence is distinct from task performance and is "the degree of belief one has about the likely success of a variety of mental operations" (Fleming, 2024). Metacognitive "sensitivity" and "bias" can be quantified independently (Fleming & Lau, 2014; Maniscalco & Lau, 2012), and the evidence below concerns bias: shifts in the overall level of confidence at constant accuracy.

Metacognitive confidence has a direct role in action. Low confidence triggers verification of previously encountered epistemic cues, an additional round of the information sampling whose attentional cost Section 2.2 proposed. At matched accuracy, "Participants were more likely to sample additional information before giving a response in the condition with low confidence" (Desender et al., 2018). This link, however, has no guaranteed direction. In normative models, a noisy confidence signal can prescribe more checking or less: misplaced doubt sends the agent back to verify, while misplaced certainty lets errors pass unexamined (Schulz et al., 2023). The link is nonetheless causal: feedback-induced ability beliefs influenced information seeking entirely through their effect on confidence, while manipulating training difficulty had the same effect independently of confidence (Van Marcke & Desender, 2025).

Under LV conditions, confidence and performance clearly dissociate. Lei and colleagues (2024) found that in PLVs, "[r]ecognition performance was best predicted by contrast sensitivity, whereas confidence was best predicted by visual acuity." Evidence for the directionality of LV's effect on confidence is scarce.

The only study of which we are aware regarding action confidence and affordance judgments under simulated LV found that simulated central and peripheral visual field loss correlated with both lower confidence and slower decision times compared to subjects' normal vision baseline (Orthy et al., 2026). In normally sighted observers, confidence for perceptions in the peripheral field followed a similar pattern: "higher sensory evidence in the periphery was needed to equate confidence choices between central and peripheral perceptual decisions" (Toscani et al., 2021). Another study found that judgments about the content located in peripheral vision could also become overconfident, i.e., the direction of the effect can cut both ways depending on the context (Yildirim & Sayim, 2022). A shift has also been reported in memory tasks for blind participants, this time toward higher confidence with no gain in accuracy, in a literature the authors themselves describe as inconsistent (Bamberger & Harris, 2025). Notably, no study has yet compared normally sighted and PLV confidence levels at matched accuracy. Evidence supports the confidence-accuracy dissociation in LV, and simulated-LV evidence suggests that LV may bias subjects toward lower confidence.

We have seen evidence that low metacognitive confidence triggers information seeking and that metacognitive confidence is lower in simulated LV without necessarily degrading performance. Anecdotal evidence such as our opening narrative suggests these propositions are true of navigation settings.

Though metacognition and navigation have not been extensively studied together (see Mason et al., 2022), a number of propositions have already been formulated. Dai et al. (2018) propose that navigators monitor their confidence level and, when it is too low, exert control by consulting tools or seeking more information to raise it. Keller et al. (2020) propose to investigate behaviors to derive confidence thresholds: "A navigator may seek to accumulate enough environmental information to reach a confidence threshold associated with a decision." Ariel and Moffat (2018) found that confidence in one's spatial memory relates to spatial strategy choice, and that monitoring dynamic navigation differs from monitoring static spatial tasks. From a theoretical perspective, disorientation has been characterized as low confidence in one's current spatial representation, a state that triggers re-orientation behaviors (Fernández Velasco & Casati, 2020). These empirical proposals use behavioral observations to make inferences regarding confidence: uncertainty is inferred from what navigators do. The reverse question, whether measured moment-to-moment confidence drives information seeking during navigation, has been tested in no population. We therefore advance it as a testable conjecture.

Such a study would need to distinguish metacognitive bias from sensitivity (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012); to run in PLV participants rather than under simulation only, as Orthy and colleagues (2026) themselves plan; to measure confidence at every decision alongside verification behaviors (e.g., Brunyé et al., 2018); and to manipulate source uncertainty and confidence through separate levers (signage placement for the former, performance feedback for the latter) (Van Marcke & Desender, 2025). In this way, information seeking driven by source uncertainty can be separated from information seeking driven by self-doubt. The proposed study could also assess whether variations in metacognitive confidence prompt further visual information sampling and re-engage the information sampling-locomotion interference posited in Sections 2.1 and 2.2.

3. Implications and Design Aims

We have proposed a navigation framework in which vigilant locomotion, naive search, and fragile decisions impose cognitive and attentional costs on PLV navigators, with suggestions for the framework's empirical validation. Accordingly, we propose three aims to address each of these. We use the public transit hub as an illustrative case for how designers can evaluate the effectiveness and limitations of information solutions.

The three design aims are as follows: 1. navigational information should be continuous at step-level.

It should provide continuous information about safe routes that avoid obstacles and guide navigators at every step along their journey. 2. wayfinding information should be easy to sample. Its existence, placement, form, and accessibility should be predictable and reliable to make locating a cue and extracting the necessary information from it trivially easy throughout the whole journey. 3. navigational information should be confirmatory via paired feedback. Each cue presented at a decision point should be paired with a cue which confirms the navigator has executed the proper action, supporting low-confidence navigators. State-of-the-art turn-by-turn digital guidance systems (see Belli et al., 2026, for a review) meet two of the three aims. Many current systems provide either wayfinding or obstacle avoidance information, but not both, and the systems that do combine the two are immature (Belli et al., 2026); therefore, we cannot classify them as continuous at step-level. Digital guidance devices are easy to sample, as they frequently push navigational cues to audio and haptic or vibratory streams, but with caveats that audio can be drowned out in loud environments and learning curves can be steep for alternative sensory modalities.

Digital solutions tend not to allow users to query them to get confirmation of their navigational actions (Belli et al., 2026); we nonetheless evaluate them to be confirmatory via paired feedback because they often supply notifications like "continue straight-on," which are confirmatory. Limitations of turn-by-turn digital guidance include susceptibility to outages, research and development costs, and achieving widespread adoption.

4. Conclusion

We have argued that LV-related perturbations to navigation arise because LV strains attentional resources in line with the multiple-resource account of attention (Wickens, 2002) and lowers certainty and confidence regarding navigational decisions and actions. Circumstances which bring otherwise parallel navigational tasks into competition and which increase uncertainty in navigation should therefore produce similar deficits in the general population. There is some experimental evidence to support this: forcing foveal vision recruitment in locomotion by adding uneven terrain (Matthis et al., 2018), introducing uncertainty regarding source presence in visual search (Wolfe et al., 2005), and experimentally lowering confidence (Van Marcke & Desender, 2025) all mimic, in normally sighted subjects, the attentional costs and performance deficits observed in LV. Therefore, crowding or ground-level obstacles should increase locomotion demands just as simulated LV does; unpredictable sign placement should increase search demands and sign misses in pedestrians with normal vision; and lowered confidence should increase verification behaviors at matched accuracy. To our knowledge, none of these experiments has been run in pedestrian navigation, and no framework derives all three from a shared attentional budget.

The universal need for easy-to-traverse spaces has long shaped design practice: Passini (1996) urged consistent placement of wayfinding information as it "reduces the need for scanning and reduces the chance of overload," and aimed universal design at "situationally impaired users, of which we are all a part at one time or another."
