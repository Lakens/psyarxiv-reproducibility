Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Psychological acceptance is an emotion regulation strategy in which people allow thoughts and feelings to arise and pass without trying to change them. Although theory implies that acceptance will increase with age, empirical evidence remains fragmented, with studies varying in sample characteristics, effect sizes, and age ranges. To address this gap and clarify the association between age and psychological acceptance we conducted a meta-analysis. Our preregistered search (PROSPERO CRD420251103979) identified 25 studies with 31 independent samples (N = 12,561). Random-effects pooling yielded a small but robust positive association between age and psychological acceptance (r = 0.077, 95% CI 0.027-0.127), despite substantial heterogeneity between studies. Specifically, studies that sampled wider age ranges reported stronger associations, so narrow age spans may understate the association.

Introduction

People regulate their emotions to influence what they feel, when they feel it, and how they express it (Gross, 1998 (Gross, , 2015)). They do so through distinct strategies. Each strategy acts at a different point in the emotion-generative process and carries different consequences for affect, physiology and wellbeing (Gross, 2015). Cognitive reappraisal, for example, involves deliberately generating an alternative interpretation of a stressful situation to reduce its emotional impact (Gross, 1998). In contrast, psychological acceptance is a receptive, noninterfering stance toward momentary experience. Rather than suppress, avoid, alter or prolong an experience, acceptance lets it arise, unfold and pass naturally, regardless of valence (Lindsay & Creswell, 2019; Wolfe & Isaacowitz, 2022). It changes how individuals relate to their thoughts and feelings, not the content of those experiences, so they may regulate their emotions without modifying them (Dunn et al., 2009; Troy et al., 2018).

Because acceptance involves no attempt to modify an experience, it may demand less cognitive effort than strategies that do (Gross, 1998; Lindsay & Creswell, 2019; Wolfe & Isaacowitz, 2022). Acceptance is also associated with a range of positive outcomes. Thompson et al. (2011) reviewed mostly correlational studies using self-report measures of psychological acceptance. They found that higher acceptance went together with lower post-traumatic distress across diverse samples (Cavanagh et al., 2014; Thompson et al., 2011). Psychological acceptance has also been linked to improved adaptation to health-related stressors. For example, among breast cancer patients, acceptance was associated with lower levels of psychological distress in a crosssectional study (N = 79) (Politi et al., 2007). Similarly, in a short-term, prospective, multi-method design, Shallcross et al. (2013) provided empirical evidence linking acceptance in everyday contexts with reduced negative affect, highlighting its role as a psychological process that may support emotional well-being (Shallcross et al., 2013).

Psychological acceptance may increase with age (Shallcross et al., 2013) and peak in older adulthood (Schirda et al., 2016; Wolfe & Isaacowitz, 2022). Three explanations may account for this trend. First, as cognitive processing resources decline with age, older adults may favor emotion regulation strategies that place fewer demands on cognitive resources (Urry & Gross, 2010). Psychological acceptance is easier to implement than other emotion regulation strategies (Alberts et al., 2012; Troy et al., 2018). It may therefore require fewer cognitive resources and suit older adults (Shallcross et al., 2013; Wolfe & Isaacowitz, 2022). Second, older adulthood often brings fundamental and irreversible losses, such as the death of loved ones and physical decline (Kunzmann & Grühn, 2005; Lang, 2001). Because people cannot reverse these losses, acceptance may serve them better than strategies that aim to change the situation (Shallcross et al., 2013). Losses also tend to elicit sadness, and people use acceptance more often in response to experimentally induced sadness than fear (Vishkin et al., 2020; Wolfe & Isaacowitz, 2022). Older adults face such losses more often and may therefore rely on acceptance more. Third, older adults show a clear preference for positive affect (such as joy, contentment and interest), particularly low-arousal positive affect (Riediger et al., 2009; Scheibe et al., 2013). Psychological acceptance reduces negative affect (Kober et al., 2019a) and physiological reactivity to affective stimuli (Low et al., 2008; Troy et al., 2018). It therefore offers older adults a route to calmer, low arousal states (Riediger et al., 2009; Wolfe & Isaacowitz, 2022).

These accounts are consistent with evidence pointing to the benefits of psychological acceptance in older adulthood. In a study of adults aged 64 to 83, participants reported greater motivation to use psychological acceptance, and greater success in using it compared to either detachment or positive reappraisal strategies (Rompilla et al., 2022). Qualitative accounts converge with the pattern. Older adults living alone with chronic illness describe acceptance of their circumstances as central to coping with declining health (Birkeland & Natvig, 2009). Similarly, among community-dwelling and care-receiving older adults aged 65 to 96, higher psychological acceptance, was associated with better subjective and objective quality of life across multiple domains (Butler & Ciarrochi, 2007).

Published age-acceptance correlations differ in size and direction, and about a third of the samples we identified include fewer than 200 participants. However, single studies are often underpowered to establish the reliability and size of an effect. Furthermore, in a relatively small body of literature, a few influential studies or selective publication can inflate an apparent effect. Finally, studies reporting the age-acceptance associations were rarely designed to examine agerelated change, and they differ markedly in mean age and in the range of age represented. Thus, sample composition may itself account for part of the variation observed across samples and measures.

The present meta-analysis therefore pursues three aims that address the gaps identified above. First, it estimates the pooled association between age and psychological acceptance across adulthood. Second, it tests the robustness of this estimate against outliers, influential cases and publication bias. Third, it examines whether mean age and age range moderate the magnitude of the association. The moderator analysis offers an initial, if indirect, indication of whether age differences extend across adulthood or emerge only in later life. Beyond its scientific contribution, the study may carry practical implications. Previous studies linked psychological acceptance to better well-being (Butler & Ciarrochi, 2007; Kober et al., 2019b; Shallcross et al., 2013). If psychological acceptance rises with age, interventions for older adults that build psychological acceptance may be better aligned with their existing emotion regulation tendencies and strengths.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

We conducted a systematic literature search following the PRISMA guidelines to identify studies examining the association between age and psychological acceptance across the adult lifespan. The search was performed on 25 November 2025 across three major bibliographic databases: PsycInfo, PubMed, and Scopus. Our search strategy relied on three conceptual pillars: age-related terms, psychological acceptance as a construct, and validated psychometric instruments. We registered the protocol at PROSPERO (CRD420251103979).

To ensure comprehensive coverage, we utilized both controlled vocabulary (where available) and free-text terms in article titles, abstracts, and keywords. The targeted instruments included the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire (AAQ/AAQ-II) (Hayes, 2004) (Bond et al., 2011), the Emotion Acceptance Questionnaire (EAQ) (Kisley et al., 2025), the Kentucky Inventory of Mindfulness Skills (KIMS) (Baer et al., 2004), the Philadelphia Mindfulness Scale (PHLMS) (Cardaciotto et al., 2008), the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ) (Garnefski et al., 2001), the COPE questionnaire (Carver, 1989), the Emotion Regulation Strategy Scale (ERSS) (Kneeland et al., 2024) and the Impermanence Awareness and Acceptance Scale (IMAAS) (Fernández-Campos et al., 2021). To capture broader nomenclature, the terms "Acceptance Questionnaire" and "Acceptance Scale" were also included. The search yielded a total of 234 records. Following the removal of 51 duplicate records, 183 unique records remained and were screened at the title and abstract level. Of these, 90 records were excluded because they did not meet initial eligibility criteria. The remaining 93 full-text reports were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Of these, 1 report was not retrieved and 71 reports were excluded: 69 because they lacked sufficient statistical information, even after attempts to obtain the missing data from the authors, one because the age of the participants was under 18 and one because it was not published in English. Ultimately, 21 studies met all inclusion criteria and were therefore included in the metaanalysis. All records identified through database searches were independently screened by two reviewers at both the title and abstract level, followed by full-text review. Inter-reviewer agreement was high, and any disagreements regarding eligibility or data extraction were resolved through discussion. Instances where consensus could not be reached were resolved by a third reviewer acting as an arbiter.

To capture relevant studies that the database search might have missed, we also screened the reference lists of included studies. This process yielded 44 potentially relevant citations. Of these, 5 were excluded during the initial abstract-based screening, and 35 were excluded at the full-text stage due to insufficient reported data that we were not able to complete by contacting the authors. For the remaining 4 papers identified as potentially eligible, the second reviewer independently assessed and verified their full texts to confirm their final inclusion. Consequently, 4 additional studies were included. As a result, the final meta-analysis comprised 25 studies. Notably, two of these studies provided data from multiple independent samples. In these instances, effect sizes were calculated separately for each sample.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported empirical correlation coefficients (Pearson's r) or provided sufficient statistical data to calculate an effect size. For potentially eligible studies lacking essential data, we contacted the authors. We excluded studies that: (a) utilized non-quantitative designs or lacked primary data; (b) conceptualized psychological acceptance as a non-psychological construct (e.g., technology acceptance); (c) utilized populationspecific or context-dependent adaptations of psychological acceptance measures rather than general validated scales; (d) focused on samples under 18 years of age; (e) did not treat age as an independent variable; or (f) were not published in English. Data extraction was performed using a standardized form. The extracted variables included study identifiers and sample characteristics (authors and year, sample size, age range, mean age, and age SD), psychometric measures (scale name), and statistical indices necessary for effect size calculation (Pearson's r, Cohen's d, η², t values, regression coefficients B and β, confidence intervals, and p values). A formal risk of bias assessment tool was not utilized, as the scope of this correlational meta-analysis focused primarily on bivariate associations across heterogeneous observational designs.

Effect Size Calculation and Data Processing

The primary effect size metric used in the analysis was Pearson's correlation coefficient (r). Where r was not reported directly, it was calculated from alternative statistical indices using algebraic transformation formulas. Specifically, for Enoki et al. (2018), the psychological acceptance score comprised two subscales, and we averaged their correlations with age (r = .11 and r = .17) into a single effect size. For Mahoney et al. (2015), the effect size was derived from the t-statistic and sample size using the formula: where an eta-squared value was reported, the magnitude of r was calculated using the square root transformation formula |𝑟| = √η² .

Because transformations from squared indices discard directional information, the sign of the converted effect size was manually recovered and assigned by cross-referencing the direction of the descriptive statistics and textual reporting in the original paper. To ensure a consistent interpretation of the pooled estimates, correlation coefficients from instruments measuring constructs opposite to psychological acceptance had their signs reversed, establishing a convention where a positive value consistently indicates greater psychological acceptance at older ages. The reversed measures included the AAQ (9-item) (Hayes, 2004), AAQ-II (Bond et al., 2011) and CFQ-7 (Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire) (Gillanders et al., 2014) In the reported study, AAQ-II and CFQ-7 total scores were summed to produce a total psychological inflexibility score, which was used in the present analysis. To account for the skewed distribution of correlation coefficients, all values were transformed into Fisher's z scores (z_r) for analysis.

The sampling variance (𝑣) for each study was calculated as 𝑣 = 1 𝑁-3 .

All final results were back transformed to Pearson's r for interpretative clarity.

Statistical Analysis

Given the expected between-study heterogeneity, we employed random-effects models using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator via the metafor package (version 4.6) (Viechtbauer, 2010) in RStudio (version 4.5.1) (Posit Team, 2026; R Core Team, 2026) This approach assumes that the true effect size varies across studies, acknowledging that our sample is drawn from a larger distribution of possible effects. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q-test and the 𝐼 2 index with its 95% confidence interval, and the between-study variance τ² along with its square root tau (each reported with a 95% confidence interval). In addition, a 95% prediction interval was calculated to estimate the expected range of true effects in future comparable studies. All R scripts, analysis code, and the processed dataset used to conduct the meta-analysis are publicly available on the Open Science Framework (OSF) and can be accessed at https://osf.io/kfq3b/overview?view_only=bdb6212271f04646a1ec11829d23e960.

To evaluate the robustness of our results, we performed diagnostic analyses. We conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and influence diagnostics to identify any studies exerting disproportionate leverage on the model. We then assessed potential publication bias using a funnel plot and Egger's regression test. To address the asymmetry identified by these tests, we applied the 'Trim and Fill' method. However, because both Egger's test and the Trim and Fill method can perform poorly under conditions of substantial between-study heterogeneity, we additionally implemented a selection model as a complementary check.

To explore potential moderators of the relationship between age and psychological acceptance, we performed a meta-regression analysis using a Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator. Because previous studies were rarely designed to examine age-related differences and varied substantially in their sample age distributions, we included the mean age and the age range of the sample as continuous predictors to assess whether variations in these demographic characteristics accounted for significant heterogeneity in the observed effects. Age range was calculated as the difference between the maximum and minimum age reported within each sample. Of the 31 studies included in the overall meta-analysis, 23 provided sufficient information and were retained in this specific analysis, whereas 8 studies were excluded due to missing demographic details. Prior to the analysis, potential multicollinearity between the two predictors was evaluated.

To evaluate the robustness of the meta-regression, we re-estimated the model 23 times, omitting one sample each time. We recorded the coefficients and 95% confidence intervals for mean age and age range from each refit. We also inspected studentized residuals, Cook's distances and hat values (Viechtbauer et al., 2015). Studentized residuals flag samples whose effect size lies far from the model prediction. Cook's distances flag samples whose removal shifts the coefficients. Hat values measure leverage, meaning how far a sample's mean age and age range lie from those of the other samples. A sample with high leverage can pull the fitted regression line towards itself. We therefore repeated the test of the moderators in two ways. The Knapp-Hartung adjustment modifies the standard errors of the coefficients (Knapp & Hartung, 2003). It also replaces the normal and chi-square reference distributions of the Wald test with t and F distributions, as Wald tests can be anticonservative when the number of samples is small, meaning that they yield pvalues that are too small (Knapp & Hartung, 2003). The permutation test randomly reassigns the moderator values among the samples 10,000 times and refits the model each time. It then compares the observed test statistic with the resulting distribution (Higgins & Thompson, 2004). Both methods provided adequate control of the Type I error rate in simulations (Viechtbauer et al., 2015).

We then re-estimated the model three more times to check that specific analytic choices did not drive the result. First, we excluded the effect sizes derived from other test statistics, because they come from conversion formulas rather than reported correlations. Second, we excluded the sample recruited in two disjoint age bands (18 to 29 and 60 to 86 years). Its age range of 68 years describes a sample with no participants aged 30 to 59 years. The range therefore does not reflect a continuous distribution of ages. Third, we excluded papers that contributed more than one sample, because samples from the same paper share methods and measures and may yield correlated effects. Supplementary Table 1 identifies the samples removed in each analysis.

Results

All included effect sizes represented independent groups of participants. For the study by Mitchell et al. (2023), which featured 5 measurement time points, only baseline data were extracted to ensure independence and avoid potential confounding from age-by-time interactions.

Sensitivity and Publication Bias Analyses

To ensure the robustness of the data, we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, which confirmed that no single study disproportionately influenced the model, as the pooled estimate remained consistently significant across all iterations. Subsequently, influence diagnostics (including Cook's distance, studentized residuals, and hat values), together with the leave-one-out results, supported the robustness of the model. Although the study by Herreen et al. (2022) showed the largest studentized residual (rstudent = 2.83), it did not exceed the Bonferroniadjusted threshold for outlier detection (3.15). Moreover, omitting this study yielded a nearly identical pooled effect that remained statistically significant (r = .067, 95% CI [.021, .113], p = .0046) compared to the full model (r = 0.077, 95% CI [0.027, 0.127], p = 0.0027). Accordingly, the study was retained in the final analysis. Addressing potential reporting biases involved inspecting funnel plot asymmetry, complemented by Egger's regression test, which yielded a significant result (z = -2.0700, p = 0.0385). The results are illustrated in Figure 4. While reporting bias is often presumed to reflect the tendency to publish significant results over non-significant findings, the Trim-and-Fill analysis revealed a different pattern: the algorithm imputed 7 potentially missing studies on the high-effect (right) side, indicating that the funnel plot is sparse in this region and that smaller studies in this domain tended to yield smaller effects. The adjusted pooled effect size remained statistically significant and higher than the original estimate (𝑟 = 0.125, 95% CI [. 071, .18], 𝑝 < .0001). Erring on the side of caution, we do not treat the imputed estimate as a corrected effect, because the imputation falls on the high-effect side rather than the low side that selective suppression would produce.

To complement these analyses, we implemented a selection model (Vevea & Hedges, 1995). The model estimates how the probability of a study being reported varies with its p-value and adjusts the pooled estimate for that pattern. It also performs better under conditions of substantial between-study heterogeneity. The step-function selection model yielded an adjusted pooled effect size of (𝑟 = 0.133 (95% CI [. 045, .218]), which remained statistically significant (𝑝 = 0.003). Furthermore, a likelihood-ratio test compared the selection model with a model in which reporting does not depend on p-values. The test was not statistically significant (LRT = 2.7263, 𝑑𝑓 = 2, 𝑝 = 0.256), indicating no evidence of publication bias. Altogether, a small-study effect is present, and nothing in the data indicates the pooled estimate is inflated by reporting bias.

Meta-Regression Analysis

To account for the observed heterogeneity, we performed a multiple meta-regression analysis, examining the impact of mean age and age range on the effect size. Age range was operationalized as the difference between the maximum and minimum age reported within each sample. Studies that did not provide sufficient data to calculate this range were excluded (8 effect sizes were dropped, leaving 𝑘 = 23). The model accounted for 36.86% of the total heterogeneity (QM(2) = 9.5163, p = 0.0086), with significant residual heterogeneity remaining (𝑄𝐸(20) = 88.8627, 𝑝 < .0001). The results indicated that while mean age was not a significant moderator (b = 0.0012, p = 0.4790), age range significantly predicted the magnitude of the correlation (b = 0.0041, p = 0.0038), suggesting that studies with a wider age range reported stronger positive correlations. Prior to the analysis, potential multicollinearity between the two predictors was evaluated. A Pearson correlation test revealed no significant collinearity between mean age and age range (𝑟 = 0.16, 𝑝 = 0.461).

To confirm that the age-range effect did not depend on any single sample or on particular analytic choices, we conducted a series of robustness checks. Omitting each sample in turn produced coefficients between b = 0.0031 and b = 0.0046, and none of the corrabprated with confidence interval included zero. The effect also held under the Knapp-Hartung small-sample adjustment (p = 0.011), under a permutation test (p = 0.010), after excluding the two effect sizes converted from other test statistics (b = 0.0033, p = 0.035), after excluding the sample using an extreme-groups design (b = 0.0039, p = 0.011), and after excluding papers contributing more than one sample (b = 0.0049, p < 0.001). One sample, Andrew & Dulin (2007), carried high leverage because it combined an unusually high mean age with a narrow age range, but omitting it left the coefficient significant (b = 0.0031, p = 0.035). Mean age was non-significant in every specification (smallest p = 0.085). Full diagnostics appear in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

Across 25 studies and 31 independent samples (N = 12,561), age correlated positively with psychological acceptance. The association was small (r = .077, 95% CI [.027, .127]) and robust to influence and publication-bias analyses. It also varied widely between studies (95% prediction interval -.17 to .32). Samples with wider age ranges showed stronger associations (b = .0041), whereas mean age did not moderate the association. The findings offer the first meta-analytic test of the lifespan prediction that psychological acceptance rises with age. They also show that the age span sampled in a study shapes the association it will detect, so studies of narrow age bands may underestimate the association between psychological acceptance and age.

The positive association aligns closely with foundational lifespan frameworks. Socioemotional selectivity theory (Carstensen, 1993; Carstensen et al., 1999) proposes that perceiving one's remaining time as limited shifts priorities toward emotionally meaningful experiences and close social relationships. Psychological acceptance may support these priorities by helping people respond to circumstances they cannot change without becoming caught in a struggle against them. Psychological acceptance may be particularly relevant in later life, when coping with uncontrollable and irreversible losses (Kunzmann & Grühn, 2005; Lang, 2001; Shallcross et al., 2013). A complementary perspective attributes the increase in acceptance to growing wisdom (Ardelt, 2000) that is characterized by the ability to recognize and manage the uncertainty inherent in human life (Baltes & Smith, 2008). From this perspective, psychological acceptance may reflect a growing capacity to integrate uncertainty and impermanence into one's understanding of life. The wisdom account offers a possible developmental explanation for greater psychological acceptance with age, although wisdom should not be assumed to increase uniformly across individuals. Sample age range, rather than mean age, moderated the association. Studies reporting ageacceptance associations were rarely designed to examine broad age-related change. Instead, many studies focus on relatively narrow age ranges. The age-range effect is consistent with, though does not establish, a lifespan account in which psychological acceptance increases across adulthood, as wider age spans allow developmental differences to emerge more clearly. However, the same pattern may also reflect a statistical range-restriction effect, whereby studies including narrower age ranges have reduced ability to detect age-acceptance associations due to limited variability in age. Because the present analyses were based on cross-sectional associations, they cannot distinguish between these explanations. Longitudinal research will therefore be required to determine whether psychological acceptance increases continuously across adulthood or whether changes are concentrated during specific developmental transitions.

Age range explained part of the variability between studies, but substantial residual heterogeneity remained. The included literature differed in ways we could not test, including samples composed exclusively of a single gender, specific occupational groups (e.g., psychiatrists), or clinical cohorts (e.g., individuals with acquired brain injury). We did not evaluate these sources of variation because they were not specified as a priori moderation hypotheses and statistical power for subgroup comparisons was limited.

Residual heterogeneity may also reflect age-related challenges that constrain the use of psychological acceptance under certain conditions. Physiological changes can leave older adults less equipped to recover from sustained emotional arousal (Charles, 2010). Perceived and actual proximity to death may pose additional challenges, as losses and declines near the end of life can overwhelm otherwise effective emotion regulation (Palgi et al., 2014). In line with this, research indicates that psychological acceptance is more frequently deployed in response to low-intensity rather than high-intensity negative emotions (Lennarz et al., 2019). Future primary studies could measure emotional intensity and proximity to death alongside psychological acceptance.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the data are predominantly cross-sectional, so they cannot detect age trajectories in psychological acceptance. Second, the substantial between-study heterogeneity and the prediction interval indicate that significant residual variance remains unexplained by sample age range alone. The pooled estimate therefore represents a broad central tendency rather than an identical developmental trajectory shared by all individuals.

Measurement also constrains the findings. Our search strategy identified five distinct types of questionnaires (AAQ, CERQ, COPE, PHLMS, and COF), several of which included multiple adaptations, such as short forms, older and updated versions, or translations. Despite this breadth, the published evidence base was dominated by studies using the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire, with 22 out of the 31 extracted effect sizes belonging to the AAQ family (and particularly the AAQ-II). Consequently, current empirical knowledge remains disproportionately weighted toward findings derived from this specific measure. Furthermore, the literature mainly relies on self-report methodologies. Although inclusion was restricted to psychometrically validated instruments, self-reports remain vulnerable to age-related shifts in item interpretation, response styles, or social desirability biases.

A further limitation concerns the studies that could not be included. Of the 95 full-text reports assessed for eligibility, 73 were excluded because they did not report sufficient statistical information despite attempts to contact the authors. Age was rarely a focal variable in these reports, so whether an age-acceptance correlation was reported at all may depend on what that correlation showed. The present synthesis therefore rests on a subset of studies that reported the association, and the direction of any resulting bias cannot be determined from the available data.

Finally, the funnel plot warrants comment. Egger's test indicated asymmetry, but its direction ran counter to selective suppression: smaller studies reported smaller associations, not larger ones. A selection model found no evidence of selection, so the analyses gave no indication that the pooled estimate is inflated by reporting bias. The asymmetry reflects a small-study effect. The present moderators explain only part of the heterogeneity, so the source of this effect remains unclear.

To address these limitations, future research should prioritize longitudinal designs capable of capturing intra-individual trajectories of psychological acceptance across adulthood. Such studies should employ measurement instruments explicitly validated for psychometric equivalence across diverse age cohorts alongside behavioral indices of psychological acceptance. Future primary studies should also record participant demographics so that potential moderators of the residual heterogeneity can be tested.

Conclusion

Across 31 independent effect sizes, we observed a small but reliable positive association, indicating that psychological acceptance tends to be higher at older ages. Meta-regression showed that studies employing wider sample age ranges yielded significantly larger effect sizes, indicating that narrow age ranges may understate the association across adulthood.
