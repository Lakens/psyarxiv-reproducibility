Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Number line estimation is widely used to examine numerical magnitude processing and its relationship with mathematics. However, it remains unclear whether mathematical achievement is more closely associated with estimation accuracy or with characteristics of estimation responses. The present study examined these relationships among 192 adults who completed bounded and unbounded number line estimation tasks and mathematics assessments covering arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages. Linear, logarithmic, mixed log-linear, proportional judgment, and scallop models were compared to characterize estimation responses. We also evaluated the relationships of estimation accuracy and fitted-response characteristics with mathematical achievement. Linear models best predicted bounded estimation, whereas mixed log-linear and selected scallop models offered small improvements for unbounded estimation. Greater linearity and stronger proportional judgment model fit were associated with greater bounded estimation accuracy, while mixed log-linear characteristics were related to estimation error in both tasks. Bounded estimation accuracy was more consistently associated with mathematical achievement than unbounded accuracy. Most fitted-response characteristics accounted for little additional variation in mathematics after considering estimation accuracy, although exploratory analyses indicated that mixed-model characteristics contributed modest additional information. The findings highlight the value of distinguishing estimation accuracy from the mathematical characteristics of estimation responses when investigating individual differences in mathematical achievement.

1. Introduction

Number line estimation is frequently used to investigate numerical magnitude representation and its association with mathematical competence. In a typical number line estimation task, participants are presented with a numerical magnitude and must indicate its corresponding position on a spatial line. Accurate performance requires establishing a relationship between numerical quantities and spatial distances, making the task a valuable measure of spatial-numerical processing. Extensive research has linked number line estimation performance to mathematical achievement, including arithmetic, fraction knowledge, and broader mathematical competence (Schneider et al., 2018; Ünal et al., 2024). However, despite the widespread application of number line estimation tasks, significant debate persists about the cognitive processes underlying estimation performance and how much these processes explain its relationship with mathematics.

Traditionally, number line estimation has been interpreted as reflecting the organization of internal numerical magnitude representations, with increasingly linear estimates indicating more developed numerical knowledge (Siegler & Opfer, 2003). Proportional judgment accounts challenge this interpretation, proposing that estimation responses may reflect the use of spatial reference points and proportional reasoning rather than the structure of numerical representations alone (Barth & Paladino, 2011; Slusser et al., 2013). The distinction has become particularly important with the introduction of the unbounded number line estimation task, which removes the labeled upper endpoint characteristic of traditional bounded estimation and may therefore reduce opportunities for endpoint-based proportional judgment (Cohen & Blanc-Goldhammer, 2011; Reinert & Moeller, 2021).

Although both bounded and unbounded estimation are used to assess numerical magnitude processing, previous research suggests that they may differ in their relationships with mathematical achievement (Jung et al., 2020). Understanding these differences requires distinguishing estimation accuracy from the mathematical characteristics of estimation responses. For example, a participant may produce estimates that closely approximate a linear function without accurately positioning the target magnitudes, while different estimation processes may generate similarly accurate responses. Therefore, examining estimation accuracy alongside linearity and characteristics associated with proportional judgment or iterative scaling may clarify which aspects of number line performance most closely relate to mathematical competence. Siegler and Opfer (2003) investigated numerical estimation among second-grade, fourth-grade, and sixth-grade children and adults. They found that younger children more frequently produced logarithmic estimation patterns, whereas older participants increasingly produced linear estimates. These patterns varied with numerical range, suggesting that the organization of estimates depends partly on experience with particular magnitudes. Subsequent studies similarly showed that linear estimation emerges earlier for familiar ranges and is associated with numerical knowledge (Booth & Siegler, 2006; Siegler & Booth, 2004).

1.1. Numerical Magnitude Representation and the Development of Number Line Estimation

Logarithmic functions create greater spatial separation among smaller magnitudes and compress larger magnitudes, whereas linear functions allocate approximately equal spatial distances to equal numerical intervals. Although increased linearity is often interpreted as evidence of more precise magnitude representation, similar response patterns may result from interpolation between learned reference points. Thus, mathematical fit alone does not uniquely identify a participant's internal numerical representation.

More recent work has tested whether a common representational model can describe both task formats. Kim and Opfer (2017) examined number line estimation in children and found that a mixed logarithmic-linear model characterized BNL and UNB responses, and that its parameters were associated with arithmetic performance. Qin et al. (2024) extended this comparison across multiple task variants involving children and adults and likewise found substantial support for mixed log-linear functions. These findings preserve a role for numerical representations and highlight the value of comparing mixed functions with both simpler functions and reference-based accounts.

Proportional judgment accounts propose that numerical placement depends partly on comparing a target with available spatial reference points. Barth and Paladino (2011) applied psychophysical models of proportional judgment to number line estimation and found that they could explain response patterns previously interpreted as evidence of a developmental shift from logarithmic to linear magnitude representations. Their findings suggested that changes in how effectively children use proportional information may contribute to improvements in estimation.

For BNL, explicitly labeled endpoints define the numerical interval. A one-cycle proportional judgment model describes patterns consistent with estimating a target relative to those endpoints, whereas a two-cycle model also accommodates a midpoint reference that divides the interval into smaller segments. The exponent of these models describes systematic curvature, but an exponent of one produces a linear function. Consequently, proportional judgment and linear estimation are not mutually exclusive: effective reference-based placement can generate approximately linear responses. Slusser et al. (2013) found that proportional judgment models described developmental estimation patterns better than a simple logarithmic-to-linear shift account. Ashcraft and Moore (2012), however, reported evidence implicating both developmental changes in numerical knowledge and the use of spatial benchmarks. Together, these studies suggest that numerical representation and proportional judgment may operate jointly; model fit alone cannot establish which process produced an estimate.

1.2. Bounded and Unbounded Number Line Estimation

BNL presents a spatial interval with numerical labels at both endpoints, making the whole range available for proportional comparison. UNB removes the labeled upper endpoint and instead provides an origin and numerical unit that participants must extend to locate a target. This design was introduced partly to reduce dependence on endpoint-based judgments and investigate numerical magnitude processing under different reference-point conditions (Cohen & Blanc-Goldhammer, 2011). In experiments with adults, Cohen and Blanc-Goldhammer (2011) observed systematic biases in bounded estimates consistent with proportional judgment, whereas unbounded responses showed patterns consistent with iterative scaling or dead reckoning. They proposed single, dual, and multiple scallop power functions to describe response patterns that may arise when an individual repeatedly extends a reference interval. Scalloping thus provides a theoretically relevant alternative to fitting only linear or logarithmic functions to UNB responses. Link et al. (2014) also observed differences between bounded and unbounded estimation among children, suggesting that a labeled endpoint changes the task demands. In a systematic review, Reinert and Moeller (2021) found substantial support for using UNB to assess numerical magnitude processing but noted methodological variation and evidence of systematic bias in some studies. Removing an upper endpoint therefore reduces one source of reference-based judgment without eliminating other sources of estimation bias.

For mathematics, Jung et al. ( 2020) compared BNL and UNB among students in Grades 5 through 7. Bounded estimation showed stronger associations with basic arithmetic, whereas unbounded estimation had weaker or nonsignificant associations with those outcomes. However, mixed log-linear modeling suggests that aspects of UNB performance can also inform arithmetic ability (Kim & Opfer, 2017; Qin et al., 2024). Accordingly, the literature does not establish which task universally provides a better measure of mathematical competence.

1.3. Number Line Estimation Accuracy and Mathematical

Achievement Large-scale syntheses have established a relationship between number line estimation and mathematics. Schneider et al. (2018) reported an overall association between estimation performance and mathematical competence. Their moderator analyses suggested a stronger association for BNL than UNB, although the unbounded estimate was based on very limited independent evidence. This limitation makes within-participant comparisons of the two tasks particularly informative. Unal et al. (2024) likewise found associations between number line performance and mathematics, including whole-number and fraction estimation. After accounting for general cognitive abilities, whole-number number line performance remained associated with fraction competence, but not consistently with computation or numeracy. This domain-specific pattern raises the possibility that estimation performance is differentially associated with distinct forms of mathematical knowledge.

Arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages involve overlapping numerical competencies but differ in their demands for calculation, part-whole reasoning, symbolic relationships, and proportional scaling. Comparing BNL and UNB across these outcomes may help determine whether the relative mathematical relevance of the tasks is consistent across domains or concentrated in particular forms of mathematical knowledge.

1.4. Estimation Accuracy, Linearity, and Strategy-Related Characteristics

Estimation accuracy and the mathematical form of an estimation function represent distinct aspects of performance. Accuracy measures the distance between an estimate and the correct numerical position, while linearity reflects the extent to which responses conform to a straight-line function. Participants may generate highly linear estimates that are systematically offset from the correct positions, or produce reasonably accurate estimates that vary enough to reduce linear model fit. Therefore, linearity should not be equated with accurate numerical placement.

This distinction extends to proportional and scalloped models. Ruiz et al. (2023) compared several functional accounts of bounded estimation among kindergarten children and found that multiple models often described an individual's responses comparably well. Mathematical achievement differed across estimation patterns, but the overlap among models complicated inferences about strategy. Qin et al. (2024) also found that mixed log-linear model parameters related to arithmetic achievement. Neither result, however, establishes that a particular functional characteristic explains mathematics performance independently of estimation accuracy.

A recent study more directly differentiated strategy classification from accuracy. Dorneles and Nogues (2026) examined fourth-grade students and categorized their estimation approaches as no identifiable strategy, counting, or proportional judgment. Children employing identifiable strategies generally demonstrated higher estimation accuracy; however, strategy classification was not significantly associated with mathematics performance, whereas estimation accuracy was. This finding suggests that accurate numerical placement may be more consistently related to mathematics than the specific approach used to achieve it. Nevertheless, as the present study infers strategyrelated characteristics from fitted functions rather than direct observation, these two types of evidence should be distinguished.

Moreover, model fit and estimation error are calculated from the same responses, potentially creating mathematical dependence between these measures. It is therefore useful to examine their relationships while separately testing whether fitted response characteristics provide additional information about mathematical achievement after accuracy is considered.

1.5. The Present Study

Although previous research has shown that bounded and unbounded number line estimation differ in their response characteristics and associations with mathematical achievement (Jung et al., 2020; Schneider et al., 2018), an important question remains: what are the relative contributions of estimation accuracy and strategy-related response characteristics to these associations? Specifically, it remains unclear whether functional characteristics consistent with proportional judgment, iterative scaling, or mixtures of logarithmic and linear estimation explain mathematical achievement beyond estimation accuracy. This question matters because different cognitive processes can produce similar response patterns.

Another limitation pertains to the developmental focus of prior research. Much of the literature on estimation strategies and their associations with mathematical achievement has concentrated on children, whose numerical representations and estimation strategies are still developing. Although previous studies have demonstrated that numerical magnitude estimation remains relevant to mathematical achievement in adulthood (Ayesu et al., 2026), less is known about whether bounded and unbounded estimation differ in their associations with mathematical competence among adults. This distinction is particularly salient because adult estimation responses typically approximate linear functions, potentially reducing the developmental variation emphasized in traditional accounts of number line estimation. Examining both tasks within the same adult sample thus offers an opportunity to determine whether their differential associations with mathematics are attributable to estimation accuracy, characteristics of reference-based estimation processes, or a combination of these factors.

To address these gaps, the present study compared bounded (BNL) and unbounded (UNB) number line estimation in adults, focusing on the mathematical characteristics of estimation responses, their associations with estimation accuracy, and their relationships with mathematical achievement. We considered linear, logarithmic, and mixed log-linear models in both tasks, along with one-cycle and two-cycle proportional judgment models for BNL and single-, dual-, and multiple-scallop models for UNB. The mixed model was included because representational accounts explicitly predict a mixture of logarithmic and linear components. Functional model characteristics were interpreted as descriptions of response patterns, not direct observations of cognitive strategies.

Three research questions guided the analyses. The first research question (RQ1) examined which mathematical functions best characterize BNL and UNB estimation in adults. Adult responses were expected to be predominantly linear, but the mixed log-linear, proportional judgment, and scalloped functions were also evaluated to characterize potentially representational and reference-based patterns. The second research question (RQ2) investigated how linearity, mixed log-linear logarithmic weight, proportional judgment characteristics, and scalloped estimation patterns were associated with estimation accuracy. These analyses examined whether functional response characteristics were related to more accurate placement and whether the association between linearity and accuracy differed across tasks. The third research question (RQ3) examined which task was more strongly associated with mathematical achievement and whether functional response characteristics explained additional variation beyond estimation accuracy. Consistent with prior BNL findings, bounded accuracy was expected to show a stronger association with mathematics than unbounded accuracy. Overall mathematics achievement was the primary outcome, with arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages examined as secondary out-comes. The incremental contributions of response characteristics beyond accuracy were examined as a further question.

By comparing the two estimation tasks and differentiating estimation accuracy from mathematical response characteristics, this study sought to clarify which aspects of adult number line performance are most closely associated with mathematical achievement.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Participants

The final analytic sample comprised 192 psychology students (120 females and 72 males; mean age = 22.1, = 1.81), recruited through the Psychology PREP participant pool at a mid-sized university in Atlantic Canada. Students received course credit toward eligible psychology courses in exchange for their participation. The study was approved by the university's institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent before testing.

2.2. Measures

>Measures

2.2.1. Bounded Number Line Estimation

Bounded number line estimation was assessed using a computerized task in which participants estimated the positions of numerical magnitudes on a spatial line with explicitly labeled endpoints. The line extended from 0 at the left endpoint to 115 at the right endpoint. Participants were presented with a target number and instructed to indicate its corresponding location by selecting a position on the line. The task consisted of 20 estimation trials with the following target magnitudes: 18, 34, 66, 96, 104, 66, 52, 98, 13, 72, 112, 42, 76, 108, 35, 68, 84, 15, 91, 56. Each trial presented a numerical target and a horizontal response line. Participants indicated their estimates by clicking the location they believed corresponded to the target magnitude. The horizontal position of each response was recorded and converted into a numerical estimate relative to the bounded interval. The presence of both numerical endpoints provided participants with an explicitly defined range within which to position the target. Consequently, the task allowed participants to use the endpoints and potentially the midpoint as reference points when determining numerical locations. However, the task did not directly assess whether participants used these reference points or any particular estimation strategy.

Estimation accuracy was assessed using mean absolute error (MAE), calculated as the average absolute difference between each participant's estimated numerical position and the corresponding target value across the 20 trials. Lower MAE scores indicated more accurate estimation. In addition to accuracy, the mathematical characteristics of participants' estimation responses were examined using linear, logarithmic, one-cycle proportional judgment, and two-cycle proportional judgment functions. These functions were used to characterize response patterns potentially consistent with different numerical estimation processes. The fitting procedures and associated model characteristics are described in the Data Analysis section.

2.2.2. Unbounded Number Line Estimation

Unbounded number line estimation was assessed using a computerized task adapted from the unbounded estimation paradigm described by Reinert et al. (2015). Unlike the bounded task, the unbounded number line did not display a labeled numerical endpoint. Instead, participants were provided with a starting point and a numerical reference interval that could be used to estimate the position of a target magnitude. In the recorded task implementation, the line was anchored at 0 and included a reference interval corresponding to 6 units. Participants were instructed to estimate where a target number should be located by extending the numerical scale represented by the reference interval. The absence of a labeled upper endpoint reduced opportunities for judgments based on the proportion of a fixed, explicitly defined numerical range.

The task consisted of 20 trials with the following target magnitudes: 71, 23, 60, 71, 80, 13, 39, 18, 105, 53, 36, 112, 96, 52, 67, 32, 55, 42, 66, 103. Each target was presented separately, and participants indicated their estimates by selecting a position on the horizontal line. Presenting the trials individually prevented participants from using previously displayed responses as simultaneously visible spatial reference points. Responses were recorded as horizontal positions and converted into numerical estimates using the task's programmed coordinate transformation. The software used a nominal conversion scale of 120 units for the available response space; this was a computational feature rather than a numerical endpoint displayed to participants. Estimation accuracy was calculated using MAE, representing the average absolute difference between the estimated and target values across the 20 trials. Lower scores indicated more accurate estimation.

In addition to estimation accuracy, participants' responses were characterized using linear, logarithmic, and scalloped power functions. Following the theoretical framework of Cohen and Blanc-Goldhammer (2011), single, dual, and multiple scallop functions were evaluated to examine patterns potentially consistent with iterative numerical scaling. Because the functions were fitted to observed responses, their characteristics were interpreted as mathematical descriptions of estimation patterns rather than direct evidence of particular cognitive strategies. Figure 1 illustrates the bounded and unbounded task formats.

2.2.3. Mathematical Performance

Mathematical performance was assessed using multiple-choice questions adapted from the Mathematical Placement Test administered by the Department of Mathematics and Statistics at the university where the study was conducted. The placement test was developed by mathematics educators to assess competence across core mathematical domains through approximately Grade 12, including arithmetic, fractions, algebra, percentages, geometry, and other aspects of mathematical reasoning.

For the present study, the selected questions assessed four mathematical domains: arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages. The archived survey dataset contains 44 scored items comprising 14 arithmetic questions, 15 fraction questions, 10 algebra questions, and 5 percentage questions. Each question presented four response options and required participants to S D apply their conceptual or procedural mathematical knowledge to identify the correct answer. Arithmetic questions assessed numerical computation and the simplification of mathematical expressions. Fraction questions assessed operations involving fractions and decimals, including the manipulation and simplification of rational numbers. Algebra questions required participants to solve equations and determine the values of unknown variables. Percentage questions assessed the application of proportional relationships to numerical calculations involving percentages.

Mathematical performance was expressed as the percentage of correct responses, with higher scores indicating better performance. Overall achievement was calculated by dividing the number of correct responses by the number of mathematics items with recorded responses and multiplying by 100. Arithmetic and fraction domain scores were calculated as percentages of observed responses, whereas unanswered algebra and percentage items were scored as incorrect using their full domain denominators. Overall mathematics was the primary outcome, and arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages were secondary outcomes.

2.3. Procedure

All testing was conducted in person in a university laboratory. Participants were assessed individually using a computer, with the tasks administered through Qualtrics. Standardized instructions were presented before each task, and participants completed practice trials to familiarize themselves with the number line estimation procedure. Participants completed both bounded and unbounded number line estimation tasks. The order of administration varied across participants: some completed the bounded task first, followed by the unbounded task, whereas others completed the tasks in the reverse order. Each task consisted of 20 trials, presented individually. Participants estimated the location of each target number by selecting its position on the computerized number line. After completing both number line tasks, participants completed the mathematics assessment, which evaluated arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages. The four mathematics blocks were presented in randomized order. Participants were allotted 15 minutes each for arithmetic, fractions, and algebra, and 10 minutes for percentages. Calculators were not permitted.

4. Discussion

The present study examined whether bounded and unbounded number line estimation differed in the response functions that best characterized adults' estimates, how those functions related to estimation accuracy, and whether accuracy or fitted-response characteristics were more closely associated with mathematical achievement. Three findings address these questions. First, linear functions provided the best prediction of bounded number line responses, whereas mixed log-linear and selected scallop models captured small additional features of unbounded responses. Second, greater linearity and stronger fits to proportional judgment models were closely associated with bounded estimation accuracy, and the logarithmic contributions estimated by the mixed models were associated with estimation error in both tasks. Third, bounded estimation accuracy was more consistently related to mathematical achievement than unbounded accuracy. Although most fitted-response characteristics accounted for little additional variation in mathematics once estimation accuracy was considered, the two mixed-model weights jointly accounted for a modest exploratory increment. These findings highlight the importance of distinguishing estimation accuracy from the mathematical characteristics of estimation responses.

4.1. Theoretical Implications

The strong performance of linear functions, particularly for bounded estimation, is consistent with developmental evidence that linearity increases with numerical experience (Siegler & Opfer, 2003). However, the theoretical meaning of linearity requires careful consideration. Accounts emphasizing numerical magnitude representation suggest that increasingly linear estimates may reflect greater precision in representing numerical magnitude. Proportional judgment accounts offer an alternative explanation: estimates may also appear linear when participants use reference points and proportional relationships effectively. Because these processes can produce similar response functions, a strong linear fit does not establish that numerical magnitude is represented internally on a linear scale. The present findings therefore demonstrate the predominance of linear response patterns, but do not resolve whether these patterns arose primarily from internal magnitude representations or proportional judgment.

The bounded task provides a more specific test of the competing functions. The linear model predicted responses excluded during each round of cross-validation more accurately than the one-cycle and two-cycle proportional judgment models. This finding indicates that the additional curvature specified by those proportional models did not improve prediction of participants' responses.

It does not, however, imply that participants disregarded reference points. The exploratory analysis showed lower estimation error near the labeled endpoints, consistent with the possibility that participants used these boundaries when making judgments. In contrast, there was no clear accuracy advantage near the midpoint. Moreover, endpoint differences may partly reflect the distribution of target numbers or constraints on response placement. The evidence therefore supports a possible contribution of endpoint information without identifying a particular proportional judgment strategy.

The unbounded task showed somewhat greater variation in response form, although the evidence for departures from linearity was limited. The mixed log-linear model and selected scallop models yielded slightly lower cross-validated prediction error than linear regression, but these improvements were small. Scallop models in which the interval was freely estimated did not predict responses more accurately than the linear model. These findings are compatible with modest nonlinear components, as proposed in mixed-representation accounts (Kim & Opfer, 2017; Qin et al., 2024), and with aspects of reference-based estimation (Cohen & Blanc-Goldhammer, 2011). They do not establish either explanation. All unbounded targets exceeded the visible six-unit reference interval, limiting the extent to which repeated use of that interval could be identified from the observed responses. Further, the numerical scaling used to estimate the mixed model did not provide participants with a visible upper endpoint. Consequently, the mixed and scallop functions are best interpreted as alternative statistical descriptions of unbounded estimates rather than direct evidence of the cognitive processes that produced them.

A central finding was that bounded estimation accuracy was more consistently associated with mathematical achievement than unbounded accuracy. Adults with lower bounded estimation error generally performed better on the overall mathematics assessment, with associations also evident across the assessed mathematical domains. The corresponding relationships for unbounded estimation were weaker. A direct bootstrap comparison supported the difference between tasks for overall mathematics. At the domain level, the clearest evidence of a difference concerned arithmetic; although bounded correlations were descriptively stronger for fractions, algebra, and percentages, the analyses did not establish reliable differences in every domain. This distinction is important because statistical significance for one correlation but not another does not, by itself, demonstrate a difference between them. The findings extend evidence relating number line estimation accuracy to mathematical competence (Schneider et al., 2018; Ünal et al., 2024) and are consistent with previous work showing a stronger relationship between bounded estimation and arithmetic (Jung et al., 2020).

The second issue was whether the mathematical characteristics of an individual's estimates provided information about mathematics beyond estimation accuracy. Several fitted-response characteristics were strongly associated with estimation error, particularly linearity and proportional judgment model fit in the bounded task. These relationships are relevant to understanding variation in estimation performance, but they are not independent evidence of distinct cognitive processes: accuracy and model characteristics were derived from the same responses. When accuracy in both tasks was considered first, most response characteristics accounted for little additional variation in mathematics. The main exception arose in an exploratory analysis that added the mixed log-linear weights from both tasks. Together, these weights produced a modest increase in the variation explained in overall mathematics. The unbounded weight was associated with mathematics after adjustment even though its correlation with mathematics alone was weak, suggesting that shared variation among predictors or a suppression effect may be involved. This pattern should not be interpreted as direct evidence that a stronger logarithmic component improves mathematical achievement. Because the analysis was exploratory and multiple model extensions were examined, independent replication is needed. Nevertheless, the finding cautions against concluding that all fitted-response characteristics are unrelated to mathematics once estimation accuracy is considered.

The weak association between unbounded accuracy and mathematics in the present sample should also be considered alongside previous adult research. Ayesu et al. (2026) reported associations between unbounded estimation and arithmetic, fractions, and algebra. Differences in reference-unit administration, target distributions, response scaling, participant characteristics, or mathematics scoring may help explain this variation across studies. The present data do not identify which of these factors is responsible. Rather, the contrasting findings suggest that the mathematical relevance of unbounded estimation may depend on how the task is implemented and assessed. Bounded and unbounded number line tasks should therefore not be assumed to provide interchangeable measures of numerical magnitude processing.

4.2. Educational Implications

The findings have implications for the interpretation of number line estimation in educational research and assessment. Bounded estimation accuracy was more consistently associated with overall mathematical achievement and each assessed domain than unbounded accuracy in this adult sample. This suggests that the bounded task may be informative when studying individual differences in mathematical competence. However, the associations do not establish bounded estimation as a diagnostic measure or demonstrate that improving number line accuracy would improve mathematics achievement. The stronger mathematical association for bounded estimation must also be considered in light of the task's explicitly labeled endpoints, which may support proportional judgment and numerical placement in ways that differ from the unbounded task.

The distinction between estimation accuracy and fittedresponse characteristics is also relevant to assessment interpretation. High linearity or a strong fit to a proportional judgment model does not necessarily indicate accurate placement of numerical magnitudes, and such mathematical functions cannot independently establish which strategies an individual used. Consequently, accuracy and response form should not be treated as interchangeable indicators of numerical competence. Although the exploratory mixed log-linear analysis suggested that certain response characteristics may contribute information beyond accuracy, this finding requires replication before it can inform assessment design or instructional decisions.

For mathematics education, these results support further investigation of how reference points, numerical scaling, and proportional relationships influence estimation performance. Studies involving students, varied mathematical content, and direct observations of strategy use could determine whether different number line formats reveal distinct mathematical competencies or respond differently to instruction. Because the present research involved adults and did not evaluate an educational intervention, its findings should guide future assessment and instructional research rather than be interpreted as evidence favoring a particular teaching approach.

4.3. Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. First, the crosssectional design supports associations between estimation and mathematics, but cannot establish their developmental or causal direction. Second, participants' estimation strategies were not assessed directly through systematic strategy reports, think-aloud protocols, or eye tracking. Accordingly, linearity, proportional curvature, logarithmic mixture weights, and scalloping describe fitted response patterns rather than observed strategies. Third, accuracy and model characteristics were derived from the same estimation trials. Although cross-fitted analyses reduced overlap between the trials used for fitting and evaluation, common variation in response consistency may still contribute to the associations between these measures. Fourth, the mixed loglinear model and the standalone linear and logarithmic models were not identically parameterized. The mixed model imposed constraints on the scale and mixture weight and did not include a free intercept, whereas the standalone regressions included intercepts. These differences limit the extent to which small predictive advantages can be interpreted as evidence favoring one cognitive account over another.

The tasks also differed in their visible reference information and in how responses were transformed into numerical estimates. Consequently, differences in raw mean absolute error should not be interpreted as evidence that one task is inherently easier or provides a more valid measure. Each participant completed only 20 targets per task, limiting the precision with which complex nonlinear response functions could be identified. In addition, unbounded targets were not densely sampled near the six-unit reference interval, making repeated reference-unit use difficult to evaluate. The predictive advantages of the more complex unbounded models were small, and several analyses, including the mixed-weight regressions, were exploratory and not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Finally, the percentage domain contained only five items, restricting the precision of conclusions about associations involving that domain.

Future research should examine bounded and unbounded estimation under more closely matched target distributions and response metrics, while experimentally varying endpoint information and reference-unit length. Combining model fitting with direct strategy reports, eye tracking, or other process measures would help determine when linear, proportional, or scalloped response functions reflect particular estimation procedures. A larger number of targets, especially near the unbounded reference interval, would provide a stronger basis for evaluating iterative scaling and mixed log-linear accounts. Studies that assess score reliability and replicate the exploratory mixed-model associations in independent samples would also clarify whether these response characteristics contribute to mathematical achievement beyond estimation accuracy.

5. Conclusion

The results indicate that bounded and unbounded number line estimation tasks are differentially associated with mathematical achievement in adults. Although responses on both tasks were predominantly linear, higher accuracy on the bounded task was correlated with superior mathematics scores. In contrast, accuracy on the unbounded estimation task did not show a significant association with overall mathematical achievement. Mathematical features of estimation responses, including linearity and patterns indicative of proportional judgment or iterative scaling, were related to estimation accuracy but explained little additional variance in mathematical achievement beyond accuracy itself. Exploratory analyses suggest that mixed logarithmic and linear response characteristics may provide further insight; however, this finding requires additional investigation. Overall, estimation accuracy emerges as a more reliable indicator of mathematical achievement than specific response patterns, and bounded and unbounded number line tasks should not be considered interchangeable measures of numerical competence.
