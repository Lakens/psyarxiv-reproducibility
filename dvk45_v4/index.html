How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

The use of Therapeutically Applied Role-Playing Games (TA-RPGs) in mental health treatment settings has grown in popularity, with early efficacy investigations appearing promising. However, the use of TA-RPGs for the treatment of anxiety and depression has rarely been studied in people who present with comorbid complex mental health issues.

Whilst one recent waitlist-controlled trial reported that a TA-RPG (Dungeons and Dragons with embedded CBT) reduced mental health symptomatology, this now needs to be replicated with a more robust experimental design. Publishing this protocol methodology before the study is completed aims to increase transparency and aid other researchers conducting similar research in this advancing field.

The authors propose a two-group randomised controlled trial testing the efficacy of a CBT-infused, therapeutically applied, 8-week Dungeons and Dragons group program. The intervention group will participate in the TA-RPG, whilst an active control group will play board games for a comparable amount of time. Participants will consist of consumers receiving care at a community mental health centre with complex care needs and will be randomly assigned to either the intervention or active control group. Outcome measures for anxiety and depression symptomatology will be collected before and after participation in both groups, with the intervention group also completing outcome measures at 12 months follow up. In this protocol paper, the intervention will be outlined in detail.

A randomised controlled trial aims to add high-quality quantitative evidence of the efficacy of TA-RPG to the existing field. Sharing this protocol ensures that the results of the study can be replicated by future researchers.

[div-01]

Consumers of NSW Community Mental Health (CMH) Services experience a variety of co-morbid disorders and complex healthcare needs, commonly presenting with psychotic and bipolar disorders (including schizophrenia), alongside high rates of affective, anxiety, and trauma-related symptoms (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2024). Given the diagnostic heterogeneity of community mental health populations, broadly applicable interventions are required. Social anxiety disorder (SAD) is one of the most common conditions encountered in this setting (Achim et al., 2011; Belzer & Schneier, 2004; Pavlova et al., 2015), highlighting the need for interventions that are transdiagnostic and suitable for diverse consumers of CMH services.

Current clinical practice guidelines recommend individual cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) as a first line treatment for adults with SAD (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2013). CBT trials for SAD typically recruit participants with a primary diagnosis of SAD and exclude individuals with primary bipolar or psychotic disorders (Hall et al., 2025). Consequently, the existing evidence base may have limited generalisability to adults receiving care in community mental health services, where diagnostic complexity and psychiatric comorbidity are common. Standard CBT protocols may require significant modification when applied to individuals with complex mental health presentations, characterised by comorbidity, trauma histories, cognitive difficulties, and interpersonal challenges (Michail et al., 2017; Tarrier & Johnson, 2015). Therefore, mental health clinicians have started to experiment with new approaches to delivering psychological therapies to their consumers.

One alternative approach that has increased in popularity in recent years is the use of therapeutically applied role-playing games (TA-RPGs) (Arenas et al., 2022; Hazel, 2021; Yuliawati et al., 2024). This innovative and novel approach is offered by specialised services in the United States (e.g., Geek Therapeutics, n.d. ; The Bodhana Group, n.d.), and departs significantly from a typical CBT format. Elements of therapy are delivered within a fantasybased role-playing game, with each participant playing the game as a character within a fictional world. While a variety of role-playing games can be used in this context, 'Dungeons & Dragons' is the most widely recognised and therefore the most accessible. The typical format involves players sitting around a table while the game is led by a Dungeon Master, whose role is to facilitate the game, and ensure that the rules are applied fairly. While participation in social games of Dungeons & Dragons (i.e., outside of a mental health context) has been associated with improvements in symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress (Merrick et al., 2024), as well as social connectedness (Konieczny, 2019), TA-RPG's are intentionally designed to achieve specific therapeutic objectives.

In clinical TA-RPG's, mental health clinicians combine the role of Dungeon Master with that of a group facilitator, enabling them to support participants to work collaboratively and complete quests that are specifically designed to maximise the therapeutic benefits of the intervention. Social skills are taught and practiced through interactions with non-player characters such as local merchants, the captain of the guard, and innkeepers. Therapeutic elements are often placed within the storyline and there is ample opportunity for teachable moments within the game. TA-RPG's have been used in therapeutic contexts to address a range of mental health and neurodevelopmental difficulties, including depression, SAD, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and substance use. Clinical accounts have suggested that TA-RPG's may facilitate engagement in therapy among military veterans while promoting confidence, well-being, self-esteem, empathy, and frustration tolerance (Kilmer et al., 2023).

Similarly, a recent article highlighted the growing use of role-playing games, including 'Dungeons & Dragons', by clinical psychologists in therapeutic settings across the United States, particularly with children and adolescents experiencing anxiety, depression, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), impulsivity, aggression, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and ASD (Abramson, 2025).

Collectively, these reports suggest that TA-RPGs may have useful therapeutic potential across a range of clinical populations. However, due to the novelty of this approach, the current experimental literature demonstrating the efficacy of TA-RPG's is in its infancy.

Two recent scoping reviews have been conducted, with Yuliawati et al. (2024) highlighting the potential for TA-RPGs to promote social skills, problem-solving skills, empathy, and creativity. Arenas et al. (2022) examined table-top, computerised, live action role-play and massively multiplayer online RPGs, concluded that out of 50 studies included in the review, 90% reported positive benefits for the participants, with symptom reduction associated with several mental health conditions, including anxiety, depression, and substance use disorders, as well as supporting individuals with ASD. However, only 16% of the included studies used randomised controlled trials, five of which were conducted on a computerised role-playing game CBT intervention called Smart, Positive, Active, Realistic, X-factor thoughts (SPARX).

Further, these studies seldom incorporated participants presenting with complex comorbidities.

Numerous randomised controlled trials with the computerised SPARX have demonstrated improvements in mental health metrics across diverse adolescent populations, including improvements in emotional regulation and symptoms of depression and anxiety (Fleming et al., 2012; Kuosmanen et al., 2017; Merry et al., 2012; Perry et al., 2017; Poppelaars et al., 2016). Although SPARX currently offers the strongest evidence supporting the effectiveness of a gamified CBT intervention, its computerised design differs notably from that of TA-RPG's. TA-RPGs are typically delivered in group settings and are facilitated by a mental health professional, offering a more interactive and social experience. Despite growing interest in this format, high-quality experimental research on the efficacy of TA-RPGs remains limited and it is not clear whether, and under what conditions, TA-RPGs produce meaningful clinical benefits.

To our knowledge few studies have investigated the effectiveness of TA-RPG's for individuals with complex mental health conditions accessing community mental health services. A recent waitlist-controlled trial explored the use of TA-RPG's in an adult community mental health setting in Sydney, Australia (Rae et al., 2025). The transdiagnostic sample included 33 participants that were aged 18-65, and the most common primary mental health diagnoses included schizophrenia, first-episode psychosis and bipolar disorder. The role-playing game was 'CBT-infused', meaning that the CBT concepts were embedded throughout an existing role-playing game framework. This was designed to occur as organically as possible within the game, while maintaining player immersion. The study reported that relative to a waitlist control group receiving treatment as usual, the TA-RPG significantly reduced participants self-reported symptoms of social anxiety, depression, and self-esteem. Whilst these results are encouraging; the study was limited by the small sample size and quasi-randomisation to the waitlist control vs. experimental group.

The current protocol details a randomised controlled trial (RCT) using the same intervention, with an active control group that play commercially-available board games.

Analogous to Rae et al. (2025), the present RCT focuses on the presence and severity of clinically-relevant symptoms rather than categorical diagnoses, with outcome measures selected to reflect the intervention's therapeutic targets and mechanisms. Social anxiety symptoms are specified as the primary outcome because the TA-RPG is designed to incorporate core CBT strategies for social anxiety, including behavioural exposure, cognitive reappraisal, emotional regulation, and rehearsal of social and assertive behaviours (Gilboa-Schechtman & Azoulay, 2022; Goldin et al., 2014). Depressive symptoms and self-esteem are specified as secondary outcomes, reflecting the potential downstream benefits of increased social engagement, mastery, and reduced avoidance (Beck & Dozois, 2011). Post-traumatic stress symptoms are also included as a secondary outcome, given the high prevalence of trauma exposure among community mental health consumers and the intervention's focus on emotion regulation, narrative processing, and interpersonal safety. (Hassija & Cloitre, 2015; Phipps et al., 2019). Finally, board games were chosen as an active control group to control for non-specific effects of group participation, social interaction, enjoyment, and engagement, while differing from the TA-RPG in the specific therapeutic mechanisms hypothesised to drive change. This design therefore allowed the effects of the TA-RPG intervention to be distinguished from the broader benefits of participating in a structured, enjoyable group activity.

The hypotheses and analysis plans were preregistered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry [link redacted for peer review]. It is hypothesised that an 8week, CBT-infused TA-RPG group program will (1) show a significant difference in the reduction of self-reported social anxiety symptoms as compared to a board game control group, (2) show improvements in self-reported symptoms of depression, self-esteem, and PTSD, and (3) these improvements will be maintained at a follow-up 12 months after completion of the group. A secondary aim of the study is to further test the effectiveness of running TA-RPG groups within the current structure of the services that are provided in a public mental health system.

A CBT-infused TA-RPG is not intended to replace a structured CBT program, as it does not provide the same level of structure, organisation, and predictability as a traditional CBT intervention. However, given the limitations of delivering structured CBT to people with complex mental health presentations, the results of the current study hopes to demonstrate evidence for the effectiveness of a creative alternative for consumers of community mental health services. It is anticipated that the results of this study will add to the growing literature base for the use of TA-RPGs in clinical practice, and improve the likelihood of public mental health services adopting therapies that reflect their diverse consumers.

Primary Outcome Measure

Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale Self-Report (LSAS-SR; Fresco et al., 2001).

The LSAS has 24 items that measure the level of fear and avoidance of social situations that is typical of people who experience social anxiety. On a 4-point Likert scale, participants how fearful they are, and to what extent they would avoid, a variety of social situations. Examples include telephoning in public, meeting strangers, and going to a party.

The LSAS-SR has good internal consistency, convergent and discriminant validity (Fresco et al., 2001; Rytwinski et al., 2009), and is sensitive to treatment change (Baker et al., 2002).

Recent investigations support the continued psychometric soundness of the LSAS (Baroni et al., 2023), and demonstrate its applicability in community mental health samples (Rae et al., 2025), including those with autistic traits and experiences of psychosis (Boulton & Guastella, 2021; Smith et al., 2021).

Secondary Outcome Measures

Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; Beck, 1996)

The 21 items in the BDI-II measure the severity of symptoms that are indicative of depression including pessimism, worthlessness, sadness, and irritability. A comprehensive review of the psychometric properties of the BDI-II across 118 studies concluded that the measure demonstrates high internal consistency, good test-retest reliability, strong validity, and an excellent ability to discriminate between depressed and non-depressed individuals (Wang & Gorenstein, 2013). Thus, it has broad applicability in both research and clinical settings. More recent research has provided further evidence for the psychometric properties of the BDI (Lee & Park, 2022), with studies also supporting its use in clinical samples, including individuals with autistic traits and young adults/adolescents receiving mental health care (Keller et al., 2020; Rae et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2021).

PTSD Check List -Civilian Version (PCL-C; Blanchard et al., 1996)

The PTSD checklist measures the frequency and severity of symptoms relating to PTSD. The scale includes 17 items that require participants to rate the extent to which they are bothered by trauma-related issues and problems in the last month, ranging from "not at all" to "extremely." These problems include hypervigilance, emotional numbness, intrusive thoughts, nightmares or images, difficulty sleeping, and angry outbursts. A review of the psychometric properties of the PCL-C across diverse populations concluded that the measure demonstrates acceptable to strong reliability, with good internal consistency across military, clinical, and community samples, and acceptable test-retest reliability (Wilkins et al., 2011), supporting its use as a measure of PTSD symptom severity. More recent investigations report excellent psychometric properties of the PCL-C in young adults from diverse cultural and clinical backgrounds (El Khoury et al., 2026; Rae et al., 2025; Youngren et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). While the PCL-C was selected based on its use in the evaluation of the intervention by Rae et al. (2025), a more recent version, the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers et al., 2013) is available and may be considered in future research.

The Multi-Dimensional Self-Esteem Inventory Scale (MSEI; O'Brien & Epstein, 1988).

This 61-item scale uses a 5-point Likert scale and measures specific components of self-esteem. Five subscales will be used including competence, personal power, self-control, likeability, and global self-esteem. These scales measure how capable and assertive an individual feels, how in-control of their behaviours, their perceived likability by their peers and the degree to which they pleased with themselves. Internal consistency of the MSEI subscales is reportedly high (Lysaker et al., 2008; Rae et al., 2025).

Treatment

The Nature of Role-Playing Games

Each group program is one continuous story that takes place over 8 weeks using the 5 th Edition of Dungeons and Dragons (Crawford et al., 2014). Dungeons and Dragons is owned by 'Wizards of the Coast' and represents a system of rules and mechanics that allow a game to be played (without providing the content of a game). There are two main options for playing Dungeons and Dragons, the first is to purchase a commercially available published campaign and the second is to create an original or "home brew" game. The intervention used in this study represents the latter, using an original storyline with some concepts and themes that have been drawn from popular culture and science fiction. The primary facilitator (Dungeon Master) is responsible for setting the scene, role-playing the non-player characters that will interact with the participant's characters, and provide the rules and structure of the game. The secondary facilitator assists the Dungeon Master with monitoring and managing the group dynamics as well as assisting the participants within the game.

The secondary facilitator plays the game alongside the participants, using their own character. From this vantage point, they can assist the participants with the challenges that they are facing, in a subtle way that won't break the immersion. One example would be if the participants are asked what they would like to do in a given situation and are undecided. The secondary facilitator will be able to use their character to make suggestions of things that might be helpful.

The Storyline

The intervention's original storyline was developed through a series of brainstorming sessions involving mental health clinicians, peer workers, and individuals with lived experience of mental health challenges. The story begins with a call for aid from the Kingdom of Waterkeep, which faces a dire threat. An evil sorcerer has unleashed a magical plague upon one of its cities-a curse that manifests with symptoms mirroring that of depression. To save the kingdom, a group of adventurers is enlisted to locate four enchanted crystals. Each crystal represents a mental health theme; trauma, anxiety, depression, and social isolation or exclusion, and the participants battle a monster that corresponds to that theme. For example, a wraith represents depression, and uses powers such as motivation drain, hopelessness, slowed motor functions, and a cognitive fog. By defeating each monster and answering psychoeducation questions, the players can activate each crystal. Only by activating and uniting all four crystals can the curse be broken and the evil sorcerer defeated in a final showdown.

Throughout the campaign, adventurers will encounter a variety of non-player characters (NPCs) designed with distinct personality traits and communication styles. These include traits such as perfectionism, optimism, arrogance, low emotional intelligence, friendliness, and abruptness. These personalities were intentionally selected to represent diverse interpersonal interaction styles, thereby providing opportunities for participants to practise social communication skills. The perfectionist character was additionally designed to facilitate self-reflection on participants' own perfectionistic traits, where relevant.

Delivery of Intervention

Each group will consist of up to six participants, a primary facilitator, and a cofacilitator. Groups will commence once a minimum of four participants have enrolled. The two-hour sessions will be delivered primarily in person, with occasional online attendance available for participants who are unable to attend face-to-face. Groups will typically be conducted once per week, with multiple groups scheduled consecutively rather than running simultaneously. Once a group has commenced, it will continue through to completion provided at least one participant remains enrolled. If participant attrition results in a group falling below four participants after commencement, the group will continue where feasible rather than being discontinued.

The Intervention Manual

The therapeutically applied Dungeons & Dragons program used in this study was intentionally designed to incorporate elements of Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT). A generic, transdiagnostic CBT protocol was developed to reflect the complex presentations of participants in community mental health services. The intervention incorporates evidencebased CBT strategies from multiple protocols, with the strongest emphasis on social anxiety. These strategies include psychoeducation, repeated in-session exposure to social interactions, and the identification and restructuring of unhelpful cognitions (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2013). Social skills training, problem solving, and collaborative teamwork are also present throughout the intervention. This approach provides facilitators with the flexibility to address diverse clinical needs while maintaining a coherent therapeutic framework. The intervention manual can be accessed on the Open Science Framework (Rae & Jennings, 2026).

Given the flexible and dynamic nature of role-playing games, there is no fixed sequence in which CBT skills are practiced during the 8-week program. However, all key skills are introduced to participants at various points throughout the sessions, often reinforced multiple times. Previous evaluation of the intervention demonstrated reductions in social anxiety symptoms irrespective of the order in which the therapeutic skills were introduced (Rae et al., 2025).

Due to the interactive nature of role-playing games, each intervention varies according to participants' decisions and gameplay. However, all groups are required to complete the core therapeutic content, which includes four mandatory modules addressing (1) depression, (2) trauma, (3) anxiety, and (4) social inclusion and identity concerns. In addition, all groups participate in structured social encounters and a final encounter with the primary antagonist. The treatment manual also includes a series of optional encounters, which the group facilitator may incorporate at their discretion based on participant engagement, group progress, and available session time. Where appropriate, the Dungeon Master integrates participants' character backstories into optional side quests to enhance engagement and personalise the therapeutic experience.

Board Game Control Group

Participants in the board game control group will be asked to meet at a regular weekly time over the course of 8 weeks, for 2 hours in duration. This will mimic the structure of the Dungeons and Dragons intervention group. The sessions will be run by the PI, with the help of a co-facilitator. During the first session, expectations will be discussed including group safety and boundaries. For example, participants are informed about what to expect from the group, including the procedures that will be followed if a participant becomes distressed.

They are also invited to identify factors that would help create a safe and supportive environment conducive to full participation. The role of the facilitators is to ensure the safety of the participants, while encouraging the use of the board games. The participants will be provided with a variety of commercially available, non-role play games to play for the duration of each session. Data Analysis

Data Analysis Plan

All data collected will be stored securely and de-identified to ensure confidentiality. A linear mixed-model analysis will be used to examine significant changes in anxiety scores over time as a function of intervention/control group assignment. Individual participants (first level analysis) will be nested in weekly participation groups (second level analysis). Variables at the first level will include age, gender, time (pre vs.post), intervention group (intervention vs. control) and the interaction between time and intervention group. The nesting of participants within specific weekly participation groups will be specified through a random effect for the participant group-level structure (Level 2). Where a significant interaction is found, the magnitude of change (pre vs. post) will be compared between groups and contextualised with reference to minimal important difference. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to help identify and determine the effect of potential confounders (such as primary mental health diagnosis or medication use). Analyses will be repeated to examine the effects of intervention/control group assignment on depression scores, PTSD symptoms and selfesteem over time.

Population Being Analysed

Participants that complete at least five out of eight sessions and complete both pre and post outcome measures will be included in the data analysis. A minimum attendance threshold of five of the eight sessions was established, as participants missing three or more sessions would not receive over one-third of the intervention content, potentially reducing treatment effectiveness. Missing data will not be imputed. Reasons for drop out will be documented (where possible), to inform future interventions. Participants who drop out after randomisation but before the intervention begins, will be counted separately to those who drop out after the intervention begins. Drop-out rates (proportion) will be compared between groups using Chi Squared test.

Post Intervention Satisfaction Questionnaire

Results of the post group evaluation questionnaire will be examined to identify trends in responses. This will provide important contextual information that can help in interpret the results and may inform future versions of the intervention program.

12-month Follow-Up

A paired-samples t-test will be used to compare the immediate post-intervention outcome measures to the 12-month follow-up to determine whether improvements made in the intervention group are maintained 12 months after its completion. Bayesian t-tests will be used to quantify the strength of evidence for the null vs. alternative hypothesis.

Power Analysis

Seventy-two participants will be recruited with an expected drop-out rate of 15% based on the drop-out rate recorded for the previous wait-list trial for the same intervention (17%; Rae et al., 2025). A power analysis conducted in G*Power (11) suggests that a total of 60 participants (30 in each group) with alpha of 0.05 gives 92% power to detect an effect size of f = 0.2 for the critical interaction between group (intervention/wait-list) and time point (pre/post intervention), for both primary outcome measures. Within-subject correlation of 0.6 and effect size of f = 0.2 are both estimated on the basis of the previous study utilising this intervention (Rae et al., 2025).

Conclusion

TA-RPG's are increasing in popularity as a potential treatment for symptoms of depression and anxiety in mental health settings. A recent study identified that a CBTinfused, TA-RPG can reduce self-report symptoms of social anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem in a sample of adult consumers in community mental health services in Sydney Australia (Rae et al., 2025). The primary aim of the current study is to replicate the findings using a more robust experimental design, including random group assignment and a larger sample size. It is anticipated that this study will contribute to the current evidence base of TA-RPG's for adults with complex mental health issues.

It is hoped that by publishing this protocol, providing a transparent methodology, and the details of our TA-RPG intervention, other researchers in this field will be able to replicate our study design, advance it, or provide criticism in good faith.
