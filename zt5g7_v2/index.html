Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Introduction: Increasing usage of novel nicotine products, including e-cigarettes/vapes and oral nicotine pouches risks proliferating long-term nicotine addiction and adverse health consequences among a new generation. Public health responses are evolving, but need to be informed by a deep understanding of complex and emerging behaviours. While previous research has explored patterns of vaping and nicotine use, less is known about their perceived functions and social meanings. The present study sought to explore attitudes and perceptions of vaping and nicotine pouch use, and the perceived functions that they have among users.

Methods: This study employed an exploratory, qualitative design, adopting an epistemological stance of social constructionism. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with users of vapes and nicotine pouches aged ≥16 years in Ireland. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis. An analytic structure with five themes was generated.

Results: Participants described vaping as a means of managing stress and anxiety, framing vaping particularly as a coping strategy. Nicotine more broadly was constructed as a lifestyle or wellness supplement which supported concentration and productivity. Despite widespread perceived normalisation, participants described feeling ashamed of being a nicotine user.

Conclusion: These findings show that participants continue to vape and use nicotine pouches not only because of dependency, but also due to perceived emotional, cognitive and lifestyle functions attributed to nicotine. Prevention and cessation efforts are therefore likely to benefit from addressing the perceived functions of vaping and nicotine pouch use, whilst challenging misconceptions surrounding the benefits of nicotine and avoiding stigma associated with nicotine dependency.

Statement of contribution

What is already known on this subject?

• Vaping (the use of e-cigarettes), while less harmful than smoking, is not risk-free and may lead to long-term nicotine dependence among a new generation of users.

• Vaping devices and novel nicotine products, such as oral nicotine pouches, are increasingly marketed for purposes beyond smoking cessation, using claims that they can enhance performance, concentration, or productivity.

• Vaping and nicotine pouch use are often perceived as socially acceptance. While previous research has identified features of these products that are viewed as appealing, less is known about the perceived functions of these products that may encourage continued use.

What does this study add?

• Vapes and nicotine pouches are constructed as a stress management and performance aid by users.

• Perceived normalisation of vaping and pouch use can coexist with shame about dependence.

• Vape and pouch users report concerns about nicotine intake and dependence, and difficulty controlling their use.

1. Introduction

The emergence of vaping and oral nicotine pouches is disrupting traditional approaches to tobacco control, presenting complex challenges, and demanding well-informed, new responses. Approximately 6.5% of people aged 15-24 in Europe say that they vape regularly, according to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD/European Commission, 2024). In some European countries, this same figure exceeds 25%, and exceeds 10% among all adults aged 15 and over. Ireland is a recognised leader in tobacco control, however, smoking prevalence reductions have stalled since 2019 (Brennan et al., 2025), and a tobacco endgame policy goal in 2025 remains undelivered (Cosgrave et al., 2024). Like many other countries, such as New Zealand (Jagroop-Dearing et al., 2025) and the Netherlands (Raad et al., 2025), it is struggling to keep pace with the challenge of vaping and nicotine consumption (Gannon et al., 2026). Nicotine consumption is characterised by an increasingly complex set of patterns of use, including exclusive vape users, dual-users (vaping in tandem with smoking regular tobacco), and increasingly, those who are interchanging vape devices with novel products such as nicotine pouches.

Vaping devices were initially positioned as a smoking cessation aid, with evidence that vaping is more effective than nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) for continuous smoking abstinence long-term (Li et al., 2022; Lindson et al., 2025; Wagener et al., 2026).

However, there are several areas of concern, such as real-world usage of vape devices outside the context of clinical trials, where they are usually combined with behavioural support, their status as a commercial product rather than licensed medicine, and wider concerns regarding 'health-washing' in the context of escalating youth use (Hawkins & van Schalkwyk, 2025).

This means that there are different views on whether vaping should be seen as part of the public health response to tobacco use, as summarised in a recent European Union report (Perez Cornago et al., 2026). Regardless of its intended use, and while there is reduced toxicant exposure compared to combustible tobacco, vaping has also been associated with disease (Glantz et al., 2024), and exposure to harmful chemicals and heavy metals which may cause inflammation and other respiratory problems (Granata et al., 2024). In terms of psychological wellbeing, vaping can lead to nicotine dependence, resulting in withdrawal symptoms such as anxiety, irritability, and sleep problems (Simpson et al., 2021). Some vape users may be inclined to use more potent and direct forms of nicotine delivery (e.g. nicotine pouches), especially as tolerance and dependence levels increase (Gravely et al., 2025). At national level, 1% of adults in Ireland use nicotine pouches daily, and 10% have tried them (Department of Health, 2025). While they are not inhalant-based products, pouches may be damaging to oral health (Travis et al., 2025), and cause an increase in heart rate and blood pressure, given their higher nicotine content (Mallock-Ohnesorg et al., 2024).

Vaping has become increasingly popular, partly through the marketing of products towards young people, both in-store, using attractive packaging, and online, via targeted social media campaigns (Evans et al., 2026), whereby the tobacco industry reuse historical and effective marketing tactics that were once used to promote tobacco smoking (Lovato et al., 1996). As summarised in the recent World Health Organization report on the scientific basis of tobacco product regulation, most countries are developing responses that have struggled to keep pace with the market (World Health Organization, 2025) Nationally, an age restriction on the sale of vapes has been enacted (2023), a legislation has recently been finalised to ban the sale of single use vapes (Department of Health, 2026), and further legislation is in preparation which will address the marketing of vaping (Houses of the Oireachtas, 2026). These regulatory responses are being complemented by media and schoolbased campaigns to build knowledge and skills to prevent vaping and nicotine initiation (Health Service Executive, 2026). Many vape users who have developed an addiction to nicotine find it difficult to obtain dedicated supports (Coyne et al., in press), and smoking cessation providers in turn struggle to provide care to vape users whose motivations and needs may differ vastly.

Given the adverse effects of vaping and nicotine pouches, it is important to understand users' motivations for initiating and continuing to use these products. Some qualitative studies have explored reasons for vaping beyond smoking cessation, mostly among young adults and adolescents (Hanafin & Clancy, 2020; Harrell et al., 2019).

Motivations among young people have largely been attributed to product characteristics, including the appeal of flavours and relative discretion compared to smoking (Lindpere et al., 2023). Vaping is also viewed as socially acceptable by young people, who report being drawn to vaping through their friendship groups and social media (Dowrick et al., 2025). Far less is known about attitudes towards nicotine pouches, beyond findings that their use is motivated by curiosity and experimentation (MacGregor et al., 2025), and by the appeal of their concealability compared to inhalant-based products (Fitton et al., 2026). It remains unclear how these products are understood to affect the health and wellbeing of those who use them, or what perceived benefits exist. This is important to understand amidst attempts to market nicotine pouches as offering performance-enhancement effects (Borowiecki et al., 2026). The research to date has also largely focused on young people and adolescents, despite the fact that many adults also vape and potentially have different motivations for doing so. The perceived roles that these products play in their lives has largely not been captured by existing literature. Doing so is important and will have implications for designing evidencebased public health interventions; perceptions of vaping and nicotine pouches will influence continued use and willingness to seek support.

Several previous qualitative studies have identified reasons people vape, such as appealing product characteristics, or facilitating environmental conditions. However, there remains a limited understanding the perceived functions of vaping and using nicotine pouches, how users understand their relationship to these nicotine-containing products, and how these subjective evaluations motivate continued use. Understanding how vaping and nicotine pouch users perceive their use of these products is important, as such perceptions shape behaviour, willingness to quit (or seek support to quit), and response to public health interventions. To address these gaps in knowledge, using a reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) approach, this qualitative study sought to address the following research question: What are vape and nicotine pouch users' perspectives on their use of these products?

2. Materials & Methods

The reporting for this study is presented in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) (O'Brien et al., 2014).

2.1 Qualitative approach and research paradigm

This study employed an exploratory, qualitative design using a series of one-to-one semi-structured interviews. The epistemological stance taken in this study was one of social constructionism, which posits that reality is understood through language, with knowledge generated through a historical and cultural lens (Burr & Dick, 2017).

2.2 Sampling strategy and study context

A total of 24 participants were recruited. Individuals aged ≥16 years, having a reasonable command of the English language, residing within the Republic of Ireland, and using either vaping or non-combustible nicotine products (including nicotine pouches) were eligible to participate. Participants could self-define as either a daily or occasional user of either type of product. Recruitment was primarily conducted via relevant social media channels, and flyers distributed within the local community, and via a Students' Union newsletter. The decision to stop data collection was guided by the concept of information power (Malterud et al., 2016), whereby sample size is determined by the quality and richness of the dataset, the analytic approach, and the overall study aims. Therefore, after 24 interviews with participants from a range of backgrounds in terms of age, gender and product usage patterns, the dataset was deemed to contain a rich, diverse set of articulations relevant to the central research question (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Questions centred on participants' initiation into vaping and/or nicotine product use, perceptions and attitudes, social and cultural influences, risk perceptions and health beliefs, and their thoughts on becoming nicotine-free. Prior to the interviews, each participant received a participant information leaflet by email. Written informed consent was also obtained and participants completed a basic demographic questionnaire and patterns of vaping, smoking, and nicotine product use. All interviews lasted between 30-45 minutes and were audio-recorded using Microsoft Teams to generate written transcripts for data analysis.

2.3 Data collection methods

Transcripts were subsequently edited in consultation with the recordings to ensure accuracy, pseudonymisation, removal of potentially identifiable information, and correct formatting of transcripts. Transcripts were imported to NVIVO 15 for analysis.

2.4 Data analysis

We followed the recursive, six-stage RTA approach to analyse the interview data.

Given the exploratory aim of this study, RTA was deemed the appropriate method of analysis as it facilitated interrogating the dataset in a manner whereby both latent and semantic observations could be made (Rowland & Conolly, 2024). The initial phase of familiarisation began with cross-checking recorded transcripts against interview recordings, and by making initial observations. The second phase of data coding involved identifying patterns of meaning across the dataset that were both salient and relevant to the central research question. Data was coded over two rounds; the first generating initial inductive codes, and the second refining these by separating out broader codes into multiple, or by combining repetitive or overlapping codes. The inductive codes were then clustered, with the aim of identifying clusters which together possessed a 'central organising concept', or a through-line among clusters of codes. Phase three (initial theme generation) involved forming these clusters, and evaluating initial, candidate themes. In phases four and five, the research team and a public and patient involvement (PPI) representative held a theme development and review meeting, in which co-authors and PPI acted as 'critical friends' (Smith & McGannon, 2018). Trustworthiness and credibility were enhanced through the development of an audit trail to record analytic decisions, using the 'project mapping' feature within NVIVO, as well as the data visualisation toolkit LucidChart. The lead author also engaged in reflexivity by keeping a reflexive journal, documenting how assumptions evolved, and acknowledging how the researcher's background influenced interpretations. This reflexivity statement can be found in Supplementary Material A.

3.1 Sample characteristics

The sample of 24 participants comprised of 14 males, nine females, and one non-binary person. Two participants were aged 16-17, eight were aged 18-24, 11 were aged 25-34, and the remaining three were aged 35-44 years. Almost all (22/24) participants vaped (with the remaining 2 being daily nicotine pouch users); 14 were daily vape users, and 8 reported vaping occasionally. Of the nine participants who reported being a non-combustible nicotine product user, all nine reported using nicotine pouches (two daily, and seven occasionally), with three of these participants also using snus (a moist, smokeless tobacco product), and one also using heated tobacco. Of the 24 participants, 16 were dual-users (i.e., both vaped and smoked regular tobacco), with two participants being daily smokers, and 14 being occasional smokers.

3.2 Overview of findings

Overall, the findings showed that participants frequently construed vaping as a means of coping with everyday stress and anxiety. Nicotine consumption was framed as a method of boosting concentration and productivity, and thus was understood as helpful more than harmful to one's health. By contrast, widespread internalised shame and self-stigma, coupled with a shared view among participants that vaping is inherently undesirable or uncool, exacerbated their negative self-perception of their own vaping or pouch use. Participants found it difficult to envision themselves becoming nicotine-free due to perceived widespread normalisation of these products. Participants engaged in a variety of dissociation tactics to resolve this inner tension, such as deliberately avoiding thinking about long-term health implications. This overall narrative is elaborated upon through five analytic themes (see Figure 1).

4.1 Summary and implications of findings

In the context of the emerging challenge of informing the best regulatory and public health response to vaping and nicotine product use among young people, this qualitative study explored how young people perceive their use of vapes and nicotine pouches. Participants rarely described their use of these products as solely a recreational activity; rather, vaping and nicotine pouches were framed as coping strategies for managing stress and anxiety, and as a supplement for supporting productivity and performance within their daily lives. Widespread normalisation and ubiquity of the behaviour of vaping was contrasted with embarrassment and shame for being a vape user. Together, our findings suggest that vaping and nicotine pouch use is characterised by a disconnect between perceived importance for psychological functioning, and perceived addiction, within a social environment where the behaviour has become normalised. These findings have several implications for research, clinical practice, and health promotion.

The subtheme Wellness-ification of nicotine characterised participants' perception of nicotine as a productivity stimulus or lifestyle supplement, particularly those using nicotine pouches. These products were repeatedly framed by participants as something which could be used to obtain peak daily performance. There is evidence that the tobacco industry is deliberately cultivating such an image, in an attempt to market products as beneficial (a tactic that is not new; (Lovato et al., 1996). This promotional activity does appear to be influencing perceptions. For example, participants in (Watts et al., 2024) identified 'claimed sport performance enhancement' as one reason for a perceived increase in the use and popularity of nicotine pouches. A recent report identified several strategies employed via social media to market pouches using wellness and fitness influencers, such as claiming that taking nicotine pouches pre-workout enables the user to 'train like athlete(s)' (Borowiecki et al., 2026). A systematic review, however, concluded that nicotine pouches do not enhance physical performance and may impair cardiovascular readiness, anaerobic activity, and endurance (Alansi et al., 2026). Reported positive effects, the authors noted, are likely due to relief from withdrawal symptoms. When considered alongside the present study's findings, which indicate a similar belief regarding cognitive performance, this demonstrates a need for public health-led vaping and nicotine prevention and cessation interventions that address misconceptions around nicotine. For instance, reframing the 'boost' obtained from nicotine as a symptom of dependence can target misconceptions without telling the individual that their subjective experience is wrong. Future research is needed to evaluate whether such reframing changes the underlying belief, rather than simply increasing awareness.

The theme Unconscious dependence and subtheme Coping through nicotine both captured different aspects of nicotine dependence articulated by participants in this study.

Vape users in this study reported engaging in a range of dependency-related behaviours, such as relying on vaping to manage anxiety and stress. Frequent and repetitive vaping was framed in terms of sensation-seeking and stimulation, rather than addiction. Previous qualitative findings from dual-users also indicated that users tended to minimise their dependence, perceiving it as not solely due to nicotine, but also influenced by behavioural aspects (Loud et al., 2022). Another interesting point is that participants viewed nicotine less as something pleasurable, and more as a necessary coping mechanism. This is a distinction which has implications for prevention and cessation programmes. For example, someone who believes vaping to be an essential coping strategy may not respond well to an intervention which focuses only on health risks. Cognitive-behavioural approaches, which can help users to identify triggers and manage negative affect (e.g., (Webb et al., 2023), are more likely to help them form sustainable coping strategies.

The seemingly paradoxical nature of normalisation and stigma was captured across the themes Dissonance and (temporal) dissociation. On the one hand, vaping is perceived as ubiquitous, socially acceptable, and indoor use occurs almost everywhere. On the other hand, participants reported hiding their vapes and nicotine pouch use from others, feeling embarrassed, and framed their dependence negatively, as presented within the theme It's a dirty habit -this apparent contradiction aligns with theories of stigma. (Link & Phelan, 2001) describe how stigma attaches to a labelled and socially devaluated identity (e.g., "addict", "nicotine dependent", etc), rather than to the behaviour itself. Thus, although vaping may have become normalised and socially acceptable, the perceived identity of being nicotine dependent may remain stigmatised. In other words, normalisation of the behaviour does not translate into an acceptance of nicotine dependence. There is also previous evidence that vape users have expressed self-stigmatisation towards their dependency. A previous qualitative study identified 'emerging regret and protectionism' among their participants, who reported feeling embarrassed to be addicted to vaping, and wanted to shield vaping from younger youths (Graham-DeMello et al., 2024). This interpretation has several implications for public health-led prevention and cessation responses. For example, services should avoid inadvertently stigmatising people who vape when offering support by using non-stigmatising language and by framing dependence as a bio-behavioural pattern, rather than a personal failing. It is also important to explore users' perceived functions of vaping (e.g., stress management, concentration).

4.2 Strengths and limitations

Despite being among the first studies to explore vaping and nicotine pouch users' self-perceptions of their product use, using a RTA approach which allowed for rich exploration of how participants make sense of their use, and the inclusion of multiple nicotine products which enabled the findings to be interpreted within an overall nicotine dependency context, there are still limitations to be considered. Firstly, only a minority of participants were over the age of 34. While vaping and nicotine pouch use is considerably less common in Ireland among adults aged 34 and older (Department of Health, 2024), this limitation likely influenced the data collected in several ways. For instance, the idea that nicotine is a 'wellness' or performance-enhancing product may be particularly relevant to younger adults, whereas older participants, who may be more likely to have switched from smoking to vaping, may conceptualise these products primarily as a harm reduction tool (Brennan et al., 2025). The findings may therefore have limited transferability to populations with different demographic characteristics and nicotine use histories. Recruitment was also self-selected, meaning that those who decided to participate in this study likely had greater awareness of their nicotine consumption, and therefore greater opportunity to reflect on their behaviour. This is especially relevant to expressions which framed vaping as an unconscious or habitual behaviour. Future research should explore the extent to which this automaticity is perceived within the wider population. Thirdly, while this study examined both vaping and nicotine pouches, these products differ considerably in terms of their sensory experience, visibility, and method of nicotine delivery. Certain findings, such as expressions regarding perceived normalisation, may therefore manifest differently across the two types of products. However, this study was designed to capture perceptions about vapes and nicotine pouches in an exploratory manner, rather than to systematically compare the two. This would be a worthwhile avenue for further research to pursue, in the event that targeted health promotion campaigns are needed.

5. Conclusion

Vaping and nicotine product use is a complex and emerging public health challenge, and rich evidence concerning behavioural patterns is needed to inform prevention and cessation programmes to complement the evolving regulatory response. This qualitative study has highlighted some of the contemporary meanings attached to newer forms of nicotine use. Vaping and nicotine pouches were frequently discussed as serving important, embedded daily functions, including managing stress and anxiety, and enhancing productivity. At the same time, product use was frequently described as largely automatic and difficult to control, with feelings of shame and embarrassment attached to the identity of being a nicotine user, despite the behaviour being socially normalised. These findings suggest a complex interplay of psychosocial factors which have important implications for encouraging users to quit, and how cessation is supported. Public health responses may benefit from moving beyond solely health consequences to address perceived functions that are sustaining use. Vaping and nicotine pouch prevention and cessation supports should take care to avoid perpetuating self-stigmatisation.
