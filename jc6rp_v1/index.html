No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Full text

Abstract

Little is known about the dynamics of children's conversations outside of urban Western contexts, where research focuses on one-on-one interactions with parents. Our study addresses this gap, aggregating naturalistic speech data gathered in 10 countries across multiple labs, and using novel speech diarization algorithms to extract and classify different types of conversation. We analyzed 23,000 hours of child-centered audio recordings taken from 844 children (mean age 23 months; 389 girls) across urban, agricultural, and foraging societies. We found that multiparty conversations, involving at least three speakers, are more frequent than any other type of conversation. Across settings, children's relative exposure to conversation differed based on their age, number of siblings, and mother's education. We conclude that the kinds of conversation most often studied differ from those children typically encounter. Greater focus on children's conversations in multiparty contexts may provide a more representative picture of their daily experience with language.

Multiparty conversation predominates in children's language environments across diverse cultural settings

Introduction

Conversation is a cornerstone of human social interaction, with considerable evidence suggesting that human cognition is specifically geared toward back-and-forth linguistic exchanges (Levinson, 2016). Home-based observational studies have found that more parent-child conversation is positively associated with children's language development (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015). Recently, it has also become possible to analyze longer and more naturalistic samples of conversation using speaker diarization algorithms, which can process long-form audio from recording devices worn by children throughout a day (e.g., LENA: Gilkerson et al., 2017; Voice Type Classifier: Lavechin et al., 2020). These systems can track conversational turns, or alternating utterances between children and other speakers that occur in close temporal proximity, which are also positively associated with the pace of children's language development (Zimmerman et al., 2009; Romeo et al., 2018; Ferjan Ramírez et al., 2020; Donnelly & Kidd, 2021).

Most of the language development literature focuses on children's dyadic interactions with adult caregivers (e.g., Coffey & Snedeker, 2026), the frequency of which is sensitive to both individual-and household-level factors. Children from households with relatively more siblings hear less speech and engage in fewer dyadic activities with adults (Gurgand et al., 2023; Laing & Bergelson, 2024). Household education has also been positively associated with the amount of speech children hear from adults (Piot et al., 2022). Older children also more actively contribute to conversations as they grow older (Gilkerson et al., 2017), and girls have also sometimes been found to elicit more conversation from their adult caregivers than boys, which may persist cross-culturally and partially mediate the relationship between sex and language ability (VanDam et al., 2022; Gurgand et al., 2025; cf. Bergelson et al., 2023).

Little research has used long-form audio analysis to study children's back-and-forth conversations with other speakers or with multiple partners (e.g., Ramírez-Esparza et al., 2017). This is a potentially serious gap, as children's language is also impacted by who and how many people they are talking with. For example, children have been found to produce more mental state vocabulary (i.e., referring to their own or somebody else's thoughts, as in I changed my mind) during conversations with friends and peers than with their mothers (Brown et al., 1996). Children have also been reported to produce more speech during triadic interactions with their mother and an older sibling than during dyadic interactions with the mother alone (Barton & Tomasello, 1991; Rabain-Jamin, 2001). These types of interactions also expose children to a wider and richer vocabulary (Oshima-Takane & Robbins, 2003) and may help them attain conversational skills earlier in life (Hoff-Ginsberg, 1998).

It is also difficult to determine whether these findings are specific to households in the U.S. and Europe, where most studies of language acquisition take place (Kidd & Garcia, 2022). Children in urban, market-integrated societies have been found to engage more often in dyadic interactions with adult caregivers compared to children from rural, subsistence-based societies, who are exposed to relatively more overheard speech, more speech from peers, and more speech from multiple conversational partners (Schieffelin & Ochs, 1986). These arguments have been corroborated by studies that quantify children's linguistic input across diverse cultural and socioeconomic settings (Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Cristia et al., 2019; Loukatou et al., 2022). There are a number of possible explanations for these differences. For instance, households in less urbanized societies tend to have more children. Children with older siblings are also exposed to less child-directed speech from adults and more overheard speech directed to another child, compared to children without any siblings (Galindo & Montag, 2025). At the same time, the presence of other children in the home may increase the incidence of conversations with other children (Kondaurova et al., 2023). These factors and their interactions might also account for varied patterns of conversation observed across different cultural settings.

Living in a society with fewer children per household may result in more exposure to speech from adults over time (as in Gilkerson et al., 2017), while living in a society with more siblings per home may result in less speech from adults but more with other children (as in Cristia et al., 2023). There may also be more complex interactions between variables in these settings. For example, household education has been negatively associated with the amount of adult-child conversation in studies of recently urbanized families (Ma et al., 2024).

Some recent studies have attempted to address these shortcomings by leveraging long-form audio corpora composed of daylong recordings from global samples of children.

In a large-scale study of child-centered adult speech in 12 countries, Bergelson et al. (2023) found that neither child age, gender, nor household education consistently predicted differences in speech input. In another study, Bunce et al. (2024) compared child-directed and adult-directed speech in randomly sampled clips across five countries. They found that children were exposed to more total speech during periods when more speakers were present, and that overall adult-directed speech, but not child-directed speech, increased with child age. However, they did find that child-directed speech from other children increased relative to female adult speech with age.

By analyzing long-form audio data with a novel system for automatically classifying conversation, we gain a unique glimpse into children's daily verbal experiences, beyond their dyadic interactions with caregivers. In this paper, we examine a massive cross-national dataset assembled as a part of a data-sharing initiative between a global consortium of researchers (EL1000 Consortium, 2026). The diversity of home environments and caregiving practices represented by this dataset captures not only dyadic adult-child conversations, but also other, less explored types of interactions: child-child, multiparty, and overheard conversations. We utilize speaker diarization algorithms to automatically classify different types of conversation in everyday speech, allowing us to analyze far more (https://doi.org/10.12751/g-node.ky76mk).

Methods

Data

Our analysis includes data from 13 corpora obtained through the EL1000 consortium, a multi-lab initiative to compile data in children's daily speech exposure from around the world (EL1000 Consortium, 2026) (Figure 1). Among these corpora, 6 were supersets of data included in Bunce et al. (2024) (Bergelson, Lucid, Rossel Island, Tseltal, Warlaumont, and Winnipeg) and 6 had at least some overlap with corpora studied in Bergelson et al. (2023) (Bergelson, Cougar, Lucid, Tsimane, Warlaumont, and Winnipeg). A full description of each corpus included in our analysis can be found in Supplementary Materials (S1). Each of these studies used child-worn recording devices to collect audio from naturalistic home-based interactions throughout the day. Studies were included based on the availability of information about the target child's age, gender, number of siblings, and maternal education. These variables were selected for their potential impact on conversations in the home, as well as being the most commonly collected variables across corpora. We excluded from the analysis children who had been identified by individual data curators as having atypical development that might have greatly affected their data, such as children with ASD or who are hard of hearing (n = 11 children). In many cases, this was based on caregiver-reported information and may not reflect a diagnosis received after completion of the study. Recordings of target children with missing data were excluded from the analysis (n = 31). To ensure recordings were of sufficient length to calculate hourly rates, recordings less than one hour in length were also excluded (n = 4).

The resulting data are composed of 23087 hours of audio collected from 1820 recordings of children in households across 10 countries, spanning urban market-based societies and rural subsistence societies (Table 1). Children (N = 844; 389 female) ranged from 0 to 85 months old (mean = 23 months, SD = 15). Households varied greatly in size, with children reported as having anywhere between 0 to 8 siblings (mean = 1, SD = 2).

Educational attainment ranged from no formal schooling to professional degrees (mean = NA years, SD = 5) (Table 2; see Figure 2 for visualization).

Procedure

The exact data collection procedure for each study is summarized in Supplementary Materials (S1). In general, researchers placed a small audio recording device in or on the target child's shirt. Depending on the study, the device was either a LENA© audio recording device (Gilkerson et al., 2017), an Olympus WS-832 (Olympus WS-832, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), or a USB audio recording device (Cristia et al., 2023). The device was typically active until the maximum recording duration (e.g., 15 hours in the case of the USBs) was reached or the device ran out of batteries or was picked up again by researchers, but in some cases, the researchers set a different target recording duration (e.g., 10 hrs total between 8am and 7pm for the Warlaumont corpus). During this time, the device recorded ambient audio in the child's vicinity from the target child's perspective.

In some cases, caregivers were able to pause the recorder or request that sections be deleted due to privacy concerns. Each audio recording was analyzed offline using Voice Type Classifier, an open-source speaker diarization software, to identify and diarize speech by different speaker categories (VTC2; Charlot et al., 2025). VTC2 utilizes a machine learning approach called BabyHuBERT to derive speech representations thanks to pre-training on speech extracted from long-form recordings. VTC2 was fine-tuned on human-annotated speech data from numerous languages (including languages as diverse as Taiwanese, Ju|'hoan, and Tsimane') representing at least six language families (Indo-European, Sino-Tibetan, Khoisan, Japonic, Mosetenan, and Austronesian). VTC2 does not identify individual speakers; rather, it attempts to categorize vocalizations into one of four speaker classes: target child (i.e., the child wearing the recording device), female adults, male adults, and other children. Unlike LENA, which treats all cases of overlap between any two classes as a single 'overlap' category, VTC2 allows speaker labels to overlap, providing start and end times for each individual voice type, which means that this overlap does not inflate non-speech estimates. VTC2 has been shown to be more accurate than LENA in diarizing speech across all speaker categories, based on data from urban, English-speaking settings (Charlot et al., 2025).

Measures

Conversation metrics. Conversational turns in each recording were quantified using the ChildProject package in Python (Gautheron et al., 2022). We define four classes of conversation, guided by the previous literature: adult-child (involving the target child and female or male adult(s)), child-child (target child and other child or children), multiparty (target child with an adult and another child, with two adults of different genders, or all speaker categories), and overheard (any conversation without target child).

As VTC2 can only distinguish between speaker categories, and not individual speakers, it was not possible to determine definitively how many speakers are present in a conversation.

For example, an adult-child conversation between the target child and a female adult could have more than one female adult speaker. We return to this limitation and its impact on the interpretation of our results in the Discussion. We also report the results of a follow-up analysis dividing multiparty conversations into adult-only interactions (i.e., target child, female adult, and male adult) and mixed age interactions (i.e., target child, other children, and adult(s)) in Supplementary Materials (S5).

To evaluate the validity of the conversation schema, we asked human annotators to manually classify 297 minutes of conversation in a series of audio clips taken from the Natovich corpus. This corpus contained recordings of two-parent homes with two children, ensuring each possible type of conversation could be represented. We used these annotations to gauge the accuracy of the automatic classifier while varying two parameters: how close in time vocalizations needed to occur to be considered a conversational turn (either 1s or 5s), and how much a speaker needed to contribute to a conversation to be considered a participant for the purposes of classification (between 1% and 20%). We selected the values that maximized the average accuracy across all categories. This resulted in a definition of conversation as any sequence of vocalizations by different participants that occur within 5 seconds of one another and a definition of participant as any speaker category that contributed to at least 6% of the total length of a conversation (Supplementary Materials S3).

Predictors. We compared children's exposure to different forms of conversation to two child-level predictors: child age and sex; and two household-level predictors: number of siblings and maternal education. Prior to formal analysis, we outlined several possible relationships between these factors and children's conversation which we detail in Supplementary Materials (S2). Age was calculated across corpora by comparing the child's date of birth to the date of the recording session. Because some studies reported years of education and others the highest attained education, we developed a schema to convert education to the desired format (e.g., high school degree = 12 years; bachelor's degree = 16 years), which we describe in Supplementary Materials (S3). Educational norms differed greatly across studies: for example, not finishing secondary schooling would be considered a low amount of education in the US-based studies, whereas in other studies having any amount of secondary schooling would be considered highly educated. As a result, we computed mothers' relative education within a study sample. This was done by converting years of reported education to a z-score centered at zero, such that each point represented a standard deviation higher or lower educational attainment within that participant's study. Although our samples did not necessarily represent the full range of education within each society, this approach allowed us to determine whether relative educational attainment related to conversational turns in the same way across corpora. To determine whether highest attained education also predicted children's conversation, we conducted another analysis where education was treated as a categorical variable with three levels: less than secondary education, completed secondary education, and post-secondary education. Because both analyses produced the same results, we report the results of this analysis in Supplementary Materials (S3). The remaining variables were reported by researchers from the individual corpora.

Analysis

We constructed mixed effects Bayesian regression models using the brms package, with conversational turn counts as the dependent variable (Bürkner, 2017). We assumed a negative binomial distribution, which is used to model over-dispersed count data, where the variance is much greater than the mean. This approach has been used in previous studies of language input derived from long-form audio recordings (e.g., Bunce et al., 2024). To account for differences in conversational turn counts due to the length of individual audio recordings, we include an offset term for the log recording duration. We fit a random intercept for corpus, to capture the variance different settings and study designs might have introduced, and, where applicable, a random intercept for child, to capture variance from multiple observations per child within corpora. We selected adult-child conversation as the base comparison case, as it is the most commonly tracked form of conversation in child language studies. Next, we re-examined our predictors across-corpora, constructing a single regression model for each conversation type that included data from all corpora. This analysis was meant to find predictors of conversation that are consistent across societies. In addition, we examined possible predictors of conversation within-corpus, constructing a separate set of models (one for each conversation class) containing each our four predictor variables (child age, sex, number of siblings, and maternal education). This analysis allowed us to examine whether the presence of associations between conversation and the predictor variables differed across societies. We report these results in Supplementary Materials (S6).

Each model was constructed by running four Markov chains for 4000 iterations each.

Model fit was assessed by performing posterior predictive checks. Chain convergence was determined by ensuring all R = 1. Given the large size of our dataset and the novelty of our analysis, we used weakly informative priors recommended by the brms package for negative binomial regression modeling.

Flat priors were used for predictor variables.

We report raw standardized coefficients (i.e., log increases in turns per hour) in our tables, and exponentiated coefficients (i.e., percentage increases in turns per hour) in the text for practical interpretation. We also report the posterior mean and the highest density interval (HDI) at 95% for each coefficient. To determine whether the evidence for an effect was compelling, we computed the posterior probability that the coefficient was greater than or less than 0. Probabilities greater than 95% were considered strong evidence for an effect.

Results

Description of conversational types

The corpora varied greatly in the total amount of conversation identified, from 334.79 turns/hour in Winnipeg (Canada) to 1,134.62 turns/hour in Natovich (Israel) (Figure 3).

We found that children most often engaged in multiparty conversations, roughly 3.08 times as often as they engaged in adult-child conversation measured by turns (ß = 1.12, 95% HDI = [0.70, 1.54], pd > 0.999). Conversely, children were found to engage in 2.31 times as much adult-child conversation as child-child conversation (ß = -0.84, 95% HDI = [-1.58, -0.08], pd = 0.016). No overall differences were found between adult-child and overheard conversation (ß = -0.24, 95% HDI = [-0.89, 0.41], pd = 0.228) (Figure 4).

Predictors of conversation

Results are reported in Table 3 (see Figure 5 for visualization).

Child-level factors. We found strong evidence that child age was associated with turns across different kinds of conversation. Target older children were more likely to engage in child-child conversations (ß = 0.59, 95% HDI = [0.41, 0.79], pd > 0.999) and less likely to be exposed to overheard conversation (ß = -0.35, 95% HDI = [-0.52, -0.19], pd = 0). Converting the log coefficients to probabilities by taking the exponent, we find that each SD increase in age within the sample predicts a 80% increase for child-child turns, and a 29% decrease in overheard turns. No evidence for an association of conversational turns with child gender was found, suggesting no systematic differences in conversation for boys versus girls.

Household-level factors. We found strong evidence that the number of siblings a child had was associated with conversational turns. Children with more siblings were more likely to be exposed to multiparty conversations (ß = 0.09, 95% HDI = [-0.02, 0.21], pd = 0.956), child conversations (ß = 0.44, 95% HDI = [0, 0.88], pd = 0.975), and overheard conversation (ß = 0.09, 95% HDI = [0.02, 0.18], pd = 0.992), and were less likely to engage in adult-child conversation (ß = -0.21, 95% HDI = [-0.34, -0.11], pd = 0.001). Each SD increase in sibship size resulted in a 10% increase in multiparty conversational turns, a 55% increase in child-child turns, a 10% increase in overheard turns, and a 19% decrease for adult-child turns.

Children whose mothers had relatively higher education engaged in less child-child conversation (ß = -0.17, 95% HDI = [-0.39, 0], pd = 0.974) (Table 4). This was equivalent to a 16% decrease in conversational turns. No evidence for an association with other forms of conversation was found.

Modeling interactions between predictors

Children's engagement in child-child conversation was strongly related to almost every predictor. We conducted follow-up analyses of statistical interactions that we hypothesized could be present based on prior research. First, we modeled the interaction between child age and number of siblings. We hypothesized that the positive relationship between child age and child-child conversation would be moderated by the presence of other children in the home. After adding this term to our model, we found an over-additive interaction between the predictors, such that older children were exposed to even more child-child speech if they had more siblings (ß = -0.17, 95% HDI = [-0.38, 0], pd = 0.974).

However, we found little evidence that the interaction term improved the overall predictive accuracy of the model, as measured via Bayesian leave-one-out (LOO) cross validation (ELPD difference = -1.23, SE difference = 3.26).

Next, we modeled the interaction between maternal education and number of siblings. We hypothesized that the negative relationship between maternal education and child-child conversation could be moderated by the negative relationship between maternal education and number of siblings. We found that having more siblings reduced the negative relationship between education and child-child conversation (ß = 0.14, 95% HDI = [0.03, 0.29], pd = 0.966), indicating that the influence of education on child conversation is strongly tied to its relationship to family size. As with our previous analysis, adding this term to the model did not noticeably improve model fit (ELPD difference = 0.14, SE difference = 2.92). Thus, while we found more complex interactions between our predictor variables, we did not find that they substantially improved the fit of our predictive models.

Discussion

Conversations across cultural settings

In our analysis of 844 children from 13 studies, spanning 10 countries, we explored how children's experiences with different forms of conversation varied within and across settings. We found that across these societies as a whole, children much more often engaged in multiparty conversations than they did in dyadic conversations. This is a striking finding given the overwhelming prevalence of research on one-on-one interactions between a mother and a child (Coffey & Snedeker, 2026), and the relatively scarce research on interactions with multiple speakers. Multiparty interactions are potentially a rich source of linguistic input to children, having been found to elicit more speech from young children and exposing them to a wider range of words (Barton & Tomasello, 1991; Rabain-Jamin, 2001; Oshima-Takane & Robbins, 2003). It is also notable that the frequency of multiparty conversations was not specific to rural, non-Western samples, which are more associated with these types of interactions (e.g., Schieffelin & Ochs, 1986). Rather, even in our U.S. and Canadian samples, multiparty conversation was as common or more than adult-child conversation. The consistency of this pattern suggests that multiparty interactions may be equally impactful across cultural settings. Indeed, children's language skills have been directly linked to their exposure to child-directed speech in multiparty situations (Ramírez-Esparza et al., 2017), and in households with multiple adults, children's vocabulary is better predicted by total input than by input from the primary caregiver alone (Shneidman et al., 2013). Overheard and child-child conversation varied greatly in frequency across corpora.

The influence of these forms of speech on children's language development has been widely debated. While there is not much evidence that either of these forms of speech predict individual differences in children's performance on tests of language ability (Coffey & Snedeker, 2026), children have been shown to learn words from overheard speech in both lab-based studies (e.g., Floor & Akhtar, 2006) and naturalistic contexts (Foushee & Srinivasan, 2024). Likewise, children have been found to attend closely to speech produced by other children (Schick et al., 2025), and in some instances learn preferentially from child speech over adult speech (Erskine & Seidl, 2026). In addition, both overheard and child speech have been found to predict differences in children's vocal activity (Schick & Stoll, 2025; Coffey et al., 2026). Both forms of interaction are also related to multiparty conversation; many multiparty interactions may originate as overheard interaction that children interject in or are invited to join. Multiparty interactions may also serve as vehicles for children to engage more closely with both adults and other children simultaneously which may have implications for various other aspects of child development.

Taken together, our results suggest a gap between the contexts in which language acquisition is most frequently studied (dyadic interactions with adults) and those in which children most frequently encounter speech (multiparty interactions with adults and peers).

Consistent predictors of conversation across corpora

In the analysis of child-level predictors, older children were found to engage in more child-child conversation than younger children. Some previous studies have found that children spend more time with other children as they grow older, particularly in smaller-scale rural societies, resulting in more child-directed speech from these sources (Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Cristia et al., 2019; Loukatou et al., 2022). Our findings suggest that this trend also persists when factoring in larger-scale industrialized societies. Older children also encountered less overheard conversation as they grew older. This may be because children's growing language skills allow for greater participation in nearby conversations. There was no evidence for age associations with adult-child conversation, aligning with the results of a recent global study that found no consistent association between child age and adult vocalizations across countries (Bergelson et al., 2023; Bunce et al., 2024).

No effect of child gender was found, suggesting that boys and girls experience similar amounts of conversation across the societies surveyed (similar to Bergelson et al., 2023).

Future research could examine how children's participation in conversation was impacted by differences in their developmental status. A previous study found that children's vocal activity, but not their adult speech input, was negatively associated with atypical development (Bergelson et al., 2023). As a result, we might expect that children at risk of language delay may engage in less conversation in total, but potentially be exposed to more overheard conversation.

In the analysis of household-level predictors, children with more siblings were found to experience more total turns in all types of conversation, except for adult-child conversation, which they experienced less of. Intuitively, children with more siblings would have more opportunities to engage in conversation with other children. There would also be fewer opportunities to engage in one-on-one conversations with adults, but more opportunities for joint multiparty conversations and to observe conversations between adults and other children. Our findings suggest that the presence of children may have a complex association with children's early language environments, changing not only how often children are able to converse with each other, but also the ways in which they typically engage with their adult caregivers. These patterns may also change with the age of other children in their homes. Children with older siblings might find themselves being watched more often by other children than by adults, and children with younger siblings may find themselves acting as babysitters. In either case we would expect more child-child conversation, but in the former scenario the target child would receive more speech input from other children, while in the latter they would serve as sources of speech input for their younger charges. Thus, researchers examining the influence of siblings on children's language exposure should note the age of other children living in the home and if possible whether they have responsibilities that would make them less present in the key child's environment, such as schooling.

Children whose mothers had higher education, both relative to their populations and overall, engaged in less child-child conversation. Although previous studies have found that more highly educated mothers tend to have fewer children, this effect persisted for both definitions of education even after controlling for children's number of siblings. There was also strong evidence of an interaction (albeit one that did not improve model predictiveness), suggesting that education was relatively less related to lower child-child conversation in homes with more siblings. One possible interpretation of these findings is that education changes parent behavior in the presence of other children, such that more educated mothers might converse with their children more often. Depending on the household, this could result in more adult-child, multiparty, or overheard conversation, but would uniformly reduce the amount of child-child conversations across homes. There was also no evidence that education was related to adult-child conversation. Previous studies have found both positive associations (Piot et al., 2022) and negative associations between speech input and education (Ma et al., 2024), and it is likely that the relationship between these variables is contingent on the broader socioeconomic context of the communities to which families belong (e.g., active vs. passive urbanization, see Ju et al., 2025).

Limitations

There are some important limitations to note in our analysis. First, the classification of conversation is a difficult problem in speaker diarization, as traditional definitions of conversation rely on information that these algorithms do not have access to (e.g., what is being said, who is the addressee). Even for conversational turns, which only rely on the temporal co-occurrence of speech, accuracy estimates are generally lower than those for raw vocalizations (Ferjan Ramírez et al., 2021). While we were able to approximate the accuracy of classification for different types of conversation, this estimation was limited to speech samples from one corpus with an unusually uniform household structure (i.e., each speaker category was present and each had only one representative). We do not know the degree to which the thresholds we selected for conversational turn latency and participation vary cross-culturally. As a result, the values that maximized accuracy in this corpus might not do so in other corpora. Even if these values were consistent across corpora, any amount of classification inaccuracy could skew the results of the analysis. In light of this, our findings should be interpreted judiciously, particularly for overheard conversation, which was found to have the lowest overall accuracy (Supplementary Materials S3).

Second, the multiparty speech category, as we have defined it, is fundamentally limited by the use of a voice type classifier, which can only segment audio by speaker category (e.g., female or male adult), as opposed to a speaker diarizer which segments audio by individual speakers (e.g., mother and grandmother). As an example, what looks like a conversation between the target child and a female adult may actually involve multiple female adults that are not differentiated by the classifier. In societies where children receive large amounts of care from several female adult relatives, this constraint would result in the underestimation of multiparty conversation.

Third and relatedly, the multiparty category contains many different possible speaker combinations: two children and an adult, male and female adult and the key child, as male adult with two children, female adult and two children, or all speaker categories. These types of multiparty subcategories may differ in key aspects. For instance, while there was strong evidence to suggest that children with more siblings engage in more multiparty conversation, the same was not true when examining a sub-category containing the target child and at least one male and one female adult (see Supplementary Materials S5).

Lastly, there may be differences in exposure to conversation across corpora that result from differences in how data was collected, rather than differences inherent to the daily lives of the families involved. Although all families were asked to record children for as much of the day as possible in or around the home, there were differences in the contexts in which recordings took place. Other differences in conversation might result from constraints on the kinds of data that can be collected across settings. For instance, children from more urban settings might spend more of their time in daycare or preschool settings than children in more rural settings. These settings are particularly rich in child-child conversation (Soderstrom & Wittebole, 2013; Perry et al., 2018). However, due to the difficulty of collecting data in these settings, which typically requires consent from both the institution and all families whose children may potentially be recorded, most studies request that families only record their children at home. This may result in an underestimation of child-child conversation in urban communities relative to rural communities, where children more often spend time with other children outside of these settings.

Conclusion

Our adaptation of recently developed speech diarization software gives us a unique glimpse into the daily lives of children growing up around the world. We developed a coding scheme that allowed us to capture more diverse forms of conversation than are typically captured in traditional studies of child speech. We found that adult-child conversations were less common than multiparty conversation across almost all corpora.

We found evidence for consistent predictors of conversation across highly diverse child populations, with some findings running contrary to accepted wisdom in studies of childhood conversation. Notably, we failed to find strong evidence that maternal education was positively related to adult-child conversation, using both relative and absolute measures of educational attainment. We believe these findings speak to the strength of using long-form audio recording analysis to study child conversations in different settings.

We hope to inspire more collaborative and interdisciplinary efforts to establish more generalizable theories of language development through the construction and analysis of large cross-cultural datasets.
