Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

1. Introduction

Theoretical models have long posited that parents' decision to raise their children multilingually is shaped not only by their attitudes towards the individual languages they are transmitting, but also by their attitudes towards multilingualism (e.g. De Houwer 1999). 1There is a longstanding tradition of empirical research regarding attitudes towards individual languages, which has shown that such attitudes have two main evaluative dimensions: status and solidarity (e.g. Dragojevic et al. 2021). It has been confirmed that parental attitudes towards individual languages do influence whether these languages are transmitted; however, differences exist between the influence of status-related and solidarity-related attitudes, which highlights the importance of considering attitude dimensionality in research regarding intergenerational transmission (Kircher 2022). By contrast, attitudes towards multilingualism constitute a relatively new area of empirical enquiry. While there is an emerging body of research regarding such attitudes in educational contexts (e.g. Dekker et al. 2021), there is a particular paucity of studies examining parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism. The only known studies of their dimensionality found that these attitudes also have multiple evaluative dimensions: status, solidarity, and cognitive development (Kircher et al. 2022 (Kircher et al. , 2026; see below for more details). However, this multidimensionality has only been investigated in a single research context, namely the Canadian province of Québec. A few studies in different contexts have focused on the relationship between parents' attitudes towards childhood multilingualism and different aspects of children's language exposure in the home (e.g. Surrain & Luk 2023), but they all treated these attitudes as unidimensional. No known research has examined whether status, solidarity, and cognitive development emerge as distinct evaluative dimensions of attitudes towards childhood multilingualism in contexts other than Québec -and whether attitudes on one or more of these dimensions influence whether parents actually transmit multiple languages at home. The study presented here addresses this gap by investigating the dimensionality of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism, as well as their influence on multilingual child-rearing at home, in two new research contexts: Nordfriesland and Fryslân.

Nordfriesland is the northernmost district of Germany, located in the state of Schleswig-Holstein, just south of the border to Denmark. Multilingualism is pervasive in Nordfriesland: the majority language is German, but the district is also home to almost all speakers of North Frisian (a national minority language) as well as many speakers of Danish (also a national minority language) and Low German (a regional language). Virtually all regional/minority language speakers know German, and many of them also speak one or both other regional/minority languages (see e.g. Hawkey & Langer 2016 for an overview of the historical circumstances that led to the current multilingualism in Nordfriesland). Fryslân is a province in Germany's neighbouring country to the west, the Netherlands. Fryslân, too, is highly multilingual: the majority language is Dutch, but a substantial share of the province's population speaks West Frisian (a national minority language) and there are numerous speakers of Low Saxon (also a national minority language) and minority varieties such as Bildts, which have resulted from Dutch-Frisian language contact (but hold no official status). Almost all minority language/variety speakers know Dutch, and many of them also speak one or more of the other minority languages/varieties (see e.g. Hilton et al. 2020 for an overview of the historical circumstances that led to the current multilingualism in Fryslân). Additionally, both Nordfriesland and Fryslân are home to speakers of various immigrant heritage languages. They generally also speak the local majority language and oftentimes learn regional/minority languages as well (e.g. Krol-Hage & Kircher 2020).

In both research contexts, our main focus is on multilingualism involving Frisian. Frisian used to be spoken throughout a vaster part of northern Europe, but Nordfriesland and Fryslân are now the two largest remaining language areas. Since they are no longer physically connected to each other, the way Frisian is spoken in these areas has developed differently. Nowadays, many speakers deem North and West Frisian mutually unintelligible, and they are considered different languages. Both can be classified as autochthonous heritage languages.foot_1 North Frisian has an estimated 4,000 speakers (Versloot 2021) and is severely endangered (UNESCO 2010), while West Frisian has approximately 424,000 speakers (Dekker et al. 2025) and is considered vulnerable (UNESCO 2010). Traditionally, language policy and planning to promote the acquisition of Frisian in both contexts has focused on the education system (e.g. Terhart 2022). 3 However, there is an increasing awareness of the crucial role that intergenerational transmission in the home can play, especially in the early years. Children's early multilingual development -and particularly the acquisition of their heritage languagescan affect their well-being and healthy identity development, as well as family dynamics and interpersonal relationships between family members (see e.g. De Houwer 2021 for an overview of the literature). Additionally, children who grow up to be active, proficient multilinguals and whose linguistic repertoires include their heritage languages can collectively contribute to the maintenance and revitalisation of these heritage languages (e.g. King et al. 2008).

The present study set out to serve two goals. First, we aimed to make a contribution to attitude theory by generating new knowledge that sheds light on the dimensionality of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism and the potential influence of such attitudes on multilingual child-rearing. Second, we aimed to make a practical contribution by elucidating parental attitudes specifically in the two research contexts under investigation, to provide a basis for new research-informed science communication. The latter can enable policy makers and language planners to develop effective support measures to foster multilingual child-rearing and the intergenerational transmission of Frisian.

To investigate parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism in Nordfriesland and Fryslân, we collected questionnaire data from Frisian-speaking parents of young children across both contexts (n=645). The research questions (RQs) we sought to answer were: • RQ1: Do status, solidarity, and cognitive development emerge as separate evaluative dimensions of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism?

• RQ2: Are parents with more positive attitudes towards childhood multilingualism more likely to raise their children multilingually in the home? • RQ3: Are there attitudinal differences between parents in Nordfriesland and Fryslân? To contextualise our study, we begin by providing an overview of the relevant literature, which informed our hypotheses (Hs).

2. Parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism

Attitudes are composed of three components: affect, the feelings elicited by an attitude object; cognition, the beliefs held about an attitude object; and conation, the behaviour directed at an attitude object (e.g. Bohner 2001). Language attitudes were thus traditionally defined as any affective, cognitive, or conative index of evaluative reactions towards individual languages/varieties (and their speakers) (e.g. Ryan et al. 1982). However, considering the growing body of research regarding attitudes towards different linguistic features and phenomena -such as vocal fry (Yuasa 2010), code-switching (Dewaele and Wei 2014), and multilingualism (e.g. Surrain and Luk 2023) -this definition ought to be expanded. A more appropriate contemporary definition of language attitudes encompasses any affective, cognitive, or conative index of evaluative reactions towards languages/varieties, linguistic features, or linguistic phenomena (and their users). Here, the focus is on an under-researched linguistic phenomenon, namely parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism.

2.1 Dimensionality

The results of several small-scale, qualitative studies conducted in various contexts indicated that parents think of childhood multilingualism as entailing different types of benefits: they mentioned, for example, its usefulness, the way it allows children to connect to people, and the fact that it facilitates language learning later in life (see e.g. Hollebeke et al. 2023 for an overview of the literature). Kircher et al.'s (2022) large-scale study of quantitative questionnaire data from Québec was the first to empirically support the multidimensionality of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism, showing that they comprise three distinct evaluative dimensions: status, solidarity, and cognitive development. Parents with positive status-related attitudes considered childhood multilingualism to hold high utilitarian value, especially for their children's future socio-economic mobility and prospects. Parents with positive solidarity-related attitudes deemed childhood multilingualism beneficial for their children's social identities and salient group memberships. Parents with positive cognitive-development-related attitudes believed that childhood multilingualism benefits their children's thinking, reasoning, and other learning abilities. These three dimensions were attested in the attitudes of parents who were raising their children with Québec's societal languages, French and English, as well as parents transmitting immigrant heritage languages in addition to French and/or English. Additionally, it emerged that positive attitudes on the different dimensions were not mutually exclusive. Kircher et al.'s (2026) large-scale study of qualitative questionnaire data collected from the same Québec-based parents corroborated the findings from the previous study. Based on this, we hypothesised the following for our study in Nordfriesland and Fryslân: • H1: Status, solidarity, and cognitive development emerge as separate dimensions of attitudes towards childhood multilingualism.

2.2 Influence on multilingual child-rearing

The other known studies of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism treated them as a unidimensional construct. 4 Nonetheless, these studies provided empirical evidence for the influence that parental attitudes have on different aspects of young children's language exposure in the home. Hollebeke et al. (2022) conducted a large-scale survey study of families in Belgium's Flemish community who were raising young children with Dutch and at least one immigrant heritage language. Their results revealed a robust correlation between parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism and a variable they labelled 'child's exposure at home', which includes the languages most frequently spoken by the parents, grandparents, and siblings. The more positive parents' attitudes were, the more likely the family was to facilitate multilingualism by providing heritage language input in the home. Surrain and Luk (2023) also found a significant relationship between parental attitudes and home language practices. Their large-scale survey study in the U.S. focused on families raising young children with English and Spanish, with the latter being an immigrant heritage language in this research context. Their results showed that parents who perceived multilingualism as more valuable were more likely to engage in several key home language practices with their child in the heritage language: namely mealtime talk, telling stories, and singing songs. Fostering heritage language exposure in the home can be considered a way of indirectly promoting childhood multilingualism because children generally receive sufficient exposure to the dominant societal language (from friends and once they enter daycare or school) to acquire it, even when it is not spoken at home. Yet, to date, it remains unknown whether parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism influence parents' decision to actually transmit multiple languages in the home. Taking the results of the previous research as an indication, we hypothesised the following for our study in Nordfriesland and Fryslân: • H2: Parents with more positive attitudes towards childhood multilingualism are more likely to raise their children multilingually in the home.

2.3 Nordfriesland versus Fryslân

In a large-scale study in Wales, Baker (1992) established that attitudes towards multilingualism are conceptually distinct from attitudes towards the individual languages in a multilingual context. Kircher et al.'s (2022 Kircher et al.'s ( , 2026) ) findings generally supported this notionbut they indicated that parents' status-related attitudes towards childhood multilingualism may nonetheless be shaped by the context and the languages that are being transmitted. In Québec, this manifested in the fact that parents who were raising their children multilingually with immigrant heritage languages (that have no official status and hold little utilitarian value in Québec society) expressed less positive status-related attitudes towards childhood multilingualism than parents who were raising their children with the two societal languages (which both hold high utilitarian value). Notably, the status and role of North versus West Frisian differ markedly: unlike North Frisian, West Frisian is recognised as a co-official language alongside the local majority language, it enjoys greater institutional support, plays a more prominent role in the education system, and it is more likely to facilitate access to certain professions than North Frisian (e.g. Hilton et al. 2020). Consequently, a recent study found that parents in Fryslân held significantly more positive status-related attitudes towards Frisian than parents in Nordfriesland did (Kircher et al. 2026). In light of this, we hypothesised: • H3a: On the status dimension, context predicts attitudes towards childhood multilingualism: parents in Fryslân hold more positive attitudes towards childhood multilingualism than parents in Nordfriesland. Kircher et al.'s (2022 Kircher et al.'s ( , 2026) ) results indicated that parents' solidarity-related attitudes towards childhood multilingualism may also be shaped by the context and the languages that are being transmitted.

Yet, the aforementioned study of attitudes towards Frisian found no significant attitudinal differences between parents in Nordfriesland and Fryslân (Kircher et al. 2026). This is unsurprising since speakers' social identities and ingroup loyalties tend to be largely independent of their language's social standing in society (e.g. Dragojevic et al. 2021). Therefore, we hypothesised: • H3b: On the solidarity dimension, context does not predict attitudes towards childhood multilingualism: there is no significant difference between parents in Nordfriesland and parents in Fryslân. Kircher et al. (2022 Kircher et al. ( , 2026) ) found that parents' cognitive-development-related attitudes towards childhood multilingualism were not shaped by the specific context or the specific languages that were being transmitted.

Moreover, the aforementioned study of attitudes towards Frisian found no significant differences between parents' cognitive-developmentrelated attitudes in Nordfriesland and Fryslân (Kircher et al. 2026). We thus hypothesised: • H3c: On the cognitive development dimension, context does not predict attitudes towards childhood multilingualism: there is no significant difference between parents in Nordfriesland and parents in Fryslân.

>Methodology

3.1 Method

Data were collected with the Adapted Attitudes towards Childhood Multilingualism Questionnaire (A-ACMQ), based on the original ACMQ developed by Kircher et al. (2022).

Here, the focus is on the quantitative A-ACMQ data pertaining to the dimensionality of parental attitudes as well as their implications for multilingual child-rearing. Elsewhere, we examine the qualitative A-ACMQ data that were collected to obtain additional insights into the dimensionality of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism (Kircher et al., in preparation) and the quantitative A-ACMQ data pertaining to potential predictors of such attitudes (Heinzova et al., in preparation).

The research questions, hypotheses, and data analysis plan for this study were preregistered. The A-ACMQ, the pre-registration, and our R analysis code are available on the Open Science Framework: https://osf.io/qhc7u/

3.2 Participants

The participant sample consisted of 645 Frisian-speaking parents: 79 living in Nordfriesland and 566 in Fryslân, which reflects the diverging demographics of the Frisian-speaking populations in these contexts. 5 Parents from throughout the relevant geographical areas participated -with the exception of the halligen in Nordfriesland, and the islands of Schiermonnikoog and Vlieland in Fryslân. Most participants were born in Nordfriesland or Fryslân (88.61% and 91.87%, respectively), identified as female (77.21% and 85.51%, respectively), and their mean age was in their mid-thirties (36.00, SD=6.03, and 34.61, SD=5.18, respectively). Most participants had learnt Frisian either as their sole L1 (27.89% and 63.96%, respectively) or as one of their L1s alongside the local majority language and/or another language (44.31% and 24.74%, respectively). 3.4 Questionnaire items and statistical analyses

Attitudes towards childhood multilingualism were elicited with twelve items whose response options were five-point Likert scales (1 = don't agree at all,5 = agree completely). Four items each were designed to tap status, solidarity, and cognitive development (see Table 1, below). Exploratory factor analysis with maximum likelihood factoring, using an oblique rotation method, namely direct oblimin, was employed to answer RQ1: Do status, solidarity, and cognitive development emerge as separate evaluative dimensions of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism?

A multiple response item (Q16) was used to ascertain with which language(s) participants' children were being raised in the home. The response options were German (in Nordfriesland) / Dutch (in Fryslân), Frisian, and other(s). If the latter was selected, participants were asked to specify the other language(s). Participants were categorised as raising their children monolingually if they selected only one language, or multilingually if they selected multiple languages. 8 Subsequently, exploratory bivariate analysis followed by binary logistic regression analysis -with participants' attitudes scores as the independent variable and multilingual child-rearing (yes vs. no) as the dependent variable -was used to answer RQ2: Are parents with more positive attitudes towards childhood multilingualism more likely to raise their children multilingually in the home?

Finally, linear regression analysis and equivalence testing were used to answer RQ3: Are there attitudinal differences between parents in Nordfriesland and Fryslân? Linear regression analysis was employed to test H3a, with context (Nordfriesland vs. Fryslân) as the independent variable and participants' status-related attitudes scores as the dependent variable. Equivalence testing was employed to test H3b and H3c. Group comparisons (Nordfriesland vs. Fryslân) for solidarity-related and cognitive-development-related attitudes were made using the Two One-Sided Test procedure.

The results of the factor analysis supported H1 (i.e. status, solidarity, and cognitive development emerge as separate dimensions of attitudes towards childhood multilingualism). Table 1 9 shows that items Q21N/Q20F, Q22N/Q21F, Q23N/Q22F, Q26N/Q25F, and Q31N/Q30F loaded onto factor 1, indicating that they all tapped the same dimension: cognitive development. Items Q24N/Q23F, Q27N/Q26F, Q29N/Q28F, and Q32N/Q31F loaded onto factor 3, indicating that they all tapped the same dimension: solidarity. The items that loaded onto each factor had high internal consistency: α = 0.915 for the cognitive development factor, α = 0.903 for the status factor, and α = 0.855 for the solidarity factor. The three factors accounted for much of the variance: cognitive development for 27%, status for 21%, and solidarity for 21% -so, 69% altogether.

All items loaded onto the factor pertaining to the dimension they were designed to tap, except for Q22N/Q21F: Growing up with more than one language helps children, later on, achieve success in the education system. This item had been designed to tap status-related attitudes, but it loaded onto the cognitive development factor. Notably, the same had previously happened with the original ACMQ item from which Q22N/Q21F was adapted: Knowing more than one language will, in the long term, help my child do well in school. Kircher et al. (2022) noted that this was likely due to the unintentionally ambiguous phrasing of the original item: 'doing well' can be interpreted as either (a) achieving good grades, which allow for subsequent socio-economic opportunities -thus, relating to status; or (b) learning easily and understanding subject matters -thus, relating to cognitive development. While the original ACMQ item had been designed to tap the former, participants of the Québec study interpreted it to mean the latter. This was why the item was adapted for the A-ACMQ. However, it seems that 'achieving success in the education system' was still too ambiguous, and that the participants from Nordfriesland and Fryslân also interpreted it as relating to cognitive development rather than to status. Yet, this has no bearing on the overall results of the factor analysis.

Following the factor analysis, the items that loaded onto each factor were combined and their mean was calculated to create an overall variable for cognitive-development-related attitudes (mean=4.019, SD=0.905), an overall variable for status-related attitudes (mean=3.625, SD=1.035), as well as an overall variable for solidarity-related attitudes (mean=4.173, SD=0.877). This was done for all participants who had responded to at least three of the items pertaining to each new variable (79 in Nordfriesland, 565 in Fryslân). Moving forward, these new overall variables were used for the analyses.

4.2 Influence on multilingual child-rearing 10

Next, exploratory bivariate analysis using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests was undertaken to assess if a statistically significant difference exists between the attitudes towards childhood multilingualism (on the status dimension, the solidarity dimension, and the cognitive development dimension) held by participants who were raising their children multilingually versus those who were raising their children monolingually. No significant difference was found for status-related attitudes (W=51,212, p=0.960) or cognitive-development-related attitudes (W=49,735, p=0.493); however, a significant difference was identified for solidarityrelated attitudes (W=44,806, p<0.005).

Subsequently, a binary logistic regression -with participants' solidarity-related attitudes scores as the independent variable and multilingual child-rearing (yes versus no) as the dependent variable -was employed to further explore this relationship. The results depicted in Figure 3 show that solidarity-related attitudes towards childhood multilingualism predicted the likelihood of multilingual child-rearing. The coefficient for solidarity-related attitudes was positive and significant (β=0.235, SE=0.092, z=2.556, p<0.05), indicating that participants with more positive solidarity-related attitudes were more likely to raise their child multilingually. The odds of raising a child multilingually increased by a factor of 1.2655219 for each one-unit increase in solidarity-related attitudes. The results thus supported H2 (i.e. parents with more positive attitudes towards childhood multilingualism are more likely to raise their children multilingually).

5. Discussion

The study presented here investigated parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism, examining the dimensionality of these attitudes as well as their influence on multilingual child-rearing. The focus was on multilingualism involving Frisian in Nordfriesland and Fryslân. While the participant samples reflected the diverging demographics of the Frisianspeaking populations in these contexts, they were not representative, and we therefore make no claims regarding the generalisation of our results. Nonetheless, the results provided meaningful insights.

5.1 Dimensionality

RQ1 asked: Do status, solidarity, and cognitive development emerge as separate evaluative dimensions of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism? The results revealed that status, solidarity, and cognitive development did indeed emerge as separate evaluative dimensions in Nordfriesland and Fryslân, thereby supporting H1 and providing new empirical support for the multidimensionality of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism. Parental attitudes on these three dimensions accounted for much of the variance in the data, namely 69%, which underscores their importance. The results of this study showed that these dimensions are not Québec-specific, indicating that they may characterise parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism across contexts. The results also indicated that these dimensions characterise the attitudes of parents raising their children with all possible language combinations: not only multiple societal languages or different constellations of societal and immigrant heritage languages, as previously shown in Québec (Kircher et al. 2022 (Kircher et al. , 2026) ) -but also different constellations involving autochthonous heritage languages, as shown in Nordfriesland and Fryslân. More research with these different language constellations in additional contexts is needed to confirm this. Nevertheless, the current study makes a contribution to attitude theory by advancing knowledge regarding the dimensionality of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism.

5.2 Influence on multilingual child-rearing

RQ2 asked: Are parents with more positive attitudes towards childhood multilingualism more likely to raise their children multilingually in the home? The results revealed that parents with more positive attitudes on one of the dimensions -namely solidarity -really were more likely to raise their children multilingually in the home, thereby providing at least partial support for H2. More research is needed to explore the underlying reason for this finding. As noted above, Baker (1992) established that attitudes towards multilingualism are conceptually distinct from attitudes towards the individual languages in a multilingual context, and Kircher et al.'s (2022 Kircher et al.'s ( , 2026) ) findings generally supported this notion -but they also indicated that parents' status-and solidarity-related attitudes may still be influenced by the context and the languages transmitted. Therefore, while all A-ACMQ items were phrased without reference to specific contexts and specific languages, the results of the current study may in fact be shaped by its focus on Nordfriesland and Fryslân, and childhood multilingualism involving the specific languages transmitted in these contexts. In both contexts, there is only one dominant societal language. Childhood multilingualism therefore automatically involves autochthonous and/or immigrant heritage languages. The participants of this study held particularly positive solidarity-related attitudes towards childhood multilingualism, which means they believed multilingualism to be particularly important for their children's social identities and salient group memberships -for example, as part of their families and their cultural communities which do not operate in the main societal language. Future studies (in contexts like Québec, where there are multiple societal languages) are needed to ascertain whether the link between parents' solidarity-related attitudes and multilingual child-rearing is specific to childhood multilingualism involving autochthonous and/or immigrant heritage languages. Yet, regardless of the outcome of such future studies, the research presented here contributes to attitude theory by showing that -at least in heritage language contexts -the influence of parents' attitudes towards childhood multilingualism can go beyond the previously-attested aspects of young children's language exposure in the home (Hollebeke et al. 2022; Surrain and Luk 2023).

5.3 Nordfriesland versus Fryslân

RQ3 asked: Are there attitudinal differences between parents in Nordfriesland and Fryslân?

The results revealed no significant differences between the two contexts, thereby lending support to H3b and H3c, but not to H3a. Based on the aforementioned findings from Kircher et al. (2022 Kircher et al. ( , 2026) ) and Kircher et al. (2026), we had hypothesised that -unlike solidarityrelated and cognitive-development-related attitudes -status-related attitudes would differ between the two research contexts, based on the different legal status and role of Frisian in Nordfriesland compared to Fryslân. It is unclear why this was not the case. On the one hand, the fact that there were no significant differences in parents' status-related attitudes underscores the need for additional research regarding the manner and extent to which parents' attitudes towards childhood multilingualism are shaped by the specific context and the specific languages transmitted. This is an emergent area of empirical investigation and much remains to be ascertained. On the other hand, the fact that there were no significant attitudinal differences between parents in Nordfriesland and Fryslân allows us to provide cross-context, research-informed science communication for policy makers and language planners who are aiming to foster multilingual child-rearing.

The results of the current study showed that parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism in Nordfriesland and Fryslân have three distinct evaluative dimensionsstatus, solidarity, and cognitive development -but that only solidarity-related attitudes have a significant influence on parents' likelihood of raising their children multilingually in the home. This indicates that the promotion of positive solidarity-related attitudes would constitute the most effective way of fostering multilingual child-rearing, and thereby the intergenerational transmission of Frisian in the home. As noted above, the latter is a key component of language maintenance and revitalisation. Based on this knowledge, we are currently preparing research reports directed at policy makers and language planners in Nordfriesland and Fryslân. These reports summarise the results of the current study and outline a range of potential measures to ameliorate parents' solidarity-related attitudes towards childhood multilingualism. Notably, in addition to fostering multilingual childrearing and the intergenerational transmission of Frisian in the home, the promotion of more positive attitudes towards childhood multilingualism could potentially also encourage parents to become more proactive in seeking out multilingual spaces and heritage language exposure for their children in contexts outside the home, as their children grow older (e.g. Montanari et al. 2022).

6. Conclusion

The study presented here makes a theoretical contribution by showing that the multidimensionality of parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism that was first attested by Kircher et al. (2022 Kircher et al. ( , 2026) ) is not Québec-specific. Status, solidarity, and cognitive development emerged as distinct evaluative dimensions of parental attitudes in Nordfriesland and Fryslân, as well, suggesting that they may characterise parental attitudes towards childhood multilingualism across contexts. Additionally, the study revealed a positive and significant relationship between parents' solidarity-related attitudes towards childhood multilingualism and the likelihood of multilingual child-rearing in the home. This is evidence that the influence of parental attitudes goes beyond the previously-attested aspects of young children's language exposure in the home (Hollebeke et al. 2022; Surrain and Luk 2023). More research, ideally with representative participant samples from a range of different contexts, is needed to ascertain whether these findings can be generalised. However, even as they are, the insights provided by the current study constitute a suitable basis for the development of research-informed science communication aimed at policy makers and language planners who wish to foster multilingual child-rearing and the intergenerational transmission of Frisian in Nordfriesland and Fryslân, thereby contributing to language maintenance and revitalisation.

We use multilingualism as an umbrella term for multiple languages, regardless of the exact number; it thus includes bilingualism, trilingualism, quadrilingualism, etc.

A heritage language is a language that is learnt at home in childhood, and that is not the majority language of society (e.g.Rothman 2009). In North American contexts, a distinction is commonly made between immigrant heritage languages and Indigenous heritage languages. In European contexts, the term autochthonous is more commonly used when discussing populations that are native to a region but have been minoritised (e.g.Byers- Heinlein et al. 2025). The term autochthonous heritage language is thus used to categorise North Frisian(Litty et al. 2026) -and the same logic also applies to West Frisian.

Henceforth, we use the term Frisian when referring to both, North and West Frisian, as well as in contexts where it is evident which language is being referred to.

Quirk et al.'s (2025) small-scale study of parents raising young trilinguals in Québec constitutes an exception to this. However, this study -which adapted the questionnaire fromKircher et al. (2022) -focused on family language strategies rather than specific practices, and is therefore not of direct relevance here.

The pre-registered target sample size was 352: 176 from each context. Ultimately, data were collected from 123 participants from Nordfriesland and from Fryslân.

However, 120 were excluded from the analyses because they did not meet one or more of our inclusion criteria: living in Nordfriesland/Fryslân, being a Frisian speaker, minimum age of 18 and maximum age of 59 years, raising a child that does not have any health issues that might affect their language development, and responding to at least 75% of the questions relevant for the analyses presented here.6 This follow-up study examined the predictors of parental attitudes towards Frisian in both contexts; however, it focused exclusively on the parents who were raising their children multilingually.

We preregistered data collection from 50% of participants who were raising their children monolingually (without Frisian) to best explore the relationship between parents' attitudes towards childhood multilingualism and monolingual versus multilingual child-rearing. Despite concerted efforts and an extension of the time period for data collection, this was not possible. Nonetheless, we were able to obtain meaningful results (see below).
