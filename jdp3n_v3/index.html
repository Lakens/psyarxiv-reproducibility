Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction 1.1 Audio-Visual Speech Processing

Speech perception is a complex process involving integrating auditory and visual information. In face-to-face interactions, people leverage auditory speech and visual cues, known as audiovisual (AV) speech, to understand spoken language. This cross-modal enhancement relies on temporal and phonetic cues (Chandrasekaran et al., 2009; Munhall et al., 2004). Studies with native English speakers show that sensitivity to visual temporal cues (i.e., opening of the mouth that indicates onset of speech) emerges in infancy (around four months) and becomes highly developed by six months (e.g., Grant, van Wassenhove & Poeppel, 2004; Lalonde & Werner, 2019; Lewkowicz, 2000 Lewkowicz, , 2010)). Sensitivity to visual phonetic cues (i.e., mouth movements that correspond to specific speech sounds, termed audiovisual [AV] cues) appears to mature more slowly, typically reaching full development around ages 6 to 9 in monolingual speakers of languages such as English (Lalonde & Holt, 2016), Dutch (Baart et al., 2014; 2015), and French-Canadian (Tremblay et al., 2007).

The 6-9-year age range is not arbitrary; it coincides precisely with the onset of reading instruction, a pivotal phase characterized by the transformative power of reading on phonological awareness (PA), an ability to recognize, discriminate, and manipulate sounds of a language (Anthony & Francis, 2005; Jerger et al., 2009). As children engage with written text, they move beyond mere auditory decoding. Their sensitivity to visual speech cues, linking lip and articulatory movements to phonemic representations, begins to increase. For example, knowing that the word 'bat' starts with a bilabial closure of the mouth that corresponds to the /b/ sound indicates that a child has phonemic awareness of the sound /b/. As a result, PA, which relies heavily on both the speaker's (Carrol et al., 2003; Johnson & Goswami, 2010) and the listener's LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION mouth articulation movements (Jerger et al., 2009), develops into a sophisticated cognitive resource vital for proficient reading (Gillon, 2017). This dual-channel AV processing not only enhances children's PA but also augments their ability to decode written language (Carrol et al., 2003; Johnson & Goswami, 2010). However, the possible convergence of reading acquisition and increased sensitivity to AV speech cues remains understudied, despite the developmental overlap in the emergence of these skills.

1.2 PA and AV Speech Sensitivity

Although interest in the development of AV speech perception has grown, relatively little research has examined its relation with children's PA and reading skills. Decades of research has demonstrated that PA in children is strongly associated with their reading skills (e.g., Deacon & Kirby, 2004; McBride-Chang et al., 2005; Torgesen et al., 1997), as well as with their sensitivity to visual speech cues (e.g., Heikkilä et al., 2017; Norrix et al., 2007). Some phonemes might be hard to distinguish by access to auditory signals alone but are relatively easy to distinguish by having access to complementary visual information (e.g., /n/ and /m/; Campbell, 2008). Thus, the information conveyed by visual speech perception is incorporated into phonological representations and facilitates the development of PA.

In support of this claim, studies with deaf populations show that deaf individuals who use cued speech, a system that uses mouth articulations of speech (i.e., visual speech) in combination with hand gestures (Cornett, 1967), have better English PA skills than deaf individuals who do not use cued speech in combination with hand gestures (Colin et al., 2007: language = English). Furthermore, some studies suggest that deficits in AV speech perception may relate to impaired PA skills. These studies report that visual information has less influence on speech perception in individuals with phonological processing impairment (Meronen et al., 2013: language = Finnish; LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION Norrix et al., 2007: language = English). For example, Norrix et al. (2007) examined the influence of visual speech in 4-5-year-old children with specific language impairment, characterized primarily by deficits in phonological processing. The authors reported that only 14% of children with language impairment correctly identified incongruent AV speech (visual [bi] paired with auditory [gi]), compared with 54% of children with no language impairment. This evidence suggests a relationship between PA and AV speech perception skills.

1.3 Literacy Instruction and AV Speech Sensitivity

The onset of reading instruction may strengthen the link between PA and AV speech sensitivity by increasing the demand to integrate auditory and visual cues. Reading entails establishing connections between phonemes (auditory/articulatory representations of sounds) and graphemes (visual representations) (Ehri, 2005; Ziegler & Goswami, 2005). To successfully establish these associations when reading, children need to accomplish several tasks. They must first be able to auditorily identify phonemes while accounting for allophonic variations (i.e., natural variations in a phoneme's pronunciation). They must then learn to visually recognize and differentiate graphemes, and map target phonemes with their corresponding grapheme (Horlyk et al., 2012). Thus, the ability to detect and match phonemes to graphemes involves both auditory and visual codes, which play a crucial role in deciphering spoken and written language.

Given that the integration of graphemes and speech sounds develops during reading acquisition, it is reasonable to expect a bidirectional relationship between reading skill and the developmental shift in sensitivity to AV speech cues in speech perception. In fact, in a study examining the association between reading skills and phonemic discrimination (discriminating between native [English] speech sounds and non-native [Hindi] speech sounds), Burnham (2003) LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION found that 6-and 8-year-old children with higher reading scores showed higher phonemic discrimination scores for native contrasts versus non-native contrasts.

Research on children with phonological deficits suggests a relationship between reading and sensitivity to AV speech cues that is mediated by PA. Indeed, children who struggle with decoding exhibit poorer processing of AV speech cues compared to their typically reading peers.

For example, dyslexic children, who present with challenges in PA, combine visual and auditory information to a much lesser extent compared to their typically reading peers (Cavé, Stroumza, & Bastien-Toniazzo, 2007), suggesting that reading skills may be linked to sensitivity to AV speech cues. In Kujala and colleagues' study (2001), dyslexic children who trained with AV stimuli improved their reading skills. Similarly, McBride-Chang (1996) tested whether AV speech perception impacts children's reading ability directly or indirectly via its impact on PA.

The results showed that 8-to 10-year-old children with better AV speech perception skills showed higher PA scores, which in turn facilitated their reading skills.

1.4 Orthographic Transparency, PA, and AV Speech Processing

Orthography likely further moderates the link between PA, reading, and sensitivity to AV speech. Languages exhibit a spectrum of orthographic depth, ranging from high opacity to high transparency: in orthographically "opaque" languages, the degree of phoneme-grapheme correspondence is very low (e.g., English, non-diactritical Hebrew); in orthographically "transparent" languages, there is a one-to-one correspondence between graphemes and phonemes (e.g., Turkish, German) (Katz & Frost, 1992). Uzbek is an example of a language with transparent orthography, where mapping sounds to written symbols is relatively consistent; one grapheme corresponds (roughly) to a single phoneme, with about 31 phonemes transcribed with 29 graphemes (Sjoberg, 1963). This contrasts sharply with English, for example, where about 40 LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION phonemes are transcribed with only 26 graphemes. Acquiring reading skills is relatively less challenging and develops more quickly in orthographically transparent languages such as Uzbek than in orthographically opaque languages such as English (Durgunoğlu & Öney, 1999; Seymour, Aro & Erskine, 2003). The only studies to examine the relationship between AV speech perception and reading skills in typically developing school-aged children used English-speaking children tested with English (opaque orthography) language stimuli. These studies found that increased sensitivity to language-specific phonetic cues (i.e., PA) mediated the relationship between AV speech sensitivity and reading ability (e.g., Erdener & Burnham, 2013).

However, the relationship between multimodal speech perception and reading skills in languages with transparent orthography remains uncharted territory. To this goal, this study examines the relationship between sensitivity to AV speech perception cues and reading skills (reading and PA) in children who speak Uzbek. Uzbek is one of the less-studied languages in language acquisition and reading development. Unlike widely researched languages on AV speech perception and reading (e.g., English, Dutch), Uzbek has a transparent orthography. This feature offers an opportunity to investigate how children acquiring reading skills in a language with orthographic transparency use AV cues during reading.

2.2.2 PA and Literacy

The assessment of children's PA skills in Uzbek included untimed tasks of initial sound identification, initial sound discrimination from the Early Grade Reading Assessment (EGRA; Gove & Wetterberg, 2011; RTI International, 2009), and phoneme segmentation from the Yopp-Singer segmentation task (Yopp, 1995).

The initial sound identification task was exclusively administered to younger children (age 5-6 years). During this task, children were presented with 10 monosyllabic and bisyllabic words in Uzbek, such as "bo'sh" or "sho'x." Their objective was to recognize and verbally express the first sound of each word, such as b for "bo'sh" or sh for "sho'x."

The initial sound discrimination task was given to school-aged children (grades 1-4). In this task, the children listened to sets of three words and identified which word initiated with a different sound than the others in the set. This task involved 10 sets of three words each; in each set, two words began with the same sound, and the initial sound for the third word was different, such as "bo'sh", "sho'x", "bor". The position of the "different" word in the set varied. The experimenter read each group of three words aloud slowly, two times, and asked the child to choose the word that begins with a different sound.

The phoneme segmentation task was administered to all children. During this task, children listened to a list of 10 monosyllabic CVC or bisyllabic CVCVC words one by one. Subsequently, they were required to recognize and articulate each sound within the word.

Children's reading proficiency in Uzbek was assessed using timed letter, non-word and passage reading tasks from the Early Grade Reading Assessment (EGRA) previously used in Uzbekistan (Harden et al., 2022, RTI International). 2.3 Procedure

Testing was completed in a quiet room. The AV speech perception task was administered via Pyschopy (v2021.2.3) using a 13-inch MacBook Pro (2.3 GHz Dual-Core Intel Core i5, macOS 13.0.1) and professional monitor headphones (ATH M20x). The reading and PA tasks were administered using physical copies of each task.

For the AV speech portion task, participants were presented with non-target, target, and foil trials. Participants were told that they would hear a stream of syllables (non-target and foil trials) and were instructed to press any key on a computer keyboard upon hearing a different syllable (target trials). Target (N=10) and foil (N=10) trials were randomly mixed with the continuous presentation of the non-target syllable /ba/, occurring at a rate of one every 3 seconds in each experimental condition. During non-target trials, there were no changes in the auditory and visual signals from the background syllable. Target trials featured the syllable /da/, whereas, in foil trials, the visual cue suggested the target trial, but the auditory signal continued to be the background trial signal. The inclusion of foil trials aimed to ensure that participants responded to AV signals rather than just visual cues (Lalonde & Werner, 2019). To habituate participants with non-target trials, 83% of the trials were non-target and foil (50 trials), while 17% were target trials (10 trials). The sequencing of the trials was randomized, with no more than two identical trials occurring in succession. Each stimulus had a duration of 2000 milliseconds with an LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION inter-stimulus interval (ISI) of 1000 milliseconds. A 15-second break was provided between conditions.

To eliminate bias, the experimenter remained seated across from the participant, behind the computer screen throughout the experiment, and prompted participants to focus on the screen when needed. Furthermore, children wore headphones to ensure that the experimenter could not hear the syllables being presented. The entire procedure of the AV speech perception task lasted approximately 10 minutes per child. For the PA and reading tasks, the experimenter first explained the task requirements to each child. Following the introduction of the task requirements, each child was given a practice item to ensure their complete understanding of the task. To avoid potential order effects, the sequence of testing for the AV speech perception, PA, and reading tasks was counterbalanced across participants. This counterbalancing ensured that the order of task presentation did not systematically favor one task over the other, minimizing any potential biases introduced by the testing order.

2.4.1 Data Preparation

Data preparation and analyses were conducted using the R statistical software (v4.3.1, R Core Team, 2023). Several data transformations and processing steps were undertaken to prepare the data for further statistical analyses.

The PA task scores were averaged to create a composite PA score. Due to the non-normal distribution of PA composite scores, a rank transformation was applied to normalize the data between 0 and 1. Rank transformation helps meet the normality of residuals assumption by LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION mapping the data to a uniform distribution. This transformation served the dual purpose of normalizing the distribution and mitigating the impact of potential outliers.

As reading tasks had different scoring scales, the reading scores were standardized across all grades. This ensured that all scores were on the same scale for comparison. This approach enabled the comparison of reading performance and the creation of composite scores that combined data from multiple reading-related tasks. Composite scores for reading were calculated by combining standardized scores as follows: for grades 2, 3, and 4, the composite score was the average of the z-scores for Nonword and Passage reading; for grade 1, the composite score was the average of the z-scores for Letter Reading and Nonword Reading. These composite scores provided an overall measure of reading ability. In the next step, outliers were identified and removed from composite scores using the interquartile range method, resulting in the removal of 2 observations.

Participants' ability to discriminate between target and non-target syllables in the AV speech perception task was calculated using the sensitivity index d' based on the signal detection theory (Green & Swets, 1966). The d' scores were calculated based on hit (target phoneme is present, and the participant responds), miss (target phoneme is present, but the participant fails to respond), false alarm (target phoneme is not present, and the participant responds), and correct rejection (target phoneme is not present, and the participant makes no response) rates using the dprime function from the psycho package in R (Makowski, 2018). In the next step, outliers were identified and removed from d' scores using the interquartile range method, resulting in the removal of 8 observations. The raw d' scores were censored at about +4, resulting in a non-normal distribution.

4. Discussion

Our study explored the relationship between sensitivity to AV cues for speech perception, reading skills, and PA in Uzbek-speaking children aged 4 to 10 years. Our findings shed light on the intricate interplay between reading development and sensitivity to AV cues, particularly in the context of learning to read in Uzbek, a language with a transparent orthography. Broadly, our results showcase a relationship between reading skills and sensitivity to AV cues, with more LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION skilled readers displaying increased sensitivity to AV cues. However, our results show no mediation effect of PA in the relationship between reading and sensitivity to AV cues for Uzbek.

4.1 Reading Skills and AV Speech Sensitivity

In a simple way of testing whether the onset of exposure to reading instruction was associated with increased AV speech sensitivity, we compared groups of pre-readers (i.e., 5-6-year olds) and readers (i.e., 7-to 10-year olds). Contrary to our predictions, we did not find effects of pre-reader vs reader group on AV speech sensitivity. This might be due to potential influences of exposure to print materials and reading-related activities prior to formal schooling.

Children in our study population likely had some degree of reading exposure through activities such as being read to at home or in pre-school, exposure to nursery rhymes, and language games.

Previous research suggests that such early reading exposure contributes to the enhancement of vocabulary and PA, which are associated with increased sensitivity to AV cues (Jerger et al., 2018). Therefore, it is plausible that the lack of significant difference in AV sensitivity observed in readers vs pre-readers could be attributed, in part, to both groups receiving some reading exposure, be it at home or at school. However, the specific nature and extent of this preschool reading exposure were not assessed in our study, representing a limitation. Future research could benefit from exploring the role of preschool reading exposure and the home reading environment in shaping speech perception abilities and its interaction with formal reading training in school.

Moreover, it is important to note that the onset of reading instruction was confounded with age, and older children generally demonstrated better AV speech sensitivity compared to younger children. Isolating the effects of formal reading instruction on AV speech sensitivity (i.e., beyond the impacts of age) would require longitudinal research tracking individuals throughout pre-reading and reading stages to provide deeper insights into the developmental LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION trajectories of AV speech perception in readers vs pre-readers. Outside of contexts where children have limited access to written materials prior to starting school and the age at which children enroll in school is highly variable (i.e., low-resource contexts in the Majority World; Whitehead et al., 2024; Jasińska et al., 2023), comparisons of pre-readers and readers have an age confound. Hence, examining variation in reading skill in relation to AV speech perception is key.

Although exposure to reading instruction (i.e., being a reader relative to a non-reader) was not itself associated with greater AV speech sensitivity, stronger reading skills were associated with increased AV speech sensitivity.

This suggests that higher reading proficiency is associated with an overall enhanced ability to perceive and integrate auditory and visual information. Importantly, even after accounting for the effect of age, reading skill was still a significant predictor of speech perception. This indicates that while age-related factors predict speech perception, reading skill still explains additional variance in speech perception beyond age (Sekiyama & Burnham, 2008). However, it remains unclear whether higher reading skills lead to improved speech perception or if children with better speech perception tend to develop higher reading skills. The directionality of the relationship between AV speech sensitivity and reading skills necessitates further investigation assuming a longitudinal design.

4.2 PA, Reading Skills, and AV Speech Sensitivity in a Transparent Orthography

To investigate whether greater sensitivity to AV speech in stronger readers was related to stronger phonetic and visual integration (i.e., in PA), we explored the mediation effect of PA in the relationship between reading and sensitivity to AV cues. Even when controlling for mediating effects of PA, we found a significant total and direct effect of reading on sensitivity to AV cues, underlining the connection between reading proficiency and perceptual sensitivity, as observed in

LITERACY AND AUDIOVISUAL SPEECH PERCEPTION our previous models. However, PA did not significantly mediate the relationship between reading skill and AV speech sensitivity in Uzbek.

This lack of a mediation holds significance when viewed through the lens of literature emphasizing the transformative impact of reading acquisition on phonological representations (Harm and Seidenberg, 1999, 2004; Jasińska et al., 2024; Muneaux and Ziegler, 2004; Taft and Hambly, 1985; Taft, 2006). Prior studies have consistently shown that learning to read influences phonological representations; literate individuals demonstrate superior performance in phonological tasks compared to non-literate counterparts (Castro-Caldas et al., 1998; Reis & Castro-Caldas, 1997), indicating the enhancement of PA skills driven by reading exposure.

According to Pattamadilok et al. (2010), the initiation of reading acquisition introduces a novel input pathway connecting orthographic information to phonetic codes.

This integration reflects the brain's adeptness at processing orthographic as well as auditory input. In languages with opaque orthography, the multiple spelling variations for words with similar sounds result in less regular mapping, prompting increased cognitive effort to reconcile phoneme to grapheme inconsistencies, likely leading to prolonged reaction times for speech perception (Harm and Seidenberg, 1999; 2004; Pattamadilok et al., 2010). As a result, in opaque orthographic languages, the incongruity between spelling and pronunciation might necessitate reliance on multiple information sources, such as visual speech cues to a greater extent than the auditory-only speech cues, to comprehend spoken language effectively, at least in the contexts when visual speech cues are relevant. This echoes findings from languages with opaque orthography (Lalonde & Werner, 2021 for review), where children showed increased sensitivity to AV cues at the period that corresponds to the onset of reading instruction (age=6-9 years).

Our current findings, with a transparent orthography, such as Uzbek, suggest that challenges related to phoneme-grapheme correspondences could, to some extent (i.e., ~33%), contribute to differential sensitivity to AV speech cues at the onset of reading instruction. The direct and reliable mapping between orthography and phonology in a language like Uzbek might enable readers to effortlessly associate visual symbols with their corresponding auditory representations; i.e., without having to sort through multiple visual-to-auditory correspondences in multi-modal cue integration. This streamlined cognitive process is conducive to efficient decoding and comprehension of written and spoken language. As children learning to read in transparent orthographies progress in reading proficiency, their increased PA likely contributes to a heightened sensitivity to AV cues.

4.4 Cross-Linguistic Variation and Future Directions

It is imperative to recognize that speech development during childhood is a nuanced process that does not universally prioritize a singular modality over another. This foundational acknowledgment underscores the complexity inherent in understanding the developmental trajectory of speech perception in early life. While our study highlights the link between reading and AV speech perception, it is crucial to recognize that these findings might not fully capture the diversity of linguistic experiences across different languages. The link between reading acquisition and AV speech perception could vary significantly depending on linguistic characteristics such as phonological structure and orthographic transparency. Therefore, future research endeavors should aim to investigate these phenomena cross-linguistically to uncover the generalizability of our findings and to delineate the specificities of how reading influences speech perception in diverse linguistic contexts.

Conclusion

Our study revealed that more proficient readers displayed greater sensitivity to AV cues, and that the relationship between reading proficiency and AV speech sensitivity in a phonologically transparent language was not significantly dependent on PA. Future investigations should delve into diverse linguistic contexts, scrutinize age-related influences more comprehensively, and explore how the mediating role of PA on the relationship between reading proficiency and AV speech sensitivity might differ as a function of orthographic transparency in greater detail. Such efforts will contribute significantly to our understanding of how these elements converge and, in turn, inform the development of tailored interventions for children facing challenges in reading and speech domains.
