What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Despite age being a significant sociodemographic correlate of conspiratorial mentality, surprisingly little is known about its precursors and consequences among older people. Older people are often considered at risk of psychological distress, which often relates to conspiratorial mentality. Our study therefore assumed bidirectional relations between these constructs. Distress was represented by loneliness, anxiety, and depression. We also investigated potential moderation by social media use, as conspiracy theories tend to be disseminated on such media. We tested these associations linearly and quadratically using data from a two-wave longitudinal study of Czech adults aged 60+ (NW1 = 4133, MageW1 = 69.1). Conspiratorial mentality was not predicted by other variables, indicating that these are probably not risk factors for such mentality. Conversely, conspiratorial mentality positively predicted only anxiety. Social media use moderation and quadratic effects were unsupported. Overall, the study suggests that conspiratorial mentality can be an anxiety-provoking experience among older people.

Introduction

Conspiracy theories allege that two or more powerful individuals (or groups) have secretly colluded to bring about a nefarious outcome to benefit themselves and harm the common good (Biddlestone et al., 2025; Bowes et al., 2023). Belief in such theories is considered widespread in the contemporary interconnected world and has the potential to infiltrate public discourse. These beliefs are also associated with outcomes that are harmful to our society, such as undermining democracy (e.g., Albertson & Guiler, 2020; Mari et al., 2022) or motivating political violence (Rousis et al., 2022; Vegetti & Littvay, 2022). Given that conspiracy theories are widespread on the internet, it is no surprise that social media use is considered a risk factor for conspiratorial mentality (Pilch et al., 2023), despite its impact not reaching the commonly feared potential (Douglas et al., 2019).

The potential harm is not only at the societal level, but negative consequences can also occur for the individual. Belief in conspiracy theories is often associated with worse individual well-being or mental health (Bowes et al., 2023; Flaskerud, 2021; Pilch et al., 2023; Zwar et al., 2022), although robust directionality is not yet established. Explanations in the existing literature suggest that conspiracy theories are especially attractive to people seeking to satisfy basic unmet psychological needs, specifically those rooted in epistemic, existential, and social motives (Biddlestone et al., 2025; Douglas et al., 2017). Respectively, people believe in conspiracies to find comprehensive explanations for complex phenomena, to understand uncontrollable existential threats and regain a sense of autonomy over them, and to maintain and defend the image of oneself and the groups to which one belongs. However, conspiratorial mentality may be more appealing than satisfying as it does not necessarily fulfil these motives in a healthy way (Liekefett et al., 2021; van Prooijen, 2019). That is, conspiratorial mentality can lead to psychological distress that was formerly tried to cope with. This might be one of the reasons that belief in conspiracies is often associated with symptoms of psychological ill-being, such as anxiety (Bowes et al., 2023; Pilch et al., 2023), loneliness (Biddlestone et al., 2025; Bierwiaczonek et al., 2024) or depression (Pilch et al., 2023; Zwar et al., 2022).

Although this motivational mechanism received general support, evidence on more specific age cohorts is scarcer. This is surprising because age is considered one of the most important sociodemographic correlates of conspiratorial mentality (Bordeleau & Stockemer, 2025). The present paper focuses on conspiratorial mentality among the older population. Although existing research suggests that young people tend to believe in conspiracy theories more than older people (Bordeleau & Stockemer, 2025; Enders et al., 2024; Klofstad et al., 2019), this does not imply that older people are without any risks. Specifically, older people are more likely to disseminate false information online (Brashier & Schacter, 2020; Guess et al., 2019; Ramos & Van Boven, 2026) and can turn to potentially conspiratorial online communities to deal with unpleasant experiences to which older adults are more at risk, such as social isolation (Meini & Rosola, 2025), friendship breakdowns (Vivion et al., 2024), or anxiety (Jafarnezhad Dehmiani et al., 2025). Furthermore, although generally having lower levels of conspiratorial mentality, older adults might still be more susceptible to believing specific conspiracy theories (Bordeleau & Stockemer, 2025), such as those related to health (e.g., about vaccines; Enders & Smallpage, 2019; Klofstad et al., 2019).

In this paper, we aim to expand this line of research and focus on the older population. Although they are much less studied, this age group can still be at risk, especially of relational breakdowns, which might foster their susceptibility to conspiracy theories and their dissemination online. Specifically, we want to longitudinally investigate the association between conspiratorial mentality and selected factors of psychological distress (i.e., loneliness, anxiety, and depression). Given that people can encounter conspiracy theories on social media, where these theories are often disseminated (Douglas et al., 2019), we also consider the role of social media use.

Conspiratorial mentality among older people

Although older age is associated with a lower tendency to conspiratorial mentality (Bordeleau & Stockemer, 2025; Enders et al., 2024; Klofstad et al., 2019), some inconsistencies exist in the literature. Specifically, some studies found that older people were more likely to be exposed to and share conspiracy theories on social media (Brashier & Schacter, 2020; Grinberg et al., 2019; Guess et al., 2019). Studies have proposed several explanations for this finding, typically referring to the decreased ability to discriminate between false and accurate information among older people (e.g., Guess et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2024). However, such an approach can be overly simplistic and potentially harmful due to neglecting the experiences of older people. For example, older people tend to have more fragile social networks and are thus at risk of loneliness or social isolation (Vivion et al., 2024). Other authors link these experiences to internet use, as older people often have lower digital literacy when dealing with online news media. Combined with interpersonal trust, which tends to be higher among older people, this can also potentially foster their susceptibility to conspiracy theories, especially when shared by familiar people online (Brashier & Schacter, 2020).

Several characteristics of older adults may also increase their vulnerability to conspiratorial mentality. Older people can experience increased stress, anxiety, or worries when encountering online (mis)information and when experiencing information overload (Vivion et al., 2024). Moreover, Meini and Rosola (2025) argue that older people might be more susceptible to false information online not only due to epistemic distress (i.e., the need for coherent information) but also due to social isolation and loneliness. In other words, seeking reinforcement of social identity and a sense of belonging can lead one to fall easily into online "epistemic bubbles" (Meini & Rosola, 2025), which may also be associated with exposure to conspiracy theories. Furthermore, loneliness is generally considered a risk for older people (e.g., Nyqvist et al., 2021; Xing et al., 2020), and it is also associated with psychological ill-being (Dahlberg et al., 2021; Fernandez-Rodrigues et al., 2022; Hubbard et al., 2021).

At the same time, the relationship may also operate in the opposite direction. That is, unmet psychological needs may not only contribute to higher levels of conspiratorial mentality, but conspiratorial mentality itself may further increase psychological distress because it ultimately fails to satisfy those needs. For example, people might turn to conspiracy theories for epistemic certainty and to alleviate anxiety associated with uncertainty. However, given that conspiracies are inherently uncertain, they can be anxiety-provoking and promote the psychological distress that they were originally trying to mitigate (Bowes et al., 2023; Liekefett et al., 2023; van Prooijen, 2019).

Additionally, it is essential to consider the role of social media, where conspiracy theories are often disseminated, and older people can engage with them. Social media use might also explain differences in conspiratorial mentality across age cohorts, as young people are more engaged with new technologies and social media (e.g., Deckman, 2024) and are potentially more exposed to conspiracy theories. This exposure, either intentional or accidental, makes social media use a potential risk factor for conspiratorial mentality (Pilch et al., 2023). Thus, older people may be less likely to think conspiratorially because they tend to be less active on social media, and higher social media use might be linked to stronger conspiratorial mentality among them.

Current study

In this study, we focus on conspiratorial mentality (i.e., the general belief that conspiracies are happening) among older people, who, despite being generally less prone to such mentality, are not without risks. We focus on three factors of psychological distress associated with belief in conspiracy theories: anxiety, loneliness, and depressive symptoms. Based on existing literature, these factors can serve either as predictors (i.e., conspiratorial mentality seen as "coping" against these experiences or as an attempt to satisfy unmet needs underlying them) or as outcomes (i.e., conspiratorial mentality being an unsatisfying attempt to satisfy unmet psychological needs). Thus, we assume bidirectional associations and employ a twowave longitudinal survey to establish the direction of these relationships.

Additionally, existing literature suggests that social media use may be a risk factor for conspiratorial mentality, as conspiracy theories are widespread on such platforms and people often engage with them (e.g., by reading or sharing them). Thus, we assume that psychological factors, on their own, might not necessarily be associated with the adoption of a conspiratorial mentality among older people, but that this association is more pronounced among those who are more active on social media. This is because people seeking to mitigate unpleasant psychological experiences may engage in conspiratorial mentality more when they are also more exposed to conspiracy theories online.

Based on the reasoning above, we proposed the following hypotheses: H1. Anxiety, loneliness, and depressive symptoms will positively predict conspiratorial mentality.

H2. Conspiratorial mentality will positively predict anxiety, loneliness, and depressive symptoms.

H3. Social media use will positively predict conspiratorial mentality. H4. Anxiety, loneliness, and depressive symptoms will positively predict conspiratorial mentality more strongly with higher social media use.

Additionally, we also explore the possibility that at least some of the proposed effects are nonlinear rather than linear. Specifically, it is possible that only markedly higher levels of conspiratorial mentality predict psychological distress. For example, low or mild levels of conspiratorial mentality might represent healthy epistemic scepticism that does not necessarily cause further distress, but only high levels can become more disturbing. This aligns with the fact that belief in conspiracies can be anxietyprovoking, which might be especially pronounced for people with strong conspiratorial beliefs. Thus, we also investigate the quadratic effects of the hypothesised associations to explore this possibility of nonlinearity. Including quadratic effects is also beneficial when testing interactions for detecting potentially spurious moderating effects (e.g., Belzak & Bauer, 2019; MacCallum & Mar, 1995).

Because existing literature overwhelmingly tests only linear effects, this part is exploratory. Also, given that this work is based on secondary analyses, it was not preregistered.

Method Participants

This study uses two-wave longitudinal data on Czech older adults with a time lag of approximately twelve months. The data were collected as part of a larger project (blinded for review). As this project is still ongoing at the time of writing this work, data will be publicly available after the end of the project in 2029. Participants were recruited via a professional agency and were compensated in accordance with the agency's rules. Informed consent was obtained at the beginning of the questionnaire, and participants could withdraw from the study at any time during data collection. Ethical approval for the study was obtained by the (blinded for review).

We conducted a power analysis using semPower.powerPath command from the semPower package (v. 2.1.3; Moshagen & Brader, 2023) in R, targeting a power of .80 and an alpha level of .05. The results indicated a sample of 2071 participants having sufficient power for testing cross-lagged medium effects (β = .07; Orth et al., 2024) between two relatively stable (.70) variables in a two-wave cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) with five variables in each wave.

The sample comprised 4133 participants (2257 females and 1876 males) aged 60 to 95 years (M = 69.15, SD = 6.07) in Wave 1.2769 participants (1517 females and 1251 males) also participated in Wave 2. In Wave 1, 1323 (32.0%) participants had completed higher education (i.e., undergraduate, postgraduate), and 2810 (68.0%) had secondary or lower education.

Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) on demographics and variables included in the main analysis was used to examine attrition between Wave 1 and Wave 2. Results indicated that participants who completed both waves differed significantly from those who dropped out after Wave 1 (Pillai's V = .01, F(8, 4030) = 7.41, p < .001). Univariate tests revealed statistically significant (i.e., p > .05) differences for loneliness, anxiety, social media use, age, and education (i.e., people with lower education were more likely to drop out). However, none of these reached the threshold for small effect sizes (i.e., η² = .01), indicating that the differences across waves were overall negligible.

Measures

Conspiratorial mentality was measured by the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire (Bruder et al., 2013). The scale consisted of five items (e.g., "Government agencies closely monitor all citizens") rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 -Completely disagree to 5 -Completely agree). Due to the high residual correlations between two of the items (i.e., r > .10), we decided to drop one of the problematic items with the lowest factor loading ("Politicians usually do not tell us the true motives for their decisions"; λW1 = .62, λW2 = .51) from the model. The final scale demonstrated an internal consistency of McDonald's ω = .86 in Wave 1 and McDonald's ω = .85 in Wave 2.

Loneliness was measured by the three-item version of the UCLA loneliness scale (Hughes et al., 2004). The items (e.g., "I feel left out") were rated on a four-point frequency scale (1 -Never to 4 -Often). The scale demonstrated an internal consistency of McDonald's ω = .82 in Wave 1 and McDonald's ω = .85 in Wave 2.

Depression and anxiety were measured by the Czech version of the DASS-21 (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995; Vilimovský & Kučera, 2018) questionnaire. Depression was measured with seven items (e.g., "I felt as if I was unable to experience any pleasant feelings") and anxiety was measured by six items (e.g., "I felt dry in my mouth."). Both scales used a four-point frequency response scale (1 -Didn't apply at all to 4 -It applied almost all the time). Both scales demonstrated an internal consistency of McDonald's ω = .92 for depression and McDonald's ω = .87 for anxiety in Wave 1, and McDonald's ω = .94 for depression and McDonald's ω = .85 for anxiety in Wave 2. Social media use was measured by one item, "How often do you do these activities? -Using social media". The item used a five-point frequency response scale (1 -At least once a day to 5 -Not at all in the past year). The responses were reversecoded for the analyses so that higher values represent higher social media use.

Wordings of all items are provided in Online Supplementary Material 1. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported the unidimensionality and at least longitudinal metric measurement invariance of all included scales. Factor loadings and the results of the measurement invariance tests are provided in Online Supplementary Material 2.

Analysis

The hypotheses were tested in lavaan 0.7-3 (Rosseel, 2012) through a CLPM (for a conceptual diagram, see Figure 1). Conspiratorial mentality, loneliness, anxiety, and depression were specified as latent factors with multiple indicators. The number of indicators for anxiety and depression was lowered to three through parcelling by using an item-to-construct technique (Little et al., 2002). Because the response scale for loneliness represented frequencies in ordered categories, we specified indicators for this scale as ordered categorical by the ordered command in lavaan and used the WLSMV estimator. We also included age, gender, and education as time-invariant confounders for "control variables" purposes. Missing data were handled by pairwise deletion. The analysis consisted of two major steps. First, we tested H1-H3 using the twowave CLPM, which included only the main effects. Second, H4 was tested by extending the model to include interaction terms between social media use and loneliness, anxiety, and depression. The interactions were analysed using the modsem package (v.1.0.16; Slupphaug et al., 2025), creating the interaction terms via the product indicator approach with double mean centering. Finally, we conducted exploratory analyses by including quadratic terms in the modsem model. Because models with latent interaction variables tend to produce incorrect estimates of model fit, we treated the fit indices of the first model (with main effects only) as a lower bound for the model with interaction terms, as suggested by Schoeman and Jorgensen (2021). We also conducted a robustness check with listwise deletion for interaction and quadratic models. Since models with interaction terms tend to yield incorrect estimates of model fit, we will examine changes in R2s for H4's dependent variable (i.e., conspiratorial mentality at Wave 2) across models to identify the bestfitting model for interpretation. For this, we will employ Hayes's (2021) saturated correlate approach (i.e., specifying correlation instead of regression between the predictor and the outcome to calculate R² for the simpler model) to compute ΔR 2 and use Cohen's (1988) cutoffs to evaluate ΔR 2 size (.02 for small, .13 for medium, and .26 for large differences, respectively). Because CLPMs include autoregressive effects that represent the stability of the variables in question, we used guidelines outlined by Orth et al. (2024) to infer cross-lagged effect sizes (i.e., β = .03 for small, β = .07 for medium, and β = .12 for large).

For the R script for these analyses, see Electronic Supplementary Materials 3.

Apart from that, there were several significant temporal relations between loneliness, depression, and anxiety. Social media use had no significant temporal association with other variables. Also, given the significant autoregressive effects, all variables can be considered relatively stable. For the full results, see Supplementary Materials 4. The interaction model converged without issues. However, interaction effects were overall non-significant (with βs ranging from -.029 to .055), and thus H4 was not supported. As a result, we tested the quadratic model without these interaction terms.

Because the model with all quadratic effects yielded improper solutions (i.e., standardised coefficients exceeding 1), we tested the quadratic effects of loneliness, depression, and anxiety separately from the quadratic effects of conspiratorial mentality. Overall, three of the quadratic effects were statistically significant: the effects of anxiety (β = .079, p = .011, 95%CI [.019;.139]) and social media use (β = -.066, p = .001, 95%CI [-.106;-.025]) on conspiratorial mentality, and the effect of conspiratorial mentality on loneliness (β = -.071, p = .003, 95%CI [-.117;-.025]). See Figures 3, 4, and 5 for plots of these effects. However, the inclusion of none of these quadratic effects exceeded ΔR2 = .02 for their respective dependent variable ( ΔR2 = .014 for conspiratorial mentality, ΔR2 = .005 for loneliness, ΔR 2 < .001 for depression, ΔR2 = .001 for anxiety, and ΔR 2 < .001 for social media use). This indicates that the quadratic effect explains only a negligible portion of the variance in their dependent variables. Discussion

This work examined the longitudinal relationships between conspiratorial mentality and several indicators of psychological distress and social media use among older people. Overall, the results did not support the hypotheses that our indicators of psychological distress predict conspiratorial mentality. Although exploratory results on quadratic effects showed few significant results, those explained only a marginal amount of variance and thus will not be interpreted further to avoid overinterpretation. On the other hand, conspiratorial mentality positively predicted subsequent anxiety, suggesting that such a mentality style is more distress-provoking than satisfying among older people. Social media use did not moderate any of the hypothesised associations, suggesting that it might not be such a risk factor for conspiratorial mentality among older people.

Our main linear results provided no support for H1, suggesting that older people probably do not engage in conspiratorial mentality to cope with their psychological distress. One potential explanation is that older people are more ingrained in society and have stable lifestyles (Bordeleau & Stockemer, 2025; Enders et al., 2024), thus having potentially other coping mechanisms. Alternatively, the association may not have manifested because older people have more ingrained thinking styles (either conspiratorial or otherwise) and are thus less likely to change in response to prior distress in comparison to younger people. It is also possible that many people engage in a conspiratorial mentality for reasons other than compensating for their distress, ranging from simple entertainment (van Prooijen et al., 2022) to rationalising ideological affiliations (Pierre, 2025).

H2 was supported only regarding anxiety. In other words, conspiratorial mentality predicts higher anxiety, suggesting that it might be anxiety-provoking for older people. This can also be hypothetically associated with a greater need to satisfy existential motives (Biddlestone et al., 2025; Douglas et al., 2017), meaning that a conspiratorial mentality might heighten a sense of existential threat, compelling a person to address it. This corresponds to the fact that threat is a crucial component of conspiracy theories (Douglas & Sutton, 2018; van Prooijen & Vugt, 2018), and that existential threats are also a relevant covariate of conspiratorial mentality (Biddlestone et al., 2025). Given that conspiracy perception tends to exacerbate the perceived threats (van Prooijen, 2020), the results might also suggest a potential vicious cycle between conspiratorial mentality and anxiety. However, our results did not provide much support for such a cycle, which would require further research with more waves of data.

The role of social media use, hypothesised in H3 and H4, was not supported, suggesting that general social media use is probably not a risk factor regarding conspiratorial mentality among older people. Given that older people use social media primarily to connect with others (Cotten et al., 2022; Wilson et al., 2021), general social media use encompasses activities unrelated to conspiratorial mentality. Although the role of general social media use was not supported, it is still possible that more specific social media behaviours relate to conspiratorial mentality. Most notably, using social media to engage with online news may be more relevant, as this behaviour is associated with exposure to false information (Brashier & Schacter, 2020; Grinberg et al., 2019). Thus, it could be helpful for future research to incorporate more nuanced measures of online behaviour that capture a range of social media activities. Lastly, social media use may also be more closely related to beliefs in specific conspiracy theories than to a more general conspiratorial mentality.

Implications

Our main results imply that conspiratorial mentality can be an anxiety-provoking experience. While anxiety itself is not necessarily pathological, this can have implications for the well-being of older people. That does not mean that conspiratorial mentality necessarily leads to psychological disorders, as it is usually considered a rather more normative phenomenon. Nevertheless, strong conspiratorial mentality can still foster anxiety-related experiences or play an important role as a potential cognitive style in fostering potential future psychopathologies.

Given that anxiety is often associated with existential threats (Biddlestone et al., 2025; Douglas et al., 2017; van Prooijen, 2019), these threats may be an important outcome of older people's conspiratorial mentality. This is interesting in light of Bowes et al.'s (2023) meta-analysis, which suggests that social motives, in contrast to existential and epistemic, are the most supported. The role of existential threat, however, depended more on the measurement used. Given that Bowes et al. (2023) did not test the moderating effect of age, it is possible that the role of specific motives depends on this sociodemographic characteristic. As mentioned above, older people tend to have more stable lifestyles and worldviews (e.g., Bordeleau & Stockemer, 2025; Enders et al., 2024), potentially having other means of satisfying their social and epistemic motives. These inferences about potential age differences, however, would require further research, ideally with different cohorts.

Limitations

The first limitation is the use of only two waves of data. This significantly constrained our options for conducting more complex analyses that could yield more precise and robust inferences. For example, we could not distinguish between-and within-person levels of variance, which is an important feature of the random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (Ri-CLPM; Hamaker et al., 2015). Still, our research remains well-suited to addressing predictive research questions (Hamaker et al., 2020).

Also, the one-year interval between waves might have influenced our estimates. Existing literature typically does not provide robust guidance on appropriate time intervals, leaving researchers to make educated guesses (Zhao et al., 2024). Thus, our non-significant results do not necessarily indicate the absence of a psychological mechanism, as some effects might require a different time interval to manifest, and we advise caution when comparing our results with those from other research. Despite that, we believe our work provides important inferences, as we were interested in capturing more enduring experiences for which longer time intervals are more appropriate (Zhao et al., 2024).

Another limitation is the use of a one-item measure of social media use, which makes it impossible to assess internal consistency and measurement error, and which also means that this single item can encompass multiple potential activities on social media. Because of that, our results cannot provide further insight into the different forms of online behaviour that may be associated with conspiratorial mentality, especially those related to news or online information (e.g., Brashier & Schacter, 2020; Grinberg et al., 2019). Still, we believe that our results provide important insights into general social media use and underscore the need for a more nuanced approach to online behaviour.

Social desirability bias is also an issue here, as some respondents might have negative associations with the conspiratorial mentality measure. Some respondents might also underreport their psychological distress due to social desirability. This might be the case for anxiety and depression, where the distributions were more skewed in comparison to conspiratorial mentality and loneliness. However, we do not consider this drawback critical, as the data were obtained anonymously, the skewness was not substantial, and the variability of our variables remained sufficient.

Although not exactly a limitation, the issue of distinguishing between general conspiratorial mentality and belief in specific conspiracy theories should also be mentioned. Although the psychological mechanisms should, in general, operate in the same way in both cases, there is documented heterogeneity in empirical support depending on the measure used (Bowes et al., 2023). Thus, it is possible that the effect sizes or statistical significance would differ if we measured beliefs in specific conspiracy theories. However, as mentioned, we do not consider this a limitation per se, as conspiratorial mentality in general is also relevant and has important implications (Bowes et al., 2023).
