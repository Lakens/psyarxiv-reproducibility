Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Reading is a fundamental life skill, and at its basis is the ability to process single words.

Researchers often study single-word processing using lexical decision tasks. However, most research is conducted in a single language by separate research groups, limiting comparability, replicability, and generalizability. In addition, best-practice knowledge is often only transmitted informally within labs, leading to heterogeneity and reduced reproducibility across labs. Here, we, the TRUST Consortium, established a consensus-based research guideline for current best practices in psycholinguistic reading research by integrating expert knowledge. We used German as a case study and formulated guidelines for visual lexical decision experiments. We reached consortium-level consensus through multiple feedback circles and quantified agreement using importance ratings. The guidelines are openly available as a living guideline document on a webpage (https://trust-guidelines-v01.readthedocs.io/en/latest/), with git-versioning infrastructure that makes changes transparent and enables discussion. To validate the guidelines, we implemented the German Lexicon Project, providing an empirical feedback loop to test our suggestions. The living, open nature of the guidelines invites broader community feedback. It also enables future extension to other languages, groups (e.g., dyslexics), and methods (e.g., brain imaging), as it is openly available and includes instructions for hosting an adapted version at no cost. Thus, we provide a document intended to raise research standards in reading research worldwide, as a service to the community, potentially increasing replication rates and generalizability of relevant effects and phenomena.

Consensus-based TRansparent, sUStainable, and Transferable (TRUST) best-practice guidelines for visual word recognition research Psychological science faces scrutiny for low diversity [1, 2], lax scientific standards (e.g., low reproducibility [3]), low replicability [4]), and a lack of explicit, testable theories [5, 6, 7] even though in reading research several implemented models can be tested [8]. Addressing these concerns is essential to building a credible, generalizable evidence base that can support the development of theories and models of psychological phenomena [9, 10, 11], inform public outreach, and guide educational applications [e.g., 12, 13]. Making rigorous research standards explicit and accessible can help researchers produce and evaluate such evidence [e.g., 14] and, consequently, increase trust in scientific discovery. Within cognitive psychology, applying these standards requires attention to research questions, study designs, and participant characteristics.

Particularly, in language-related research, such as the study of reading, understanding how cognitive processing varies with language characteristics is central to understanding the phenomenon as a whole [6]. Nevertheless, our understanding of reading has largely relied on studies in English and its orthography [1, 15]. Efforts to articulate research standards in this area should therefore make their scope explicit and allow for contributions from researchers working across languages, populations, and methods.

Here, we provide a description of the TRansparent, sUStainable, and Transferable (TRUST) Guidelines for psycholinguistic reading studies: an open, living document with the goal of describing the research standards for psycholinguistic reading, based on the explicitly collected agreement of a group of reading research experts. We wrote the guidelines with a target audience in mind that includes new researchers. Open guidelines facilitate knowledge sharing with new researchers beyond individual labs, and we believe this also represents an important step toward increasing academic diversity in the field. As a general goal, we aim to promote the development of consensus-based guidelines that span perspectives. Our proposed guidelines can be easily expanded across domains: from basic to applied research, across languages, groups (e.g., multilinguals), and methodological approaches, for researchers with cognitive, educational, genetic, and neuroscientific foci.

Reading research has gained a wealth of insights from lexical decision paradigms (see Fig. 1), in which participants categorize lexical items (alphabetic strings of letters) as words or nonwords (e.g., [16, 8, 17]). Insights usually come from smaller studies posing specific questions on how we implement cognitive or neuro-cognitive mechanisms of reading and word recognition [e.g., 18, 19, 16], typical and atypical reading development [e.g., 20, 21], or crosslinguistic investigations [e.g., 22, 23]. On a larger scale, megastudies of lexical decision tasks -so-called Lexicon Projects [e.g., 24, 17, 25, 26] -also contribute valuable data to answer these questions, serving to identify which word-level characteristics affect reading efficiency and how they interact with individual differences [27]. From an applied perspective, lexical decision tasks have also been used to estimate individual differences in foundational word-level reading abilities or vocabulary size (e.g., [28, 29]).

Even in a classic, relatively simple two-alternative task like a lexical decision study, there are hundreds, if not thousands, of procedural, methodological, and statistical decisions that a researcher must make [30, 31]. In published articles, it is difficult to describe and justify every step, as many steps are not even perceived as explicit decisions (i.e., they may have become an implicit default; see [32]). Progress has been made as materials, experiments, data, and analysis scripts have become more openly available. Despite this, clear guidance or consensus on how best to implement and report a word-recognition study using lexical decision tasks remains lacking. Such methodological heterogeneity compounds other sources of heterogeneity inherent in psycholinguistic research (e.g., differences between languages or individuals), impeding comparisons and the integration of findings across studies, especially when investigating disputed phenomena [e.g., 23]. Thus, when conflicting results are reported, it becomes difficult to integrate the findings into a coherent understanding of the processes underlying word recognition. Rather than revealing theoretically relevant insights of TRUST 6 interest, differences between studies may instead stem from methodological choices, material-specific factors (e.g., effects differing between languages or stimulus sets), or participant-specific issues (e.g., effects differing between samples of a population), which can also inherently limit the informativeness of meta-analyses or systematic reviews, not to say that heterogeneity, when documented accurately, can be highly valuable.

To further complicate cross-lab comparisons, information on how to implement a task is often only transmitted informally within the lab [33]. Researchers often learn to conduct a study in a particular way (e.g., implementing a lexical decision task with a specific font or inter-stimulus interval) from established laboratory practices or methods reported in the literature. These procedures may have been informed by theoretical considerations, previous evidence, or practical piloting, but the rationale for individual choices is not always explicitly documented. Consequently, researchers adopting them may have limited information about why they were selected or whether they are appropriate for a new study. Sometimes, such procedures are based on informal piloting and testing to reduce noise and maximize effect sizes. Other choices lack justification and merely reflect the adoption of existing routines. Often, there is no consensus on which specific methodological choices matter in a lexical decision task. For example, we now know that how pseudoword foils are created affects word processing [16, 34, 35], a previously neglected issue because hypotheses largely focused on word reading. By contrast, little empirical work has examined how the type and timing of fixation targets before letter-string presentation affect cognitive processing (as opposed to other vision tasks; see [36, 37]). Furthermore, relying on within-lab transmission of the required knowledge may prevent researchers outside such a lab from conducting a state-of-the-art experiment, which, in turn, limits researcher and research diversity.

To provide explicit and accessible guidance on implementing and reporting visual word recognition studies, we formed the TRUST consortium of experienced reading researchers from Germany, Switzerland, Austria, and several non-German-speaking countries (see list https://gewonn.github.io/trust/network/) to produce the TRUST guidelines: consensus-based best-practice guidelines for visual word recognition research. As a starting point, we focused on typical readers reading in German. We chose German as a starting point mainly because it is the primary language of expertise of the consortium. German has unique features, such as extensive compounding [e.g., 38] or noun capitalization [e.g., 39, 40]. It also shares features with other non-English orthographies, such as relatively high letter-to-sound transparency in reading but not in writing, and the use of an inflectional orthography and an alphabetic system [41]. Furthermore, focusing on German offers advantages, as examining a language other than English can help overcome anglocentricity in reading research [15], and contributions from researchers focused on German have been substantial, helping to better understand cognitive, neurocognitive, and cross-linguistic word recognition mechanisms [e.g., 23, 42, 43, 44, 45]. From now on, anyone can adjust the guidelines by specifying language-specific features and adapting them to any target language.

Central to developing the guidelines was a consensus-building process [14] to identify variability in practices and assess whether we could reach agreement on best practices for different methodological and statistical steps. By providing both the resulting guidelines and information on how to apply the consensus-building process, we aim to offer well-founded best-practice guidance to reading researchers of all experience levels.

The guidelines are intended for researchers designing, conducting, or reporting lexical decision studies of visual word recognition, including those new to the area and experienced researchers seeking methodological guidance. The target audience includes cognitive and neurocognitive scientists unfamiliar with word recognition research, aspiring psycholinguistics researchers, and experienced researchers working across different domains of reading research. Furthermore, the guidelines can help researchers justify specific methodological choices, supported by explicit agreement, or transparently report and justify any deviations from our proposed guidelines. Researchers unfamiliar with German, or with the specifics of the German language, may also benefit from the guidelines, as they provide references to useful resources that introduce information specific to German language and orthography. This could help researchers conducting cross-linguistic research [e.g., 23], those interested in theoretically relevant research questions that can be addressed by leveraging specific features of the German language [e.g., inflection; 46], or researchers relocating to a German-focused lab. In doing so, we offer guidance to researchers with psycholinguistic expertise in other languages in transferring their research into German, even without personal proficiency in the language 1 . To ensure the practicality and completeness of the guidelines, we developed them in parallel to an implementation of the German Lexicon Project, a megastudy of lexical decisions in German. In the German Lexicon Project, TRUST Consortium members and additional associates tested the applicability of the guidelines by implementing them and collecting lexical decision data for tens of thousands of words and nonwords from thousands of participants across German-speaking countries.

We established the guidelines adhering to Open Science principles. We implemented the guidelines as a living document, open to extensions and updates, with a platform supporting expert discussion as the guidelines evolve. The main aims of the TRUST guidelines are: • Make methodological knowledge more accessible • Support transparent and reproducible research 1 Although theoretically, a lexical decision study can be conducted without knowledge of a language, it is advisable to consult speakers of the language to check the items when selecting and evaluating stimuli.

• Promote comparability across lexical decision studies

Such consultation can help identify unintended lexical, semantic, or cultural associations that may not be apparent from corpus-based measures and assess their relevance to the study (e.g., to remove words or pseudowords which may have negative cultural associations that are not evident from corpus data; examples in German may include terms that have an offensive connotation when used as slang).

Such that the guidelines provide best-practice guidance on procedures, materials, analysis, and reporting, promoting comparability across studies that use lexical decision tasks with adult readers in German. By creating them, we aim to improve the accessibility of methodological knowledge in reading research and to propose a workflow that encourages and enables researchers to share their materials, procedures, analysis scripts, and data with the wider research community. The guideline document is openly available and provides researchers across the behavioral and social sciences with a template for developing best-practice guidelines in their own tasks, fields, populations, or languages of interest.

Results

TRUST Guidelines

The guidelines consist of four sections: Procedure (see Fig. 2), Materials, Analysis, and Documentation. The full text of the guidelines is available here: https://trust-guidelines-v01.readthedocs.io/ Procedure. This section comprises eleven subsections (∼1,940 words) addressing considerations for implementing a lexical decision experiment. For example, there are subsections outlining considerations for programming the experiment (e.g., platforms, text encoding, trial structure), referencing resources for determining sample size via power analysis, and outlining possible participant inclusion criteria. We describe different design options, including controlled experiments, semi-controlled regression designs (e.g., lexicon projects), and crowd-sourced projects.

Materials.

The materials section includes 23 subsections (∼ 3,180 words) and addresses stimulus selection in general, as well as resources available for German more specifically. One subsection describes where to find words most effectively (i.e., in available psycholinguistic corpora). The next subsection describes variables to consider when characterizing words using psycholinguistic properties (e.g., word frequency). We also discuss how to account for "taboo" words and German-specific issues related to inflections, compound words, parts of speech, lemmatization, and TRUST 13 word-characteristic-based filtering. The guidelines similarly list variables to consider in nonword selection and recommend how best to create nonwords.

Analysis. The analysis section of the guidelines provides recommendations for data preprocessing, transformation, and statistical analysis of lexical decision data from typical readers, in six subsections (∼ 1,960 words). We describe sanity-check analyses (e.g., estimate the word frequency effect), discuss how best to assess the effect of interest (e.g., using crossed random effects with linear mixed models), and give some ideas about additional analysis options (e.g., model-based analysis).

Reporting. The final reporting section of the guidelines addresses how to report the results of psycholinguistic studies of German in twelve subsections (∼ 1,350 words).

This section provides crucial information on how to maximize reproducibility while avoiding information irrelevant to the methodology or cognition of reading. Here, we recommend how to report the rationale of a study, document procedures, contributions, and resources, and provide specific guidance on documenting methodology, experimental procedures, results, and analysis. Finally, we recommend optimal data storage and sharing.

Ratings

Inspecting how the consortium rated the importance of each item, we found that importance was rated at maximum ("very high") in more than half of the cases (57%; see Fig. 3a and separated for parts in b), as "high" in 28% of cases, and only 15% of ratings were 3 or below. We aggregated the ratings by calculating the median rating for each topic across participants. On this aggregated item level, half of the items had a median rating of 4, and the other half had a median rating of 5. The guideline homepage shows individual importance ratings next to each item using a 5-point indicator, where the number of red rectangles represents the median rating (e.g., indicates a median rating of 4). We can measure a proxy of consensus based on the variability of the ratings (see Fig. 3c). Only 7% of the ratings were more than 1 point from the median importance TRUST 15 rating (i.e., 31 ratings). In contrast, 53% of ratings were equal to the median, leaving 40% differing by 1 from the median. This low variability in ratings indicates high agreement among raters overall.

The seven most controversial items, across all the ratings, identified by a standard deviation threshold larger than 1, came from the materials (N = 3), reporting (N = 3), and procedure (N = 1) sections. For procedures, the "how to implement text encoding" item showed relatively high rating variability. For materials, ratings diverged substantially on the importance of word characteristics and how to filter on them, especially word length and frequency. For reporting, data storage, manuscript/publication recommendations, and documentation of contributions/sources, ratings diverged. When separating the data by guidelines part, we found that the analysis part was the least controversial (Mean distant rating = .29; see Fig. 3d), and, as indicated by the most controversial items identified above, that materials (M = .52), reporting (M = .59), and procedure (M = .52) were slightly more controversial.

Discussion

We describe best-practice consensus-based guidelines for implementing a lexical decision task for typical adult readers with a focus on German. We obtained the guidelines through a consensus-finding procedure within the TRUST Consortium, which consists of expert reading researchers. We identified and collated well-established practices across labs described in the procedure, materials, analysis, and reporting sections. To validate the guidelines, we also implemented the German Lexicon Project All experts contributed content to the guidelines and gave feedback at multiple stages. Our guidelines may be used as a template for implementing guideline documents for the same task in a different language, a different task in German, or for a consensus-building process in practice (see also [9] for an example in a different field).

In addition, the guidelines in their current form may not be fully complete, as the consortium may not encompass all relevant expertise, and some topics may have been overlooked or inadequately addressed. However, the current guidelines, as a living document using GitHub, allow researchers beyond the consortium to comment on individual recommendations and propose additions or revisions. From now on, researchers can change the TRUST guidelines using the established mechanism (e.g., raising an issue for discussion) already provided in the open repository.

As part of the guidelines creation process, consortium members provided importance ratings. These importance ratings are provided alongside the title of each corresponding subsection in the guidelines document. All items received median ratings of 4 or 5, showing that experts consistently identified the issues we focused on as important. We also used the ratings to estimate consensus among members. The results indicated relatively high consensus, as only a small fraction of items showed considerable differences between raters. Inspecting the ratings for each part separately, we found that the analysis section, in particular, was the least controversial among experts, although all other sections showed similarly low divergence between raters. Thus, we have evidence that the procedure effectively aggregated the knowledge of a medium-sized group of experts and produced a document with high consensus. This result is not challenge: First, researchers must keep in mind that the information may not always reflect the most current best practice, as standards may have recently changed. Second, a dedicated and enthusiastic community will play an important role in continuously monitoring and updating the guideline document.

Our open, consensus-based guideline document is a step towards increasing continuity in studies conducted across labs in reading research, concerned with visual word recognition. We not only established and documented consensus about the guidelines, but also ensured they could serve as a template for similar initiatives seeking to reduce researchers' degrees of freedom across different languages, populations, and measurement tasks. Furthermore, the guidelines are informed by their implementation in the German Lexicon Project, which successfully tested their applicability. Thus, it offers not only a platform for methodological discussion but also the foundation for new, higher-quality studies to better understand human reading and word recognition processes across labs.
