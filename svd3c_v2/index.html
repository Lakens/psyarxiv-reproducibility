Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 35 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Humans often learn from outcomes defined by abstract goals rather than explicit rewards. This flexibility supports real-world behavior but typically causes slower and less efficient learning compared to directly observable rewards. We asked where the cognitive bottleneck arises as abstract goals are transformed into learning signals. The reward function compression hypothesis proposes that this inefficiency arises because evaluating abstract goal outcomes burdens limited cognitive resources otherwise directed toward learning, and that this cognitive cost can decrease over iterations when a specialized, efficient goal-dependent outcome evaluation function is learned. Alternatively, this cost could simply arise from an immediate dual-task effect, where goal maintenance imposes a cost on decisions. To distinguish these two hypotheses, we manipulated the visibility of goal and non-goal information throughout learning and feedback. While the memory-offloading account predicts that this manipulation should immediately rescue goal-based learning, the reward function compression hypothesis predicts a gradually acquired benefit proportional to the compressibility of the match/mismatch rule. Our results (N = 61) supported the reward compression hypothesis. Visible goals gradually reduced learning efficiency costs, eventually approaching the point-based learning performance. An equivalent decrease in memory load (visible non-goals) did not boost learning as much, highlighting the role of outcome evaluation in goal-directed learning. Computational modeling revealed that outcome evaluation efficiency, proxied by participants' "reward collection" response time, specifically influenced participants' learning processes, rather than decision noise. These results show that learning from goals depends on our ability to train efficient estimators of outcome values, which is facilitated by keeping goals in sight.

1 Introduction

In daily life, much of human learning is guided by abstract goals we pursue -such as mastering Chinese calligraphy, finding a favorite hiking route or perfecting a baking recipe -that go beyond obtaining tangible rewards like food or money. Goals define what we want to achieve and give value to the outcomes of our actions, providing signals that guide learning. For example, when learning a master calligrapher's style, we compare our brushstrokes with their work to evaluate each attempt and track our progress. The criteria for this evaluation can change with our subjective goals or the context [Molinaro and Collins, 2023a , Castegnetti et al., 2021 , Frömer et al., 2019]. We may focus on the shape of individual strokes when reproducing a particular model, but place greater weight on the overall flow when aiming for a more expressive style. Learning guided by goals is therefore highly flexible, but this flexibility requires learners to continually translate outcomes into reinforcement signals that can guide learning, based on their current goals.

The flexibility of goal-dependent learning comes at cognitive cost. When the value of an outcome must be derived from an abstract goal rather than provided directly by familiar rewards, such as numeric points, learning is slower and SIGHT -NOVEMBER 12, 2026 less accurate [McDougle et al., 2022, Molinaro and Collins, 2023b]. Despite these behavioral costs, goal-derived and explicit rewards appear to rely on overlapping reward-learning circuitry. Goal-congruent outcomes evoke reward-like signals in corticostriatal circuits, including shared value representations and reward-prediction-error signals [McDougle et al., 2022]; similarly, cognitive feedback (e.g., a green circle for correct and a red circle for incorrect) and monetary rewards recruit largely overlapping dopaminergic and striatal regions during learning [Daniel and Pollmann, 2010].

The critical difference may therefore lie not in the downstream learning system itself, but in the additional executive processes required to derive the value of an outcome from the current goal. Indeed, learning from novel goal-defined outcomes is associated with increased interactions between prefrontal control regions and subcortical reward circuits [McDougle et al., 2022]. This raises a central question: which cognitive operations make the transformation from a goal and an observed outcome into a learning signal costly? Identifying these operations is essential for understanding the cognitive bottleneck of goal-dependent learning.

One possibile cause of this additional cost is the working-memory demand of maintaining the current goal. In typical goal-dependent learning tasks, learners must actively maintain the goal information until feedback is received [McDougle et al., 2022]. Maintaining goal-relevant information is itself a core function of working memory and cognitive control [Kane and Engle, 2003, Braver, 2012]. Working-memory contributions to learning are capacity limited, with learning performance declining as memory load increases [Collins and Frank, 2012], and imposing an additional working-memory load concurrently with instrumental learning can further impair learning performance [Huang et al., 2025]. Maintaining the current goal may therefore consume limited working-memory resources, imposing a relatively general cognitive cost throughout the learning process (Figure 1A).

A second possibility is that the additional cost arises more specifically during outcome evaluation. Whereas explicit numeric rewards such as (+1) or (+0) directly specify the value of an outcome, abstract feedback must be evaluated relative to the learner's current goal before it can provide a learning signal. This computation can be described by a reward function evaluation, which assigns value to an outcome according to the current goal. Recent behavioral work suggests that this goal-dependent outcome evaluation may itself constitute an important bottleneck. Faster goaldependent reward processing was associated with better learning, and learning improved when the goal space contained structure that allowed goal information to be simplified [Molinaro and Collins, 2026]. This work proposes that, when the goal space contains shared structure, learners can reduce this cost through reward-function compression, extracting regularities across goals and transforming complex goal information into a simpler rule that can be applied more efficiently when evaluating subsequent outcomes. Under this account, the additional cognitive demand is concentrated around feedback, when an observed outcome must be converted into a value that can update learning (Figure 1B).

To distinguish these possible mechanisms, one strategy is to externalize goal information by keeping it visible throughout learning, including when feedback is received. Externalizing goal-relevant information is common in everyday learning, such as reminders, checklists, and reference materials, which can reduce the need to maintain information internally [Risko and Gilbert, 2016 , Stein et al., 2016 , Gilbert et al., 2020]. By shifting part of the representational burden from internal memory to the environment, these external cues can preserve access to goal information while freeing limited working-memory resources for other computations involved in learning.

Goal visibility, however, may do more than simply reduce memory load: it may also change how feedback is processed.

If the primary cost arises from goal maintenance, keeping the goal visible should reduce the general working-memory demand of maintaining and retrieving it throughout learning. If the cost instead arises during outcome evaluation, having the goal visible may allow learners to directly compare an observed outcome with the desired outcome, rather than first retrieving the goal from memory. Returning to Chinese calligraphy, during línmó practice, learners can keep a master calligrapher's copybook visible while practicing and directly compare each produced stroke with the desired form. Repeated access to such a reference may also allow learners to develop a simpler, reusable comparison-to-value rule for evaluating subsequent outcomes. This possibility connects goal visibility to reward-function compression: whereas compression may emerge by extracting structure from internally represented goals, externally available goal information may provide another source of structure that simplifies the computation of outcome value.

A visible non-goal provides an important comparison. Like a visible goal, it externalizes task-relevant outcome information and may therefore provide similar memory support. Critically, however, it changes how the external reference must be mapped onto value: a match with a visible goal signals a desirable outcome, whereas a match with a visible non-goal signals an undesirable outcome. Previous reinforcement-learning studies suggest that positive and negative outcomes can sometimes be weighted differently during learning, although such asymmetries are not universal and may partly reflect other choice processes [Palminteri et al., 2017 , Katahira, 2018 , Sugawara and Katahira, 2021 , Lefebvre et al., 2017 , Frank et al., 2007]. More broadly, valuation can flexibly reverse with the learner's current goal, such that the same option is processed differently when the goal is to identify the best versus the worst outcome [Frömer et al., 2019]. Comparing visible goal and visible non-goal conditions can therefore help distinguish a general benefit of externalizing information from a more specific benefit of making the desired outcome directly available during outcome evaluation.

The current study asks how abstract goals are transformed into learning signals and which cognitive operations create the cost of this transformation. We use goal visibility to probe this process, asking how making goal information externally available facilitates goal-dependent learning and which cognitive operation best explains this benefit. Specifically, we characterize the visibility benefit along two dimensions: its dynamics over experience and its magnitude across visibility conditions (Figure 1C).

First, the time course of the benefit can distinguish a general goal-memory offloading account from an experiencedependent change in outcome evaluation. If visibility primarily reduces the need to maintain goal information in working memory (Figure 1A), its benefit should be available as soon as the goal is externally accessible, producing an immediate advantage that is already present in Phase 1 and remains in Phase 2 (Figure 1D&E). In contrast, if visibility facilitates the development of a more efficient comparison-to-value rule during outcome evaluation (Figure 1B), the benefit may emerge gradually with experience and become larger in Phase 2 (Figure 1F&G).

Second, the relative magnitude of the VG and VNG benefits can reveal whether the mechanism supported by visibility operates similarly across different external references. A symmetric benefit would suggest that VG and VNG provide comparable support, either by offloading similar memory demands or by enabling similarly efficient mappings from feedback to value (Figure 1D&F). In contrast, an asymmetric benefit would indicate that the identity of the external reference matters: the two conditions may differ in how much memory demand they alleviate or in how readily the visible reference can be used to evaluate feedback (Figure 1E&G).

Together, the temporal dynamics and relative magnitude of the visibility benefit can reveal whether goal visibility primarily relieves a general memory burden or changes how goal information is used to evaluate outcomes, helping identify where the cognitive bottleneck arises as abstract goals are transformed into learning signals.

2.1 Participants

Sixty-six participants were recruited via Prolific (http://www.prolific.com), provided informed consent, completed the experiment online on their own devices, and received monetary compensation ($12 for approximately an hour of their time). The study protocol was approved by the [anonymous University's] Institutional Review Board. To ensure task engagement and reliable learning, five participants were excluded for poor data quality: more than 25 missed trials across the experiment; more than 25 consecutive identical choices; or reward-collection sensitivity (d ′ ) less than or equal to 0, following elbow points in the ordered distribution of group-level performance [Molinaro and Collins, 2023b, 2026]. The final analyses included 61 participants (25 female; M age = 28.97 ± 5.97 years).

2.2 Experiment

The experimental design was adapted from prior work on goal-dependent reinforcement learning [McDougle et al., 2022, Molinaro and Collins, 2026]. Participants learned stimulus-action associations by selecting one of three response keys based on trial-by-trial feedback, which was presented as either numerical point outcomes or novel fractal images, depending on the condition (Figure 2). The experiment began with 16 practice trials using the same procedure as the Points and Goal conditions (eight trials each) in the learning stage, followed by 528 learning trials divided into eight 66-trial blocks with a short resting period between blocks, and a final 144-trial testing stage.

The learning stage comprised four conditions: Points, Goals, Visible Goals (VG), and Visible Non-Goals (VNG). Each condition was presented for two independent blocks in pseudo-randomized order; we defined the first presentation of each condition as Phase 1 and the second as Phase 2. Each block introduced six new stimulus-action associations, with 11 iterations per stimulus. Stimuli within a block were highly discriminable and nameable images that belonged to the same semantic categories (e.g., six vegetable images). Trial sequences were pseudo-randomized to produce an approximately uniform distribution of repetition lags, ranging from 1 to 11 trials between successive presentations of the same stimulus.

Model estimation and evaluation followed established best-practice recommendations for computational modeling [Wilson and Collins, 2019].

The optimization was repeated from 40 starting points, including one default initialization and multiple random initializations, and the best-fitting solution was retained.

Model evidence was summarized using participant-level ∆AIC, group mean ∆AIC with standard errors, and the proportion of participants best fitted by each model. Subject-level ∆AIC values were tested against zero using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

Model space exploration.

Model comparison proceeded in stages. First, simpler models with fixed parameter values ablating specific mechanisms (e.g. decay, learning asymmetry, mixture) were compared with the full model to assess the contribution of individual model components. Second, static-and dynamic-noise models were compared. Third, we compared models in which RCRT separately modulated each parameter to identify which parameter yielded the greatest improvement in model fit. Fourth, we compared RCRT with choice RT, hit RCRT, and correct-rejection RCRT to test whether block RCRT provided a better account of choice behavior than the alternative reaction-time measures.

Model validation.

To validate the winning model against competing models, 40 agents were simulated for each participant and model using the participant-specific fitted parameters and observed task structure. Behavioral measures were first averaged across simulated agents within each participant and then summarized across participants. Model validation assessed how well each model reproduced the observed learning curves and participant-level accuracy.

Model recovery.

We sought to verify model identifiability within groups of models we relied on to arbitrate between competing accounts of our findings. Specifically, we separately assessed identifiability of the best three single-parameter modulation models (α, κ, and ϕ); and identifiability of the full three-parameter modulation model, the p ER -modulated, and a baseline model. For each participant and generating model, we simulated 10 datasets using participant-specific fitted parameters. Results were summarized in row-normalized confusion matrices.

Parameter recovery. 3.1 Learning performance differs across conditions and phases.

3.1.1 Learning Stage

Participants learned stimulus-action associations across all conditions: accuracy increased with stimulus iteration (χ 2 (1) = 914.67, p < .0001), and learning slopes were positive in every condition and phase (all ps < .001). Learning accuracy also differed by condition (χ 2 (3) = 85.00, p < .0001; Figure 3A). Observed learning accuracy was highest for Points (76.4%; all ps < .0001), followed by VG (67.7%) and VNG (62.9%), which did no differ significantly (p = .197), and lowest for Goal (56.8%; all ps ≤ .0055). Accuracy was higher in Phase 2 than Phase 1 (χ 2 (1) = 31.51, p < .0001; Figure 3A).

Crucially, learning trajectories differed systematically by condition and evolved over time in each condition, reflected in a significant condition × phase × stimulus iteration interaction (χ 2 (3) = 8.63, p = .035; Figure 3B&C).

Except for Goal, all learning conditions showed steeper learning slopes in Phase 2 than in Phase 1 (all ps < .05; Goal: p = .116). In Phase 1 (Figure 3B), Points showed the steepest learning slopes (β = 0.464, SE = 0.040), significantly exceeding all other conditions (all ps ≤ .037). Nevertheless, the goal-based conditions, Goal (β = 0.244, SE = 0.037, VG (β = 0.335, SE = 0.038), and VNG (β = 0.333, SE = 0.038) did not differ significantly from one another (all ps ≥ .358).

In Phase 2 (Figure 3C), both visible conditions (VG and VNG) showed steeper learning slopes than Goal (all ps ≤ .015), with VG showing the largest gain.

Most notably, VG perfomance (β = 0.642, SE = 0.044) no longer differed from Points ((β = -0.037, SE = 0.061; p = .551). By contrast, both VG and Points had steeper slopes than Goal (β = 0.329, SE = 0.039) and VNG (β = 0.474, SE = 0.041; all ps < .01). VNG also showed a significantly steeper learning slope than Goal (p = .015).

Overall, phase-related changes were driven primarily by the visible conditions: Goal remained relatively stable, VNG improved modestly, and VG increased enough to converge with Points. Given the asymmetric learning benefits of VG and VNG, we took a closer look at potential causes in the Supplementary Materials (see S1.2).

Importantly, if visibility merely reduced maintenance or recall demands, its benefits should have emerged immediately in Phase 1 and remained stable. Instead, the delayed and asymmetric advantage shows that visibility may affect not only how goal information is maintained, but also how outcomes are evaluated.

3.1.2 Testing Stage

During testing, participants received no feedback, so accuracy indexed the persistence of learned stimulus-action associations. Testing accuracy differed by condition (χ 2 (3) = 69.35, p < .0001; Figure 3D). Observed accuracy was highest for Points (79.6%), which exceeded VG (75.1%; p = .0308), VNG (67.0%; p < .0001), and Goal (63.4%; p < .0001). VG also exceeded VNG (p = .0016) and Goal (p < .0001), whereas VNG and Goal did not differ significantly (p = .1932). These condition differences did not vary detectably with the phase in which items were learned (χ 2 (3) = 2.98, p = .395). Items learned in Phase 2 showed higher observed testing accuracy (79.8%) than those learned in Phase 1 (62.7%), although the phase effect did not reach significance after controlling for original block number (χ 2 (1) = 3.51, p = .061). The ordering across conditions thus broadly mirrored the learning-phase pattern.

We further assessed retention by comparing accuracy for the same stimuli during late learning (iterations 9-11) and testing. Condition interacted with stage (χ 2 (3) = 12.56,p = .0057; Figure 3E), indicating that the retention-related decline, the accuracy change from late learning to testing stage, varied across conditions. Descriptively, accuracy declined by 9.8 percentage points in Points, 7.8 in VNG, 5.9 in VG, and 4.6 in Goal. The decline was greater for Points than Goal (beta = -0.614, SE = 0.178, p = .0034); no other pairwise difference was significant (all ps ≥ .085).

Additionally, the decline was larger for items learned in Phase 1 than in Phase 2 (χ 2 (1) = 13.18, p = .00028). However, this phase difference in decline did not vary detectably across conditions (χ 2 (3) = 4.94,p = .177). As suggested by previous work Collins [2018], a greater retention decline may reflect stronger reliance on WM for maintaining stimulus-action associations during learning. The larger decline in Points relative to Goal may therefore suggest that fewer WM resources were needed for other learning processes, such as goal maintenance and outcome evaluation, leaving more resources available for maintaining stimulus-action associations.

We first sought to establish a baseline process description of learning in this task while accounting for participants' inattention. To this end, we implemented two alternative noise formulations. Dynamic noise models trial-by-trial fluctuations between a task-engaged state and a random state using lapse (p ER ) and recovery (p RE ) probabilities [Li et al., 2024]. Static noise is a nested special case in which these probabilities are constrained to sum to one, consistent with the standard ϵ-greedy assumption of stable lapse behavior [Nassar and Frank, 2016].

Within each noise estimation, we started from the RL-habit model architecture that was found to best capture behavior in a similar experiment focusing on points and goal conditions [Molinaro and Collins, 2026]. This includes a learning rate (α), a learning rate modulator for negative outcomes (ζ) and a forgetting rate (ϕ), which impact a standard, descriptive delta rule algorithm which we assume captures multiple processes (including working memory and RL); a habit learning rate (τ ); a mixture weight (κ) governing the contribution of RL versus habit learning; choice stickiness (η); and noise parameters lapse probability (p ER ), recovery probability (p RE ). As an initial check, to assess whether including the full set of parameters improved model fit across model families, we compared the full-parameter model with variants in which one parameter was fixed, separately for static and dynamic noise. Given the fit advantage using dynamic noise estimation, we focused subsequent computational analyses on full-parameter models with dynamic noise.

3.4 RCRT modulates learning process

Building on our behavioral results above and prior work linking RCRT to outcome-processing efficiency [Molinaro and Collins, 2026], we used computational modeling to test whether block-level median log RCRT modulates specific components of learning and decision-making. We constructed a family of dynamic-noise RL-habit models in which RCRT directly modulated one or more model parameters, with modulation strength governed by a parameter (ω). Model performance was evaluated using ∆AIC relative to a baseline model without RCRT modulation.

Single-parameter modulation revealed that incorporating RCRT improved model fit for most parameters relative to baseline (all ∆AIC < 0; all ps ≤ .0015), except stickiness (η) (∆AIC = -0.10, p = .285). Among these, modulation of the learning rate (α; ∆AIC = -11.52), the mixture weight (κ; ∆AIC = -10.66) and forgetting rate (ϕ; ∆AIC = -8.01) produced the largest reduction in AIC (Figure 5A). Each of these three models fit better than the other five single-parameter models (all ps ≤ .0491).

Model recovery analysis indicated that these learning-related models are identifiable (Figure 5C). In direct AIC comparisons, the α model fit better than the κ model (mean ∆AIC α-κ = -0.86, p = .0129) and the ϕ model (∆AIC α-ϕ = -3.51, p < .0001); the κ and ϕ models did not differ significantly (p = .198; Figure 5B). Among these three models, the α model best fit a slightly larger proportion of participants (Figure 5B). Thus, support was strongest for α, with κ and ϕ plausible.

These three models produced qualitatively similar behavioral predictions. Although the α model had the best AIC, this similarity limits conclusions about which learning mechanism RCRT influences. By contrast, models in which RCRT modulated decision noise or processes unrelated to outcome valuation received weaker support: although several improved fit relative to baseline, each of the three learning-related models fit better than these alternatives (allps ≤ .0491; Figure 5E).

Together, these results suggest that goal visibility benefits primarily act on outcome-based learning mechanisms directly rather than decision noise or unrelated processes.

To further examine this interpretation, we fit models in which RCRT simultaneously (but independently) modulated pairs of parameters drawn from α⊣,ϕ,⊢κ. Relative to single-parameter models, joint modulation generally improved fit over κ or ϕ modulation alone, but showed no consistent advantage over α alone (Figure 5A)). Specifically, α + κ and κ + ϕ each outperformed both κ and ϕ (all ps ≤ .0106), whereas neither outperformed α (p = .134 and p = .852, respectively). The α + ϕ model outperformed ϕ (p = .0019) but fit worse than α (p < .0001). 3.5 RCRT-associated changes in learning and decision mechanisms

We next examined how RCRT modulates learning and decision mechanisms by visualizing the model-implied relationship between RCRT and the effective values of key parameters in the three-parameter {α, ϕ, κ} RCRT model (Figure 5F-H).

Learning rate (α) showed a negative relationship with RCRT (Figure 5F). With slower reward collection, the effective learning rate decreased, indicating less efficient updating of stimulus-action associations when outcome processing was less efficient. This pattern suggests that reduced reward processing efficiency primarily manifests as attenuated integration of outcome information.

The mixture weight (κ), which governs the balance between outcome-sensitive and habit-based choice, remained relatively low at faster RCRT and increased gradually as RCRT slowed (Figure 5G), indicating a transition toward habit-dominated behavior under reduced reward processing efficiency.

Finally, the forgetting rate (ϕ) showed a modest increase with RCRT (Figure 5H). With faster RCRT, ϕ remained low, indicating minimal forgetting of learned associations. Slower RCRT showed a graded increase in ϕ, implying that inefficient reward processing was associated with increased decay of previously acquired values.

Together, these model-implied patterns link slower reward collection primarily to weaker value updating and a shift in habit policy weighting, with a smaller change in forgetting. Alongside the model-comparison results, reward function efficiency influences various learning processes, consistent with a process account in which reward function compression broadly reshapes learning efficiency and access to outcome-based policies rather than simply modulating choice difficulty and stochasticity.

4 Discussion

In this study, we investigated which cognitive processes contribute to the cost of transforming abstract goals into learning signals by manipulating the visibility of goal information during learning and feedback. Consistent with prior work, learning from goal-dependent outcomes impose a learning cost relative to explicit point feedback [McDougle et al., 2022, Molinaro and Collins, 2026]. Making goal information externally available substantially reduced this cost, but critically, the benefit of visibility was not static: performance in the visible conditions (VG and VNG) improved across learning phases, with the largest gain in VG, which ultimately converged to the numeric point-based performance.

This temporal pattern is difficult to explain by a purely static memory-offloading account, under which the benefit of externalizing goal information should be available as soon as that information becomes visible. Instead, the gradual increase in the visibility benefit is more consistent with an experience-dependent change in outcome evaluation. In line with the reward-function compression account [Molinaro and Collins, 2026], repeated access to external goal information may support the development of a simpler and more efficient mapping from observed outcomes to value.

Together, these findings suggest that the central cognitive bottleneck in goal-dependent learning arises from outcome evaluation, when goal information must be used to transform observed feedback into a value that can guide learning.

Our findings build on prior work [McDougle et al., 2022 , Molinaro and Collins, 2023b , 2026] to extend the idea of reward function compression to a new setting. In earlier work, reward function compression was primarily described as arising from the structure of the goal space itself: simpler goal spaces, and more commonalities across concurrently pursued goals allow for faster learning of an efficient outcome evaluation function [Molinaro and Collins, 2026]. In the present study, the underlying goal space remained large and unstructured across conditions; instead, visibility provided a stable external reference for evaluating the outcome from a single match or non match feature. With experience, repeated access to this reference may allow learners to develop a simpler and more reusable mapping from outcome comparison to value. Our findings therefore suggest that efficient reward-function learning may flexibly exploit structure that enables a more direct mapping from outcomes to value, including structure provided externally through goal visibility at the time of outcome evaluation. This possibility raises important questions about the scope and generalizability of learned reward functions, as well as how such efficient reward functions are constructed in the first place.

The asymmetric benefit of goal visibility provides further insight into how external information supports outcome evaluation. Although VG and VNG were designed to provide comparable external memory support, VG showed a substantially larger improvement, suggesting that outcome evaluation remained more costly in VNG even when the relevant information was externally available. At least two explanations could account for these additional demands.

One possibility is that the two conditions differ in the computation required to map perceptual comparison onto value.

In VG, a match between feedback and the visible reference directly signals a desirable outcome, whereas in VNG, a match signals an undesirable outcome and value must instead be inferred from a mismatch. Match-and mismatch-based comparisons can differ in processing demands [Farell, 1985 , Egeth, 1966], potentially making the VNG mapping less efficient. A second possibility is that VNG may be disadvantaged by a more general Pavlovian-instrumental transfer (PIT)-like process. Because the visible non-goal represents an outcome that should be avoided, the cue itself may acquire negative or avoidance-related valence. This cue-driven response could compete with the task-defined evaluation of the feedback, biasing outcome processing away from the value specified by the current goal and toward the valence carried by the visible cue itself [Huys et al., 2011]. Under this account, the additional cost in VNG arises from resolving a conflict between an automatically evoked avoidance tendency and the instrumental value that should guide learning. Our follow-up analyses suggest these accounts may be intertwined, with their relative influence shifting across the course of learning: in early learning, a general PIT-like approach-avoidance bias may have limited VNG performance, as VNG showed no immedate match-based advantage and was less accurate than VG; later in learning, match-/mismatch-based reward evaluation may have become more influential, as VNG reached comparable accuracy and showed clearer performance gains, potentially offsetting the earlier PIT-like disadvantage (Figure S8 in the Supplementary Materials).

The behavioral and computational relationship between reward-collection response time (RCRT) and learning provides converging evidence that the efficiency of outcome evaluation shapes subsequent learning. Behaviorally, RCRT, used here as a readout of outcome-valuation efficiency, tracked individual differences in learning performance. Computationally, RCRT was associated with multiple learning-and decision-related mechanisms, including learning rate, forgetting, and the balance between value-based and habitual policies, suggesting that its influence was not restricted to a single component of learning. Specifically, faster RCRT was associated with more effective value updating, consistent with prior work linking reward-processing efficiency to learning rate [Molinaro and Collins, 2026]. Slower RCRT predicted faster decay of learned values, suggesting that inefficient outcome evaluation made value representations more vulnerable to forgetting or interference from previous trials [Collins, 2025 , Collins et al., 2017 , Collins and Frank, 2012]. As learned values become less stable, behavior may consequently rely less on value-based control and relatively more on habitual or repetition-based policies [Collins, 2025].

Several additional analyses suggest that these relationships were specific to outcome evaluation rather than reflecting general differences in response speed or task engagement. The qualitative pattern of RCRT modulation was consistent across dynamic-and static-noise models, indicating that the effects were robust to how engagement dynamics were modeled. Moreover, the effects were specific to RCRT, driven primarily by hit-trial RCRT (Figure ?? in the Supplementary Materials), rather than choice RT (Figure ?? in the Supplementary Materials). RCRT also showed little association with parameters related to non-learning or non-policy processes such as choice noise, choice stickiness, and the habit process itself. Together, these converging results suggest that RCRT reflects variation in outcome-evaluation efficiency that is specifically linked to how stimulus-action values are acquired, maintained, and expressed in choice, rather than general motor speed, response variability, or task engagement.

The retention results further suggest that working-memory resources may have been allocated differently across learning conditions. Although Points showed the highest accuracy at test, its decline from late learning to testing was greater than that observed in Goal. One possible explanation is that explicit point feedback required fewer working-memory resources for goal maintenance and outcome evaluation, leaving more capacity available to temporarily support stimulusaction associations during learning . This temporary support may have boosted learning performance but blocked more sustained learning, as revealed once working memory was no longer available at test [Collins, 2018 , Collins et al., 2017 , Collins and Frank, 2018]. Goal-dependent learning may instead have recruited more working-memory resources for maintaining goal information and evaluating outcomes, leaving less temporary support for stimulus-action associations.

The model-implied relationship between RCRT and ϕ is compatible with differences in the stability of learned values during learning, but ϕ does not directly measure how working memory was allocated across goal maintenance, outcome evaluation, and stimulus-action learning. We therefore view this account as a possible explanation of the Points-Goal retention difference, rather than a mechanism established by the present analyses.

More broadly, our findings contribute to a growing view emphasizing that reward is not a fixed property of outcome, but is constructed relative to the learner's current goal during learning and decision making [Juechems and Summerfield, 2019]. In particular, prior work has shown that goal congruency can exert a stronger influence on behavior and neural value signals than reward value alone [Frömer et al., 2019], illustrating how the same outcome can take on different behavioral significance depending on what the learner is trying to achieve. Beyond valuation, goals also organize decision making by prioritizing goal-relevant information and supporting commitment to ongoing objectives [Holton et al., 2024 [Holton et al., , 2025]]. Goals, thereby, shape the representations that support flexible decisions, such that different features of the same object become relevant depending on their usefulness for the current objective [Castegnetti et al., 2021].

This flexibility is typically considered in terms of changes in the goal itself: pursuing a different goal changes which features of an outcome are relevant and how that outcome is valued. Our findings highlight a complementary source of flexibility. Even when the goal itself remains unchanged, changing how goal information is represented and made available can alter the efficiency with which outcomes are evaluated. Goal-dependent learning may therefore depend not only on what goal is pursued, but also on how that goal is represented and accessed during learning.

This perspective also connects goal-dependent learning to broader accounts of distributed cognition and external representation. People routinely use the environment to offload information that would otherwise need to be maintained or manipulated internally, reducing reliance on limited cognitive resources [Intons-Peterson and Fournier, 1986 , Schönpflug, 1986 , Risko and Gilbert, 2016]. Importantly, however, external representations can do more than support memory: by making task-relevant relations directly available to perception, they can also change the computations required to perform a task [Larkin and Simon, 1987 , Zhang and Norman, 1994 , Kirsh, 2010]. Keeping goal information visible may similarly reduce the need to maintain it internally, but more importantly, it provides an external reference that can simplify the computation of outcome value. This principle aligns with work on external representations in education and interface design, which emphasizes that representations are most useful when they make task-relevant relations easier to perceive or compute, rather than simply providing additional information [Ainsworth, 2006, Scaife and Rogers, 1996]. Our findings suggest a corresponding design principle for goal-dependent learning: an effective goal representation should not only remind learners what they are trying to achieve, but also make it easier to evaluate their current performance relative to that desired state. Reference examples, progress displays, and other goal representations may therefore be particularly useful when they support a direct comparison between the learner's current outcome and the goal they are trying to reach, much as a visible copybook supports direct comparison during Chinese calligraphy línmó practice.

5 Conclusion

In sum, our findings suggest that a key cognitive bottleneck in goal-dependent learning lies in outcome evaluation: how efficiently observed outcomes can be translated into value to guide learning. Making goal information visible facilitated this process and, with experience, substantially improved learning. The present work extends the reward-function compression account beyond the internal structure of the goal space by raising the possibility that efficient valuation can also be supported by structure available during outcome evaluation. More broadly, these findings suggest that how goals are represented and made available can be leveraged to facilitate learning.

S1.1.1 Choice RT Correlation Analysis

To show that our findings are specific to outcome evaluation and not due to a broader difficulty or engagement factor, we perform the same analyses done on reward collection reaction time (RCRT) using choice reaction time instead. Specifically, we examine the relationship between choice response time and learning performance: we correlated the condition-specific differences in accuracy (relative to Points) with corresponding differences in choice response time. Across all non-Points conditions, there were no significant correlation between choice response time and learning performance: Goal (ρ = 0.187, p = .149, 95%CI = [-0.07, 0.42]); VG (ρ = -0.048, p = .715, 95%CI = [-0.30, 0.21]); and VNG (ρ = 0.205, p = .114, 95%CI = [-0.05, 0.43]; Figure 4F-H). This finding should be contrasted to the same analysis with reward collection response time (RCRT; Figure 4C-E). To better understand the asymmetry between VG and VNG, we analyzed reward-collection performance and reward collection response time (RCRT) separately for hit (when a goal was obtained) and correct-rejection trials (when a non-goal was obtained). This analysis was intended to inform the potential explanations of the VG-VNG asymmetry: increased mismatch-based comparison effort or general Pavlovian-instrumental transfer (PIT)-like.

Under the mismatchbased comparison effort account, performance depends on the demands of outcome evaluation itself. Specifically, the outcome must be compared against the goal representation to determine whether it signals reward, and mismatch-based comparisons are hypothesized to be more effortful and slower than match-based comparisons. Under the PIT-like account, VNG is expected to be generally disadvantaged because non-goal images carry a less favorable valence, promoting avoidance, but are present on the screen when a goal outcome is obtained, whereas goal outcomes in VG carry a more favorable valence, further promoting approach in correct trials.

Trial-wise accuracy (hits vs. misses; correct rejections vs. false alarms) was analyzed using generalized linear mixedeffects models (GLMMs) with condition, phase, block (included as a control covariate), and the condition × phase interaction as fixed effects, along with a random intercept for subjects. Trial-wise RCRT was analyzed using Gamma GLMMs with a log link and the same fixed-and random-effects structure. Pairwise comparisons were Holm-corrected.

Because our theoretical question centered on the asymmetry between the two visible-goal conditions, we focused primarily on the VG-VNG comparison. We also considered phase-specific effects, because changes across phases were informative for distinguishing between the two accounts. Accordingly, we report the phase-specific comparisons for Phase 1 and Phase 2 separately, as well as the overall effect across Phase 1&2

In hit trials, participants received rewarding goal feedback, a context in which VG should benefit from both a direct match between the outcome and the visible goal and a general approach bias, whereas VNG should be disadvantaged because reward evaluation requires confirming a mismatch with the visible non-goal and may also be influenced by an avoidance-like bias. For accuracy, we observed a significant main effect of condition (χ 2 (3) = 25.03, p < .0001) and a significant Condition × Phase interaction (χ 2 (3) = 21.09, p = .0001), but no main effect of phase (p = .10). Across both phases, VG showed a higher hit rate than VNG (Phase 1: p < .0001; Phase 2: p = .0005; combined across Phases 1&2: p < .0001; Figure S8A). For RCRT, we found significant main effects of condition (χ 2 (3) = 153.58, p < .0001) and phase (χ 2 (1) = 31.50, p < .001), as well as a significant Condition × Phase interaction (χ 2 (3) = 45.69, p < .0001).

VG also showed faster RCRT than VNG in both phases (Phase 1: p = .0067; Phase 2: p < .0001; combined across Phases 1 and 2: p < .0001; Figure S8C).

Together, these results showed that when reward evaluation can rely on a direct match, VG is both more accurate and faster than VNG, and this advantage remained consistent across the whole learning trajectory.

In correct-rejection trials, VNG should benefit from a direct match to the visible non-goal but may be disadvantaged by a PIT-like bias, whereas VG does not afford the same match-based advantage but may instead benefit from a PIT-like bias toward the visible goal. For accuracy, we observed a significant Condition × Phase interaction (χ 2 (3) = 8.45, p = .04), but no significant main effects of condition or phase (all ps > .10). In Phase 1, VNG showed lower correct-rejection accuracy than VG (p = .028), but this difference was no longer present in Phase 2 (p = .97). Collapsing across phases, VNG was as good as VG (p = .12; Figure S8B). For RCRT, we found significant main effects of condition (χ 2 (3) = 80.12, p < .0001) and phase (χ 2 (1) = 17.34, p < .0001), as well as a significant Condition × Phase interaction (χ 2 (3) = 71.12, p < .0001). Figure S8D). These results showed that the VNG advantage in correct-rejection trials emerged gradually across learning.

Early in learning, VNG did not show a response-time benefit and even showed lower accuracy than VG, but by Phase 2, it achieved similarly high accuracy while showing faster RCRT than VG. Notably, this Phase 2 effect was strong enough that the across-phase average largely reflected the later-emerging VNG advantage.

Taken together, these results raise the possibility that the PIT-like account and the mismatch-cost account may be intertwined, with their relative influence shifting over the course of learning. Early in learning, PIT-like effects may be more dominant, as VNG did not immediately show the expected benefit of a match-based comparison. By Phase 2, however, match-based comparison benefits appeared to become more dominant, as VNG showed clearer performance gains, potentially offsetting the earlier PIT-like disadvantage. We treat this interpretation as exploratory, and further experiments will be needed to test it directly.
