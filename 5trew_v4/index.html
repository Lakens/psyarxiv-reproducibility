Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Szczygieł, M., & Hohol, M. (2026). The gender gap in math anxiety (and in the link between math anxiety and math achievement) is not so salient when other anxieties are controlled for.

Introduction

Numeracy proficiency and participation in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) are widely recognized as important predictors of higher earnings and better financial and health decisions (Black et al., 2021; OECD, 2016 OECD, , 2024)). For this reason, numerous initiatives have been undertaken worldwide to strengthen students' math achievement at all stages of education. However, the effectiveness of these efforts may depend not only on interventions aimed at improving math achievement, but also on efforts addressing the determinants of these skills. Among the predictors of math achievement, both individual factors (sociodemographic, cognitive, affective, motivational) and environmental factors (family-, school-, peer-related) have been identified (Lau et al., 2022; Luttenberger et al., 2018; Meece et al., 1990; Retanal & Maloney, 2026).

Math anxiety (MA) is one of the most extensively studied and well-established correlates of math achievement (Pelegrina et al., 2025). MA is defined as "a feeling of tension and anxiety that interferes with the manipulation of numbers and the solving of mathematical problems in a wide variety of ordinary life and academic situations" (Richardson & Suinn, 1972, p. 551). In line with this definition, MA is a multidimensional construct encompassing anxiety related to learning, testing, and problem solving, with these dimensions strongly interrelated and also reflected in general feelings toward mathematics (Dowker et al., 2016; Hart & Ganley, 2019; Hopko et al., 2003). MA is typically understood and assessed as a trait (Orbach et al., 2019), and this is the approach we adopt in the present study. MA develops gradually from childhood, peaks in adolescence, and persists into adulthood, even after the end of formal education (Hart & Ganley, 2019; Hembree, 1990). This persistence may be important for the transfer of MA to subsequent generations, as previous research indicates that parental and teachers' MA is associated with children's MA and their mathematics achievement (Beilock et al., 2010; Maloney et al., 2015; Ramirez et al., 2018b). MA has attracted considerable attention because most studies involving children, adolescents, and adults suggest that girls and women report higher levels of this anxiety (Eidlin-Levy et al., 2023), which may be linked to lower female participation in STEM fields (Lunardon et al., 2022; Megreya et al., 2025).

Previous research suggests that reducing MA may enhance math achievement and participation in STEM, particularly among women (Ramirez et al., 2018a). However, planning effective interventions requires a deeper understanding of the factors associated with gender differences in MA. The present study aims to investigate the factors that mediate the relationship between gender and MA and to examine whether gender differences exist in the association between MA and math achievement. To address these aims, we additionally analyze the correlates of MA and gender differences in these correlates.

Correlates of math anxiety

To better understand the mechanisms underlying gender differences in MA, it is essential to examine the correlates of MA, which have themselves been widely investigated with regard to gender differences. Research on the specificity of MA has been extensive, and findings indicate that it is a distinct form of anxiety that is related to, but not reducible to, neuroticism (Lunardon et al., 2022), general (Malanchini et al., 2017), test (Caviola et al., 2022), or spatial anxiety (Delage et al., 2022; Sokolowski et al., 2019). Although all types of anxiety can be analyzed both as stable traits and as situational states, here we focus on traitlevel constructs. Math achievement is another key correlate of MA that is conceptually distinct from anxiety and has been extensively investigated (Barroso et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2019).

Neuroticism Neuroticism (the inverse of emotional stability) is a broad personality trait reflecting a stable tendency to experience negative affect, emotional reactivity, and difficulty regulating distress across situations (Gosling et al., 2003). Individuals low in emotional stability tend to be more sensitive to stressors and more prone to experiencing worry, tension, and mood fluctuations. Previous studies indicate that neuroticism is positively and weakly-to-moderately related to MA (e.g., r = .32, Lunardon et al., 2022), likely acting as its precursor by increasing the likelihood that mathematics-related situations are appraised as threatening and by amplifying worry and emotional reactivity during mathematical problem solving. Women tend to report higher levels of neuroticism than men (Chapman et al., 2007; Lunardon et al., 2022), which may partially explain gender differences in MA, as greater emotional reactivity and worry in women may increase vulnerability to anxiety in mathematics-related contexts.

General anxiety

General anxiety is conceptualized as a stable disposition to experience worry and tension across a wide range of situations (Spielberger, 1983). Individuals high in general anxiety tend to perceive ambiguous or demanding situations as more threatening and to respond with elevated and persistent worry. Although general anxiety and neuroticism originate from different theoretical traditions, they are conceptually similar in that both capture relatively stable individual differences in emotional vulnerability that may predispose individuals to higher levels of MA. Consistent with this view, previous research indicates substantial statistical overlap between general anxiety and neuroticism (e.g., r > .70; Jylhä & Isometsä, 2006).

Therefore, in the present paper, we treat both constructs as manifestations of trait anxiety vulnerability. Similarly to neuroticism, general anxiety is positively and weakly-to-moderately associated with MA (e.g., r = .30, Malanchini et al., 2017; rs = .11-.29, Szczygieł, 2022), likely because individuals prone to excessive worry are more likely to appraise mathematics-related situations as stressful. Women also tend to report higher levels of general anxiety than men (McLean & Anderson, 2009), which may contribute to gender differences in MA by increasing women's vulnerability to anxiety in mathematics-related contexts.

Test anxiety Test anxiety is conceptualized at the trait level as a relatively stable tendency to experience negative affective, cognitive, and physiological reactions in evaluative situations such as exams (Cassady, 2023; Caviola et al., 2022). Individuals high in test anxiety tend to anticipate failure, worry about negative evaluation, and exhibit heightened emotional arousal across testing contexts. Previous research indicates that test anxiety is positively and moderately related to neuroticism (e.g., r = .45, Chamorro-Premuzic et al., 2008), general anxiety (e.g., r = .45, Liu et al., 2025), and MA (e.g., rs = .45-.51, Kazelskis et al., 2000; r = .46, Caviola et al., 2022). A high level of test anxiety may contribute to MA, as solving math problems often involves fears of making mistakes, receiving poor grades, or being judged negatively by teachers or peers (Szczygieł & Pieronkiewicz, 2022). Women tend to report higher levels of test anxiety than men (von der Embse et al., 2018), which may further contribute to observed gender differences in MA. Elevated test-related worry and evaluative sensitivity among females may increase the likelihood that mathematics, often learned in evaluative contexts, is experienced as particularly threatening, thereby reinforcing math-specific anxiety.

Spatial anxiety

Spatial anxiety refers to fear and apprehension related to spatial processing (Lyons et al., 2018). Following Lyons et al. (2018), spatial anxiety comprises three main components: (a) mental manipulation anxiety, referring to anxiety experienced during tasks that involve mentally transforming or rotating objects; (b) spatial imagery anxiety, referring to anxiety associated with forming and maintaining detailed mental images of objects or spatial layouts; and (c) navigation anxiety, referring to anxiety related to wayfinding and orienting oneself in large-scale environments. These components capture individual differences in affective responses to three out of the four core spatial domains most relevant to spatial anxiety (Uttal et al., 2013). Research on the nature of spatial anxiety remains relatively limited compared to other anxiety constructs (Geer et al., 2024). Previous findings indicate that spatial anxiety is positively and weakly-to-moderately related to general anxiety, suggesting partial overlap driven by shared emotional vulnerability (e.g., rs = .19-.32 in Sokolowski et al., 2019; rs = .29-.35 in Delage et al., 2022; rs = .24-.44 in Malanchini et al., 2017). Indeed, similar results were observed when analyzing the relationship between spatial anxiety and emotion control (r = -.37; Pazzaglia et al., 2018) and between self-assessed visuo-spatial inclinations (defined as a combination of the attitudes toward orientation and spatial anxiety) and emotion control (r = .41; Carbone et al., 2019). To our knowledge, prior research has not systematically examined the associations between spatial anxiety and test anxiety.

Spatial anxiety shows positive but weak-to-moderate associations with MA (e.g., rs = .09-.50 in Sokolowski et al., 2019; rs = .23-.48 in Delage et al., 2022; rs = .32-.41 in Malanchini et al., 2017). Spatial anxiety and MA are similar in that both are domain-specific constructs, however, spatial anxiety may represent a proximal precursor of MA, as negative affect experienced during spatial processing can generalize to mathematics. To our knowledge no longitudinal studies have directly examined the nature of the relationship between MA and spatial anxiety. However, although the available evidence remains primarily correlational, prior research has often conceptualized spatial anxiety as a predictor of MA rather than vice versa (Delage et al., 2022; Sokolowski et al., 2019). Evidence on gender differences in spatial anxiety suggests a differentiated pattern across its components. Women consistently report higher levels of navigation anxiety and mental manipulation anxiety (Delage et al., 2022; Lyons et al., 2018; Malanchini et al., 2017). In contrast, findings for spatial imagery anxiety are less consistent, with some studies reporting no gender differences (Lyons et al., 2018) and others indicating small female elevations (Delage et al., 2022).

Math achievement A small-to-moderate negative cross-sectional relationship between MA and math achievement is well established (e.g., r = -.28 in Barroso et al., 2021; r = -.32 in Zhang et al., 2019). Moreover, meta-analytic longitudinal evidence indicates a reciprocal relationship, with prior MA predicting later math achievement (b = -.11) and prior achievement predicting later MA (b = -.12; Pelegrina et al., 2025). These findings align with the view that MA may stem from repeated mathematical difficulties (Deficit Theory) and, in turn, impair performance through cognitive interference and avoidance (Debilitating Anxiety Theory), ultimately forming a reciprocal cycle (Reciprocal Theory; Carey et al., 2016). Evidence regarding gender differences in math achievement is mixed, with most meta-analyses indicating small or negligible average differences.

Earlier syntheses reported a slight male advantage, particularly in adolescence and among high performers (Hyde et al., 1990 (Hyde et al., , 2008)). More recent meta-analyses have generally found very small overall gender differences, with results varying depending on the sample, mathematical domain, and outcome measure; some studies reported near-zero differences or small female advantages (Else-Quest et al., 2010; Leyva, 2017; Lu et al., 2023; Voyer & Voyer, 2014). However, an updated large-scale meta-analysis covering studies published between 2010 and 2022 identified a small but significant overall male advantage, especially in later grades and in spatially intensive domains such as geometry (Caviola et al., 2026). Cross-national assessments further show substantial variability in the gender gap across countries, highlighting the role of sociocultural factors (OECD, 2016 (OECD, , 2024)).

Mediators of the relationship between gender and math anxiety

Previous research (Barlow, 2000) suggests a hierarchical organization of anxiety constructs, ranging from broad dispositional vulnerability to domain-specific manifestations.

Neuroticism and general anxiety reflect the most general level of emotional vulnerability, with test anxiety often conceptualized as a situationally focused expression of general anxiety (Zeidner, 1998). In turn, MA has been described as a more domain-specific form of anxiety nested within test anxiety (Chamorro-Premuzic et al., 2008). Consistent with this framework, spatial anxiety occupies an intermediate but partly distinct position: although related to broader emotional vulnerability, individual differences in spatial anxiety are not fully explained by general anxiety and it should therefore be treated as a domain-specific construct (Alvarez-Vargas et al., 2020; Oliver et al., 2023). Overall, these findings suggest that more specific anxieties (e.g., spatial anxiety and MA) may develop on the basis of broader dispositional risk factors while retaining meaningful domain specificity within the hierarchy of anxieties.

Importantly, previous research indicates that all types of anxiety (though findings on spatial imagery anxiety are less consistent) are higher in women than in men (Caviola et al., 2022; Delage et al., 2022; Eidlin-Levy et al., 2023; Lunardon et al., 2022; Lyons et al., 2018; Sokolowski et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2024). Therefore, if MA and other anxieties share a common core of negative valence, and if women, on average, report higher levels of all these anxieties than men, then it is likely that they all contribute to gender differences in MA. In other words, a general tendency to experience negative emotions may be associated with higher levels of specific types of anxiety. Therefore, gender differences in MA may be explained, at least in part, by other types of anxiety. Although gender differences in math achievement are generally small and inconsistent across developmental stages (Caviola et al., 2026; Else-Quest et al., 2010; Hyde et al., 1990; Lu et al., 2023; Voyer & Voyer, 2014), it may still function as a mediator of the gender-MA relationship. Even modest gender differences in math achievement can contribute to differential experiences of success and failure in mathematics, which are known to shape MA (Pelegrina et al., 2025). Therefore, the math achievement pathway remains a theoretically plausible mechanism underlying gender differences in MA.

Taken together, it can be assumed that various forms of anxiety and math achievement may function as important mediators of the gender-MA relationship. At the same time, it should be noted that this represents only one of several plausible theoretical models. Given the substantial intercorrelations among these variables, they may also mutually influence one another over time (e.g., spatial anxiety → MA, MA → spatial anxiety, math achievement → MA, MA → math achievement; Pelegrina et al., 2025). However, although empirical evidence for mediation remains limited, findings from the previous studies across different age groups support the assumption that the gender gap in MA can be accounted for, at least in part, by less domain-specific forms of anxiety and by math achievement.

The research findings on this topic are as follows. Lunardon et al. (2022) showed that neuroticism partially mediated gender differences in numeracy among STEM students and was positively related to MA. As the study did not directly test neuroticism as a mediator of the gender-MA relationship, this specification appears theoretically weak, given that neuroticism is typically conceptualized as a broad, relatively stable dispositional trait that precedes, rather than results from, domain-specific anxiety such as MA. No other variables were included as mediators or statistically controlled for in that study. Szczygieł (2020) tested first-and second-grade primary school children longitudinally, concluding that general anxiety fully mediates gender differences in MA. She did not include any other mediators in the analysis. Girls reported higher levels of general anxiety, which in turn predicted higher MA, indicating that broad emotional vulnerability may contribute to domain-specific math-related anxiety early in education. These findings are theoretically consistent with models positing that more specific anxieties emerge from broader dispositional tendencies (Barlow, 2000). At the same time, there is likely no evidence from adult samples, leaving it unclear whether the mediating role of general anxiety persists across development or is partly replaced by more domain-specific mechanisms such as test or spatial anxiety.

To our knowledge, the mediating role of test anxiety in the gender-MA relationship has not yet been directly examined. However, such a pathway appears theoretically plausible, as test anxiety is higher in women than in men (von der Embse et al., 2018) and is moderately related to MA (Caviola et al., 2022; Kazelskis et al., 2000). In the opinion of some researchers, MA represents a domain-specific form of test anxiety (Chamorro-Premuzic et al., 2008; Caviola et al., 2022). Moreover, learning mathematics typically occurs in evaluative educational contexts that may heighten test-related worries about performance. Consistent with this view, some authors have argued that MA largely reflects a specific manifestation of test-related evaluative anxiety (Hembree, 1990; Zeidner, 1998). Delage et al. (2022) and Sokolowski et al. (2019) showed that gender differences in MA among adults are mediated by spatial anxiety in the areas of manipulation and navigation, but not in the area of imagery. These results were observed regardless of whether general anxiety was controlled for (Sokolowski et al., 2019) or not (Delage et al., 2022), suggesting that spatial anxiety contributes to gender differences in MA beyond general emotional vulnerability and reflects a more domain-specific mechanism. Many mathematical tasks rely on spatial representations and transformations, making anxiety experienced during spatial processing particularly likely to generalize to mathematics.

Despite sharing a common negative valence, neuroticism, general anxiety, test anxiety, and spatial anxiety differ in their degree of conceptual overlap: neuroticism and general anxiety show the greatest overlap, whereas test anxiety and spatial anxiety are more domain-specific and overlap to a lesser extent. Accordingly, each construct is expected to account for a unique proportion of the variance in gender differences in MA, with the exception that, due to their substantial overlap, neuroticism and general anxiety should not be included simultaneously in the same analyses. Taking into account the prominent positive associations between spatial ability and math achievement (Mix et al., 2016), and findings that women tend to perform worse on spatial tasks and report higher spatial anxiety than men (Geer et al., 2024), it can be expected that, among various types of anxiety, spatial anxiety particularly contributes to the relationship between gender and MA. Next, test anxiety can be expected to emerge as a significant mediator, given that MA is often emphasized as being closely related to situations involving testing and evaluation. By contrast, a general propensity to experience negative emotions, conceptualized as neuroticism or general anxiety, may play a smaller role. However, it is important to control for this factor in order to account for the specificity of the remaining constructs.

Although studies examining math achievement as a factor linking gender and MA are relatively scarce, their findings remain inconsistent. Some evidence suggests that math achievement may act as a mediator of the gender-MA relationship. For example, Delage et al. (2022) found that gender differences in math achievement were associated with differences in MA. In contrast, Sokolowski et al. (2019) did not observe a significant mediating role of math achievement. These mixed results may reflect differences in samples, analytic strategies, or the extent to which other relevant affective factors were controlled. Given the well-established reciprocal relationship between MA and math achievement, further clarification of the role of math achievement in explaining gender differences in MA is warranted. Therefore, the present study aimed to revisit these contradictory findings and to examine whether math achievement contributes to the gender gap in MA.

Gender differences in the relationship between MA and math achievement

Previous meta-analyses have confirmed that MA is weakly to moderately and negatively related to math achievement in children, adolescents, and adults (Barroso et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2019). However, inconsistent results have been observed regarding the gender differences in significance and strength of the relationship between MA and math achievement (Yu et al., 2024). While many studies suggest a stronger link between MA and math achievement in girls than in boys (Erturan & Jansen, 2015; Geary et al., 2019; Van Mier et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2024), others have found no statistically significant gender differences in this relationship (Hill et al., 2016; Reali et al., 2016) or have found a stronger link for boys than for girls (Szczygieł, 2020).

Although Szczygieł (2020) concluded that MA affects boys' math achievement more than that of girls, she did not compare the strength of the correlation by using a statistical test. Therefore, to make the results comparable, we conducted an additional analysis on Szczygieł's (2020) dataset and found that, regardless of whether general anxiety was controlled for, boys showed a significantly stronger negative relationship between MA and math achievement than girls in first grade (Z = -1.76, p = .04; controlled for general anxiety: Z = -2.31, p = .01) but not in second grade (Z = -.69, p = .25; controlled for general anxiety: Z = -.86, p = .19).

Importantly, Szczygieł's (2020) study was conducted in children, while other research suggests that MA in childhood largely depends on general anxiety (Szczygieł et al., 2024).

Moreover, boys and girls are exposed to stereotypes regarding mathematics learning and emotional expression, which may reinforce boys' belief that they should possess high mathematical competence, as well as their reluctance to admit experiencing MA (Justicia-Galiano et al., 2023). In adulthood, such tendencies may be particularly evident, as MA is shaped over the course of the entire education. Note that Szczygieł (2020) controlled only for general anxiety, whereas gender differences in the MA-math achievement relationship may also be influenced by test anxiety and spatial anxiety. Therefore, the contribution of various types of anxiety should be controlled for when testing gender differences in the relationship between MA and math achievement to clarify the unique contribution of MA. To our knowledge, previous studies have not controlled for the above-mentioned types of anxiety when examining the relationship between MA and math achievement in women and men. A notable exception is the study by Lunardon et al. (2022), which identified neuroticism as a significant mediator linking MA and math achievement in women only, highlighting the potential role of broader emotional vulnerability in shaping gender-specific performance outcomes.

The inconsistent findings regarding gender differences in the MA-math achievement relationship may partly stem from insufficient control of broader emotional vulnerability factors. Many studies examining gender moderation have not simultaneously accounted for neuroticism, general anxiety, test anxiety, or spatial anxiety, despite strong theoretical and empirical links between these constructs and MA. Because girls and women typically report higher levels of several anxiety forms, the observed gender moderation effects may partly reflect shared emotional vulnerability rather than MA-specific mechanisms. Therefore, simultaneously controlling for multiple anxiety-related constructs appears crucial for clarifying whether gender differences in the MA-math achievement relationship reflect domain-specific MA effects or broader affective dispositions. This integrative approach may help reconcile inconsistent findings in the literature and provide a more precise estimate of gender-specific MA effects.

Current study

Although a substantial body of research has examined MA (Dowker et al., 2016; Hart & Ganley, 2019), important gaps remain regarding the relative contribution of different affective and math achievement-related factors to gender differences in MA in adulthood and to gender differences in the relationship between MA and math achievement. In particular, prior studies have rarely considered multiple anxiety constructs simultaneously, despite their partial conceptual overlap and their connections with both gender and MA. As a result, it remains unclear which factors uniquely contribute to gender differences in MA and in the MA-math achievement relationship, and how much variance in MA can be explained when these constructs are examined jointly.

The present study addresses this gap by adopting an integrative approach that situates anxiety constructs along a hierarchical continuum, from more domain-specific forms of anxiety (spatial and test anxiety), to broader emotional vulnerability (neuroticism and general anxiety), while also including math achievement within a single analytic approach. This approach is grounded in hierarchical models of anxiety, which posit that more specific anxieties may emerge from broader dispositional vulnerabilities while retaining their domain specificity.

Given the substantial conceptual and empirical overlap between neuroticism and general anxiety, only neuroticism was retained in the joint analyses to avoid redundancy and to more precisely estimate the unique contribution of the remaining predictors. The study applied a correlational cross-sectional design and examined the proposed mediation mechanism and the covariation between variables. Although both models are theoretically plausible, they do not constitute evidence of causal effects, allowing alternative explanations. First, we examine the mediating effects of spatial anxiety in the relationship between gender and MA and then assess the proportion of MA variance explained by gender and spatial anxiety to replicate the most robust prior findings (Delage et al., 2022; Sokolowski et al., 2019). Next, we extend the model by adding neuroticism and test anxiety to evaluate their incremental mediation effects and assess the additional variance explained beyond spatial anxiety. Finally, we include math achievement to determine whether it serves as an additional mediator and accounts for further variance in MA not captured by the affective factors. Our stepwise strategy allows us to assess both the incremental mediation effects and the cumulative variance explained by the proposed predictors (see Figure 2). Then, we examine whether the strength of the relationship between MA and math achievement differs by gender, both before and after controlling for multiple anxiety-related constructs (spatial anxiety, test anxiety, neuroticism). This analysis is important because previous findings on gender differences have been inconsistent and may reflect insufficient control for broader emotional vulnerability. Accordingly, we include spatial anxiety, test anxiety, and neuroticism as covariates, given their conceptual and empirical overlap with MA (see Figure 3). Taken together, the study aims to answer three research questions: Do various types of anxiety and math achievement mediate the relationship between gender and MA? What percentage of the variance in MA can be accounted for by gender, various types of anxiety, and math achievement? Does the relationship between MA and math achievement differ significantly in women and men when various types of anxiety are controlled for?

Regarding the first research question, we expected to replicate the findings of Delage et al. (2022) and Sokolowski et al. (2019), namely that spatial anxiety, particularly in the domains of navigation and manipulation, mediates the relationship between gender and MA. We further expect test anxiety to function as an additional significant mediator, given that MA is closely linked to evaluative contexts. Finally, neuroticism, as a broader disposition reflecting negative emotionality, is expected to play a comparatively less prominent role. Regarding math achievement, we did not pose a hypothesis due to the contradictory findings of previous studies.

As for the second research question, we expected that models of increasing complexity would account for progressively more variance. Finally, regarding the third research question, given the inconsistent findings regarding gender differences in the MA-math achievement relationship and the limited number of studies that have simultaneously controlled for multiple anxiety-related constructs, the present study treats this issue as an open empirical question.

Accordingly, we do not formulate a directional hypothesis regarding gender differences in the strength of the association between MA and math achievement. Instead, we examine whether such differences emerge before and after controlling for broader emotional vulnerability and domain-specific anxieties.

Method Participants

In total, 273 adults residing in the UK with fluent English participated in the online study. Four participants were excluded from the analysis because they failed at least two out of five attention checks. Data from 269 participants were analyzed (157 women, 112 men; Mage = 41.1 years, SDage = 12.9, range = 20-75).

Materials

Math anxiety

Three scales were used to capture the multidimensional nature of MA. The first was the Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale (Hopko et al., 2003), consisting of nine items with two subscales: testing MA (α = .88) and learning MA (α = .90), comprising four and five items, respectively. An example item from the former subscale is "Taking an examination in a math course", and from the latter, "Starting a new chapter in a math book". Participants indicated their level of anxiety in a given situation using a five-point Likert scale (1 -"low anxiety", 5 -"high anxiety"). The second scale was the Math Anxiety Questionnaire for Adults (Szczygieł, 2022), consisting of 19 items (α = .95) and measuring MA related to math problem-solving (e.g., "Calculation of the surface area of a sphere"). Participants were asked to imagine that they were in a situation in which it was necessary to solve a math problem and to indicate whether they felt anxious in such situations (a four-point Likert scale; 1 -"I definitely do not feel anxiety", 2 -"I rather do not feel anxiety", 3 -"I rather feel anxiety", 4 -"I definitely feel anxiety"). The third scale was the Single-Item Math Anxiety Scale (Núñez-Peña et al., 2014), consisting of the question: "On a scale from 1 to 10, how math anxious are you?" (1 -"not anxious", 10 -"very anxious"). In all cases, higher scores indicate higher levels of MA. To capture the multidimensional nature of MA, we created a composite MA score based on all the scales (see Appendix). Its McDonald's ω for the second-order latent factor was .94.

Spatial anxiety

We quantified spatial anxiety using the Spatial Anxiety Questionnaire (Lyons et al., 2018), consisting of three subscales with eight items each (24 in total): spatial imagery anxiety (SA-I, α = .88), navigation anxiety (SA-N, α = .92), and mental manipulation anxiety (SA-M, α = .96). Participants were asked how anxious they would feel in response to the situation described in each item, and the possible responses were as follows: "not at all", "a little", "a fair amount", "much", and "very much". Example items are as follows: "Asked to give a detailed description of a person's face whom you've only met once" (SA-I), "Memorizing routes and landmarks on a map for an upcoming exam" (SA-N), and "Asked to imagine and mentally rotate a 3-dimensional figure" (SA-M). Higher scores indicate greater spatial anxiety.

Neuroticism

Neuroticism was measured using the Ten-Item Personality Inventory (Gosling et al., 2003). Although other personality traits were measured, only the neuroticism subscale consisting of two items, i.e., "Anxious, easily upset" and "Calm, emotionally stable" (α = .72), was analyzed. Participants responded using a seven-point slider (1 -"disagree strongly", 2 -"disagree moderately", 3 -"disagree a little", 4 -"neither agree nor disagree", 5 -"agree a little", 6 -"agree moderately", 7 -"agree strongly"). For interpretative consistency with other anxiety measures, the scale was reverse-scored so that higher scores indicated higher neuroticism.

Test anxiety

To measure test anxiety, we used the second edition of the Cognitive Test Anxiety Scale (Cassady, 2023; α = .97), consisting of 24 items (e.g., "I lose sleep over worrying about examinations"). Participants indicated how typical the situations described in each item were for them ("not at all typical of me", "somewhat typical of me", "quite typical of me", "very typical of me"). Higher scores indicate higher levels of test anxiety.

Math achievement

Because mathematics comprises partially distinct domains, we assessed participants' achievement across core content areas (i.e., arithmetic, algebra, and geometry) to obtain a broad and representative estimate of mathematical competence. Math achievement was assessed with 20 single-choice math problems, each with five response options (α = .77). They targeted arithmetic ("600 g of cake costs £8.40. How much does 1 kg of this cake cost?") and algebra ("Which number belongs to the solution set of the inequality (x-2)(x+3) < 0?"), as well as geometry ("A regular hexagonal pyramid has…"). The problems were developed based on materials for primary students (Karpińska & Radtke, 2019), students preparing for final high school exams (matemaks.pl), and for testing adults (Szczygieł & Sarı, 2024). The same set was used in Obidziński et al.'s (2025) study, which yielded moderate positive correlations between performance on these problems and both objective and subjective measures of math skills. Task difficulty was averaged by dividing the total number of points obtained by all participants by the maximum number of points possible for them. It ranged from easy (.78) to very difficult (.15), with an average of .55, indicating a moderate level. Higher accuracy indicated better math achievement. There was no time pressure.

Procedure

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Research at the Faculty of Philosophy, Jagiellonian University and run online in Qualtrics XM, lasting approximately 30 minutes. Participants were recruited via Prolific and received £6. They were informed during recruitment that they would complete questionnaires and solve mathematical tasks. Each questionnaire was displayed on one screen. Because all questions were mandatory, there were no missing data. The participants completed the questionnaires in the following order: Cognitive Test Anxiety Scale, Spatial Anxiety Questionnaire, Ten-Item Personality Inventory, Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale, Math Anxiety Questionnaire for Adults, Single-Item Math Anxiety Scale, State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, and Math achievement problems. Finally, participants answered demographic questions and could share their opinions about the study.

We did not analyze the results from the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory due to an excessively high level of overlap between the anxiety-related variables.

Plan of analysis

All analyses were conducted in R, and the script is available on the Open Science Framework at https://osf.io/rgcy5/. Prior to testing hypotheses, we examined the relevant test assumptions and found no evidence of serious violations.

In the first step, we conducted zero-order Pearson correlation analyses and independentsamples t-tests to examine gender differences in MA, other forms of anxiety, and math achievement.

Then, we addressed the three research questions in two analytic steps. To address the first and second research questions, we tested three multiple-mediation models in lavaan (Rosseel, 2012), with gender (women = 1, men = 0) as the predictor and the MA composite score as the outcome. Model 1 included the three dimensions of spatial anxiety (imagery, navigation, mental manipulation) as mediators. Model 2 added neuroticism and test anxiety. Model 3 additionally included math achievement. The ordering of models allowed us to evaluate both the incremental mediation effects and the additional variance in MA explained by progressively broader sets of predictors. In all models, the mediators were allowed to covary, enabling estimation of the unique indirect effect associated with each mediator while accounting for shared variance among mediators. All three models were just-identified (df = 0); therefore, global SEM fit indices and likelihood-ratio tests of model fit were not informative and are not reported. The incremental contribution of added mediators is indexed by changes in the variance in MA explained (R²), reported alongside the R² of each mediator. Indirect effects are reported as unstandardized coefficients (B) with their corresponding BCa confidence intervals; effects whose CIs excluded zero were considered significant.

To address the third research question, which concerns whether the strength of the MAmath achievement relationship differs between women and men, we computed Pearson zeroorder and partial correlations between MA and math achievement separately for women and men. The partial correlations controlled for spatial imagery anxiety, navigation anxiety, mental manipulation anxiety, neuroticism, and test anxiety. Gender differences in the magnitude of these correlations were tested using Fisher's z test for two independent correlations. This analysis treats gender as a moderator of the MA-math achievement association and is conceptually complementary to, but distinct from, the mediation analyses, in which gender is treated as a predictor of MA.

The two strategies address different questions: whereas the mediation models examined whether gender differences in MA could be statistically accounted for by the proposed mediators, the separate correlation analyses for women and men tested whether the association between MA and math achievement differed by gender after controlling for shared variance with the affective variables. Both analytic approaches were included because the study aimed to examine complementary hypothetical models within a correlational framework.

Discussion

Our study addressed three research questions in a sample of adults with a wide age range.

First, we found that gender differences in MA are significantly weaker when spatial anxiety, test anxiety, neuroticism, and math achievement are considered as mediators. Second, we demonstrated that gender, all the aforementioned anxieties, and math achievement together account for 66% of the variance in MA levels. Third, we found that the strength of the relationship between MA and math achievement in women and men is weaker when other anxieties are controlled for. Moreover, there are no significant differences between women and men in the strength of the relationship between MA and math achievement, regardless of whether other anxieties are controlled for.

Mediators of the Relationship between Gender and MA

Given the inclusion of multiple affective mediators and math achievement across models, we discuss their mediating roles in the gender-MA relationship sequentially (spatial anxiety, neuroticism and test anxiety, and math achievement).

First, we examined mediation using the domain-specific affective mediators, namely the different dimensions of spatial anxiety. Previous research (Delage et al., 2022; Sokolowski et al., 2019) has found spatial anxiety to be a robust mediator of gender differences in MA in young adults in the areas of mental manipulation and navigation. Notably, contrary to Delage et al. ( 2022), but consistent with Sokolowski et al. (2019), we did not find significant gender differences in spatial imagery anxiety. Therefore, we fully replicated the findings on spatial anxiety reported by Sokolowski et al. (2019) and partially replicated those reported by Delage et al. (2022), extending their generalizability to an adult population spanning a wider age range.

The results indicating that mental manipulation and navigation, but not imagery, mediate gender differences in MA are convincing given that the corresponding Spatial Anxiety Scale subscales primarily tap dynamic spatial transformations and route-planning processes that overlap with mathematical reasoning demands, whereas the imagery subscale focuses mainly on static visual memory and descriptive detail, which are less directly related to mathematics. Importantly, this pattern is consistent with meta-analyses showing that gender differences are largest in mental rotation, moderate in spatial perception, and small or inconsistent in spatial visualization (Linn & Petersen, 1985; Voyer et al., 1995). Research by Lawton (1994) suggests that gender differences in spatial abilities and spatial anxiety may stem from differential reliance on navigation strategies, unequal spatial experience, and sociocultural constraints on environmental exploration among girls, which may help explain inconsistencies across studies regarding gender differences in various dimensions of spatial anxiety.

Beyond spatial anxiety, high neuroticism may increase the risk of developing MA by heightening threat perception and weakening emotional regulation in math-related situations.

Correspondingly, high level of test anxiety may be associated with MA, as math tasks often evoke fears of failure and negative evaluation. As studies generally show that these types of anxiety are higher in girls and women than in boys and men (Caviola et al., 2022; Lunardon et al., 2022; Lyons et al., 2018), each can be considered a potential mediator of the relationship between gender and MA. Indeed, Szczygieł (2020) found that general anxiety fully mediated gender differences in MA in children. Given MA's persistence in adulthood (Hart & Ganley, 2019; Hembree, 1990), a similar pattern could be expected in adults. However, our results do not support the mediating role of neuroticism or test anxiety in the relationship between gender and MA, when spatial anxiety (and math achievement) are controlled for, or not. Although gender predicted neuroticism, its indirect effect on MA was weak and non-significant. Test anxiety significantly predicted MA but was not significantly related to gender, ruling it out as a mediator. Thus, while affective factors are associated with MA overall, they are not so important in accounting for gender differences in adults when different types of spatial anxiety (and math achievement) are controlled for. Therefore, gender differences in MA in adults are more strongly related to differences between women and men in emotions associated with spatial navigation and mental manipulation than to gender differences in neuroticism and test anxiety.

This pattern suggests that, whereas in childhood general anxiety may account for much of the gender differences in MA (Szczygieł, 2020), in adulthood more domain-specific anxieties, likely shaped through educational experience, appear to be more closely linked to MA.

Next, we revisited the potential association of math achievement with the relationship Although we used a different set of problems to assess participants' math achievement, our approach more closely resembles the latter one. Previous research findings on the gender gap in math achievement are inconclusive (Kaiser & Zhu, 2022), however, if such gender differences are observed, they may contribute to higher levels of MA in women than in men.

Finally, we investigated what percentage of the variance in MA can be accounted for by gender, various types of anxiety, and math achievement. We found that gender and spatial anxiety accounted for 48% of the variance in MA, which increased to 56% with the addition of neuroticism and test anxiety. When math achievement was added, the total explained variance in MA rose to 66%. These results are consistent with Sokolowski et al. (2019), who found that gender, spatial anxiety, and perceived and observed spatial manipulation abilities accounted for 65% of MA variance. Although the full model explained a substantial proportion of variance in MA (66%), the unique contribution of gender was relatively modest (5%). This suggests that gender is best conceptualized as a distal correlate whose association with MA is largely accounted for by correlated affective traits and math achievement. Accordingly, the conclusions regarding gender effects should be interpreted as relatively small in magnitude. We conclude that gender, spatial anxiety, more general types of anxiety, and math achievement are key factors determining the level of MA in adults. However, we refrain from concluding that spatial anxiety is uniquely central (accounting for nearly 50% of variance in MA in our models), as we did not test alternative model specifications (e.g., models progressing from more general to more domain-specific forms of anxiety), and given the substantial intercorrelations among the examined variables. The remaining variance is likely explained by cognitive and environmental factors (Justicia-Galiano et al., 2023; Lau et al., 2022; Retanal & Maloney, 2026; Szczygieł et al., 2024 Szczygieł et al., , 2025)).

Gender differences in the strength of MA-math achievement relationship

Gender differences in the strength of the relationship between MA and math achievement were not significant, regardless of whether other anxieties were controlled for.

This pattern is consistent with several prior studies reporting comparable MA-achievement associations in women and men (e.g., Hill et al., 2016; Reali et al., 2016), although it contrasts with research suggesting stronger links in women (e.g., Yu et al., 2024) or in first-grade primary school boys (Szczygieł, 2020). The present findings contribute to the mixed literature by suggesting that previously reported gender moderation effects may partly reflect insufficient control for broader affective factors. Because girls and women tend to report higher levels of several forms of anxiety, gender differences in the MA-math achievement relationship observed in some studies may, in part, capture shared emotional vulnerability rather than MA-specific processes.

At the same time, the broader literature suggests that the magnitude of gender differences in the MA-math achievement relationship may depend on developmental stage and sociocultural context. For example, some evidence from younger samples indicates stronger associations in boys (Szczygieł, 2020), whereas cross-cultural work has reported stronger links in girls in certain educational contexts (Yu et al., 2024). These inconsistencies may reflect differences in stereotype exposure, evaluative pressure, or educational experiences across age groups and cultures. Future longitudinal and cross-cultural research is therefore needed to clarify when and under what conditions gender differences in the MA-math achievement relationship emerge.

Study limitations and future research directions

The study's limitations are as follows. The design was correlational and cross-sectional; therefore, the mediation models should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mechanisms or temporal ordering. Although we focused on traits, that is, relatively stable individual differences, alternative model specifications remain theoretically plausible. Future research should use longitudinal designs (and, where feasible, intervention paradigms) to establish temporal precedence and to better distinguish between competing directional accounts.

Because the mediation models were just-identified/saturated and differed in the set of observed variables included, they could not be formally compared using global SEM fit indices or likelihood-ratio tests for nested models. Therefore, conclusions about the incremental contribution of added mediators are based on changes in explained variance rather than on formal SEM model-fit comparisons.

In our study, the task order was fixed. As we used a correlational design and measured traits, this should not have confounded the results. Nevertheless, future studies should counterbalance the task order. We should also note that the sample included more women than men. This imbalance likely reflects typical self-selection patterns in voluntary psychological research, but it may also indicate differential willingness to participate in a study involving mathematics. As participants were informed during recruitment that they would complete questionnaires and solve mathematical tasks, self-selection related to attitudes toward mathematics, anxiety, or competence cannot be ruled out.

Importantly, anxiety-related constructs in our models are conceptually and statistically overlapping. To reduce redundancy, we did not include state anxiety in the analyses.

Nevertheless, future work could explicitly model a higher-order negative affectivity factor and examine whether domain-specific pathways persist after accounting for this shared variance.

Whereas Sokolowski et al. (2019) and Delage et al. (2022) tested both spatial abilities and spatial anxiety, we focused only on the latter. Given established links between spatial processing and mathematics, future research should include both spatial performance measures and spatial anxiety to clarify their relative and joint contributions to MA and math achievement.

Additionally, these studies and ours differed in the method used to measure math achievement.

Future studies should employ both time-pressured arithmetic tasks and broader sets of math problems to be solved without time pressure, to investigate potential task dependence underlying inconsistent findings.

The questionnaire we used to assess spatial anxiety does not include anxiety related to the extrinsic-static spatial processing, which encompasses skills potentially math-relevant, such as integrating continuous and categorical information (Uttal et al., 2013). Future research is needed to investigate the contribution of extrinsic-static spatial anxiety to the gender gap in MA, particularly because it has been found that men outperform women in tasks tapping into that dimension (Uttal et al., 2013).

Finally, it should be noted that gender differences in self-reported anxiety that we found may partly reflect differences in the social acceptability of emotional expression. Women may be more willing to acknowledge and report anxiety symptoms, whereas men may be less likely to disclose such experiences due to gender-related social expectations (McLean & Anderson, 2009).

Conclusions

Our study supports previous findings regarding the gender gap in MA, but these differences appear to be weaker when spatial anxiety, test anxiety, neuroticism, and math achievement are accounted for. We conclude that gender differences in MA are largely statistically indirect and can be substantially accounted for by associated affective factors and math achievement. We suggest that interventions aimed at reducing spatial anxiety in the domains of mental manipulation and navigation in women could help reduce the gender gap in MA. The remaining indirect paths, including test anxiety and neuroticism, were nonsignificant, suggesting that spatial anxiety in navigation and manipulation plays a key role in accounting for gender differences in MA, likely because both types are related to mathematics. Finally, the relationship between MA and math achievement, tested separately in women and men, is weaker when controlling for various types of anxiety, and the gender difference in the strength of this relationship is not statistically significant. The absence of gender differences in the MAachievement link suggests that interventions targeting anxiety may yield similar benefits for women and men in terms of math achievement.
