Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p≥0.05 The ratings did not show any significant effects for repetition and group or their interaction (p≥0.05) (Table 4, Figure 5).

The sample size estimation was based on a 3x2 repeated-measures ANOVA (CONDITION X GROUP) for detecting an interaction effect of group and condition in the psychophysiological outcome measures (pupil dilation, electrodermal activity, heart rate, respiratory rate). The calculation was used as an a priori approximation for the planned mixed-effects analyses. It did not represent a multivariate power analysis across all outcome measures. The calculation was conducted in G*Power using an effect size of f=0.295, based on skin conductance findings of Díaz-Sáez et al., 2021 [17], an alpha level of 0.05, a power of 0.80, three repeated measurements, and a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5. In Díaz-Sáez et al., autonomic responses were assessed following motor imagery or movement observation in people with chronic low back pain and pain-free participants, rather than continuously during picture presentation as in the present study. The effect size was considered an approximate planning value. This yielded a minimum total sample size of n=22 (i.e., 11 participants per group). We therefore aimed to recruit 30 participants per group (i.e., n=60 in total) to obtain more precise estimates and to accommodate potential data loss in psychophysiological and eye-tracking recordings.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Fear of movement-related pain (FMRP) contributes to disability in chronic musculoskeletal pain. However, psychophysiological correlates of activity-specific threat appraisal during FMRP-eliciting tasks remain insufficiently understood. This cross-sectional study examined self-reports and psychophysiological responses to shoulder-straining daily activities in individuals with chronic shoulder pain (CSP) and pain-free controls. Participants observed six pictures depicting shoulder movements selected as highly or minimally threatening. Each picture was presented five times in random order. Participants were informed that they would subsequently perform the activities. Following each presentation, participants rated anticipated fear, urge to avoid, and expected harmfulness. Electrodermal activity, pupil diameter, heart rate, respiratory rate, and gaze behavior were recorded continuously. Self-reports differentiated between highly and minimally threatening activities and were higher in the CSP group (p≤0.001). SCL differentiated between threat categories, with more pronounced responses in the CSP group (pBH-corrected=0.008; f² =0.029). Within the CSP group, SCL correlated weakly with ratings of fear (ρ=0.137; pBH-corrected=0.008) and harmfulness (ρ=0.127; pBH-corrected=0.013), but not urge to avoid (ρ=0.022; pBH-corrected=0.763). Pupil dilation (pBH-corrected≤0.001; f² =0.039) and gaze behavior (pBH-corrected=0.006; f² =0.067) differed between individual pictures in the CSP group but did not consistently differentiate the predefined threat categories. Heart rate and respiratory rate showed no consistent effects. This is the first shoulder-specific picture-viewing paradigm combining selfreports with several psychophysiological measures and the first study to examine pupil dilation as a potential correlate of FMRP. The findings suggest that electrodermal activity may reflect sympathetic arousal related to activity-specific threat appraisal during FMRP-eliciting picture-viewing.

1. Introduction

People with chronic pain are often afraid of movements they believe to cause harm or worsen their pain [79].

According to the fear-avoidance model [79], psychological factors such as pain-related fear and avoidance behavior are important contributors to pain-related disability. The model proposes that pain-related worrying (often referred to as pain catastrophizing [13]) and negative affectivity, driven by threatening illness information, or individual tendencies to interpret pain as harmful or threatening, heighten the chance of acquiring fear of pain, fear of movement or fear of (re)injury, subsequently leading to avoidance behavior.

The resulting outcomes may include the development of depression and functional disability [34, 78]. This vicious circle reinforces both the levels of pain intensity and unpleasantness as well as functional disability [14, 47, 78]. Consequently, fear of movement-related pain (FMRP) plays an important role in musculoskeletal pain chronification [24, 32, 57] and may predict pain-related disability better than pain intensity [15, 83]. However, FMRP does not manifest uniformly across all movements but depends on how individual activities are appraised with regard to their anticipated pain, harm, and behavioral consequences (e.g., avoidance of the activity).

The fear-avoidance model [28, 79] suggests that FMRP is acquired through classical conditioning, where an initially neutral movement (conditioned stimulus) is associated with pain or injury (unconditioned stimulus). FMRP can also be learned through indirect pathways, such as observational or instructional learning [22, 61]. Experimental paradigms commonly use imagery or movement observation to experimentally elicit activity-specific threat appraisal associated with pre-existing FMRP, for example by presenting pictures of potentially fear-eliciting movements [6, 8, 17, 26, [38] [39] [40] 56, 64, 71]. Within the present study, activityspecific threat appraisal refers to the momentary evaluation of a specific activity with regard to its anticipated pain, expected harmfulness, and urge for behavioral consequences [20, 45, 46]. Previous movement observation research has predominantly examined back-straining activities, with fewer studies addressing shoulder-or neck-straining activities [27, 56, 74]. Shoulder pain is a major individual and societal health problem [1, 81] and among the three most common musculoskeletal complains next to neck pain and low back pain [23, 35]. Affected individuals frequently suffer from pain that interferes with activities of daily living (e.g. washing, dressing) and exercise [51].

FMRP is predominantly measured by rather generalizing patient-reported outcome measurements [32, 52], with limited data on psychophysiological processing of activity-specific threat appraisal during experimental FMRP paradigms. Among psychophysiological measures, autonomic measures, including heart rate (variability), respiratory rate and electrodermal activity, have received considerably less attention [17, 56, 73].

Although autonomic responses represent established indicators of emotional arousal and defensive responding [5, 31, 80], little is known about whether different autonomic systems provide comparable or complementary information during experimentally elicited FMRP. In particular, pupillometry has not yet been investigated in this context despite its established role as a measure of sympathetic arousal in anxiety, fear conditioning, and pain anticipation research [9, 12, 19, 25, 36]. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to investigate psychophysiological responses during an experimentally FMRP-eliciting picture-based movement observation paradigm in people with CSP and pain-free controls. Specifically, we examined whether pupil dilation provides additional information on activity-specific threat appraisal alongside autonomic measures used in prior research, including electrodermal activity, heart rate and respiratory rate.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study design

The study was a cross-sectional case control study with a single measurement timepoint and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Luebeck (2025-164) on 5/15/2025. The study protocol was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/26UGD).

2.2 Participants

For this study, participants with CSP and pain-free controls were recruited. Pain-free controls were selected to be comparable in age to participants with CSP (±5 years) and to achieve a broadly comparable gender distribution at group level. All participants had to be ≥18 years old and have sufficient knowledge of the German language to understand the provided study information and the questionnaires. Participants with shoulder pain were included if the shoulder pain persisted for ≥3 months, as defined for a chronic pain syndrome [7, 75]. Exclusion criterion for both groups was uncorrected vision impairment as participants were required to clearly see the presented visual stimuli or if they reported any severe diseases interfering with pain and fear perception (e.g. anxiety, depression). Further, participants with CSP were excluded if the shoulder pain had a neurological origin (e.g. pain due to paraplegic subluxation) and pain-free controls were excluded if they had a history of chronic pain or experienced severe pain in the last 24h.

2.2.1 Participant recruitment

Participants with CSP were recruited from different orthopedic and physiotherapy outpatient clinics in Northern Germany in consultation with the practitioners and by displaying posters and sharing flyers. Painfree participants were recruited at the university campus and via social media and personal contact. Both groups were additionally recruited in sports clubs. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were verified at the beginning of the session. Eligible participants were informed about study aims and procedures by members of the research team. After deciding to participate, they provided written informed consent.

2.2.2 Sample size

The sample size estimation was based on a 3x2 repeated-measures ANOVA (CONDITION X GROUP) for detecting an interaction effect of group and condition in the psychophysiological outcome measures (pupil dilation, electrodermal activity, heart rate, respiratory rate). The calculation was used as an a priori approximation for the planned mixed-effects analyses. It did not represent a multivariate power analysis across all outcome measures. CONDITION refers to (1) baseline measure, (2) mean of psychophysiological responses to the most feared movement picture and (3) mean of psychophysiological responses to the least threatening movement picture and GROUP stand for (1) healthy participants or (2) people with CSP. The calculation was conducted in G*Power using an effect size of f=0.295, based on skin conductance findings of Díaz-Sáez et al., 2021 [17], an alpha level of 0.05, a power of 0.80, three repeated measurements, and a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5. In Díaz-Sáez et al., autonomic responses were assessed following motor imagery or movement observation in people with chronic low back pain and pain-free participants, rather than continuously during picture presentation as in the present study. The effect size was considered an approximate planning value. This yielded a minimum total sample size of n=22 (i.e., 11 participants per group). Given the uncertainty in transferring the effect size from a different population and experimental paradigm, this estimate served as an approximate lower bound rather than the recruitment target. We therefore aimed to recruit 30 participants per group (i.e., n=60 in total) to obtain more precise estimates and to accommodate potential data loss in psychophysiological and eye-tracking recordings.

2.3 Stimulus material

From the 58-pictures set from Tozzo et al. (2024), three of the six movement pictures that were rated highest in previous publications using the Avoidance of Daily Activities Photo Scale (ADAP; [2, 3]) and the Fear of Daily Activities Photo Scale (FDAP; [55]) regarding arousal [72], fear and urge to avoid [55, 74] were chosen ('pushing'; 'carrying on shoulder, hip and back'; 'lifting'). Furthermore, three out of the six movement pictures rated lowest in arousal, fear and urge to avoid were chosen as control conditions ('caring for teeth'; 'eating'; 'drinking') [74].

2.4 Stimulus presentation

To minimize bias in data acquisition, the experimental setting was informed by the guidelines of Steinhauer et al. (2023; [70]) and Mathôt & Vilotijević (2022; [37]). Lighting in the room was adapted to a moderate illumination, aiming for an initial detection confidence in pupil detection of >0.9 after calibration. The color images were presented at eyelevel on a screen placed at a 60cm distance from the participant. The images were fixed at the center of the screen. The screen resolution was 2560×1600 on a 13.3-inch true tone retina display, allowing for an automatic adjustment in tone and brightness and therefore reduction of variability in how participants perceive contrasts due to external lighting changes. All pictures were adapted to a size of 4:3 and had a standardized color profile (sRGB IEC61966-2.1), brightness and contrast.

2.5 Procedure

Participants were asked to fill out the informed consent form and patient-reported outcome questionnaires, first. Afterwards, electrodes and chest belt for the assessment of electrocardial data, electrodermal activity and respiratory data were attached and the eye tracker glasses for the pupillometry assessment were individually adjusted. Following, the participant was placed in front of the screen to get used to the display of the stimulus material and for the calibration of the eye tracker. Before the picture presentation started, the participant sat and rested for at least 5min to control for increased autonomic system reactions due to physical activity. Additionally, participants were asked not to speak and to breathe normally during the measurement.

To increase the threat anticipation during picture presentation, the participants were told that they would have to perform the displayed movements immediately after the experiment. In fact, no movement performance was required, and participants were debriefed about the purpose of the instruction at the end of the session.

For the main data collection, psychophysiological measurements were initially recorded for a 1min resting baseline phase with a grey screen background and an introduction text saying "Following, we will show you pictures of activities impacting the shoulder joint. Please imagine performing the shown movements while you are seeing them." [70]. Following, a white fixation cross on grey background screen was displayed for 5s, followed by a 6s movement-picture onset. Each picture was followed by ratings of fear, urge to avoid and expected harmfulness on a VAS-scale, before the presentation of the next fixation cross. Each loop consisted of all 6 pictures (3 most threatening, 3 least threatening) that were shown in a random order. The experiment consisted of 5 blocks (loops) of stimuli, as recommended by Steinhauer et al. (2023; [70]). The experiment was developed using PsychoPy (PsychoPy v2024.2.4, Ó Open Science Tools Ltd.), facilitating the inclusion of event markers and enabling synchronization with measurement devices. Figure 1 presents an overview of the experimental paradigm. 2.6 Outcome measures 2.6.1 Primary outcome measures (psychophysiological measures)

Pupillometry has previously been used to measure the fear reaction towards an aversive stimulus in several anxiety disorders [60, 63, 68]. Pupil dilatating muscles are mainly innervated by the sympathetic division of the autonomic nervous system, while constrictor muscles are innervated by the parasympathetic division [68]. Hence, pupil dilation serves as a parameter for the activation of the sympathetic system. Gaze behavior was additionally assessed as an exploratory measure of visual attention. FMRP-related threat appraisals may be associated with preferential orienting towards, sustained attention to, or avoidance of threatening pictures [12, 19]. The pupil diameter and gaze patterns were assessed with the Pupil Core wearable eye tracker (© 2024 Pupil Labs GmbH, Germany) with a sampling size of 120Hz, continuously during the experimental stimulation. Pupillometry data was recorded using the Pupil Capture software (Version 3.5.7; © 2024 Pupil Labs GmbH, Germany).

There is evidence that tonic electrodermal activity (e.g., skin conductance) can be used as a proxy for FMRP [17]. Skin conductance is a parameter for arousal of the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system [17], with an increase of SCL referring to a heightened sympathetic response. The galvanic skin response depends on the sweat production that is associated with the general arousal of the autonomic nervous system [59].

Changes in heart rate are commonly employed as a measure of fear conditioned responses and proved feasible as a proxy for FMRP [17, 31]. The heart rate response provides an orientation for the stimulus value and indicates a defensive response, probably reflecting learned fear [31].

Changes in respiratory rate have been identified to serve as a proxy for FMRP in only one study, so far, providing preliminary evidence that respiratory rate may reflect FMRP [17].

In the current study, electrodermal activity, electrocardial data and respiratory data were detected via electrodes of the Biosignal Plux device (© 2025 PLUX Biosignals) attached to the medial phalanx of the index and middle finger of the non-dominant hand and subclavicular as well as superior to the spina iliaca anterior, respectively. Respiration data were collected via an elastic belt positioned on the rip cage. The data were recorded at 200Hz using the OpenSignals software (© 2025 PLUX Biosignals).

2.6.2 Manipulation check/behavioral data

For every movement displayed, fear (how much do you fear performing this movement right now?), urge to avoid (how much would you avoid to perform this movement right now?) and expected harmfulness (how much would this movement cause me harm if I would perform it right now?) was rated on a 0-10 visual analog scale (VAS) and correlated with the psychophysiological measures afterwards.

2.6.3 Secondary outcome measures

Before the experiment started, FMRP was measured by the following subconstructs: for measuring fear of [53, 54, 58, 66]. Additionally, painrelated worrying was quantified using the German version of the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) [44].

Healthy controls and patients with CSP completed the TSK and PCS, whereas the FABQ was administered only to participants with CSP.

In the CSP group, the pain and disability related to daily activities was measured at baseline with the Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI) with 5 items in the pain subscale and 8 items in the disability subscale [1]. The sum score indicates the percentage of disability. Furthermore, an 11-point numerical rating scale was used to report the actual, average and maximum pain intensity at baseline. Sociodemographic data were collected from both groups and included age, gender, self-reported psychiatric disorders, and medication use (anxiolytics, antidepressants, and analgesics). In addition, participants with CSP reported pain duration, medical diagnosis, previous shoulder surgery, their individually most feared movement, the perceived origin of their shoulder complaints (e.g., repetitive work-related movements or overhead sports), and whether the affected shoulder was the right or left and dominant or non-dominant side. Pain-free controls additionally reported any previous shoulder pain or chronic pain that was no longer present at the time of participation.

2.7 Data reduction/preprocessing

Pupil diameter data and gaze position (fixation) data collected by the Pupil Core eye tracking system were preprocessed to reduce artifacts, following procedures recommended by Steinhauer et al. (2023; [21,70]) and Mathôt & Vilotijević (2022; [37]). Although the eye cameras recorded data at 120Hz per eye, data from both eyes were resampled onto a common 60Hz time grid before further preprocessing, as the two eye cameras have independent, non-aligned timestamps. Pupil samples were marked as artifacts if they contained missing values or had a confidence score below 0.6. Missing samples were replaced by linear interpolation along the time axis on the full continuous recording, restricted to interior gaps of up to 0.5s. No extrapolation beyond the first or last valid sample was performed. Interpolation was conducted before segmenting the recording into analysis windows. Additionally, a linear detrending was performed separately for each eye across the full recording to remove slow drifts and increase the emphasis on phasic pupil responses. A bilateral pupil signal was constructed, based on signal quality, by computing the mean if the signal of both eyes was usable. If only one eye provided a usable signal, that signal was retained (10.2% of recordings). No recordings had unusable signals from both eyes. Further, data driven clipping of the signal was performed with 0.1% and 99.9% quantiles of the valid pupil samples. Pupil dilation was computed as the difference from mean pupil diameter in the experimental condition to the pre-trial baseline condition [36, 62]. Pupil diameter reflected the two-dimensional detection diameter in camera pixels. The data analyzed therefore comprised detrended pixel residuals rather than raw pupil diameter in millimeters.

To additionally analyze gaze patterns, fixations and saccades were manually identified frame by frame using the world-camera video of the eye tracker. Fixations with a duration of ≥100ms were included. No upper duration threshold was applied. Predefined areas of interest (AOI) are the shoulders of the people displayed in the pictures as well as the faces. The detection of fixations in the AOI was done manually by using the world camera video of the eye tracker. Time to first fixation (TFF) in AOI and first fixation duration (FFD) were analyzed [18, 82].

The electrodermal activity, electrocardial and respiratory data were preprocessed using the NeuroKit2 tool [33]. The electrodermal activity data signal was smoothed, a low pass filtering was applied, the data were decompositioned in tonic and phasic components and a skin conductance response detection was performed, aligned with contemporary recommendations [4, 69]. A signal of exactly 0µS was reported as electrode dropout, and signal plausibility was determined using a threshold of 200µS.

ECG and respiration data were filtered, and an R-peak-detection was performed. For the ECG data, interbeat intervals were calculated and the heart rate was estimated. Data plausibility was confirmed in a heartbeat range of 40-200bpm. ECG artifacts were additionally identified based on clipping at the sensor limits (±1.47mV) and flatline periods, defined as a rolling standard deviation below 0.05mV over 1s. For respiration data, artifacts were identified based on clipping at the sensor limits (±1.50V), flatline periods with a rolling standard deviation below 0.01V over 10s and samples deviating by more than 6 standard deviations from the participant-specific mean.

The three recording systems (PsychoPy, OpenSignals, Pupil Labs) ran on independent clocks. For each participant, a device-specific time offset into PsychoPy time was therefore computed; the pupil offset was additionally verified against the first and last stimulus event. Three participants were excluded due to synchronization failure.

Quality thresholds for the data were defined upfront (Table I, supplementary material). The number and types of artifacts are reported per participant (Table II, supplementary material). Trial windows were defined as 12s (3s pre + 6s stimulus + 3s post). Inter-trial-intervals were flowing as there was no predefined time window for the self-reported ratings, adding to the 5s rest period before the new trial window started.

However, only the 6s picture-presentation period was included in hypothesis testing. Features were calculated in 1s analysis windows. An analysis window was considered valid if it contained <50% missing data.

Windows exceeding this threshold were assigned‚ missing feature values' and excluded from subsequent averaging. No whole trial was removed as a unit. Across the 8820 analyzed picture-presentation windows, 0.70% of skin conductance responses, hear rate and respiration data as well as 0.62% of pupil data were missing. At trial level, all stimulus windows were missing in 9 of 1470 trials for the SCL, heart rate and respiration rate measures as well as in 1 of 1470 trials for the pupil data. All psychophysiological data were baseline-corrected using the mean of the 3s pre stimulus period to account for lighting changes during the study session.

2.8 Statistical analysis overview

2.8.1 Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and sociodemographics

The sociodemographic data and the results of the PROMs were analyzed descriptively. After assessing for normal distribution, the scores of the PROMs were correlated to explore associations between different selfreport measures. Group comparisons were conducted by parametric test for mean differences: independentsamples t-test for age, TSK activity avoidance and TSK total; Mann-Whitney U test (non-normal distribution) for TSK somatic focus and PCS total; Monte-Carlo permutation chi-square test (sparse expected cell counts) for gender.

2.8.2 Physiological measures

2.8.2.1 Main outcomes

Candidate features were automatically extracted from the continuous psychophysiological recordings.

These features represented characteristics of the physiological responses within the pre-defined analysis windows for each participant, picture presentation, and measurement modality (i.e., mean, auc, min, max).

An automated quality audit was then conducted to identify features with sufficient data completeness for the analyses. Features with more than 50% missing values or data available for fewer than 80% of participants were excluded. Within clusters of highly correlated features (|r|>0.90), only the feature with the fewest missing values was retained. This procedure reduced the initial set of 112 computed features to 66 features. Four additional gaze-related features were included in the analyses. Thus, a total of 70 features were included in the main analyses (Table III, supplementary material).

Main effects of activity (individual picture), threat category, repetition and group as well as the corresponding interaction effects with group (activity × group, threat category × group, and repetition × group), were estimated using separate linear mixed-effects models. Each model included the respective predictor, group and their interaction as fixed effects as well as a random intercept for each participant. Therefore, signals were accumulated and averaged for the respective measurement timeframes (e.g., mean of each individual picture = 6s x 5 repetitions) [70].

To account for multiple testing, Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (BH-FDR) corrected p-values were calculated across all 70 analyzed features within each group. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's f².

For each model, the marginal R² according to Nakagawa and Schielzeth (2013; [50]) was calculated and compared with the marginal R² of a reduced model excluding the respective predictor, following the approach proposed by Selya et al. (2012; [67]).

Subsequently, the mean physiological reactions (pupil dilation, tonic EDA (e.g. skin conductance level (SCL) responses), heart rate, respiratory rate, TTF, FFD) during picture presentation were correlated with the mean ratings of fear/urge to avoid/harm expectancy of the respective pictures using Spearman correlation coefficient. The significance level was set at p≤0.05 with a confidence interval of 0.95.

2.8.2.2 Explorative analysis

The baseline psychophysiological measures of the groups "chronic shoulder pain" and "pain-free" were compared for the first 1min baseline condition and for the accumulated 3s pre stimulus presentation for the entire recorded time by linear mixed-effects models with BH-FDR to check for baseline differences between the groups.

To account for pain-related worrying and fear of (re)injury, the TSK and PCS scores were added to the linear mixed-effects models as covariates in additional exploratory analyses.

Additionally to the mean physiological reactions, various other features of the physiological measurements were analyzed (e.g., AUC, min, max), that are reported in the supplementary material (Table III).

2.8.3 Self-reported ratings

For each rating, separate linear mixed-effects models were fitted, with activity, threat category or repetition, as well as group and their interaction as fixed effects and a random intercept for participant.

4. Discussion

The expected differences in psychophysiological responses were partly supported. Tonic electrodermal activity (e.g., SCL) differentiated between individual activities and predefined threat categories, and revealed distinct patterns compared to baseline between groups. Pupil diameter differentiated between individual activities only, whereas heart rate and respiratory rate did not consistently distinguish between activities or threat categories. However, these findings did not consistently support the expected differences in psychophysiological response patterns between groups. The paradigm successfully elicited activity-specific threat appraisal, reflected by consistently higher fear, urge to avoid, and harm ratings for highly threatening compared with less threatening activities, with stronger responses in participants with CSP. No consistent repetition effects were detected. Together, these findings demonstrate that the used picture-based movement observation paradigm can reliably elicit pre-existing FMRP while revealing distinct psychophysiological response patterns across autonomic measures.

Although most experimental evidence from movement observation paradigms originates from low back pain populations [56], substantially fewer studies include neck pain [27, 48] or shoulder pain [55, 72, 73]. The present study extends existing shoulder-related research by combining activity-specific threat ratings with several psychophysiological measures, including pupillometry, in CSP.

Experimental research has repeatedly shown that pain-related fear generalizes beyond movements previously associated with pain to perceptually or functionally similar activities, even when these activities have never been directly paired with pain or injury [42, 43]. Excessive generalization may result in objectively safe activities being appraised as potentially harmful, thereby promoting persistent avoidance and disability [79]. Accordingly, the higher fear, urge to avoid, and harm expectancy ratings for low-threat pictures observed in CSP may reflect broader generalization of threat appraisal towards daily activities rather than heightened responses to a few particularly threatening movements alone.

Within the fear-avoidance model, threatening illness information is assumed to shape expectations about pain and harm, thereby facilitating activity-specific threat appraisal and subsequent avoidance behavior [79].

These mechanisms closely resemble contemporary concepts of nocebo effects, which are increasingly recognized as clinically relevant in people with musculoskeletal pain, including CSP [29, 30, 65].

Inadvertently communicated threatening information may therefore increase FMRP and contribute to persistent avoidance and disability. The differentiated psychophysiological responses observed in the present study suggest that picture-based movement observation may provide a standardized framework to examine such information-induced effects, although threatening information was not explicitly manipulated.

Among the investigated psychophysiological measures, SCL differentiated both between individual activities and between predefined threat categories and was weakly associated with self-reported fear and harm expectancy ratings in participants with CSP. These findings are consistent with a previous movement observation study in low back pain demonstrating increased skin conductance during a FMRP-eliciting paradigm [17] and extend this evidence to CSP. As electrodermal activity primarily reflects sympathetic activation associated with emotional arousal and defensive reactions [11, 69], the present findings suggest that autonomic arousal accompanies the appraisal of an activity's anticipated threat value. The observed correlations with self-reports were small, indicating a weak correspondence between self-reported appraisals and SCL.

The interpretation of SCL differences also requires consideration of the depicted activities and the experimental instructions. Highly threatening activities (e.g. pushing) may involve greater anticipated physical effort, which could contribute to arousal during movement observation [10, 16]. Furthermore, informing participants that they would subsequently perform the activities may have increased their personal relevance and anticipation of their physical demands [41]. These aspects may help explain the observed responses, although their respective contributions were not examined separately.

Pupillometry revealed a different response pattern. Pupil diameter is strongly influenced by sympathetic activation and has been widely used as an indicator of arousal in anxiety research, fear conditioning and pain anticipation [9, 25, 36]. In contrast to SCL, pupil dilation differentiated between individual activities but not between the predefined threat categories. These findings suggest that pupil responses may be sensitive to subtle differences in the appraisal of individual activities. As pupil diameter reflects sympathetic and locus coeruleus-noradrenergic activity and the intensity of emotional arousal [36, 49], individual activities within the same predefined threat category may differ in their capability to elicit arousal. Pupillometry may therefore provide complementary information on activity-specific threat appraisal and represents a promising addition to psychophysiological measures in experimental FMRP research.

Franklin et al. (2018; [19]) previously examined pupil diameter and gaze behavior during viewing of threatening back-related images. The present study extends this research by investigating pupillometry during shoulder-related movement observation in CSP and relating physiological responses to activityspecific threat ratings. Gaze behavior also differed between individual pictures, providing preliminary information about visual attention during movement observation. These measures therefore characterize responses to individual activities, although their differentiation of the predefined threat categories was less consistent than that of SCL and self-reports.

In contrast, heart rate and respiratory rate did not consistently differentiate between activities or threat categories. While both measures have previously been shown to respond during experimentally elicited FMRP [17], methodological differences, including stimulus presentation and experimental procedures, limit direct comparisons. In people with CSP, Tozzo et al. (2026; [73]) found reduced parasympathetic modulation during shoulder movement observation compared with pain-free controls. As heart rate variability reflects a different aspect of cardiovascular regulation than mean heart rate, these findings suggest that cardiovascular responses to movement observation may depend on the measure examined.

Taken together, the present findings indicate that experimentally elicited FMRP is not reflected uniformly across self-reports and psychophysiological measures. Instead, the observed response patterns suggest that different assessment modalities capture complementary aspects of activity-specific threat appraisal.

According to appraisal theories of emotion, emotional responses emerge from an individual's evaluation of anticipated consequences of a situation rather than from the stimulus itself [20, 46, 76]. Hence, observing daily activities may initiate an appraisal process in which anticipated pain, expected harm, perceived capability and behavioral relevance of the activity are evaluated. Depending on the outcome of this appraisal, physiological and behavioral response systems may become activated to varying degrees. This framework provides a plausible explanation for the different psychophysiological response patterns observed in the present study. Self-reports directly capture the conscious evaluation of anticipated threat, whereas SCL appears to reflect sympathetic arousal associated with this evaluation. Rather than identifying a single psychophysiological marker of FMRP, the present findings support examining multiple psychophysiological assessment modalities, while their complementary contribution remains to be established.

This interpretation is also consistent with our previous work demonstrating that a picture-based questionnaire primarily elicits task-or activity-specific threat appraisal that is not entirely consistent with general FMRP [55]. While FMRP represents the broader pain-related construct, experimentally elicited activity-specific threat appraisal may provide access to one of its underlying mechanisms under standardized laboratory conditions.

Neither self-reports nor psychophysiological responses changed across repeated picture presentations. For physiological measures, correction against the immediately preceding baseline may obscure habituation affecting both baseline and picture-viewing periods. One possible explanation for the absence of consistent repetition effects is that merely observing the activities did not provide an opportunity to test anticipated harmful consequences. In exposure-based interventions, the absence of expected harm may lead to expectancy violation, supporting corrective learning and reducing fear-related avoidance and disability [22, 77]. The present paradigm may provide a standardized approach to examine whether activity-specific threat appraisal changes following such experiences.

The present findings support the use of picture-based movement observation paradigms as a standardized experimental approach to investigate activity-specific threat appraisal in individuals with pre-existing FMRP. Future studies could assess anticipated physical effort and manipulate instructions, including threatening information, to distinguish their contributions to psychophysiological responses. Comparing heart rate and heart rate variability within the same paradigm and examining responses during movement execution or expectancy violation may clarify their responsiveness. Integrating self-reports, behavioral, psychophysiological and neurophysiological measures could help determine their complementary contributions and to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of FMRP and the mechanisms contributing to fear-related disability in chronic musculoskeletal pain.

4.1 Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to evaluate pupillometry as a psychophysiological correlate of experimentally elicited FMRP. Furthermore, combining self-reports with electrodermal activity, pupillometry, heart rate, respiratory rate and gaze behavior enabled simultaneous assessment of multiple response systems under standardized experimental conditions.

Some limitations should be considered. First, movement observation cannot fully reproduce the sensory and motor experience of performing potentially threatening activities. Consequently, the present findings cannot be directly transferred to real-life movement behavior. Second, participants with CSP reported only moderate levels of self-reported FMRP (measured by TSK, FABQ and PCS scores), which may have attenuated group differences in psychophysiological responses. Finally, autonomic responses are influenced by multiple factors beyond FMRP, including general emotional arousal and attentional processes, as well as technical sensitivity issues when recording them. Therefore, psychophysiological measures should be interpreted in relation to the experimental context rather than as specific biomarkers of FMRP.

4.2 Conclusion

The present study supports picture-based movement observation as a feasible approach to experimentally elicit activity-specific threat appraisal associated with pre-existing FMRP in people with CSP. While SCL emerged as the most robust psychophysiological correlate, pupil dilation provided additional information on sympathetic arousal during the appraisal of individual daily activities. Together, these findings support the use of complementary psychophysiological assessment modalities to improve the mechanistic understanding of FMRP and to further advance experimental research on fear-related disability in chronic musculoskeletal pain.
