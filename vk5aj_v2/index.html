Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Over the next decades, the nature of and possibilities for social connection will rapidly evolve with advances in artificial intelligence, mixed-reality technologies, and pervasive digital integration. Accordingly, psychological science must update how it conceptualizes and measures social connection. Here, we advance a three-part conceptual framework for understanding social (dis)connection in contemporary and future environments. First, we situate social experiences within a dynamic relational ecosystem-a system of interaction partners and channels, ambient social cues, and sociocultural contexts that together shape connection. Second, we conceptualize "alone" and "together" not as binary states but as a gradient shaped by level of physical copresence and communication (shades of aloneness and togetherness). Third, we conceptualize discrete social interactions as multidimensional, characterized by features such as interaction partner, relationship type, medium, temporality, engagement, and volition. Building on this framework, we propose five testable predictions about how social (dis)connection may evolve in the coming decades: the decoupling of objective and subjective connection, the erosion of "pure" solitude, the increasing effortfulness and value of in-person interaction, the fragmentation of shared reality, and the growing taken-for-grantedness of connection. We conclude by discussing methodological and applied implications of this framework.

Introduction

On the evening of November 2, 1920, families gathered in their living rooms to learn that Warren G. Harding had won the U.S. presidential election. For the first time, Americans received the same news at the same time through a shared broadcast medium: the radio (Slotten, 2003).

This first broadcast from a licensed radio station-Pittsburgh's KDKA-signaled a transformation in how people would relate to one another and to the broader social world. Over the century that followed, successive waves of communication technology have emerged, reshaping possibilities for social life-from the television to landline telephones, from the internet to mobile devices, and from social media to virtual reality and artificially intelligent (AI) chatbots. Beginning with early forms of written correspondence, social experiences have become more technologically mediated and less tethered to place. To illustrate, as of 2025, 98% of U.S. adults own cellphones (91% of which are smartphones; Pew Research Center, 2025); internet users globally spend more than six hours online daily (Statista, 2025); over half of new romantic partners meet online (Rosenfeld et al., 2019); and 10% of the global population uses ChatGPT (Chatterji et al., 2025). Even when people are together face-to-face, communication technologies influence the quality of social interactions (e.g., by dividing attention or supplanting casual exchanges; Kushlev et al., 2019; Baumeister et al., 2026).

These transformations have generated growing unease about the future of social (dis)connection. Policymakers, public health professionals, and scientists have raised alarms about rising loneliness and declines in face-to-face interaction (Buecker et al., 2021 https://pmc-ncbi-nlm-nih-gov.proxy.lib.umich.edu/articles/PMC7801834/ Office of the U.S. Surgeon General, 2023a; Pfeifer & Mehl, 2026; Rodriguez et al., 2025), increases in living alone across many global regions (Anderson et al., 2023; Snell, 2017), and the psychological risks of digital media use (Haidt, 2024; Twenge et al., 2020; Office of the U.S.

Surgeon General, 2023b). Global health initiatives, like the World Health Organization's

Commission for Social Connection (2025), frame loneliness and social isolation as defining challenges of contemporary life. Given the profound health implications of social connection (Holt-Lunstad, 2021), these concerns are real and urgent. Yet, they are also shaped by assumptions about what social connection is, how it is measured, and what forms of connection are most meaningful.

Looking ahead to the next century, the social landscape will become even more complex.

Advances in AI, mixed-reality technologies, and pervasive digital integration will further shape how, why, and with whom people socially engage, as well as the psychological consequences of doing so (Boase, 2025). Whereas earlier technologies connected us with information and other humans (e.g., radio, mobile phones), emerging technologies increasingly function as interaction partners themselves (e.g., voice assistants, AI chatbots; Inzlicht et al., 2024; Sundar & Lee, 2022). As social inputs become more ambient and ubiquitous-woven into homes, vehicles, workplaces, and even human bodies-people may nearly always be connected, yet they may not necessarily feel connected. Conversely, people may spend more time physically alone without necessarily feeling alone. These socio-technological developments raise fundamental questions for psychological science. What makes an interaction or experience "social" in the first place? What does it mean to be "alone" or "together" amid constant connectivity? What becomes of solitude when opportunities for social interaction are virtually impossible to escape? And finally, how can psychological science help the world navigate changes to social life in ways that promote wellbeing and social connection? Understanding how to capture and make sense of these changes-and how to help design social environments that promote human flourishing-will be a central challenge for psychological science moving forward.

In this paper, we advance a three-part conceptual framework to help psychological science understand experiences of social (dis)connection in contemporary and future environments.

1. First, we propose viewing social experiences as embedded within dynamic relational ecosystems that include interaction partners, interaction channels, ambient social cues, and broader sociocultural contexts.

2. Second, within relational ecosystems, individuals occupy a particular shade of aloneness or togetherness, reflecting how physical co-presence and degree of communication combine to shape whether they are alone, together, or somewhere in between in a given moment.

3. Third, social interactions are characterized by multiple dimensions including (but not limited to) interaction partner, relationship type, medium, temporality, engagement, and volition.

Building on this framework, we propose five testable predictions about the future of social (dis)connection. We end by discussing methodological and applied implications of this conceptualization.

Our approach draws on historical analysis of prior socio-technological shifts, theoretical extrapolation from current trends, and interdisciplinary research on close relationships, solitude, social connection, human-computer interaction, and well-being. Our goal is not to predict the future or to speculate about specific technologies that may emerge. Rather, we aim to articulate some conceptual and methodological considerations to help psychological scientists make sense of social (dis)connection as it evolves-whatever forms future technologies take.

Three Structural Shifts in Social Life

Humans have a fundamental need for social connection and belonging (Baumeister & Leary, 1995; Deci & Ryan, 2000; Dunbar, 1998). Accordingly, social life over the next decades is likely to change less in whether we connect, and more in how, when, where, and why we do. A range of socio-technological developments-including AI, mobile and mixed-reality technologies, and algorithmically mediated platforms-are rapidly restructuring social experiences. Several long-term shifts are already visible and are likely to accelerate in the decades ahead.

First, social life involves an increasingly expanded set of potential interaction partners. Although humans have long interacted with nonhuman entities-such as animals, deities, objects, and ancestors (Cerulo, 2009)-AI agents introduce new capabilities and possibilities: they can generate language (e.g., text, speech), respond in ways that are contingent on and tailored to user input, and are available around-the-clock and across physical locations.

Even at this early stage of adoption, these agents are becoming increasingly embedded in human social life. Across the globe, hundreds of millions of people interact with AI chatbots (Chatterji et al., 2025; Liu & Wang, 2026), and in some regions, embodied robots serve as companions for socially isolated adults (Dong et al., 2025; New York State Office for the Aging, 2023).

Although these systems lack human consciousness and, in many cases, physical embodiment, they can nonetheless occupy meaningful relational roles for some people (Smith et al., 2025).

Second, social life is decreasingly tethered to physical space. This shift accelerated with the rise of mobile telephony in the 1990s, which marked a pivotal reconfiguration of social life from being anchored to specific locations to unfolding anytime and anywhere. As Campbell and Park (2008) explain, mobile communication encouraged a structural shift toward a "personal communication society," blurring boundaries between public and private life, work and leisure, and availability and autonomy. Over time, these changes have heightened expectations for 24/7 accessibility (Baek et al., 2025; Bayer et al., 2016; Ling, 2012), leading some scholars to characterize the smartphone as an extension of the self (Ross & Bayer, 2021). Future shifts toward more hybrid and digital environments (de Souza e Silva et al., 2025) will reshape the affordances of social interaction (e.g., physical touch, co-presence, nonverbal cues; Lieberman & Schroeder, 2020; Smith et al., 2025) with potentially important downstream consequences for psychological experience (Baumeister et al., 2026; Bevis et al., 2026; Kraus, 2017).

Third, social life is increasingly shaped by engineered systems. Human-created algorithms and platform design choices influence what people see, whom they encounter, and how digital social interactions unfold (Matz et al., 2024; Metzler & Garcia, 2024). For example, algorithms that prioritize emotionally negative and identity-affirming content can amplify extreme viewpoints and limit exposure to opposing perspectives, contributing to political polarization and intergroup conflict (Cinelli et al., 2021; Van Bavel et al., 2021; Piccardi et al., 2025). Further, the sycophantic behavior of AI chatbots, which is designed to boost engagement, decreases individuals' willingness to take responsibility for their actions and repair interpersonal conflicts (Cheng et al., 2026). As a result, social experiences-and their psychological consequences-are increasingly structured by the values and incentive systems embedded in the design of technologies.

These developments raise important conceptual questions about what qualifies as a social interaction, and which features of interactions are most consequential for social connection and well-being. Psychological scientists have developed longstanding, robust theoretical frameworks for understanding interpersonal processes; for example, Interdependence Theory emphasizes how relationships are shaped by patterns of mutual influence between interaction partners (Kelley & Thibaut, 1978), whereas the Intimacy Process Model highlights the central roles of self-disclosure and perceived responsiveness in intimate bonds (Reis & Shaver, 1988). These frameworks remain useful for understanding close relationships; however, they were primarily developed to explain face-to-face human relationships and do not explicitly address several features that increasingly vary across contemporary social interactions, including the nature of the interaction partner (human versus nonhuman) and the communication medium (in-person versus online).

Here, following Rodriguez, Trinh, Lieberman, & Schroeder (2026), we conceptualize social interactions as reciprocal, dynamic exchanges during which individuals feel that an entity is attending to or engaging with them in ways that are contingent on their input. Under this definition, social interactions can involve both human and nonhuman interaction partners, including AI chatbots, virtual assistants, animals, and deities (Archer, 1997; Chan et al., 2019; Inzlicht et al., 2024; Telari et al., 2026). We distinguish social interactions from the social experiences, which are broader experiences involving social information, representations, or stimuli that evoke shared meaning but do not require reciprocal exchange. Such experiences may arise in the absence of direct social interaction, such as in the case of consuming traditional mass media (e.g., newspaper, radio, television, books), listening to music, or visiting a public space (e.g., public park, church, museum). These structural shifts in social life change not only how and with whom we interact, but the conditions under which social connection unfolds in the first place. Social experiences are increasingly distributed across diverse interaction partners, locations, channels, and algorithmic systems that influence attention and engagement. As a result, social connection is increasingly difficult to understand as a series of discrete, bounded interactions between humans alone, urging us to rethink how we conceptualize and measure social connection.

A Three-Part Conceptual Framework to Understand Social (Dis)Connection Part I. The Relational Ecosystem

Here, we propose viewing individuals as situated within dynamic relational ecosystems.

Drawing inspiration from classic perspectives emphasizing that human behavior is fundamentally embedded within broader social relations (Granovetter, 1985) and ecological systems (Bronfenbrenner, 1979), we define relational ecosystems as the set of interaction partners, interaction channels, ambient social cues, and sociocultural contexts that shape an individual's social environment at any given moment in time (see Fig. 1).

Interaction partners are the entities with which one is actively interacting, such as friends, pets, AI chatbots, deities, and virtual avatars. Interaction channels are the pathways through which social interaction occurs, including (but not limited to) face-to-face, texting, audio or video call, social media, and virtual reality. Ambient social cues are environmental features that signal the presence or possibility of social connection without involving direct interaction, including nearby conversations, music, people, animals, digital notifications, and technological devices.

Relational ecosystems also include broader sociocultural contexts. Social norms, belief systems, expectations, and institutions, among other factors, shape how individuals interpret and engage with their social environments-for example, whether time alone is valued or stigmatized (Rodriguez et al., 2025; Rodriguez, Baldwin, et al., in press), the extent to which AI chatbots are adopted and how they are perceived (Folk et al., 2025; Scherr et al., 2025), and how socially embedded individuals are in the first place (Campos & Kim, 2017; Markus & Kitayama, 1991).

As a result, the same objective social interactions may afford belonging and a felt sense of connection for some individuals while leaving others feeling disconnected, depending on how they align with culturally-shaped norms and expectations.

To be clear, humans have always lived within relational ecosystems. Nevertheless, a key feature that distinguishes contemporary (and future) relational ecosystems from previous ones is that they afford 24/7 accessibility to social interaction, simulated reciprocity through responsive artificial agents that perform tasks that historically required human intelligence, algorithmic curation of socially-relevant information, and personalization at scale. These features do not merely increase the quantity of potential social interactions; they also alter their structure and psychological meaning. As such, we as psychological scientists need to embrace this complexity and develop more sophisticated and layered frameworks if we want to adequately capture modern (and future) social experiences.

Within relational ecosystems, individuals can engage in multiple forms of social interaction-human and nonhuman, online and offline-simultaneously or in close succession (Campbell et al., 2026). These dynamics may influence one another in complex ways to affect subjective social connection and psychological well-being. Engagement in one modality may displace or complement others, spill over across contexts, and recalibrate expectations for responsiveness. For example, attending to one's phone during an in-person interaction (i.e., phubbing) can undermine the quality of that interaction, illustrating how engagement in one domain directly disrupts another (McDaniel, & Coyne, 2016; Vanden Abeele, 2020). In addition, recent interaction with a sycophantic AI chatbot may render a subsequent disagreement with a romantic partner more painful and frustrating. Understanding these dynamics requires moving beyond models that treat interactions as isolated events and toward frameworks that capture how social experiences are distributed across layered, continuously evolving environments.

Part II. Shades of Aloneness and Togetherness

Historically, physical aloneness implied the absence of human interaction: to be alone was to be objectively disconnected from other people. In contemporary environments, however, people are frequently socially connected even when physically alone. Accordingly, objective experiences of being "alone" versus "together" increasingly defy binary classification, which complicates how researchers conceptualize and operationalize these constructs. This tension is most clearly reflected in contemporary research on solitude. Some scholars define solitude as physical aloneness, whereas others view it as social aloneness, or "noncommunication" (Campbell & Ross, 2022; Coplan et al., 2022; Nguyen & Rodriguez, 2024; Rodriguez et al., 2025). Yet each of these definitions faces growing limitations. What type of presence is required for someone to no longer be considered physically "alone"-are you "alone" when seated on a park bench 20 feet from a stranger? Or when petting a dog or holding a newborn baby (Nguyen et al., 2026)? Or when interacting with avatars in virtual reality? And what even is "noncommunication" in a world of constant connectivity increasingly populated by potential human and nonhuman interaction partners? To accommodate these increasingly complex forms of social experience, we propose conceptualizing being "alone" versus "together" as a gradient rather than mutually exclusive states (see Fig. 2). An individual's placement within this two-dimensional space at a particular moment in time is shaped by their relational ecosystem-namely, the configuration of ongoing social interactions and ambient social cues at a given moment. Importantly, a given position in this space may reflect not a single discrete interaction, but the combined influence of multiple simultaneous or rapidly shifting layers of social experience. One may be, for example, physically alone while virtually interacting with humans or nonhuman agents; physically co-present with other humans but socially disengaged; physically surrounded by others while immersed in virtual reality; or physically alone and not interacting with any social entity at all (i.e., "pure" solitude).

These configurations-which may only become more complex as new digital communication tools, interaction partners, and forms of ambient social cues emerge (Boase, 2025)-reflect qualitatively different shades of aloneness and togetherness that may give rise to distinct subjective psychological experiences.

Across time, individuals may continuously shift between different shades of aloneness and togetherness as their patterns of communication and physical co-presence change. Notably, some positions in this gradient may reflect the absence of social interaction, whereas others may reflect one or multiple social interactions occurring simultaneously or in close succession within a particular context. Understanding social experience thus requires not only mapping where a person is situated within this experiential gradient, but also considering whether that position includes social interaction and, when it does, examining the nature, quality, and meaning of such interactions.

Part III. Social Interactions as Multidimensional

To better distinguish between increasingly heterogeneous kinds of social interactions, one useful approach is to conceptualize social interactions as multidimensional. Each social interaction can be characterized by a set of features including (but not limited to) interaction partner (e.g., human to nonhuman), relationship type (e.g., least close [e.g., stranger] to closest [e.g., romantic partner]), medium (offline to online), temporality (e.g., synchronous to asynchronous), engagement (e.g., passive to active), and volition (e.g., low [involuntary] to high [deliberately chosen]). This multidimensional approach builds on ample existing work in psychology and related fields (e.g., communication) that has distinguished between different types of social engagement.

For example, researchers have tested the effects of active versus passive social media use (Godard & Holtzman, 2024), synchronous versus asynchronous text message interactions (Park & Sundar, 2015), spoken versus written disagreements (Bevis et al., 2026), and AI versus human interactions (Li et al., 2026). Indeed, two people may spend the same amount of time engaged in social interactions on a given day, but those interactions may differ dramatically in their temporality, communication medium, and type(s) of interaction partners. Importantly, we do not propose these dimensions as an exhaustive taxonomy of social interaction. Rather, we offer them as a heuristic framework for guiding future theory development, measurement, and empirical work. In so doing, this framework encourages researchers to move beyond relying solely on the frequency or duration of social interactions and instead characterize interactions according to multiple features. It also suggests new opportunities for experimental research by allowing investigators to isolate specific interaction characteristics and examine their independent and interactive effects on social connection and well-being.

Among the dimensions listed, volition may be particularly consequential moving forward. The future of social (dis)connection may be characterized by two seemingly contradictory trends. On one hand, some forms of social interaction may require greater intentionality than in the past. Historically, social interaction was often embedded within daily life: people often lived in larger households, worked alongside others, participated in religious institutions, and encountered the same individuals repeatedly within their communities (Dunbar, 1998; Putnam, 2000). As rates of living alone continue to increase across many global regions (Rodriguez, Baldwin, et al., in press), remote work expands, and daily activities become increasingly digitized (e.g., food delivery, shopping, banking, and dating), opportunities for spontaneous (or routine) face-to-face interaction have declined (Pfeifer & Mehl, 2026). As a result, initiating and maintaining social relationships may increasingly require deliberate effort.

On the other hand, future social interactions may be characterized by reduced volition in other ways. As engagement with digital technologies becomes increasingly habitual, people are routinely exposed to social information and opportunities for social interaction through push notifications, autoplay features, and algorithmically curated feeds-even when they are not intentionally seeking them out (Bayer et al., 2016 (Bayer et al., , 2022)). In line with this idea, research on digital disconnection shows that control over attention and social availability has become increasingly valued (Vanden Abeele et al., 2024), and perceived control over media use predicts whether digital interactions are experienced as detrimental to well-being (Lee & Hancock, 2024).

Accordingly, a central question for future research is the extent to which social interactions are self-initiated, controllable, and aligned with individuals' goals and needs.

Summary of the Conceptual Framework

Taken together, relational ecosystems (Fig. 1) define the broader social environment in which individuals are embedded at a given moment in time, including the full set of interaction partners, interaction channels, ambient social cues, and broader sociocultural contexts. Within relational ecosystems, individuals occupy a particular shade of aloneness or togetherness (Fig. 2), reflecting how physical co-presence and communication jointly shape whether individuals are alone, together, or somewhere in between in a particular moment. At a more granular level, these configurations are shaped by the presence (or absence) of social interactions (Fig. 3), which are multidimensional. In all, this conceptual framework distinguishes between three complementary levels of analysis: the relational ecosystem (the broader social environment), shade of aloneness or togetherness (momentary experiential states), and social interactions (discrete social exchanges).

Subjective Social Connection (Still) Matters

Although this framework expands how psychological science can conceptualize and measure the structure of social life, mapping objective social environments is only part of the picture. A central challenge remains: understanding how individuals subjectively experience and interpret their social environments.

Psychological scientists have long distinguished between objective and subjective indicators of social connection (Baek et al., 2025; Perlman & Peplau, 1981). It is wellestablished that objective social connection (e.g., frequency of interaction, social network size, time spent with others) does not reliably map onto subjective experience (e.g., how connected people feel). One can be surrounded by-or actively interacting with-others and still feel lonely (Coyle & Dugan, 2012; Danvers et al., 2023; Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010; Perlman & Peplau, 1981; Stavrova & Ren, 2023). Accordingly, capturing subjective connection-the felt sense of closeness, belonging, or understanding-is essential. In the future, novel tools, such as passive neurobiological sensors, may increasingly allow researchers to index how (dis)connected people feel. But for now, subjective connection continues to be best assessed through self-report.

The relationship between objective and subjective connection is, and will likely continue to be, systematically shaped by individual differences. For example, prior work demonstrates that people who are more prone to anthropomorphize are more likely to feel socially connected to AI chatbots (Folk et al., 2025); socially anxious individuals are more likely to feel lonely when around others (Lim et al., 2016); people who are less trusting of humans are more trusting of AI agents (Molina & Sundar, 2024); those who view being alone as a beneficial experience are more likely to reap its psychological benefits (Rodriguez et al., 2020 (Rodriguez et al., , 2023 (Rodriguez et al., , 2025)); and people vary widely in whether they perceive social media platforms as socially connective (Rhee et al., 2021; Rodriguez, Motyl, & Schroeder, 2026). Further, individual differences in familiarity with and access to social technologies, as well as digital literacy, may also shape which forms of connection are available or psychologically meaningful (Hargittai, 2003; van Dijk, 2006).

Accordingly, as social interaction becomes more technologically mediated-and increasingly involves diverse AI agents without human consciousness-the same social environments may be experienced as connective and rewarding for some yet superficial or even aversive for others. This variability highlights the continued importance of understanding social connection not only in terms of objective structures, but also through the subjective lenses that color how it is experienced.

Predictions about the Future of Social (Dis)Connection

Building on our conceptual framework, we propose five theoretically grounded, testable predictions about how social (dis)connection may evolve in the coming decades.

Prediction 1. The Decoupling of Objective and Subjective Social Connection.

As relational ecosystems afford more continuous and diverse forms of social interaction, objective indicators of social connection-such as frequency of in-person interactions, social network size, or living arrangements-may become progressively weak predictors of subjective connection. In many cases, individuals may be persistently "connected" in an objective sense while feeling lonely and socially unfulfilled. As opportunities for interaction become more ubiquitous across physical, digital, and hybrid environments, assessing both objective and subjective forms of connection will become even more important, as these constructs may become increasingly decoupled and may predict different psychological and health outcomes.

Prediction 2. The Erosion of Pure Solitude. As possibilities for social interaction expand and become less tethered to physical space, sustained periods of "pure solitude" (both physical aloneness and noncommunication; see Fig. 2) will grow increasingly rare and more difficult to achieve. This erosion of pure solitude, in which opportunities to connect crowd out opportunities to be alone, may have important consequences. Philosophers like Kierkegaard and Nietzsche regarded separation from social influence as essential for authenticity and moral clarity, and research has documented several psychological functions of solitude including emotion regulation, self-reflection, and autonomy (Rodriguez & Campbell, 2025; Nguyen et al., 2018; Weinstein et al., 2021). If increasingly digital relational ecosystems constrain this inwardness, individuals may face not only the potential costs of reduced face-to-face interaction (Pfeifer & Mehl, 2026), but also the psychological costs of never truly being alone. Notably, the scarcity of pure solitude may increase its perceived value and meaningfulness, as reflected in emerging behaviors like "digital detoxes," whereby people deliberately carve out periods of disconnection to reclaim more authentic ways of living (Radtke et al., 2022).

Prediction 3. In-Person Interaction as More Effortful-and Also More Valuable.

Navigating close human relationships is not an easy endeavor; it requires coordination, compromise, and responsiveness to others' needs (Finkel et al., 2017; Kelley & Thibaut, 1978).

Although humans have historically engaged with lower-demand forms of social connection (e.g., pets, media figures, or casual social ties), AI chatbots are rapidly increasing the accessibility and sophistication of such lower-cost alternatives. As these highly responsive, customizable, and relatively frictionless interactions become more common, human interaction will be experienced as comparatively more demanding (Perry, 2026). On one hand, this may encourage avoidance of unpredictable human interactions-such as conversations with strangers (Epley et al., 2022; Sandstrom & Dunn, 2014; Trinh et al., 2026)-particularly as broader structural shifts, including rising rates of living alone and remote work, reduce opportunities for spontaneous in-person interactions. Yet, it may also render in-person human interaction more valuable, in part because it entails effort (Inzlicht et al., 2018; Rubin et al., 2025) and affords unique elements such as interpersonal touch, physiological synchrony, and physical protection (Baek et al., 2025; Coan & Sbarra, 2015; Packheiser et al., 2024). Consistent with this possibility, Wenger et al. (2026) found that people preferred receiving empathy from a human despite perceiving AI-generated empathy as higher in quality.

Prediction 4. The Increased Fragmentation of Shared Reality. Humans are motivated to create a sense of shared reality with others (Echterhoff et al., 2009; Higgins et al., 2021).

Historically, people experienced relatively shared social environments-such as geographicallybounded communities and overlapping social networks (Dunbar, 1998)-which facilitated this construction of shared reality. However, as previously discussed, contemporary relational ecosystems differ in an important way: algorithmically curated environments and AI-mediated interactions enable highly individualized streams of information (Matz et al., 2017). As a result, individuals may inhabit increasingly personalized social worlds, with potentially reduced overlap in the content they consume or the interaction partners they engage with, human and AI alike (Sunstein, 2018). This is likely to weaken people's sense of shared reality and widen the divergence in their beliefs and lived experiences, with potentially important downstream implications for economic, political, and health outcomes (e.g., political polarization, misinformation, health behavior adoption; Levy & Razin, 2019; Van Bavel et al., 2024).

Prediction 5. The Taken-for-Grantedness of Social Connection. As opportunities for social interaction become more ubiquitous, the around-the-clock availability and responsiveness of interaction partners (whether human or nonhuman) may come to be taken for granted-that is, experienced as default conditions rather than notable features of social life. In other words, as objective connectivity becomes even more normalized, people may become less likely to consciously appreciate connection when it occurs and more likely to notice, react to, or feel frustrated by its absence when expected responsiveness is disrupted. This pattern has clear precedent: earlier communication technologies, such as timekeeping and mobile telephony, initially felt novel and remarkable but gradually became taken for granted once they were reliably embedded in everyday routines (Ling, 2012 (Ling, , 2014;; Ross & Campbell, 2021). As the internet becomes "like oxygen," in that it is omnipresent and difficult to disengage from (Stansberry et al., 2019), social norms surrounding constant connectivity may further intensify (Bayer et al., 2016). Over time, this shift may amplify the psychological toll of disconnection.

Moving Forward

What do these predictions suggest for the future of social (dis)connection? To be sure, humans are highly adaptable and have long adjusted to shifting social and technological environments (Henrich, 2015; Richerson & Boyd, 2004). However, adaptation alone does not always guarantee optimal outcomes. As relational ecosystems continue to evolve, individuals may adapt to conditions that do not necessarily support subjective connection or well-being.

Importantly, these trajectories are not deterministic: they are shaped by human decisions, institutional structures (e.g., policy), and technological design. Understanding why and in what ways social connection is changing-as well as how it can be deliberately shaped in ways that promote well-being-will therefore be a central task for psychological science moving forward.

Below, we outline some methodological and applied considerations for meeting this challenge.

Methodological Implications

As relational ecosystems become more densely populated and complex, our measurement tools must evolve accordingly. As previously noted, contemporary approaches to capturing subjective social connection via self-report remain useful. However, approaches to measuring objective social connection are increasingly limited. For example, assessing the number of inperson interactions does not fully capture the range of social experiences people have, and capturing time spent on social media collapses across theoretically meaningful dimensions (e.g., engagement, temporality) and is too coarse to meaningfully assess online social experiences (Maheux et al., 2025; Valkenburg, 2022). Moreover, reliance on self-reported digital media use-which is only modestly correlated with objectively logged use-raises validity concerns (Parry et al., 2021).

Fortunately, the very technologies reshaping social life also offer novel and promising tools for studying it. Mobile sensing-the use of digital devices (e.g., smartphones, wearables, smart home devices) to passively collect data about people's behavior, context, and physiology-enables ecologically valid assessments of social life as it unfolds in real time (Harari & Gosling, 2023; Gordon et al., 2025; Gordon & Mendes, 2021). Simultaneous streams of data from sensors such as microphones (to capture ambient speech and spoken conversation; Dhand et al., 2026; Mehl et al., 2017), GPS (to capture location; Müller et al., 2022), and Bluetooth radio (scans of Bluetooth devices in the vicinity to capture physical co-presence and crowd density; Barnett et al., 2024) can help index multiple dimensions of social interactions simultaneously with high temporal resolution and low participant burden.

New data collection paradigms also offer novel ways of capturing how people interact with technology. One example is Screenomics, the moment-by-moment account of on-screen content (Ram et al., 2020). When paired with self-report to capture subjective experiences of social connection, these approaches allow researchers to test how different dimensions of objective socio-technological conditions map onto subjective connection. As these digital methods often originate outside of psychology (e.g., Communication, Computer Science), they underscore the importance of psychological scientists reaching beyond their own paradigms and collaborating across disciplines to better understand these matters.

Applied Implications

Beyond documenting the evolution of social (dis)connection over the next decades psychological science is uniquely positioned to help inform it. As relational ecosystems become increasingly influenced by technological advancements, social connection will be influenced not only by individual preferences and choices, but also by the social norms, incentives, and infrastructures that guide those choices (i.e., the sociocultural context). This creates both an opportunity and a responsibility for psychological scientists-who have deep insights into social connection-to inform how these relational ecosystems are built (e.g., Zhao, 2026).

Importantly, individuals' preferences and goals are themselves shaped by social norms and design choices. As a result, what people want-for example, relying on AI for emotional support instead of other humans because it feels less effortful-may not always align with what supports their health and well-being. Accordingly, interventions may need to extend beyond supporting individual agency to also target the social and structural conditions that shape whether and how connection is pursued. These efforts may include designing culturally sensitive environments (Markus et al., 2025) that nudge individuals toward beneficial forms of social connection or reduce barriers to engaging in them in the first place.

At the same time, efforts to shape the future of social connection must grapple with competing incentives across scientific, commercial, and policy domains. Technology companies are often driven by engagement-based business models that may pose risks to users (Kang et al., 2026; Haidt & Rausch, 2026), and partnerships between researchers and industry carry welldocumented risks-including reinforcing existing industry norms, limiting transparency, or serving reputational rather than substantive goals (Mitroff & Sharpe, 2017). The historical record of such collaborations is mixed, emphasizing the need for careful governance, researcher independence, and accountability in academic-industry partnerships (Allcott et al., 2024; Wagner, 2023). These challenges are compounded by incentive structures within academia itself (e.g., publish-or-perish dynamics), which can shape research priorities and constrain translational impact (Bigman et al., 2026). Achieving meaningful change will therefore require not only rigorous theoretical and empirical work, but also institutional conditions that support independence, accountability, and high-integrity collaborations.

In this context, policy represents a critical lever for shaping the future of social (dis)connection (Krems & Kaufman, 2026; Holt-Lunstad, 2023). Decisions about platform design, data governance, transparency, and accountability will very likely shape how social connection unfolds at scale. Ideally, policy can help better align industry incentives with the public good, so that promoting social connection is not perpetually in tension with profit. For example, regulation could play a constructive role here by requiring greater transparency-such as mandating that digital platforms share long-term and real-time data with independent researchers to assess the benefits and harms of digital technologies for social connection (Office of the U.S. Surgeon General, 2023a, 2023b). Furthermore, waiting for definitive causal evidence before informing policy may not be feasible-and may, in practice, cede default governance to profit-driven systems (Orben, 2020; Richtel, 2023). A more reasonable and pragmatic approach is to act on the best available evidence while committing to ongoing evaluation, transparency about uncertainty, and course correction as new evidence emerges (Murthy, 2024). With this approach, public trust in science is critical, as is clear science communication.

The stakes are high. Rates of loneliness and social isolation have increased across various nations over the past two decades (Buecker et al., 2021; Fuller-Rowell et al., 2025; Kannan & Veazie, 2023), posing mental and physical risks (Park et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023). In a Pew Research Center survey, experts across industry, academia, and government described a central tension in the next 50 years: technological innovation could dramatically improve health, longevity, and social connection-or, if left ungoverned, exacerbate surveillance, inequality, and social fragmentation (Stansberry et al., 2019). Importantly, these trajectories are not inevitable.

Rather than adopting a technologically deterministic view, we emphasize that the future of social (dis)connection can be shaped by scientific, societal, and policy choices, just as they can by technological design. How future relational ecosystems are constructed, regulated, and used can shape the extent to which they support or undermine human connection and well-being.

Conclusion

The most consequential social psychological experiments of the next century will not take place in a laboratory. They will unfold in the increasingly complex and dynamic relational ecosystems through which people live, relate to one another, and participate in social life. As the nature of and possibilities for social connection evolve, theoretical frameworks, definitions, and methodological approaches must evolve alongside them. Therefore, the challenge ahead is not simply to document how social life changes, but to ensure that our tools keep pace with transformations that may redefine the very meaning of social (dis)connection.
