Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The Integrated Motivational-Volitional (IMV) model of suicide proposes that feelings of defeat give rise to entrapment, which in turn contributes to suicidal ideation (SI). Although research generally supports this pathway, less is understood about how defeat, entrapment, and SI mutually influence each other over short periods of time. Within a three-week ecological momentary assessment period, we examined short-term temporal associations among defeat, entrapment, and SI (passive and active) in a sample of young adults (N = 156, ages 18-29; data collected 2022-2023) with a recent history of suicidal thoughts or behaviours. We used residual dynamic structural equation models to test concurrent and prospective within-person associations among variables. Higher-than-usual levels of defeat and entrapment were each associated with greater passive and active SI at the same assessment and predicted greater SI at the subsequent assessment. Defeat and entrapment also demonstrated reciprocal associations over time, such that greater levels of each predicted subsequent increases in the other. Similarly, higher-thanusual levels of passive SI predicted subsequent increases in both defeat and entrapment, whereas active SI predicted subsequent increases in defeat only. Findings support the central role of defeat and entrapment in short-term changes in SI. Further, they suggest that the relationship between defeat and entrapment may be reciprocal rather than strictly unidirectional, challenging assumptions of the IMV model. The generalizability of these findings to other age groups and clinical populations remains to be established.

General Scientific Summary

Feelings of defeat and entrapment are thought to contribute to suicidal thinking. This study found that feelings of being defeated and trapped uniquely and separately predicted increases in suicidal thoughts over the following hours and increases in either experience also predicted subsequent increases in the other. Suicidal thoughts, particularly wishes to die, also predicted increases in feelings of defeat and entrapment, suggesting that these experiences may contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle of distress rather than effects being unidirectional and sequential.

Testing Daily Relations Between Defeat, Entrapment, and Suicidal Ideation

Suicidal ideation (SI), broadly defined as thoughts about ending one's own life, is prevalent and often precedes suicidal behaviour (Nock et al., 2008b). Among youth, suicide is a leading cause of death worldwide, and rates may be increasing in some demographics (WHO, 2021). While suicide is complex and difficult to predict (Franklin et al., 2017), several ideationto-action theories have described processes that lead to the emergence of SI and subsequent transition to suicidal behaviour. The Integrated Motivational-Volitional model (IMV model; O'Connor, 2011; O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018) is a three-phase framework that explains why SI first onsets and how it escalates to suicidal behaviour. In the pre-motivational phase, background factors and triggering events heighten vulnerability to suicidal thoughts and behaviours. The motivational phase, germane to this study, describes the emergence of SI. Specifically, experiences of defeat (i.e., failed struggle) are thought to give rise to feelings of entrapment (i.e., a perceived inability to escape an aversive situation), which in turn contribute to SI. Entrapment is therefore theorized to be a proximal driver of suicidal thinking. Finally, the volitional phase describes the transition from ideation to suicidal behaviour, which is influenced by factors such as capability for suicide, access to lethal means, exposure to suicidal behaviour, and planning and preparatory behaviours.

The IMV model is particularly useful for understanding the emergence of SI because it organizes multiple risk factors within a cohesive framework while insinuating multiple distinct pathways to SI. That is, individuals may experience different background vulnerabilities and triggering events, but these experiences are theorized to contribute to SI through the proximal processes of defeat and entrapment (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). The model is also appropriate for understanding suicidal experiences on timescales in which they change (i.e., hours or days; Kleiman et al., 2017; Sherifi et al., 2026). The IMV focuses on near-term contributors to suicide outcomes that may meaningfully fluctuate in shorter timescales.

The motivational phase of the IMV model has considerable empirical support. Several studies have found concurrent positive associations among defeat, entrapment, and SI ( O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018; Souza et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2011). Entrapment mediates associations between earlier defeat and subsequent SI in longitudinal studies, in line with the IMV's hypotheses (Branley-Bell et al., 2019; Wetherall et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2026). However, other studies do not find these proposed pathways (Souza et al., 2024). Extant longitudinal research has measured IMV constructs over weeks or months and thus cannot address whether these variables contribute to short-term (i.e., within hours or days) changes in SI in the manner the theory proposes. SI can vary considerably within hours and days (Kleiman et al., 2017; Nock et al., 2009) and cognitive or affective states may drive rapid increases (e.g., Kivelä et al., 2024; Scott & Brown, 2025; Rath et al., 2019). Defeat and entrapment may also be cognitive-affective states that are not stable, even over short time periods. Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA), a tool for repeatedly assessing people in their natural environments, may therefore be well suited to examining the short-term, within-person relationships between defeat, entrapment, and SI.

Emerging EMA research partially supports the IMV but also suggests that the relations between defeat, entrapment and SI are more complex than the linear sequence the model proposes. Stenzel and colleagues (2020) found high within-person variability in defeat and entrapment and a concurrent association between the two constructs. However, defeat did not predict entrapment at the subsequent assessment approximately two hours later. Van Ballegooijen and colleagues (2022) examined defeat, entrapment, and SI among adults with recent suicidal thoughts or behaviours and found that entrapment was associated with SI both concurrently and prospectively. Further, they found reciprocal temporal associations between defeat and entrapment at approximately three-hour prompt intervals. Moscardini and colleagues (2023) found that defeat predicted entrapment only concurrently, whereas entrapment predicted suicidal desire both concurrently and prospectively. Together, these findings suggest that entrapment is particularly relevant to subsequent changes in suicidal thinking, but it is less clear how defeat and entrapment covary in short time windows. A recent review of EMA research examining IMV constructs found evidence for both concurrent and lagged associations between entrapment and self-harm thoughts and behaviours but highlighted the limited and methodologically heterogeneous nature of this literature (Winstone et al., 2024).

Another consideration is that SI encompasses a range of cognitions that vary in explicit content. Although the distinction between passive and active SI is inconsistent in the literature, one common characterization has been that passive SI is a desire or wish to die, whereas active SI involves explicit thoughts of killing oneself (e.g., Van Orden et al., 2010). Across two large samples of adults, Wastler and colleagues (2023) found that passive and active suicidal thoughts were better represented as two related constructs (i.e., generally described as thoughts reflecting a desire for death or nonexistence, and thoughts explicitly involving killing oneself) rather than as a single construct with varying "gradients" or degrees of severity. Although some ideation-toaction theories of suicide propose a progression from less to more severe forms of suicidal thinking, with active SI occurring later and more proximally to suicidal behaviour (e.g., Klonsky & May, 2015; Van Orden et al., 2010), passive SI may not simply represent an earlier or less severe cognitive state. For example, a meta-analysis of 86 studies found that passive SI was associated with suicide attempts and other established correlates of suicide risk, with largely similar correlates observed for passive and active SI (Liu et al., 2020). Moreover, some individuals with a history of suicide attempts report passive SI without ever experiencing active SI (Wastler et al., 2022), suggesting that suicidal thoughts and behaviours may not always follow a progression from passive to active SI.

Research on passive and active SI highlights the importance of considering variability in SI content. Examining passive and active SI separately may therefore provide a more nuanced understanding of whether factors associated with SI operate similarly across distinct forms of ideation (e.g., thoughts of death versus thoughts of killing oneself). However, existing EMA research on defeat and entrapment has generally not distinguished between passive and active SI, particularly regarding temporal associations with defeat and entrapment. For example, van Ballegooijen et al. (2022) assessed temporal associations between defeat, entrapment, and SI, but SI was assessed using a single item ("Right now, I feel suicidal"). Consequently, it remains unclear whether defeat and entrapment contribute similarly to short-term fluctuations in passive and active SI. This distinction has implications for suicide theories that propose separate contributors to passive and active SI (e.g., Klonsky & May, 2015; Van Orden et al., 2010) and generally for understanding how suicidal thoughts may escalate or worsen in some cases.

Taken together, gaps in our understanding of short-term changes in SI highlight the need for further intensive longitudinal research examining momentary associations among defeat, entrapment, and SI, attending to the latter's content. Clarifying the temporal sequence among these variables has implications beyond evaluating the IMV model. For example, if defeat precedes increases in entrapment, defeat might be an earlier, potentially actionable indicator of an unfolding process associated with SI. Alternatively, if entrapment is more proximally associated with subsequent SI, increases in entrapment may signal a point at which SI is more likely to emerge or intensify. Defeat and entrapment may also be independently associated with subsequent SI, rather than operating primarily through the sequential pathway proposed by the IMV model. This would raise the possibility that defeat and entrapment are part of distinct pathways to suicidal thinking. Consequently, such findings would indicate that defeat and entrapment should be investigated separately. This would challenge the notion that defeat and entrapment reflect the same general construct (Griffiths et al., 2015; Taylor et al., 2009), despite how they are described in the IMV model (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Examining these processes may be particularly important among young adults, given the high prevalence of suicidal thoughts and behaviours during this developmental period (Han et al., 2016; Nock et al., 2008a Nock et al., , 2008b)). Ultimately, establishing how defeat and entrapment contribute to SI over short timescales may help identify clinically meaningful changes in risk, and that could be viable targets for intervention.

We used a three-week EMA protocol to examine the short-term, within-person associations between defeat, entrapment, and both passive and active SI in a community-based sample of young adults with past-year SI and/or suicide attempt. We examine whether momentary changes in defeat and entrapment are associated with passive and active SI concurrently and prospectively at the subsequent assessment, as well as the prospective associations between defeat and entrapment. In line with prior research and theory, we hypothesize that within-person increases in defeat and entrapment will be associated with greater passive and active SI at the same timepoint, and that increases in entrapment will predict greater passive and active SI at the subsequent assessment. Given mixed findings regarding the shortterm temporal relationship between defeat and entrapment, we examine their prospective associations without a directional hypothesis.

Method Participants

Participants (N = 156) were young adults with a history of SI or attempt recruited from an undergraduate study pool and the broader community in a city in Eastern Ontario, Canada.

Participants were between the ages of 18 and 29 (M = 20.03, SD = 2.74). Sample demographic characteristics are reported in Table 1. 1 Of the sample, 94.9% of participants endorsed SI in the past year, and 37.8% reported having a lifetime history of suicide attempts (52.6% aborted attempt; 21.2% interrupted attempt). Frequencies pertaining to participants' suicidal thought and behaviour histories are presented in Table 2. Diagnoses were assessed using the Quick Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (QuickSCID-5; First & Williams, 2021). The most common diagnostic categories were anxiety disorders (53.2%), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (27.6%), current major depressive disorder (22.4%), and substance use disorders (18.6%). Fortynine percent of participants met criteria for two or more diagnoses (see also Sherifi et al., 2026).

Procedure

The institutional Research Ethics Board approved this study. Participants provided informed consent before completing all procedures. Data collection took place between 2022 and 2023. Participants were first screened to determine eligibility. Eligibility criteria included SI or a suicide attempt in the past year, access to a smartphone for EMA survey delivery, Englishlanguage proficiency, and residence in Ontario. To assess participants' histories of SI and/or suicide attempt, we used items adapted from the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviours Interview (Nock et al., 2007). Participants were asked whether they experienced thoughts of killing themselves in the past year and whether they had an aborted attempt, interrupted attempt, or suicide attempt in the past year. As part of safety protocols, participants provided contact information for someone close to them. Eligible participants completed two baseline appointments that included clinical interviews, questionnaires, and computerized tasks prior to beginning the three-week (21-day) EMA period. All participants were remunerated with gift cards.

EMA survey items were delivered to participants' smartphones through the SEMA3 app (O' Brien et al., 2024). Participants received detailed instructions on the use of the app and EMA survey completion during their baseline session. Surveys were delivered at quasi-random time points four times per day within preselected time buckets (9-11am, 12-2pm, 3-5pm, 6-8pm), and once at the end of the day (9pm), for a total of five surveys per day, and 105 surveys across the 21-day EMA period. Participants had 60 minutes to complete each survey.

Measures

Participants completed baseline questionnaires and responded to items pertaining to momentary defeat, entrapment, and SI at each EMA prompt.

Baseline

At baseline, participants completed a demographics questionnaire designed for the study.

They additionally completed the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviours Interview-Revised (Fox et al., 2020), which we used to assess lifetime, past year, past month, and past week history of SI and attempts.

Momentary Suicidal Ideation

Momentary SI was assessed using two items capturing passive and active ideation.

Passive SI was assessed with the item, "Right now, how strong is your desire/wish to die," rated on a scale from 0 ("not at all") to 100 ("very much"). Active SI was assessed with the item "Right now, how intense is your desire/wish to kill yourself", rated on a scale from 0 ("absent/no intent") to 9 ("extremely strong").

Momentary Defeat and Entrapment

Items measuring momentary defeat (two items) and entrapment (two items) were adapted from the Short Defeat and Entrapment Scale (SDES; Griffiths et al., 2015), an 8-item self-report measure with excellent internal consistency. Items were chosen for content (Gilbert & Allan, 1998) and are consistent with prior research examining momentary defeat and entrapment in an EMA study (Stenzel et al., 2020). Participants responded to items on a visual analogue slider scale ranging from 0 ("not at all") to 100 ("very much"). Items for defeat were "at this moment, I feel…defeated by life" and "…I feel powerless". Items for entrapment were "…like getting away from who I am and starting again" and "…like there is no way out of my current situation".

Average scores for defeat and entrapment using each of the two items were computed.

Data Analysis

We used Residual Dynamic Structural Equation Modelling (RDSEM) to examine the temporal relationships between entrapment, defeat, and both passive and active suicidal ideation. DSEM extends multilevel structural equation modelling by explicitly modelling the temporal ordering of observations, making it particularly well suited for analyzing lagged within-person processes (McNeish & Hamaker, 2020). Within RDSEM, the multilevel structure separates within-person dynamics from between-person differences. The within-person level captures how variables change over time within individuals, whereas the between-person level examines stable differences between individuals.

The cross-lagged panel models used included both autoregressive and cross-lagged effects. Autoregressive paths quantify inertia by estimating the extent to which a variable at one assessment predicts itself at the subsequent assessment. Cross-lagged paths estimate whether changes in one variable predict subsequent changes in another variable over time. Random effects were specified for intercepts, autoregressive and cross-lagged slopes, and residual variances, allowing these parameters to vary across individuals. This accounts for betweenperson differences in average levels of each construct, the strength of temporal associations, and the amount of unexplained within-person variability. In each model, we included time as a covariate to account for trends in the outcome (i.e., tendencies for increases or decreases in wish to die over the course of the study).

We estimated DSEM using Bayesian methods, which are required for this modelling framework and facilitate the estimation of complex models while providing robust handling of missing data (Asparouhov, Hamaker, & Muthén, 2018). Standardized parameter estimates were interpreted alongside their 95% credibility intervals, which represent the range within which the true parameter is estimated to lie with 95% probability. Effects were considered statistically significant when the corresponding credibility interval did not include zero. DSEM assumes equally spaced observations when estimating temporal associations.

However, EMA data commonly contain irregular spacing due to overnight intervals, missed reports, and semi-random prompt timing. To account for these irregular intervals, the TINTERVAL function in Mplus was used to create a time variable based on three-hour intervals, inserting missing data records where observations were misaligned (Asparouhov, Hamaker, & Muthén, 2018). Model convergence was evaluated using the Potential Scale Reduction (PSR) statistic, with values close to 1.00 indicating adequate convergence by demonstrating minimal between-chain variability relative to total variability (Muthén, 2010). Trace plots were also visually inspected for evidence of poor convergence, including non-random patterns and autocorrelation between draws, with no abnormalities observed. To minimize the risk of premature convergence, the number of iterations was increased until PSR values stabilized. Each model was estimated using 10,000 iterations with a thinning interval of 10. Finally, all withinperson variables were modelled as latent person-mean centred, which improves the interpretability of within-person effects and reduces Nickell's bias when estimating autoregressive parameters.

The study analyses were not pre-registered. Analyses were guided by hypotheses that were derived from the IMV model of suicidal behaviour (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). The analytic sample comprised 156 young adults, all of whom were retained in the primary analyses.

Concurrent Associations. Controlling for autoregressions, entrapment (β = .161, 95% CrI [.132, .188]) and defeat (β = .262, 95% CrI [.231, .292]) were significantly and positively associated with passive suicidal ideation at the same assessment (Figure 1A; Table 6).

Time-Lagged Associations. The first time-lagged model examined the temporal associations among entrapment, defeat, and passive suicidal ideation (Figure 1B; Table 7).

Autoregressive effects for passive suicidal ideation (β = .216, 95% CrI [.186, .245]), entrapment (β = .284, 95% CrI [.255, .313]), and defeat (β = .246, 95% CrI [.217, .274]) were all statistically significant. Concurrent Associations. Controlling for autoregressions, entrapment (β = .125, 95% CrI [.097, .154]) and defeat (β = .213, 95% CrI [.184, .243]) were significantly and positively associated with active suicidal ideation at the same assessment (Figure 1C; Table 6).

Reciprocal Associations Between Suicidal Ideation, Entrapment, and Defeat

Entrapment and defeat demonstrated significant reciprocal temporal associations in both models (Table 7). In the passive suicidal ideation model (Figure 1B), higher-than-usual levels of entrapment predicted subsequent defeat (β = .162, 95% CrI [.133, .189]), while higher-than-usual levels of defeat predicted subsequent entrapment (β = .147, 95% CrI [.118, .175]). Similarly, in the active suicidal ideation model (Figure 1D), entrapment predicted subsequent defeat (β = .170, 95% CrI [.140, .198]), and defeat predicted subsequent entrapment (β = .152, 95% CrI [.123, .180]).

Reciprocal temporal associations were also observed between suicidal ideation and these constructs. Higher-than-usual levels of passive suicidal ideation (Figure 1B) significantly predicted subsequent entrapment (β = .061, 95% CrI [.033, .087]) and defeat (β = .099, 95% CrI [.072, .125]). In contrast, higher-than-usual levels of active suicidal ideation (Figure 1D) significantly predicted subsequent defeat (β = .034, 95% CrI [.002, .066]), but not entrapment (β = .025, 95% CrI [-.005, .054]). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of findings after excluding participants who demonstrated no within-person variability in suicidal ideation across the study (see Supplementary Tables S1-S3). The findings were largely consistent with the primary analyses. However, the association between active suicidal ideation and subsequent defeat, which was statistically significant in the full sample, was no longer significant after excluding participants with no variability in suicidal ideation. All other observed prospective associations from the primary analyses remained statistically significant in sensitivity analyses.

We also conducted exploratory analyses that tested concurrent and time-lagged associations among defeat, entrapment, and suicidal ideation separately for participants with and without a history of suicide attempt (see Supplementary Figures S1-S2). Overall, the effects observed in these analyses mirrored the primary analyses.

Discussion

Our goal was to examine short-term associations among defeat, entrapment, and SI and test hypotheses grounded in the IMV model of suicide (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Several key findings emerged. First, defeat and entrapment were concurrently associated with both passive and active SI. Second, within-person increases in both defeat and entrapment prospectively predicted increases in SI (passive and active) at the next assessment. Third, defeat and entrapment were reciprocally associated across time; higher-than-usual levels of defeat predicted subsequent increases in entrapment, consistent with the IMV model, but higher-than-usual entrapment also predicted subsequent defeat. Finally, within-person increases in passive SI reciprocally predicted both defeat and entrapment, but active SI only predicted subsequent defeat, and not entrapment. Together, these findings highlight the importance of defeat and entrapment in understanding short-term fluctuations in suicidal thinking. They suggest that the temporal relationships among IMV constructs are more dynamic and reciprocal than the model proposes.

Concurrent Associations Among Defeat, Entrapment, and SI

Consistent with prior research, defeat and entrapment were concurrently associated with passive and active SI. Importantly, these associations were at the within-person level; at moments when individuals experienced greater defeat or entrapment relative to their own typical levels, they also reported more severe SI. These findings extend evidence from traditional crosssectional research by demonstrating that associations among defeat, entrapment, and SI are also evident in fluctuations occurring within individuals in daily life. They also demonstrate the value of assessing these variables repeatedly in situ. This distinction is important because betweenperson associations (e.g., that individuals who generally report greater entrapment also report greater SI) do not necessarily indicate that increases in entrapment within a given individual would forecast changes in that same person's SI.

Our results further suggest that defeat and entrapment are relevant to understanding momentary changes in forms of SI with distinct thought content. Passive and active SI are related but distinguishable types of suicide cognition, and not different locations on a spectrum of severity (Wastler et al., 2023). Our findings highlight the value of considering both the dynamics and content of suicidal thinking. They also contribute to a growing body of literature focused on understanding whether predictors have differential short-term effects on passive versus active SI.

Prior intensive longitudinal research suggests that such distinctions may be meaningful. For example, some components of sleep disturbance have shown different within-person associations with passive and active SI (Cox et al., 2023), while daily sleepiness may influence the strength of the association between the two forms of SI (Porter & Bishop, 2024). Thus, the present findings support continued examination of the distinct forms of SI when examining proximal correlates.

Prospective Associations Among Defeat, Entrapment and, SI

In the IMV model, entrapment is a proximal driver of suicidal ideation (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Our findings support this theoretical assumption and align with prior EMA research (Moscardini et al., 2023; van Ballegooijen et al., 2022). However, in contrast to van Ballegooijen and colleagues (2022), we found that defeat also predicted subsequent active and passive SI. Importantly, our findings suggest that the short-term relevance of defeat may not be limited to its proposed role as an antecedent to entrapment. Rather, elevations in both defeat and entrapment may signal proximal increases in SI over the ensuing hours. In daily life, the worsening of SI may be driven by dynamic relationships among defeat and entrapment, rather than entrapment alone. Defeat and entrapment may be associated with subsequent SI through their mutual effects on other putative near-term predictors like hopelessness, which has been associated with SI in prior EMA research (see Kleiman et al., 2017). Defeat and entrapment involve perceiving adversity as insurmountable. This is consistent with the emphasis on hopelessness in the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide (Van Orden et al., 2010), which proposes that hopelessness about changing adverse interpersonal circumstances contributes to the desire to die. Further, defeat and entrapment may reflect a common underlying construct (Griffiths et al., 2015) but are also theorized to be distinct (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Further research is needed on the construct validity of defeat and entrapment measured in EMA studies.

Additionally, future studies should examine likely mediators of the effects of entrapment and defeat on subsequent short-term changes in SI (e.g., hopelessness) to understand discrepancies between findings like ours and the IMV's predictions.

Reciprocal Associations Between Defeat, Entrapment, and SI

We also found reciprocal prospective associations between defeat and entrapment, providing partial support for the temporal ordering the IMV model proposes. We found that moments of greater defeat predicted subsequent increases in entrapment. However, greater entrapment also predicted subsequent increases in defeat, and this prospective link is not explicitly hypothesized by the IMV. Bidirectional effects between these variables replicate prior findings in studies that also considered relatively short measurement intervals (van Ballegooijen et al.,2022). We extended this work by examining these processes in relation to passive and active SI separately. Some recent EMA research has found that predictors of passive and active SI do not completely overlap (e.g., Hallensleben et al., 2019; Weires et al., 2025), and we add to a growing literature on this issue. Ultimately, determining shared versus distinct drivers of these forms of SI has important clinical implications, which we discuss below.

We contend that the related but separable states of defeat and entrapment reinforce one another. Consistent with the IMV model, repeated experiences of unsuccessful struggle may contribute to perceptions of entrapment, an interpretation that aligns with research demonstrating the effects of uncontrollable aversive experiences on subsequent escape behaviour (Maier & Seligman, 2016). Conversely, feeling trapped may activate or strengthen negative beliefs about oneself and one's ability to overcome adversity, such that subsequent difficulties are increasingly interpreted as evidence of personal failure. This is consistent with theories of suicide that emphasize the activation of suicidogenic schemas thought to precede suicidal crises (Rudd, 2006; Wenzel & Beck, 2008). In short timescales, the transactional, mutually-reinforcing relations between defeat, entrapment and SI may acutely escalate the severity of suicidal thinking. However, these relations may be specific to the within-day level and not detected when assessments are spaced further apart. Over longer timeframes, effects may be unidirectional and sequential as the IMV insinuates (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018) and some studies find (Branley-Bell et al., 2019; Wetherall et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2026). Overall, suicide theories must continue to be examined at different timescales and potentially revised to describe the shorterversus longer-term effects of key variables.

Our findings further suggest that the reciprocal dynamics between defeat and entrapment may extend to suicidal thinking itself, although this pattern was different for passive versus active SI. Passive SI predicted subsequent defeat and entrapment, but active SI only predicted subsequent defeat. However, the association between active SI and subsequent defeat was no longer statistically significant when participants with no within-person variability in SI were removed (see Supplementary Tables S1-S3); thus, the finding warrants cautious interpretation and further investigation. One possible explanation for the dissociation between passive and active SI effects is that people who endorse active SI may view suicide as a viable way to end their current state (see Al-Dajani et al., 2019). At its core, entrapment is characterized by the perception of being trapped in an aversive situation from which escape is perceived as unavailable; while some people with active SI may feel stuck, suicide may seem like a way to become "unstuck" for others, collectively weakening the connection between active SI and entrapment. In contrast, our passive SI item inquired about a desire or wish to die. In recent latent class analyses of suicide content, classes characterized predominantly by passive SI items lacked endorsement of items referring to suicide planning and preparatory actions (Martin et al., 2026). People endorsing primarily passive SI may experience less escape-related cognition; the anguish related to their passive SI may therefore increase subsequent feelings of entrapment.

However, our interpretation is speculative, particularly because we did not directly assess whether suicide was perceived as a means of escape.

Clinical Implications

Our findings also have implications for the assessment and treatment of suicidal thinking.

Defeat and entrapment may be important constructs to monitor in practice given that withinperson elevations in entrapment preceded increases in both passive and active SI. Put another way, changes relative to a person's typical level of defeat and entrapment may be clinically informative, even beyond the absolute severity of these states. This idea is consistent with interventions for suicide such as the Safety Planning Intervention (Stanley & Brown, 2012), which emphasizes identifying individualized warning signs of an emerging suicidal crisis and linking these warning signs with coping strategies, sources of support, and other steps that can be implemented as risk increases. Future research is needed to d etermine whether increases in defeat and/or entrapment may be individualized warning signs and whether monitoring within-person changes in these constructs provides actionable information beyond what is obtained in existing suicide risk assessments.

Elevated defeat and entrapment may also be useful as a signal that further assessment is needed. Elevated entrapment suggests someone feels stuck in an aversive situation but does not identify the circumstances producing that perception. These circumstances may be interpersonal (e.g., relationship conflict, social isolation), intrapersonal (e.g., difficulty coping, distressing thoughts or emotions), or situational (e.g., stressors in various domains of life) and are likely to require different clinical responses (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Thus, identifying elevated entrapment may provide an opportunity to explore what is contributing to a patient's SI and general despair. For example, entrapment arising from social isolation may require a different response than entrapment arising from interpersonal conflict, academic or occupational difficulties, or difficulty managing distressing internal experiences.

The prospective associations do not indicate that elevated defeat and entrapment alone are a marker of imminent suicide risk in all cases. Suicidal thinking is influenced by multiple interpersonal, affective, cognitive, and contextual processes (Franklin et al., 2017; O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018), and the present study examined only a subset of these factors. An important next step will therefore be to determine whether momentary entrapment provides unique information about subsequent changes in SI when considered alongside other dynamic factors. Intensive longitudinal studies incorporating constructs across multiple theoretical frameworks could help establish the incremental contribution of entrapment and identify combinations of experiences that are particularly relevant to short-term changes in SI. Such work may ultimately clarify whether monitoring changes in entrapment can contribute meaningfully to individualized approaches to assessing and responding to suicidal thinking.

Lastly, our results contribute to a growing body of research suggesting that passive SI warrants careful assessment and structured intervention in clinical practice. Historically, passive SI has received comparatively less research and clinical attention than active SI, potentially reflecting assumptions that wishes for death represent a less concerning form of suicidal thinking (Liu et al., 2020; Szanto et al., 1996). However, a systematic review and meta-analysis found that passive and active SI share largely comparable clinical and psychological correlates, including associations with suicide attempts (Liu et al., 2020). We extend this literature by suggesting that passive SI may arise not only in the context of defeat and entrapment but may also precede them. Clinically, this raises the possibility that passive SI can be part of a selfreinforcing cycle of suicidal distress. Assessment of passive SI may therefore provide an opportunity to identify and intervene on an unfolding cycle of suicidal distress, even in the absence of active thoughts of killing oneself.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of the present study point to important directions for future research.

First, although EMA and the present analytic approach allowed us to establish temporal ordering over relatively short intervals, the observed prospective associations do not establish causality.

Moreover, the study focused on defeat and entrapment without repeatedly assessing several experiences that may precede or shape these states. The IMV model situates defeat and entrapment within a broader framework that includes triggering events and experiences such as humiliation and failed social struggles (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Future intensive longitudinal research could therefore assess these experiences alongside defeat, entrapment, and SI to test more complete pathways proposed by the model. Examining specific daily-life events may be particularly informative. Interpersonal setbacks, rejection or exclusion, conflict, and academic or occupational stressors are linked to suicide-related outcomes (Liu et al., 2014; Stewart et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2024) and may be contexts in which perceptions of defeat and entrapment emerge. Measuring these contexts could clarify the circumstances that give rise to defeat and entrapment.

Second, the temporal relationships we identified occurred over the roughly three-hour interval between assessments. Relationships among these constructs may operate differently across shorter or longer periods, and processes occurring between EMA assessments could not be captured. This consideration is particularly important given evidence that suicidal thinking can fluctuate meaningfully within minutes (Coppersmith et al., 2023). The reciprocal associations between defeat, entrapment, and passive SI observed here may characterize their relationship over several hours but may not necessarily emerge in the same way over minutes, days, or weeks. Future studies using measurement-burst designs, with more frequent assessments during selected periods, could help determine whether defeat precedes entrapment over shorter intervals or whether reciprocal associations are evident even at these timescales. Examining these processes across multiple timescales could provide a stronger test of whether the sequential pathway proposed by the IMV operates at some timescales while more reciprocal processes characterize others.

Third, our study could not establish whether defeat and entrapment contribute to the initial onset of SI because participants all had a recent history of SI and/or attempts. Factors associated with changes in existing SI may not necessarily be the same factors that contribute to its first occurrence. Future longitudinal research including individuals across a broader range of suicide histories could examine whether increases in defeat and entrapment precede the emergence of new-onset SI, fluctuations among individuals with established SI, or both. Further, given the different prospective findings for passive and active SI, future research could examine within-person transitions between these forms of thinking. Such work could help clarify whether defeat and entrapment play different roles in the emergence, maintenance, or changing content of suicidal thoughts over time.

Constraints on Generality

Participants were young adults with a recent history of suicidal thoughts or behaviours, recruited from a university study pool and the broader community in Eastern Ontario, Canada.

Thus, findings may not generalize to adolescents, older adults, individuals without prior suicidal thoughts or behaviours, or clinical populations. Developmental and contextual experiences may also influence how defeat and entrapment arise and relate to SI. For example, interpersonal relationships, increasing independence, and academic or occupational transitions may be particularly relevant sources of defeat and entrapment during young adulthood (Arnett et al., 2014; Miller & Prinstein, 2019).

Our findings may be limited by the measures and assessment procedures used. We assessed defeat and entrapment using brief adaptations of established measures (Griffiths et al., 2015), whereas we assessed passive and active SI using a single item each. Different measures or operationalizations of these constructs may yield different associations. Moreover, the temporal relationships observed here reflect approximately three-hour assessment intervals over 21 days.

We expect repeated assessments in naturalistic settings to be important for capturing these within-person associations, although the specific smartphone application or survey delivery platform is unlikely to be essential. Whether similar patterns generalize to different assessment intervals, measurement approaches, and populations is an open question.

Are participants were predominantly women and White, although several other gender and racial or ethnic groups were represented. Recruitment from a university study pool and the surrounding community influenced this composition. We cannot determine whether the observed associations would generalize to other demographic groups; further research with more diverse samples is needed to establish whether these apply to diverse gender, racial, and ethnic identities.

Conclusion

We conducted a temporally fine-grained examination of relationships between defeat, entrapment, and SI in daily life. Our findings support the motivational phase of the IMV model while also suggesting a more complex pattern than a strictly sequential pathway from defeat to entrapment to SI. Defeat and entrapment were reciprocally associated over time, and both predicted subsequent increases in passive and active SI. However, these relationships were also reciprocal for passive SI, though not for active SI. Together, these findings highlight the value of examining suicide-related processes within individuals over short timescales while also considering meaningful differences in the content of suicidal thoughts. Situating these proximal processes within the broader interpersonal, developmental, and contextual experiences in which they occur may provide a more complete understanding of how suicidal thinking develops and changes.
