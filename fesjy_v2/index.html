How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Children and young people (CYP) with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) often face inequalities when accessing learning opportunities. The evidence base is mixed on the effectiveness of school-based interventions for CYP with ADHD on academic progress gains and improved attendance. We conducted a meta-analysis to specify whether school-based interventions improve the educational outcomes of CYP with ADHD. Seven databases were searched until 2nd of October 2026. The review followed the PRISMA guidelines. Of the 2164 items identified, only 16 studies (14 independent trials) were included in our analysis as they met our inclusion criteria: a) CYP with ADHD aged 5-17 years old; b) school-based interventions; c) randomized controlled trials; and d) educational outcomes. Given the substantial heterogeneity across intervention types, the findings show an inconclusive pattern of what works and what doesn't. Our main model demonstrated that overall school-based interventions showed a small effect on educational outcomes amongst CYP with ADHD. No significant moderators, such as intervention types and outcome domains, were detected. Future research should examine the broader contextual, educational, and environmental factors associated with better educational outcomes amongst CYP with ADHD, with the aim of developing more evidence-informed and targeted interventions.

Introduction

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most prevalent neurodivergences globally, with around 8% of children and young people (CYP) diagnosed with ADHD (Ayano et al., 2023). Autism, learning differences, and internalizing conditions often co-occur with ADHD, which may exacerbate educational outcomes such as poor grades and school dropout (French et al., 2024).

Academic achievement/performance and functioning are two different concepts which reflect the outcomes of learning (e.g., grades and test scores), and processes and behaviours that enable that learning (e.g., homework management skills and concentration). A number of studies argue that CYP with ADHD demonstrate sub-optimal performance in reading, writing, math and overall grade point average (GPA) and have higher rates of grade retention and dropout when compared to autistic peers or those without ADHD (Arnold et al., 2020; Henning et al., 2022; Jangmo et al., 2019; Kent et al., 2011; Mayes et al., 2020; Niemi et al., 2022; Schmengler et al., 2023; Sibley et al., 2014).

A recent systematic review mapped the predictors of educational outcomes amongst CYP with ADHD, highlighting that the literature is predominantly focused on within-person factors, namely inattention and executive function differences (Hulsbosch et al., 2025; Osborne et al., 2023; Sedgwick-Müller et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2026), overlooking the contextual factors that play a key role in educational outcomes (Cheng et al., 2022; Cherkasova et al., 2022; Flores et al., 2022) and act as catalysts for neurodivergent individuals' resilience (Black et al., 2024). For example, peer/teacher relationships and the sensory environment are also associated with school refusal in neurodivergent populations (Van Der Werff et al., 2026).

Thus, studies should move beyond the struggles that neurodivergent people face and apply a neuro-affirming approach that encompasses CYP with ADHD's strength and considerations on environmental adaptations (Allen et al., 2026; Gogne, 2025).

Despite the limited evidence base on predictors of educational outcomes amongst CYP with ADHD, interventions are developed to support these young people with learning. Such interventions involve varied programmes and happen across different settings, which can be classified into pharmacological treatments (Jangmo et al., 2019), educational instructions/accommodations (Lovett & Nelson, 2021), psychosocial interventions (e.g., behavioural managements, cognitive/organizational skill training and neurofeedback) (Tresco et al., 2010), physiological treatments (e.g., exercise) (Den Heijer et al., 2017) and multimodal interventions, which combine two or more different types of therapies or strategies, such as medication (Hinshaw et al., 2015).

Although ADHD medication (e.g., methylphenidate) has been found to be effective at improving inattention and executive function and considered as the first line of treatment (Wolraich et al., 2019), a handful of studies showed a small to medium effect on improving reading and math performance (Gosling et al., 2025). In addition, a small number of participants also reported side effects including sleepiness and loss of appetite (Faraone, 2018; Kortekaas-Rijlaarsdam et al., 2019; Peterson et al., 2024). Consequently, psychosocial interventions were recommended for CYP with ADHD first, with medication reserved for cases in which the initial approach yielded limited benefit (Pelham et al., 2016).

Behavioural management (i.e. contingency management) is the most common psychosocial intervention for CYP with ADHD (Danielson et al., 2018; Chacko et al., 2024; Shrestha et al., 2020), although its fidelity, scalability and sustainability has been questioned (Kates & LaFreniere, 2025). For instance, the effectiveness of such interventions showed some promising results for reducing disruptive behaviours (Brown et al., 2005; Zwi et al., 2011), ADHD symptoms (Evans et al., 2014; Fabiano et al., 2009; Marquet-Doléac et al., 2024; Tourjman et al., 2022) and improving academic performance (Arnold et al., 2020; Bikic et al., 2017), but the effect sizes were small (Fabiano et al., 2021) and the long-term effects were limited (Charach et al., 2011; Gosling et al., 2025).

Digital cognitive training is another popular intervention modality. However, recent reviews showed that the studies of digital interventions suffered from commercial and regulatory concerns, although potential benefits on inattention and executive function might exist (Evans et al., 2021; Gabarron et al., 2025; Simons et al., 2016). For example, Cenextra, which targets at working memory and includes gaming elements and adaptive changes in difficulty, has been found to improve academic achievement (Chan et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2022). Generally, computerized cognitive trainings were largely criticized by weak evidence, limited real-world transfer results, short-term effectiveness and over-reliance on screen time, which leads to physical discomfort and emotion reactions (Westwood et al., 2023). Lastly, few studies on multimodal interventions with the combinations of multiple behaviour management strategies and medication showed superior results to either one (Catalá-López et al., 2017; Öğütlü et al., 2024; The MTA Cooperative Group, 1999), presenting inconsistent findings that require further research (Peterson et al., 2024).

To date, the evidence base has primarily focused on proximal ADHD symptoms instead of distal functioning outcomes (e.g., Türk et al., 2023). The ultimate goals of treatment should be functional remissions (Sasser et al., 2017); however, drawing associations between symptoms and functioning does not solve the problem, but rather reinforces false narratives (Weiss et al., 2018; Weiss, 2022). Thus, addressing the needs of CYP with ADHD should go beyond the core symptoms of ADHD and focus on the needs and capabilities of CYP with ADHD.

The current meta-analysis aims to integrate evidence from randomized-controlled trials (RCT) that evaluated the impact of school-based interventions on educational outcomes exclusively in CYP with ADHD and obtain a robust estimate of the effect of different psychosocial and multimodal treatments. Meta-analyses that include RCTs alone have higher reliability and lower risk of selection bias (Fabiano et al., 2015), when compared to the ones that had within group or single subject designs (DuPaul et al., 2012). Randomised, controlled comparisons also support causal inference about average effects, which are not comparable with single-case effect sizes using within-person variances (Saldanha et al., 2022). In addition, previous reviews were often looking at academic skills (Yegencik et al., 2025, although the primary outcomes were ADHD symptoms) or one specific type of intervention (e.g., organizational skills training) (Bikic et al., 2017), overlooked overall academic achievement and other modalities and the comparable effect among them. Thus, this meta-analysis comprehensively includes school-based interventions and investigates potential predictors and moderators of treatment effects including intervention types and follow-up periods etc. By isolating educational outcomes as the primary focus, it clarifies the extent to which non-pharmacological treatments can improve learning outcomes and skills, addressing a major gap in ADHD intervention research.

Methods

The present meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidance. The protocol was registered in the Open Science Framework (Zhang et al., 2025).

Inclusion Criteria

Eligibility criteria for studies included: ADHD CYP or CYP with ADHD traits, aged from 5 to 17 years old (primary or secondary school students), primarily examined the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions to improve educational outcomes, provided effect sizes or sufficient information to calculate effect sizes by hand and were RCTs, primarily based in school (may have a home/parent component), peer reviewed, written in English, and quantitative. Pharmacological or neurofeedback interventions (e.g. electroencephalography) were excluded, given that the main aim of this meta-analysis is to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions beyond person's deficits. Qualitative research, conference papers, commentaries, letters, editorials, theses, and extended abstracts were excluded. No restrictions were applied on the publication dates of the studies.

Search strategy

Studies were searched by titles, abstracts, and/or keywords strategies through seven databases: EBSCOhost, PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of science, ASSIA, and Google scholar on the 9 th of June 2025. The search was repeated on the 2nd of October 2026, with the following search strings: ("attention deficit hyperactivity disorder" OR ADHD) AND (child* OR adolescent* OR youth OR "school-aged") AND ("academic achievement" OR "academic performance" OR "school performance" OR grades OR GPA OR "test scores" OR "standardized tests") AND ("educational intervention" OR "academic intervention" OR "instructional strategy" OR "teaching strategy" OR "school-based intervention" OR "learning support" OR "instructional support") AND ("effectiveness" OR "treatment effect" OR "outcome evaluation" OR "intervention study" OR "evidence-based" OR "RCT" OR "randomized controlled trial"). The term "test scores" and "standardized tests" contain tests on both academic achievement and functioning, such as Woodcock-Johnson III and The Homework Problems Checklist. Studies also tended to mix academic achievement and functioning in their measurements and analyses (e.g. DuPaul et al., 2006).

Screening

DZ independently screened the papers against the above inclusion and exclusion criteria. A randomly assigned 10% of titles/abstracts and 15% full texts were screened by DH to calculate inter-rater reliability with DZ. The Cohen's K was 0.77 (substantial) for titles/abstracts, and 0.72 (substantial) for full texts (McHugh, 2012). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the two researchers and OP and VS made final decisions if needed.

Quality assessment

The revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) was used to assess the quality of each trial and the risk of bias (Sterne et al., 2019). DZ independently assessed the quality of each paper and rated their scores on each dimension (randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported result). DZ then extracted the data including sample sizes, ages, intervention type, academic domains, effect sizes, and standard errors, etc. for data synthesis and further analysis.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (Version: 4.5.1), using package "metafor", "meta" and "clubSandwich". To calculate the effect sizes, sample sizes, means and standard deviations in intervention and control groups were extracted across different measurement points and follow up periods. Hedges' g instead of Cohen's d was computed to quantify the differences between groups to adapt small sample sizes of studies. The educational outcome domains were coded into 2 overall academic achievement/performance and academic functioning categories and 9 sub-categories in the same direction with higher scores indicating better educational outcomes across: reading, mathematics, writing, academic productivity, academic organization/executive function, academic engagement/classroom functioning, school attendance/conduct problems, global academic performance/achievement, and global academic functioning. The intervention types were based on the primary component and were coded as physical exercise, study and organizational skill training, cognitive training, behaviour training and multimodal, which mixes at least two aspects of other four types of interventions (not medications). A random effect three-level meta-analysis was utilized due to heterogeneity and multiple effect sizes extracted from the same study, using Cluster-Robust Variance Estimation (RVE) to account for small samples. The I² was computed using Cheung's approach to denote between and within study variances (Cheung, 2014). Moderator analyses were performed by the intervention types, measurement timepoints, educational outcome domains, informers (student or student-proxy ratings), school level, people involved in the intervention and risk of bias with cluster-robust variance estimator, type 2 (CR2) robust F-test and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) FDR adjustments for multiple testing. The collinearity between moderators was assessed by bias-corrected Cramér's V (Bergsma, 2013), which does not depend on the arbitrary order of the category codes. Moderator pairs with large associations (V > .40) were considered not separable. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted to detect the robustness of the findings using Leave-One-Study-Out (LOO) strategies for outliers and Dominant Study Removal for sample and weight skew. Lastly, small-study effects were assessed by funnel plot and adjusted by PET-PEESE tests due to the inapplicability of Trimand-fill and selection models for small numbers of trials and dependent effects per trial.

Normality of the random effects was checked by applying Shapiro-Wilk tests to the empirical Bayes estimates (BLUPs) at each level.

Results

In total, 14 independent trials from 16 studies were eligible for inclusion in this metaanalysis (Figure 1), with 5 trials with high risk of overall bias, 2 trials with some concerns and 7 trials with low risk of overall bias (see Table S1 for individual ratings of risk of bias). The number of trials included was smaller than the previous meta-analyses on school-based interventions (Moore et al., 2018; Richardson et al., 2015), as they also considered students at preschool and further education, theses/dissertations, and biofeedback trainings.

In this meta-analysis, two hundred and thirty effect sizes were extracted from 14 trials, of which 12 trials were conducted in North America, 1 in Africa, and 1 in Asia. Sixteen hundred CYP with ADHD were recruited, with the sample size ranging from 29 to 326 in each trial. All the participants were diagnosed with ADHD except for 2 trials that had CYP with high ratings of ADHD symptoms (Pfiffner et al., 2016; Pfiffner et al., 2018; Ramer et al., 2020). Nearly 77% of the sample were boys aged around 11.21 years (unweighted). Around 32.07% of the participants also reported ADHD medications (unweighted) and had co-occurring conditions such as Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD), Conduct Disorder (CD), anxiety, depression and learning differences. Seven trials were conducted in primary schools, and the other seven trials were in secondary schools. In terms of people involved in the intervention (excluding researchers, mentors or coaches), five trials had CYP with ADHD only, with another 3 trials had both CYP with ADHD and parents, 2 trials had CYP with ADHD and teachers, and 4 trials had CYP with ADHD, parents and teachers. As for intervention types, two interventions were categorized as physical exercises, two as study and organizational skill trainings, two as cognitive trainings, two as behaviour trainings and six as multimodal. Lastly, 57% of effect sizes used CYP with ADHD-proxy ratings on educational outcomes, with only 43% based on CYP with ADHD's own performance. The educational outcome measurement times were from during treatment, immediately post treatment to a year and the intervention duration and frequency varied from trial to trial (Table 1 & 2 2).

Subgroup analyses

To further explore potential sources of between-trial variability, subgroup analyses examined intervention type, educational outcome domain, informer type, measurement timepoint, school level, people involved in the intervention and risk of bias as moderators (Table 3). However, none of the moderators were significant.

Looking at the subgroup pooled estimates, significant effects were observed for study and organisational skills training (g = .57, 95% CI [0.33, 0.82], p < .001) and multimodal training (g = .22, 95% CI [0.18, 0.25], p < .001). Significant effects were also observed for academic organisation/executive functioning (g = .37, 95% CI [0.24, 0.49], p < .001), academic productivity/homework performance (g = .19, 95% CI [0.10, 0.28], p < .001), global academic functioning (g = .18, 95% CI [0.12, 0.24], p < .001) and overall academic functioning (g = .21, 95% CI [0.08, 0.35], p < .001). In contrast, effects were not statistically significant for the remaining intervention types or outcome domains. For the informer types, the significant effect was only observed in student-proxy ratings (g = .21, 95% CI [0.06, 0.36], p = .006). For measurement timepoint, significant effects were observed during the intervention (g = .26, 95% CI [0.07, 0.46], p = .008), immediately post-intervention (g = .41, 95% CI [0.07, 0.75], p = .017), and at follow-up (g = .24, 95% CI [0.18, 0.31], p < .001).

Lastly, significant effects were found in secondary school (g = .28, 95% CI [0.11, 0.45], p = .002), involving students and parents (g = .26, 95% CI [0.14, 0.37], p < .001), or students, teachers and parents in the interventions (g = .20, 95% CI [0.14, 0.26], p < .001) (Table 4).

Overall, the confidence intervals ranged from narrow (e.g., multimodal training [0.18, 0.25], six trials with consistent effects) to wide (e.g., immediate post-intervention [0.07, 0.75], where trials differed substantially). Narrow intervals from separately fitted subgroups may understate uncertainty. In addition, all these results should be viewed as inconclusive due to low power rather than confirmed effect or absences of effect, given that 80% power at n=14 would require R² ≈ 36%. Moreover, concerns on collinearity between moderators are not neglected, especially between overall academic achievement/functioning and informer types (bias-corrected Cramér's V = .54; simulated p = .013) and intervention types and people involved in the intervention (bias-corrected Cramér's V = .45; simulated p = .057). The independent effect of moderators cannot be assumed.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary estimate.

Restricting the analysis to trials assessed as having a low risk of bias (n = 7) produced a comparable effect estimate (g = .35), although the confidence interval crossed zero (95% CI [-0.25, 0.95], robust p = .22). The corresponding prediction interval was also wide and crossed zero (95% PI [-0.98, 1.68]), indicating substantial residual uncertainty in the expected effect across new trials.

Leave-one-trial-cluster-out analysis

A leave-one-trial-cluster-out analysis was subsequently conducted across 14 iterations. The pooled effect estimates ranged from g = .30 to g = .47. Hattabi (2022) and Dai (2025) were the most influential trials, with their removal resulting in the largest reduction in the pooled estimate (g = .31, 95% CI [0.001, 0.62] and g = .30, 95% CI [0.01, 0.58]). Thus, the overall conclusion of a small positive effect was robust in direction but sensitive in magnitude (Table 5).

A further sensitivity analysis examined the influence of the three largest trials.

Removing Evans (2016) resulted in g = .43 (95% CI [0.04, 0.83], p = .04), DuPaul (2006)/Jitendra (2007) resulted in g = .44 (95% CI [0.05, 0.84], p = .03), and while removal of DuPaul (2021) resulted in g = .43 (95% CI [0.04, 0.83], p = .04). Removing all three trials produced g = .49 (95% CI [0.01, 0.97], p = .047). These estimates were comparable to the primary analysis, suggesting that the pooled effect was not substantially influenced by any of the largest trials. However, the three largest trials produced a smaller and consistent average unweighted effect (mean g ≈ 0.18, with little variation between trials: I² = 44%), whereas the remaining 11 smaller trials averaged unweighted g ≈ 0.41 with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 92%). Small-study effects were assessed with PET-PEESE using an effective-sample-size predictor and CR2 small-sample inference clustered on trial. Normality of random effects

The BLUPs results demonstrated that the effect-size-level random effects showed no departure from normality (W = .993, p = .361), but trial-level random effects did (W = .762, p = .002), likely reflecting a small number of high-effect trials.

Discussion

This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate whether, and which, school-based interventions are most effective for improving academic achievement and functioning in CYP with ADHD.

Overall, the results showed that the school-based interventions had a small effect on CYP with ADHD's educational outcomes. However, there was substantial heterogeneity within and between studies limiting our ability to examine moderators such as intervention types, outcome domains, informant, measurement timepoints, school level, people involved and risk of bias. Subgroup estimates were generally small and positive, and differences between subgroups could not be established. These findings were comparable with previous reviews (Moore et al., 2018; Richardson et al., 2015), which demonstrated small improvements on CYP with ADHD's scholastic behaviours and outcomes using school-based interventions and little evidence for moderation, with daily report cards outperforming study and organisation skills training for teacher-rated academic outcomes (Moore et al., 2018) and social skills training and longer intervention durations associating with smaller effects on teacher-rated school adjustment (Richardson et al., 2015).

Our sub-analyses highlighted that significant effects of school-based interventions were observed in improving academic functioning, with the evidence for academic achievement being inconclusive. The review by Yegencik et al. (2025) partially echoed these findings, where academic skills of CYP with ADHD measured by standardised tests, grades, and non-standardised measures were improved using school-based approaches. From the studies reviewed here, many interventions solely concerned CYP with ADHD and applied a within-person deficit model to modify their behavior and increase their attention. As academic achievement serves as a direct outcome of academic functioning (DuPaul et al., 2011; Jangmo et al., 2019), the promoted academic skills of CYP with ADHD should result in better academic achievement. However, this meta-analysis could not confirm this link, as the evidence for academic achievement was imprecise rather than absent. This raises questions about the current focus on the individuals and prompts broader considerations on classroom accommodations, modifications and contextual support, given the multiple predictors of academic achievement beyond CYP with ADHD (Zhang et al., 2026).

Therefore, increasing CYP with ADHD's academic functioning might not be enough to help them achieve better in academics, unless accompanied by a suitable environment and support means which allow them to thrive. Balancing the intervention focus between CYP with ADHD and the wider contexts such as school environment and family resources could holistically promote overall educational outcomes instead of academic functioning alone (Hulsbosch et al., 2025; Langberg et al., 2013).

Multimodal training programmes showed small but consistent effects, although they did not outperform other intervention types. The systematic support, for example, which combines organisational trainings and contingency management, may be necessary to assist CYP with ADHD to learn (Langberg et al., 2008). Although, this meta-analysis did not consider medications, previous research also noted that integrated interventions with ADHD medical treatment may be superior to other modality or medication alone, achieving higher compliance and family satisfaction (Enns et al., 2017; Lv et al., 2023). While the prevalence of CYP with ADHD receiving multimodal treatments was comparably low (Riedel et al., 2021), direct comparisons of multimodal and single-component programmes in schools are needed.

The meta-analysis had high heterogeneities in the type and delivery of interventions, which is a common issue in ADHD intervention research (DuPaul et al., 2012). Studies also varied greatly from fidelity, intervention duration, control condition and ADHD sample characteristics, including CYP's medication status, which hindered further comparisons of different intervention modalities to understand what interventions work for whom to design interventions (Abikoff, 2009). It is also worth noting that none of the moderators are significant to explain the effectiveness of the interventions, probably due to limited statistical power available with 14 trials. Larger primary research, and future meta-analyses with greater number of studies, are needed before moderators of intervention effectiveness can be ruled in or out. In parallel, research directly examining how school-level factors relate to educational outcomes in CYP with ADHD would help clarify whether contextual effects are large enough to warrant tailoring intervention choice by setting (Zhang et al., 2026). Policymakers and practitioners should be cautious about recommending any specific school-based interventions for CYP with ADHD in a particular context.

Implications

Based on the Practice Model of National Association of School Psychologists (NASP, 2020), school psychologists can reasonably consider these interventions as one component of academic support for CYP with ADHD without choosing one approach over another as no intervention type or school level etc. showed an advantage under small-sample inferences.

Rather, selection should follow the student's needs, context, and feasibility and with the consultations and collaborations of teachers and families.

For policy, administrators and policymakers can treat the results as modest support for continued investment in school-based programs targeting academic functioning of CYP with ADHD (e.g. organisation and homework skills). Fundings should also be placed on intervention studies exploring contextual support for CYP with ADHD.

For further research, researchers can strengthen the evidence base by reporting school characteristics, CYP with ADHD's characteristics (including co-occurring conditions, and cultural backgrounds) and multi-informant educational outcomes. This would allow future moderator analyses, including analyses of whether interventions work equitably across ADHD individuals. In addition, greater emphasis should be given on CYP with ADHD's own perspectives, exploring how they understand and experience academic progress and whether the outcomes assessed align with their priorities, experiences, and needs. This is particularly important to ensure that interventions are evaluated not only in terms of externally observed improvements, but also in terms of outcomes that CYP with ADHD themselves recognise as meaningful.

Limitations

First, we did not test intervention durations, school conditions (mainstream/special), or co-occurring conditions as moderators, due to limited information provided across trials as well as of the small number of trials included in the analysis (n=14). Research has shown that medication history and co-occurring conditions might be important moderators to behavioural treatment response (MTA Cooperative Group, 1999; Owens et al., 2003). Second, studies in other languages were not included, limiting the evidence from the global south. Although ADHD symptomatology, school behaviours, and academic expectations are influenced and differ by culture, translational interventions across countries can also be adapted and used to inform the wider literature (Diener et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2017). Lastly, the small-study effects may exist favouring small trials with large effects, or genuine differences between larger and smaller trials in intervention types and outcome measures. Although the PET-PEESE tests yielded non-significant results, descriptively, the skewed trial-level random effects and the small averaged effects that three largest trials produced may reflect publication or selective-reporting bias. Finally, with 14 trials, the robust moderator test had very few degrees of freedom and should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis is distinctive in examining both academic achievement and academic functioning outcomes of school-based interventions for CYP with ADHD, using a three-level model with small-sample robust inference to handle dependent effect sizes.

The available evidence suggests a small effect of school-based interventions on educational outcomes for CYP with ADHD, but with substantial heterogeneity, sensitivity to influential studies and unresolved publication-bias uncertainty. No moderator reliably explained differences between trials, and the number of trials is too small to say whether any of them matter. The evidence is inconclusive, with some effects observed in organisational and multimodal trainings and in academic functioning. In terms of people involved in the interventions, involving parents and teachers was not associated with larger effects although it may still matter for how interventions are implemented. The support and reward provided by teachers and parents both at school and at home could help CYP with ADHD develop positive study skills (Evans et al., 2016).

As for the informer types, more research is needed to figure out how to measure CYP with ADHD's educational outcomes, potentially by triangulating data on intervention effectiveness and academic progress. Promoting CYP with ADHD's independence and helping them take responsibility of their behaviours might be key to sustaining positive effects (Harrison et al., 2022). Parents and teachers' support might only be complementary to elevate CYP with ADHD's autonomy, resilience and intrinsic motivations and to facilitate educational outcomes beyond interventions (Black et al., 2024; Champ et al., 2023; Martin, 2021; Morsink et al., 2022).
