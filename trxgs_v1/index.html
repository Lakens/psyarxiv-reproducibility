Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Autistic people experience high rates of mental health conditions and barriers to accessing supports.

Digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) may be one way to improve access, but their applicability is unclear. We reviewed DMHIs that focus on mental health outcomes in autistic people, examining their scope, adaptation, community involvement and evidence of effectiveness.

Five databases were searched in 08/2024 and 04/2026. Eligible studies evaluated a DMHI and reported pre-post mental health outcomes in autistic people. Findings were synthesised narratively using SWiM guidelines. Thirty-three studies reported on 1,258 autistic participants across fifteen studies were randomised controlled trials and 17 uncontrolled designs. Although anxiety was the primary focus of DMHIs, effects of DMHIs on depression, specific phobia, obsessive-compulsive disorder and trauma were also explored. Comparative findings for anxiety were mixed, and the predominantly small and uncontrolled evidence base could not establish efficacy. Most interventions were CBT-based, community involvement in study or DMHI design was very limited. DMHIs could be a promising option to support the mental health of autistic people, but research is in preliminary stages. Future studies should focus on co-producing DMHIs and embedding these into adequately powered controlled studies that are inclusive for a broader range of participants.

Lay abstract

Why did we do this study? Autistic people are more likely to experience mental health difficulties, but many find it hard to access support that is affordable, available, and suited to their needs. Digital approaches, such as therapy delivered through video calls, websites, apps, or online programs, could help make support more accessible. However, it is important to know whether these approaches work for autistic people, and for whom.

What did we do?

We looked for all of the research on digital approaches to supporting mental health of Autistic people. We found 33 published studies and examined the types of support offered, whether the support actually improved mental health, who took part in the studies, and how autistic people were involved in the research.

What did we find? We found that most of the digital supports have focussed on anxiety and used cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). The findings were mixed. Some studies found that digital interventions helped reduce anxiety, while others found little or no benefit. There may be some promising results for depression, but there were not enough high-quality studies to draw firm conclusions. Many studies were small or lacked a comparison group, making it difficult to know how much improvement was due to the intervention itself.

We also found important gaps in the research. Few studies included autistic people with intellectual disability, and most were conducted in Western countries. Autistic people were rarely involved in designing the interventions or shaping the research. Many studies also did not explain whether the tools they used to measure mental health were appropriate for autistic people.

What should we do next? Overall, digital mental health supports show promise, but there is not yet enough evidence to know which approaches work best, for whom, and under what circumstances.

Making a program available online does not automatically make it accessible or useful. Future research should work in partnership with autistic people, include a wider range of participants, compare different approaches fairly, and report both benefits and potential harms. This will help autistic people, families, clinicians, and services make better-informed decisions about digital mental health support.

Can Digital Mental Health Support Meet the Needs of Autistic People? A Systematic Review

Autistic people have an elevated likelihood of experiencing mental health disorders. Metaanalytic studies suggest that both current and lifetime rates of anxiety disorder and depression are higher in autistic compared to non-autistic populations (Hollocks et al., 2019; Lai et al., 2019; Polanczyk et al., 2015; Steel et al., 2014), with estimates suggesting a two to four times increased risk across the lifetime (Hudson et al., 2019; Nimmo-Smith et al., 2020). A meta-analysis suggests that anxiety disorders affect 42% of autistic children and 28% of autistic adults, while depressive disorders affect 14% of autistic children and 33% of autistic adults (pooled prevalence; Micai et al., 2023). Despite these elevated prevalence rates, many autistic people report unmet mental health care needs (Ishler et al., 2023; Mazurek et al., 2023; Nicolaidis et al., 2013). Consistent barriers to accessing care are reported across multiple countries, and these include limited clinician knowledge of autism, failure to adapt approaches, not fitting into service structures and inflexible delivery models (Adams & Young, 2021; Brede et al., 2022). These all occur in addition to general barriers to accessing mental health care such as long waiting lists and funding issues (Adams et al., 2025; Adams & Young, 2021). Effective mental health supports have been consistently identified as a research priority by the autistic and autism communities (Roche et al., 2021) <anonymised for review>). Supports that increase the flexibility and accessibility of mental health support may also be particularly valuable for autistic people (Adams et al., 2026).

Digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) are one approach to addressing barriers to accessing mental health supports. DMHIs deliver information, support and/or psychological therapy through technological or digital platforms, with or without therapist support. They include computerassisted therapy, apps, wearable devices, virtual reality therapy and interactive videos or DVDs.

They have been described as having "enormous potential" as a scalable solution for narrowing the service provision gap (Gan et al., 2021, pg. 764079) due to their affordability, efficiency, anonymity and accessibility. DMHIs have been widely researched in non-autistic people; a meta-analysis of 47 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with 4,958 participants showed a large effect size (g = 0.80) for DMHIs for anxiety (Pauley et al., 2023) and a medium effect size (g = 0.52; 83 studies, 15,530 participants) for depression (Moshe et al., 2021). However, given the increasing volume of evidence that the presentation and mechanisms of mental health conditions may differ in autistic people (Hinze et al., 2024; Hwang et al., 2020; South & Rodgers, 2017), it cannot be assumed that approaches that work for non-autistic people will work in autistic people. Indeed, large observational cohort studies from England show that primary care psychological therapy services were significantly less effective for treating depression and anxiety in autistic adults than adults without identified autism (El Baou et al., 2023). Therefore, whilst there is a large body of research evaluating DMHIs in the general population, it is important to evaluate the current research on DMHIs specifically in autistic people.

Establishing whether DMHIs are suitable for autistic people requires consideration beyond their effects on mental health outcomes. DMHIs may overcome some barriers associated with conventional service delivery, but digital delivery alone does not ensure accessibility or suitability for all autistic people. When evaluating evidence for DMHIs for autistic people, it is important to establish who is represented within the evidence base and who has been excluded (see <anonymised for review>), as the heterogeneity of autistic profiles means that DMHIs may not be equally acceptable, feasible or effective in increasing autistic people's access to mental health support.

Additionally, it is important to consider how relevant the DMHI is to autistic people, and whether autistic people were involved in the design of the study or the DMHI itself. Autistic adults and psychologists have identified autistic-informed, person-centred support as a core principle of neurodiversity-affirming psychology practice (Flower et al., 2025). Finally, the emerging evidence that mental health conditions may present differently in autistic people raises questions about whether outcome measures developed for non-autistic populations adequately measure intervention effects in autistic participants (Cassidy et al., 2018), so any evaluation of evidence of DMHIs in autistic people must carefully consider the outcome measures used.

Previous reviews have examined digital interventions, programs and supports focused on social skills, communication, language or other developmental outcomes (e.g. Wang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026). However, to the authors' knowledge, no systematic review has specifically examined DMHIs for autistic people. The present review therefore aimed to characterise the scope, quality and applicability of the existing evidence on DMHIs for autistic people by addressing the following research questions: 1.

What are the characteristics of studies evaluating DMHIs for autistic people, and who has been represented in this research? 2. What DMHIs have been evaluated for autistic people, how have they been delivered and adapted for autistic people? 3. Which mental health conditions and outcomes have DMHIs focused on, what evidence is there regarding their effectiveness and how appropriate are the measures used to assess these outcomes for autistic people? 4. What is the methodological and reporting quality of the existing evidence, and to what extent have autistic people been involved in the design and conduct of this research?

Method

The protocol for this systematic review was registered online with PROSPERO, an international register for systematic reviews with health-related outcomes (Registration number: <anonymised>). Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) standards were followed for all stages of this systematic review.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible if they (a) evaluated a digitally delivered psychological support, program or intervention that is (b) designed to target a mental health condition either directly or through a parent/caregiver, and they reported a (c) pre-post mental health outcome using interview, questionnaire or clinical rating data for (d) autistic people. Studies were excluded if (a) the digital component related only to pharmaceutical prescribing, (b) the study only reported on assessment or treatment adherence; if the DMHI (c) targeted parent/carer rather than autistic mental health or (d) targeted outcomes other than mental health; (e) if the study reported acceptability or experience without pre-post mental health outcomes; or (f) did not report original data. No restrictions were placed on participant age, publication language or date. Grey and non-peer-reviewed literature identified through the search was eligible to reduce potential publication bias (Johnson, 2021).

Search Strategy

The Cochrane Library and PROSPERO database were searched to confirm that no other systematic reviews of the current study topic existed or were registered as being in progress. Five electronic databases (MEDLINE, PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, Proquest) were searched in August 2024 and updated on 27 April 2026 with no date restrictions. The search terms (applied to title, abstract and keyword searches) were related to three topics, which were combined with the Boolean operator AND: autism (autis* OR asperger* OR asd OR pdd OR pervasive developmental disorder), mental health (Wellbeing OR Mental Health OR Mental Disorder* OR Anxi* OR Depressi* OR Dysthymic Disorder OR Mood Disorders OR Panic* OR Phobi* OR Obsessive-compulsive disorder OR Post-Traumatic OR Eating Disorders OR Psychotic Disorders OR Schizophrenia OR Bipolar) AND digital delivery (Digital OR online OR self-paced OR self-help OR self-directed OR selfinstruction OR self-guided OR mHealth OR eHealth OR web-based OR internet-based OR mobile phone OR text message OR SMS OR artificial intelligence OR telehealth OR remote OR App OR Mobile Applications OR Telemedicine OR Web Browser OR Virtual Reality Exposure Therapy OR Video Games OR Phone* OR Computer OR tele-health OR teletherapy OR telemedicine).

Review Strategy

Combined the original and update searches identified 4,307 records, which were imported into Covidence. Following removal of 1,311 duplicates, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers, with 98.3% agreement. Fifty-six reports underwent full-text screening by both reviewers, with 92.9% agreement. Disagreements at both stages were resolved by discussion and consensus until 100% agreement was reached.

Data Extraction

Data extracted included study and sample characteristics, DMHI characteristics, mental health targets and outcomes, measurement time points, comparison or control conditions, effect estimates and associated statistics, engagement metrics, and whether outcomes were designated primary or secondary. Data from 50% of included reports were independently extracted by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion, resulting in 100% agreement. The remaining reports were extracted by one reviewer.

Data Synthesis

Given the heterogeneity in DMHI characteristics, study designs, comparators, outcome measures, and statistical reporting, a meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. Findings were therefore synthesised narratively, following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline (Campbell et al., 2020). Studies were grouped according to the primary mental health condition the DMHI focused on. Within each group, the direction of effect on the prespecified primary mental health outcome was used as the standardised metric, with effect estimates and confidence intervals reported where available. Where studies reported multiple measures of the same mental health outcome, priority was given to the study's prespecified primary outcome. Statistical significance was not used as the primary basis for determining DMHI effectiveness.

Variation in effects was considered descriptively in relation to study design, comparator, outcome measurement, follow-up and methodological quality. Findings were tabulated by mental health target alongside these characteristics. Formal GRADE assessment was not undertaken because the evidence base was dominated by small pilot studies, uncontrolled pre-post designs, heterogeneous interventions and outcome measures, and limited comparable between-group data.

Conclusions were interpreted considering the number and design of contributing studies, methodological and reporting quality, consistency of effects and precision where available.

Methodological and Reporting Quality Assessment

Methodological and reporting quality was assessed using the Quality Assessment with Diverse Studies (QuADS; (Harrison et al., 2021). Given the heterogeneous study designs expected in the search yield, this tool allowed for meaningful comparisons across different study methodologies. The QuADS includes 13 items, each scored on a 4-point (0 to 3) scale, with the highest possible overall score being 39. Two sample items include "Appropriate sampling to address the stated research aim/s" and 'The method of analysis was appropriate to answer the research aim/s". Two authors (XX and XX) collaboratively appraised 20% of the studies. Then, they each independently appraised 60% of the studies to allow for interrater reliability, which allowed for 20% to be double rated and interrater reliability evaluated. Interrater reliability (κ = 0.703, 95% CI [0.57, 0.84]) was adequate and met the a priori (κ ≥ 0.7) threshold for acceptability (McHugh, 2012).

Community Involvement

The authorship team consists of two autistic researchers and one otherwise neurodivergent researcher who have experience in clinical and experimental psychology. Their blend of lived and professional experiences guided the design, conduct, and reporting of this review. Language use was informed by the team's lived experience and the preferences of the autistic and autism community; as such, identity-first language (e.g., "autistic people"; Kenny et al., 2016) is used. The authors are at different stages of their research careers, and all aim to adopt neurodiversity-affirming, participatory research approaches in their work.

DMHI Effects

Anxiety

Nineteen studies evaluated DMHIs primarily targeting anxiety disorders other than specific phobias.

Eleven included a comparator, of which ten were RCTs and one was a non-randomised waitlist pilot.

Eight studies were uncontrolled or did not use their control group in the analysis. Among the 11 studies with a comparator, four reported results favouring the DMHI on the primary anxiety outcome and three reported little or no difference between conditions. Two did not provide sufficient information to determine the direction of effect; one could not complete the planned between-group analysis, and one active-comparator pilot reported pre-post improvement without evidence that either support format was superior. Among studies without a usable comparator, anxiety generally decreased from pre-to post-intervention, although findings were mixed within some studies and across outcome measures. Because these studies did not include between-group comparison, they provide weaker evidence of DMHI effects.

Specific phobia/fear

Four studies evaluated DMHIs targeting specific phobia or fear. One was a randomised controlled feasibility trial, in which 4/16 participants in the intervention group versus 0/16 controls responded at two weeks. Three uncontrolled studies reported pre-post findings that indicated positive response to the DMHI. Whilst these findings are promising, they should be interpreted cautiously as the samples were small and there were limited data from control participants.

Depression

Three studies primarily targeted depression; one RCT and two uncontrolled case series. The RCT reported results favouring the DMHI. A case series showed reliable improvement in depression in one of six participants, with variable changes across participants and measures. The other uncontrolled study reported reduced depressive symptoms on the PHQ-9 from baseline to outcome, but little change on another depression measure and no significant change in weekly PHQ-9 ratings.

Overall, findings provide preliminary support for some approaches, but evidence remains limited and varies by intervention, measure and assessment timing.

OCD

Two studies evaluated DMHIs that included OCD as a primary target. Both used active-comparator designs involving different modes of therapist support. OCD symptoms improved from pre-to postintervention, but there was no clear evidence that one digital delivery format was superior to the other.

Trauma

Two uncontrolled studies evaluated telehealth-delivered trauma-focused interventions. Both reported reductions in trauma-related distress or PTSD symptoms from pre-to post-intervention, with mixed findings for resilience or related outcomes. These preliminary pre-post findings do not provide comparative evidence of efficacy.

Other/non-specific mental health

Studies targeting broader mental health, self-compassion or internalising symptoms reported mixed but generally promising pre-post changes across depression, anxiety, wellbeing, emotion regulation and self-compassion outcomes. Because these studies were largely uncontrolled or used outcomes beyond the prespecified primary target, findings are summarised descriptively rather than as comparative efficacy estimates.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review of studies evaluating DMHIs for autistic people. Findings suggest that the evidence relating to DMHIs is in the early stages; over half of the studies are pilots, feasibility studies, case series or uncontrolled studies. The range of DMHIs explored is also in its infancy; the clear focus of the research on DMHIs for autistic people has been on anxiety (69.7%) and amongst those with control comparisons, the findings have been mixed.

Although there have only been two controlled studies exploring DMHIs for depression in autistic people, both suggested positive effects of the DMHI, indicating potential for this area. However, sample descriptions were often limited, but reported demographics suggest a lack of diversity. All but one of the studies was conducted in a Western context and the mean proportion of White participants was 76%. Finally, the review identified some clear directions for future research, including DMHIs co-designed with autistic input using frameworks outside of CBT and those evaluated using measures designed and validated for autistic people.

Narrow Scope of DMHI Research

Across the studies included in this review, the clinical targets of DMHIs evaluated for autistic people were concentrated within a relatively narrow range of conditions. Anxiety was the most common target, which is understandable given that it is often described as the mental health condition with the highest prevalence among autistic people (Micai et al., 2023). This focus on anxiety, although consistent with the focus of DMHIs in the broader digital mental health literature (Crocamo et al., 2025), has left other mental health conditions relatively unexplored. This review showed one study suggesting preliminary promising evidence for DMHIs targeting depression, and the broader DMHI literature for non-autistic people suggests potential for DMHI for bipolar and psychotic disorders (Crocamo et al., 2025). Given that autistic people may experience higher rates of, and poorer outcomes associated with, these conditions (Underwood et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2021), developing and evaluating autism-focused DMHIs beyond anxiety (that consider clinical safety, crisis pathways and the preferences of autistic people) could be an important research pathway.

Although a large proportion of the DMHIs focused on anxiety, limitations in study quality and certainty of the available evidence mean that the effectiveness of DMHIs for anxiety in autistic people remains unclear. Findings cannot yet establish whether anxiety-focused DMHIs are effective for autistic people as comparative results were mixed, many studies were small pilot or feasibility studies, outcome measures varied substantially, and several studies were uncontrolled. Larger, adequately powered randomised controlled trials with appropriate comparators, autism-informed outcome measures, longer follow-up and transparent reporting of attrition and adverse effects are therefore needed. Therapeutic diversity of the DMHIs was limited, with the vast majority of DMHIs evaluated for autistic people being based on a CBT framework. This reflects the broader DMHI literature, in which CBT and third-wave CBT approaches are prominent (Crocamo et al., 2025). However, a recent meta-analysis of digitally delivered third-wave therapies for depression and anxiety in nonautistic populations reported small-to-moderate reductions in anxiety and depression, although the anxiety findings were heterogeneous (Ip et al., 2026). This suggests that DMHIs may have the potential to deliver a range of therapeutic approaches. Although (adapted) CBT has demonstrated efficacy for some autistic people (Roberts & Rankin, 2025), qualitative research indicates mixed views about its acceptability, fit and accessibility, especially when unadapted (Di Marco et al., 2025; Roisenberg et al., 2026). Future DMHI research should broaden the adaptations made to CBT along with broadening the range of therapeutic approaches available to allow autistic people a choice of approaches and forms of support that align with their needs, availability and preferences.

Autistic Perspectives in Design, Adaptation, and Evaluation of DMHIs

The need for accessible and effective autism-specific mental health supports is clear (Roche et al., 2021); <anonymised for review>). Although DMHIs may address some of the barriers autistic people face in accessing mental health supports, digital delivery alone does not guarantee accessibility or effectiveness. Autism-informed adaptation of mental health supports developed for non-autistic people may improve their effectiveness for autistic people (Linden et al., 2023). Conversely, inappropriate supports can have harmful effects; repeatedly seeking and not receiving appropriate support can cause growing feelings of hopelessness, isolation, and despair in autistic people and their caregivers, which in turn can exacerbate mental health difficulties (including suicidality), cause or contribute to burnout, and lead to disengagement from health-care services (Brede et al., 2022; Wechsler et al., 2025).

Against this background, it is concerning that only about half of the studies in this review (that focuses on papers reporting outcomes of DMHIs) reported tailoring or adapting the DMHI for autistic people. Adaptations that were undertaken were predominantly minor changes to intervention content, language, visual presentation, session structure or delivery. Adaptations were not consistently based on input from autistic people themselves. In fact, only four studies reported community involvement in DMHI design, and only half of these were described as consultation or engagement rather than co-production. Only five studies reported community involvement in the broader study design, such as outcome measures or processes. Co-design and participatory approaches can help ensure that interventions reflect autistic people's priorities, communication styles and lived experiences, and may improve engagement with supports (<anonymised for review>; (Crocker et al., 2018) and the translation of research into practice (Fletcher-Watson et al., 2019). Future research should therefore move beyond adapting existing interventions for autistic people and work alongside autistic people to co-produce the interventions, select meaningful outcomes and interpret the findings. In doing so, the aim should not be to simply make DMHI available but to develop DMHIs that autistic people recognise as safe, accessible, acceptable and useful.

Representativeness of Samples

The included samples were geographically narrow. All but one study was conducted in Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic (WEIRD; (Rad et al., 2018) countries and the participants in the only study conducted in a non-Western country only make up 2.5% of total participants. This dominance of WEIRD samples is similar to other areas of autism (Smart et al., 2026; White et al., 2025), and DMHI research (Croke et al., 2026; Fowler et al., 2023). However, it is entirely unrepresentative of the global population distribution where 88% of the population reside in non-WEIRD countries (Henrich et al., 2010). Many non-WEIRD countries have higher levels of unmet need for mental health services (Patel et al., 2025) and DMHIs could be one potential way to address this challenge (Naslund et al., 2017). Research developing DMHIs for autistic people in underrepresented countries and communities and evaluating their uptake and effectiveness is therefore needed.

Autistic people with co-occurring ID were also substantially underrepresented in the studies reviewed. Most studies excluded or did not report including participants with ID, despite estimates suggesting that a substantial minority of autistic people have a co-occurring ID (Russell et al., 2019; Salkić et al., 2026). This pattern reflects the wider and well-documented exclusion of autistic people with intellectual disability from research (<anonymised for review>; Reuben et al., 2025). It is particularly concerning in the context of DMHIs, given emerging evidence that digitally delivered interventions may be acceptable and feasible for people with intellectual disability (Borsutzky et al., 2025). Future studies should actively include autistic people with intellectual disability and report the adaptations required to support their participation.

Limitations and Future Directions

As with any review, the results must be considered alongside the limitations. The review adhered to PRISMA systematic review processes and SWiM reporting approaches. However, because of limitations in the evidence-base, it was not possible to undertake a meta-analysis with the evidence available at the current time. As research progresses, using a meta-analysis will allow for statistical synthesis of findings as well as explorations of moderators of effectiveness, which can in turn assist with tailoring of approaches into the future. An important addition of a future metaanalysis would be an evaluation of publication bias, as publishing studies with non-significant effects can be more challenging than those reporting significant effects. The review did not place any language restrictions on articles, but all search terms were in English so for articles to be found, they had to include English titles, keywords or abstracts. It may also be that some studies delivered mental health supports in digital formats but only describe this in their method; these would not have been identified using the search terms included in this review.

Conclusion and Future Directions

This review found that research on DMHIs for autistic people is promising but remains at an early stage. The evidence is concentrated on anxiety and CBT-based interventions, but findings from comparative studies are mixed and the predominance of small, pilot and uncontrolled studies prevents firm conclusions about efficacy. The current evidence base also represents only a narrow proportion of autistic people, with limited inclusion of people with intellectual disability and participants from non-Western settings.

Future progress requires more than increasing the number of DMHIs or studies on DMHIs.

Studies should be adequately powered, use outcome measures that are meaningful and appropriate for autistic people, include longer-term follow-up and report harms and attrition transparently.

Autistic people should be involved throughout the research process, including in defining priorities, co-producing interventions and selecting outcomes. Expanding both the conditions targeted and the therapeutic approaches evaluated is also essential. This would ensure that DMHIs are not simply made available, but they are accessible, acceptable and clinically useful supports for autistic people around the world. Note.
