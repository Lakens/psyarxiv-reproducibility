Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Expectancy and interpretation biases are widely considered transdiagnostic markers of psychopathology, yet the nature of their relationship remains understudied. If these processes are causally related, modifying one may influence the other and reduce associated symptoms. Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) is a transdiagnostic trait linked to biased interpretations and their modification. The present study tested a mediation model in which interpretations were hypothesized to mediate the relationship between expectancies and IU. According to this account, expectancy-related ratings were expected to predict interpretation-related ratings, which in turn were expected to predict trait IU. In an online study, N = 263 unselected participants completed a novel task in which each trial paired an expectancy sentence and rating with a subsequent interpretation sentence and rating, jointly assessing both processes. Sentence valence (neutral/emotional) and expectancy-interpretation outcome pairings (e.g., positive-positive, positive-negative) varied randomly across trials. Results showed that more negative expectancy and interpretation ratings were associated with higher levels of trait IU, anxiety, and depression, whereas more positive ratings were associated with lower levels of each. In a cross-sectional mediation model, expectancies showed significant direct paths to both interpretations and IU, but interpretations did not mediate the expectancy-IU relationship. These findings point to a central transdiagnostic role for expectancies relative to interpretations. Interventions may therefore benefit from prioritizing expectancy biases; nonetheless, the observation that expectancy and interpretation biases were associated with symptom measures in opposing and complementary directions indicates that joint assessment of both cognitive processes remains more informative than assessing either alone.

Introduction

Everyday life is inherently filled with uncertainties and ambiguous situations, and individuals tend to differ in how much they can tolerate such ambiguity. This trait refers to individual levels of intolerance of uncertainty (IU; Einstein, 2014). Some individuals often expect negative outcomes of future situations (e.g., "If I go to the party alone, then I will probably look awkward"; Aue, 2021). Such a negative a-priori expectancy bias may then affect the way in which subsequent ambiguous experiences are interpreted (e.g., "The person I met at the party ended our conversation because I looked awkward"). The tendency to interpret ambiguous information consistently in a negative manner is termed "negative interpretation bias" (Hirsch et al., 2016; Woud, 2022). This negative interpretation bias may, in turn, contribute to elevated levels of trait IU.

While expectancies are future-oriented, interpretations typically resolve ambiguity within the present. IU, in contrast, is an individual predisposition which may interact with various cognitive processes across different stages of information processing. The three concepts, expectancy bias, interpretation bias, and trait IU, characterize many mental and emotional disorders, and are thus recognized to be transdiagnostic risk factors (Einstein, 2014; Rief et al., 2015; Würtz et al., 2026). This empirical knowledge, in turn, informs the development of interventions in the context of cognitive behavioral therapy. For instance, a fundamental treatment element across anxiety disorders involves the modification of biased expectancies and interpretations, with the aim of producing downstream reductions in emotional symptoms (Woud & Hofmann, 2023). Behaviorally, it has been suggested that both biased expectancies and interpretations lead to avoidance of potentially threatening situations. This avoidance can create a vicious cycle, in which anxious individuals are unable to make correcting experiences, which, in turn, may reinforce both biases (Aue, 2021; Woud, 2022).

Although expectancy and interpretation biases have often been studied separately, some researchers have advocated studying the interplay between cognitive biases, noting that it is more informative and impactful to study both their unique and shared effects, compared to studying each bias in isolation (e.g., Abado et al., 2020; Hirsch et al., 2006). For instance, it has been found that comorbid expectancy and interpretation biases predict high levels of social anxiety and alcohol use disorder (Chow et al., 2018). At the same time, it is important to examine the unique characteristics and temporal orientation of each bias. By definition, expectancies concern hypothetical future outcomes, including the likelihood that specific valenced events and/or the severity of the consequences of such events (Aue, 2021; Ji et al., 2026; Ji & MacLeod, 2023). In contrast, interpretation concerns attributing meaning to current ambiguous events (Grenier et al., 2005; Hirsch et al., 2016; Woud, 2022).

While previous findings have largely been derived from correlational studies, cognitive bias modification (CBM) paradigms allow for the testing of causal relationships by experimentally manipulating cognitive biases through systematic training (MacLeod & Mathews, 2012).

Although CBM typically targets a single bias, some studies assess transfer across biases.

Expectancy training can generalize to interpretations, with changes in expectancies accompanied by corresponding changes in interpretations (Frommelt et al., 2023; Krans et al., 2019). For example, Krans et al. (2019) found that positive expectancy training, in which future-tense scenarios were consistently resolved positively, led to more positive interpretations on a subsequent task than did negative expectancy training. Thus, biased expectancies may shape subsequent interpretations, potentially creating a compounding cycle of avoidance and negative processing.

As trait IU involves distress in response to the inherent uncertainties and ambiguities of everyday life (Carleton, 2016), previous studies have linked IU with negative interpretation bias. While general interpretation bias paradigms typically target threat-related content (e.g., social or physical danger), IU-focused interpretation bias paradigms target the interpretation of uncertainty itself. Their stimuli are built around uncertainty-laden cues, such as the words "Future," "Uncertainty," and "Doubt." Using such a paradigm, higher IU has been found to be associated with greater negative interpretation bias, and IU-specific interpretation bias was further associated with social anxiety, general anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms (Oglesby et al., 2019). This reinforces the notion that both IU and interpretation bias correlate with symptoms of psychopathology, with potential transdiagnostic roles. Randomized controlled trials show that IU-focused CBM targeting negative interpretation bias can reduce IU (Oglesby et al., 2017) and symptoms of worry and depression (Dugas et al., 2022) and anxiety (Li et al., 2021). Taken together, these findings suggest the possibility of a directional sequence in which expectancy impacts interpretation, and interpretation impacts IU and transdiagnostic symptoms.

Of note, although most studies have focused on negatively biased cognitions, positively biased cognitions have also been investigated and are often considered adaptive (Aue & Okon-Singer, 2020) and potentially protective against depressive symptoms (Everaert et al., 2018). Consistent with this perspective, both positive and negative interpretation biases could be targeted in treatment. For instance, recent CBM studies targeting more positive interpretations found that, in CBM groups, positive interpretations increased and negative interpretations decreased after training (Abado et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2021). Thus, assessing and targeting both valences of these biases may have increased therapeutic value.

To summarize, studies highlight the transdiagnostic roles of expectancy bias, interpretation bias, and trait IU, yet most work has examined these factors separately. Expectancy and interpretation biases appear to complement one another while also operating independently, and although expectancy bias has been linked to interpretation bias (Frommelt et al., 2023; Krans et al., 2019) and negative interpretation bias to IU (Oglesby et al., 2017; Tyson et al., 2025), no study has integrated all three in one design to clarify their unique and joint relationships. It therefore remains unknown whether negative expectancy bias is associated with IU and whether interpretation bias mediates the link between expectancy bias and IU. Clarifying these interrelations will help refine transdiagnostic models of cognitive vulnerability and will inform interventions that target expectancies, interpretations, or their link to reduce IU.

The current study examined the unique and shared contributions of expectancy and interpretation biases to trait IU, systematically manipulating the valence of each. Previous work has often relied on separate measures for each process (e.g., Chan et al., 2020; Krans et al., 2019). We therefore used a novel, pre-validated paradigm that assesses expectations about an upcoming outcome and the interpretation of ambiguity within the same event.

This allows a more direct and internally coherent test of positive and negative biases in both processes. After the task, participants completed self-report measures of IU, anxiety sensitivity, depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms, and general willingness to take risks.

The latter was included as an exploratory measure because risk-taking willingness is associated with optimism (i.e., general positive expectancies; Dohmen et al., 2023).

It was hypothesized that the valence of expectancies directly affects the valence of subsequent interpretations. For instance, a negative expectancy about the outcome of an upcoming event can bias how ambiguous aspects of the situation are interpreted as the event unfolds. It is also hypothesized that both biases contribute to levels of trait IU, directly and indirectly. Specifically, it was hypothesized that expectancies will be directly associated with trait IU and indirectly associated with trait IU via interpretations. These hypotheses were expected to apply to both negative and positive biases in complementary directions: negative biases are expected to be associated with higher IU levels, whereas positive biases were expected to be associated with lower IU levels. As secondary analyses, moderated mediation models tested whether associations among expectancy-related processing, interpretation-related processing, and trait IU differed for positive versus negative outcomes.

Methods Participants

A total of N = 314 participants took part in the study online, via Prolific (https://www.prolific.com/). Sample size was determined using Schoemann's Shiny App for Monte Carlo Power Analysis for Indirect Effects (Schoemann et al., 2017). The analysis was conducted for a one-mediator model with a target power of 0.80. Standardized coefficients for the three paths were set to detect a small-to-medium effect size of 0.20, and the standard deviations of the three variables were set to 1. The results indicated that a minimum of N = 260 participants would be needed to detect the indirect effect (a*b). The study was approved by the local ethics committee (approval number #413). Data were collected between November 2025 and January 2026.

Participants were required to have at least 5 previous submissions on Prolific and an approval rate of 90-100%. Further inclusion criteria were German as a primary language, age between 18 and 40, no history of literacy difficulties, and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. To ensure data quality, participants first had to pass an image-based CAPTCHA (a simple arithmetic problem, e.g., "87 -5 = ?") before accessing the consent form. During the task, comprehension questions followed a random 50% of scenarios, asking participants to identify the scenario's theme from two options (e.g., "What was this scenario about: going to a party or buying groceries?"). Each questionnaire also contained an attention check item (e.g., "Here, please click on 'not very often'"). All participants completed the study within the expected time window (median: 59 minutes for the task, 6 minutes for the questionnaires).

Exclusion criteria were applied sequentially: (1) comprehension accuracy below 80%foot_0 (n = 33); (2) failure on attention checks in two of the three questionnaires (n = 1); and (3) a standardized score of ±3 SD or more on comprehension accuracy (n = 5) or on ratings of neutral sentences, i.e., rating plausible outcomes as unexpected or vice versa (n = 12). The final sample comprised 263 participants.

Measures

Questionnaires

Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (UI-18; Freeston et al., 1994; German translation by Gerlach et al., 2008). The UI-18 measures the degree to which individuals find uncertainty distressing and difficult to tolerate. The German version of the IU scale comprises 18 items, which can be divided into three factors: limited ability to act due to intolerance of uncertainty (IU), burden caused by IU, vigilance when experiencing IU (e.g., 'I should be able to organize everything in advance'). Participants rate each item on a five-point Likert scale (1 = not at all characteristic for me,5 = entirely characteristic for me).

Depression, Anxiety, and Stress scale (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995; German translation by Nilges & Essau, 2021). The DASS-21 is a questionnaire that measures symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress within the last week using a four-point Likert scale (0 = did not apply to me at all,3 = applied to me very much or most of the time, e.g., 'I couldn't seem to experience any positive feeling at all'). The DASS-21 comprises three subscales with seven items each.

Anxiety Sensitivity Index (ASI-3; Taylor et al., 2007; German translation by (Kemper et al., 2009). The ASI-3 measures anxiety sensitivity and includes three subscales: physical concerns, cognitive concerns and social concerns, each including six items. Participants were asked to rate their agreement level with each item using a five-point Likert scale (0 = very little,4 = very much, e.g., 'When I cannot keep my mind on a task, I worry that I might be going crazy').

Risk-Taking Willingness. Participants answered a global question on their willingness to take risks (Dohmen et al., 2011). The question was as follows: "How do you see yourself: are you generally a person who is fully prepared to take risks or do you try to avoid taking risks?" The question was answered using an eleven-point Likert scale (0 = not at all willing to take risks,10 = very willing to take risks).

Experimental Task

The Expectancy-Interpretation Rating Task assesses expectations about a potential future scenario, as well as interpretations of an ambiguous event occurring within that scenario (see Abado et al., 2026, for a similar task). Participants read emotional and neutral scenarios. Emotional scenarios described everyday, potentially anxiety-and worry-evoking situations (e.g., work, study, social performance and perception, health, and finance), whereas neutral scenarios described neutral situations, such as preparing food or doing housework.

Each scenario comprised two sequential sentences: an expectancy sentence and an interpretation sentence, designed to assess expectancies and interpretations, respectively.

Expectancy sentences were future-oriented and followed a fixed structure, while iinterpretation sentences were present-oriented and described an ambiguous event with an attributed cause (see Table 1 for examples). Each sentence initially appeared incomplete, followed by a short pause and then a final word that resolved the uncertainty or ambiguity (the outcome word). After each sentence, participants rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = not at all,6 = very much) the extent to which they expected the outcome word before seeing it.

Each sentence had two versions that differed in outcome (see Table 1 for examples). In an unselected sample, positive outcomes of emotional sentences were assumed to be more expected than negative ones. Accordingly, "congruent" refers to emotional sentences with positive outcomes and neutral sentences with plausible outcomes, whereas "incongruent" refers to emotional sentences with negative outcomes and neutral sentences with implausible outcomes. For neutral sentences, congruency was manipulated according to the objective logical plausibility of the outcome. Each scenario (a pair of expectancy and interpretation sentences) therefore had four versions, randomly assigned to each participant while keeping the number of trials constant across conditions. Scenarios were written so that expectancy and interpretation outcomes were logically independent: even if the expectancy sentence ended negatively, the interpretation sentence could equally plausibly resolve negatively or positively. Thus, although the two sentences shared a theme, the valences of their outcomes were unrelated. Neutral scenarios (29% of trials) served only as manipulation checks and were not of theoretical interest.

All sentences were validated in a separate study (N = 148), in which participants read each sentence with all possible outcomes and rated the outcomes. For emotional sentences, participants rated the positivity and negativity of each ending. For neutral sentences, they rated the expectedness of each ending. Sentences with overlapping ratings (e.g., one outcome rated as equally expected and unexpected) or reversed patterns (e.g., a plausible outcome rated as unexpected) were excluded. For each sentence, participants saw one randomly outcome: congruent (plausible for neutral, positive for emotional) or incongruent (implausible for neutral, negative for emotional).

The task began with 4 practice trials. Each practice trial was followed by a comprehension question, for which feedback was provided. Then, experimental trials appeared in random order. While on screen, each word in a given sentence was given 275 ms on, and thus a typical sentence of 14 words would have appeared on the screen for 3,850 ms. A random 50% of scenarios were followed by a comprehension question, which was answered with 'yes' or 'no' radio buttons on the screen. The sequence of the task can be found in the example trial provided in Figure 1. Design The experimental design was fully within-subject 2 x 2 x 2 design with three variables: Scenario Valence (Emotional, Neutral) x Expectancy Outcome (Congruent, Incongruent) x Interpretation Outcome (Congruent, Incongruent).

Each neutral condition included 10 scenarios (40 neutral scenarios in total), whereas each emotional condition included 25 scenarios (100 emotional scenarios in total), resulting in 140 scenarios overall.

Procedure

First, participants were asked to sign the consent form. Then, they were automatically redirected to the experimental task. The experimental task lasted approximately 50 minutes, including 3 breaks between blocks. After the experimental task, participants were automatically redirected to the questionnaires. Once done, participants were automatically redirected to Prolific with a dedicated submission code.

Data Analysis

All data handling and analysis were performed using R 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2026) in Cursor IDE. Mediation was estimated with the mediation package (Tingley et al., 2014). Linear mixed models (LMM) used lme4 (Bates et al., 2015).

The manipulation check tested whether neutral sentences with plausible outcomes were rated differently from those with implausible outcomes. To this end, an LMM predicted responses from valence (emotional vs. neutral), bias (expectancy vs. interpretation), congruence (congruent vs. incongruent), and their interactions, with random intercepts for each participant. Interaction terms were evaluated via model comparison, and the congruent vs. incongruent contrast for neutral sentences was extracted from the model as the primary outcome, with within-subject effect sizes (Hedges' g with 95% CIs). All remaining main effects and interactions are reported in the Supplementary Materials for completeness.

Mediation Analysis. Mediation analysis tested whether the association between expectancies and IU was mediated by interpretations. Analyses were run only for emotional conditions, separately for each valence (positive and negative). Predictor (expectancies) and mediator (interpretations) were measured at trial level, due to the experiment's design of matched expectancy and interpretation sentences in each trial. The outcome (IU score) was participant level. For path a (expectancies → interpretations), trial-level data was used with a LMM including random intercepts for participants. For paths b and c′ (interpretations → IU and expectancies → IU, controlling for interpretations), participant-level data was used by averaging expectancy and interpretation ratings per participant, and estimated these paths with standard linear regression. Mediation was tested using nonparametric bootstrap (5,000 simulations) to obtain confidence intervals for indirect, direct, and total effects. As secondary analyses, moderated mediation models tested whether the expectancyinterpretation-IU pathways differed across positive versus negative outcomes. These models included expectancy × interpretation interactions in path a and examined indirect effects separately for each valence condition.

Correlations. As a validation measure of the new task, Pearson correlations were analyzed between subjective ratings (positive and negative expectancy and interpretation) and questionnaires (UI-18, ASI-3, DASS-21, risk-taking willingness). Based on previous literature on the differences between positive and negative interpretations in anxiety vs. depression (Everaert et al., 2017 (Everaert et al., , 2018)), further exploratory correlations were conducted with the three DASS-21 factors (anxiety, stress, and depression; please see the Supplementary Materials).

Multiple comparisons were corrected using the FDR correction.

Reliability. Reliability was evaluated for questionnaires and ratings (positive and negative outcomes for expectancy and interpretation emotional sentences; N = 263 participants with complete data on all measures). More details, including results, can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Discussion

The aim of the current study was to examine the relationships among expectancies, interpretations, and trait intolerance of uncertainty (IU). We hypothesized that the valence of expectancies would affect the valence of subsequent interpretations, that both processes would be associated with IU, and that interpretation bias would account for part of the association between expectancy bias and IU in a mediation model. To this aim, an unselected sample of N = 263 participants completed a novel validated task online, in which participants read emotional and neutral scenarios reflecting sequentially linked expectancy and interpretation processes. Each scenario consisted of two thematically and temporally linked sentences -an expectancy sentence followed by an interpretation sentence -whose outcomes were logically unrelated. Emotional or neutral sentences could end with either a congruent outcome (plausible for neutral scenarios, positive for emotional scenarios) or an incongruent outcome (implausible for neutral scenarios, negative for emotional scenarios).

One important finding served as a manipulation check. Specifically, as intended, plausible and implausible neutral outcomes differed significantly, with a very large effect size: participants rated plausible outcomes as highly expected and implausible outcomes as highly unexpected. For emotional sentences, participants rated positive outcomes as more expected than negative ones. In this unselected sample, this pattern is consistent with the positivity bias previously reported in healthy individuals (Sharot, 2011). A different pattern emerged in a sample with high social anxiety using a similar paradigm that assessed only interpretations: ratings differed between outcomes for neutral but not emotional sentences (Abado et al., 2026). The discrepancy likely reflects sample composition: individuals with high social anxiety may show attenuated positivity and a more negative interpretation profile. In addition, the present paradigm required participants to process and rate both expectancies and interpretations within each scenario, which may have encouraged more elaborated scenario processing and, in this unselected sample, more positive interpretation ratings.

Complementary exploratory analyses (to be found in the Supplementary Materials) revealed two notable findings. First, collapsed across valence and congruency conditions, contrasts indicated that expectancies and interpretations are qualitatively distinct, as ratings for expectancy and interpretation sentences differed. The same is also true for contrasts within valences (e.g., emotional expectancy vs. emotional interpretation). This suggests that the two processes may represent distinct cognitive mechanisms rather than simple continuations or resolutions of one another, although a strong association between them exists. Second, clear differences emerged between neutral and emotional sentences. Ratings for neutral sentences followed a relatively uniform pattern, whereas emotional sentences showed greater variability, likely reflecting individual differences. This finding emphasizes the importance of using non-self-referential neutral stimuli as a reliable baseline in emotional tasks, which often instead use emotional sentences with benign endings (e.g., Feng et al., 2020) or self-referential neutral sentences, both of which may still evoke emotion (e.g., Moser et al., 2012).

The primary mediation analysis revealed that a significant path between expectancies and interpretations. This association occurred only within the same valence: positive expectancies predicted positive interpretations, and negative expectancies predicted negative interpretations. In line with our hypothesis, this finding suggests that the level of emotionally valenced expectations (positive vs. negative) is reflected in similarly valenced subsequent interpretations. Second, as hypothesized, a direct link emerged between expectancies and IU for both valences: stronger negative expectancies were associated with higher IU, whereas stronger positive expectancies were associated with lower IU. Finally, contrary to our hypothesis, interpretations did not mediate the relationship between expectancies and IU, and no direct association was found between interpretations and IU.

Thus, expectancies were independently associated with interpretations and IU in positive and negative scenarios.

Interestingly, correlations emerged within biases and between biases and with symptom measures: within biases, the higher the levels of positive expectancies, the higher the levels of positive interpretations and the lower the levels of negative expectancies. Negative expectancies also highly correlated with negative interpretations. Negative interpretations, on the other hand, did not correlate with positive interpretations, suggesting some dissociation between the two valences. Significant correlations also emerged between cognitive biases and symptom measures, as positive and negative expectancies significantly correlated with all questionnaires, in opposing and complementing directions, where more negative expectancies were associated with higher levels of psychopathology, including trait IU, depression, stress and anxiety, and anxiety sensitivity. Additionally, negative expectancies were associated with lower willingness to take risks. While a similar pattern emerged for positive and negative interpretations, correlations between both interpretation valences and willingness to take risks were nonsignificant. Lastly, all questionnaires correlated with each other. Together, these correlations highlight the transdiagnostic nature of expectancies and interpretations, with expectancies showing somewhat stronger transdiagnostic associations. These findings also highlight the transdiagnostic role of IU, as it is associated with general psychopathology symptoms as well as with differentially valenced cognitive processes (Einstein, 2014).

Conceptual and Clinical Implications

The results suggest a dissociation between the two processes: although interpretation and expectancy were strongly correlated within valence, interpretation was no longer associated with IU once expectancy was statistically controlled. Interpretation thus explained no unique variance in IU beyond expectancy, suggesting that IU is more closely related to how expectations are formed than to interpretation bias per se. By measuring both biases within the same task and modeling them jointly, the study shows that expectancy and interpretation, although closely related, differ in their relation to IU, a distinction that would be obscured if each bias were examined in isolation.

The differential associations of IU with expectancies and with interpretations align with prior work distinguishing intolerance of uncertainty from intolerance of ambiguity. Although both constructs are related to worry and anxiety, the former is future-oriented, referring to potential negative future events, whereas the latter is present-oriented, referring to the negative interpretation of current events (for a review, see Grenier et al., 2005). A later review, however, considered this dissociation tentative and called for further evidence linking future-oriented expectancies with IU (Birrell et al., 2011). The current findings provide such evidence, supporting the notion that IU is more closely associated with futureoriented thinking than with present-oriented interpretation of ambiguity.

The current results differ somewhat from previous studies which causally linked negative interpretation bias with IU (Li et al., 2021; Oglesby et al., 2017). Several factors may explain this discrepancy. For instance, Oglesby et al. (2017) selected participants with elevated interpretation bias and used a multi-session training manipulation, whereas the current sample was unselected and cross-sectional. More speculatively, reductions in negative interpretation may have been accompanied by collateral reductions in negative expectancy, which the training paradigm neither targeted nor measured. This is consistent with our findings that, although qualitatively distinct, expectancy and interpretation biases were highly correlated, and that expectancy, not interpretation, showed the more robust unique association with IU. If interpretation training transfers to expectancy, an incidental reduction in expectancy bias could account for the reduction in IU observed in the trial.

Future studies could test this by assessing changes in both biases before and after IUfocused CBM training. The present results thus point to expectancy as a candidate mechanism underlying the effects of interpretation-focused training on IU, and highlight the value of measuring both biases in future intervention studies to determine which change drives the reduction in IU.

The link between the biases and trait IU mirrors findings from a recent previous study, which examined the mediating role of expectancy bias in the link between attention bias and trait anxiety (Ventris et al., 2026). In that study, both attention bias and expectancy biases were correlated with trait anxiety, yet the association between attention bias and trait anxiety was fully mediated by expectancy bias. This parallels the present findings in that expectancy bias retained an independent link to the outcome (IU), while interpretation bias did not. Of note, expectancy bias occupied a different structural role across the two models: a proximal mediator in (Ventris et al., 2026), and a distal predictor in the present study. Together, these findings suggest that expectancy bias may play a key role in downstream pathways of biased information processing, regardless of its position (predictor vs. mediator; see also Basanovic et al., 2024, and Ji & MacLeod, 2023, for evidence of the mediating role of expectancies in the relationship between psychopathology symptoms and subsequent behavioral choices and negative thoughts). The convergence across different biases and outcome measures strengthens the view that expectancies are a robust pathway linking biased information processing to emotional vulnerability.

In line with previous research, the current study showed a link between positive expectancies and willingness to take risks (Dohmen et al., 2023). Notably, expectancies and interpretations showed a dissociation: willingness to take risks was positively associated with positive expectancies and negatively associated with negative expectancies. Despite this complementary pattern of associations, willingness to take risks was not associated with interpretations of ambiguous stimuli, suggesting that it is primarily linked to future-oriented thinking. This pattern is consistent with the unselected sample recruited in this study, in which the average willingness to take risks was near the midpoint of the scale (M ≈ 5/10), indicating no bias toward high or low risk-taking. Importantly, among all questionnaires, willingness to take risks showed the strongest correlation with IU: higher risk-taking willingness was associated with lower IU (see Dekkers et al., 2017, for similar findings). This aligns with the idea that uncertainty is associated with negative expectancies (Grupe & Nitschke, 2011), whereas willingness to take risks is more closely related to optimism (Dohmen et al., 2023). Including positively valenced measures allowed the current study to capture the adaptive, approach-related side of expectancy.

Correlational analyses underscore the value of examining both positive and negative biases separately. Whereas prior work reported a modest correlation between positive and negative interpretations (Huppert et al., 2003), this correlation was nonsignificant in the present study, suggesting that the two biases may be qualitatively distinct rather than opposite ends of a single continuum. A bivalent approach to biased processing may therefore be more ecologically valid and informative (Haj-Ali et al., 2020; Itkes et al., 2017).

Correlations with the three DASS factors replicated earlier findings: anxiety, stress, and depression correlated positively with negative interpretations, whereas only depression correlated negatively with positive interpretations. This pattern aligns with evidence that depression involves both negative interpretations and reduced positive interpretations (Everaert et al., 2017), whereas reduced positive interpretations do not characterize social anxiety (Everaert et al., 2018). Together, these findings highlight how different combinations of biased processing may distinguish disorders and may inform more disorder-specific, individually tailored treatments.

The study is grounded in a transdiagnostic and integrative framework, aiming to examine the relationships between three transdiagnostic markers of psychopathology. Clinically, the results suggest that negative cognitive biases can be considered a transdiagnostic marker of psychopathology, while positive processing can be considered a protective factor (Bean et al., 2023). Targeting expectancies during cognitive behavioral therapy or modification could have positive downstream effects, as more negative expectancies were associated with more negative interpretations and more negative symptoms, while more positive expectancies were associated with more positive interpretations and lower psychopathology symptoms. Ideally, clinical applications would simultaneously target both cognitive biases, as the reduction of one may aid in the reduction of the other, in a selfenforcing cycle. While CBM for interpretation (CBM-I) has been well-established (Salemink et al., 2023; see also Woud et al., 2012) CBM for expectancies remains an understudied area (e.g., Krans et al., 2019). By showing that expectancies are uniquely linked to IU and to symptoms, the present findings provide an empirical rationale for developing and testing expectancy-focused CBM as a complement to CBM-I.

The current study adopted the assumption that, in an unselected sample, participants are likely rate positive outcomes are more expected than negative outcomes. This is line with previous findings, showing that optimistic expectancies are stronger than pessimistic ones (e.g., Sharot, 2011). The current results corroborate this idea, as positive expectancy ratings were significantly higher than negative expectancy ratings. Yet another argument can be made, according to which pessimism can be used in a defensive manner, to prepare for potential future obstacles (Norem, 2001). According to this approach, some degree of context-dependent pessimism could in fact be adaptive, as one considers potential negative outcomes and prepares accordingly. Nonetheless, the overall pattern in this unselected sample indicates a stronger positivity bias in expectancies. This is in line with a recent study, which found that negative expectancy bias directly predicted anxiety reactivity, and predicted anxiety perseveration indirectly, through the perceived negativity of the event (Tough et al., 2025). The authors concluded that negative expectancies should be targeted in intervention, with the aim of improving emotional resilience both in anticipation of and following the anxiety-relevant event. Examining expectancies in an unselected sample thus establishes a normative baseline of optimistic expectancy. Negative expectancy bias can then be understood as a clinically relevant deviation from that baseline.

Limitations and Future Directions

The current study assumed that expectancies temporally precede interpretations, and the task was designed to test this assumption. However, the correlational design does not allow causal or temporal conclusions. Future studies could examine the temporal relationship between the two cognitive processes using EEG, which offers high temporal resolution.

Specifically, expectancy-and interpretation-related event-related potentials (ERPs) could clarify whether expectancy-related processing modulates early stages of interpretation (e.g., semantic prediction-error responses to the outcome word; see Abado et al., 2026, for interpretation-relevant ERPs). Behaviorally, presenting half of the trials in reverse order (interpretation before expectancy) would test the opposite direction: do biased interpretations of a current event shape expectancies about a similar future event? This direction is plausible, as training that induces negative interpretations has been found to increase negative expectancies about similar future events (Hirsch et al., 2007). Similarly, CBM studies could target interpretations and examine whether modified interpretations lead to modified future-oriented expectancies.

Beyond the relationship between the two biases, the current study focused on one directionality within our theoretical model, in which biased expectancies and interpretations were hypothesized to precede and maintain trait IU. This reflects our main hypothesis that both cognitive biases, separately and together, contribute to and maintain IU over time. We assume this relationship is ultimately bidirectional, such that IU, in turn, also helps maintain these biases, but this was not directly tested here. Future studies could test this using longitudinal designs or experimental manipulations, for instance by manipulating expectancies via feedback and examining downstream effects on IU.

A further limitation concerns the characteristics of the sample. The sample was relatively homogeneous in terms of ethnicity, nationality, and age range, limiting the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations. The study also relied on an unselected community sample, limiting the direct applicability of the findings to clinical populations, in which stronger or qualitatively different associations between expectancy and interpretation biases and trait IU may emerge. This sampling approach was deliberate and consistent with the study's transdiagnostic approach, as we aimed to examine these associations across the full continuum of IU rather than in a restricted high-IU sample.

Future studies should replicate these findings in more diverse and clinical samples.

Conclusion

To conclude, positively and negatively valenced expectancies predicted subsequent interpretations of the same valence, reflecting a valence-specific carry-over. Although both biases were associated with trait IU, only expectancies remained associated with IU in the mediation model, and interpretations did not mediate the link between expectancy bias and IU. Thus, once expectancies are accounted for, interpretations explain no unique variance in IU, which suggests that IU is more closely related to future-oriented thinking than to the interpretation of ambiguity. The novel paradigm was validated by showing that both biases related to symptom measures in a valence-consistent pattern: positive biases were associated with lower symptoms, and negative biases with higher symptoms. Together, these findings suggest that expectancies and interpretations are qualitatively distinct processes, each of which can be positively or negatively valenced, and that both may serve as markers of psychopathology when negative and of resilience when positive. These findings are a step toward understanding how prior expectancies relate to subsequent interpretations, and their bidirectional relationship with one another and with psychopathology warrants further investigation.
