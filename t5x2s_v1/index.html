How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Compulsive rituals in OCD present a fundamental puzzle for theories of human learning.

How can an action acquire value as a means of preventing harm even though the individual recognizes that the action has little or no causal influence on the feared outcome? We propose that this apparent contradiction can arise from automatic credit assignment, whereby actions that precede favorable outcomes are reinforced even when those actions are known to be causally irrelevant. When harm is anticipated but no adverse outcome occurs, the absence of harm may reinforce the preceding action, allowing an objectively ineffective behavior to acquire harm prevention value. We formalize this account using an actor-critic reinforcement learning model in which actions are performed under fluctuating states of subjective perceived threat. Simulations show that agents possessing such false credit assignment mechanisms develop persistent ritualistic behavior in an objectively uncontrollable environment. We find that ritualistic behavior is especially likely to persist when feared outcomes are rare and highly aversive, making the ritual appear consistently effective in preventing a catastrophe. We further describe the cost-effectiveness of ritualistic behavior, 1 and provide a computational account of how compulsive rituals can emerge as goal-directed attempts to prevent harm, persist despite explicit causal knowledge, and subsequently acquire anxiety-reducing properties. This work may help explain the persistence of compulsive behavior and inform mechanisms through which therapy can reduce it.

Introduction

Ritualistic behaviors in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) are commonly described as actions performed to prevent feared catastrophic outcomes (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Fineberg et al., 2018). Remarkably, such behaviors persist even though most individuals recognize that these actions have minimal or no causal influence on the occurrence of catastrophic events (Wolf et al., 2023). Given the outstanding human ability to follow the causal structure of the environment (Dorfman et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?rhLaEg 2019) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HZAqnT generate elaborate abstract cognitive maps (Moran et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hRo8FZ 2021) , and correctly foresee future consequences (Schacter et al., 2017) , the very existence of compulsive ritualistic behaviors poses a fundamental challenge to cognitive theories (Risen, 2016). While compulsive rituals are a hallmark of obsessive-compulsive disorder (Abramowitz & Jacobyhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?dRZVZ0 2014) , these behaviors also appear to a lesser degree in the general, non-psychiatric population (Luigjes et al., 2019). Consequently, identifying the computational cognitive processes that facilitate these behaviors is a crucial objective for understanding human behavior. Here, we build on recent findings about automatic aspects of human value learning to develop a mechanistic framework for how compulsive behavior can be developed and maintained in the absence of a causal link between actions and the catastrophe they are aimed to prevent.

Our framework is based on the idea that compulsive rituals are often reinforced by the nonoccurrence of a catastrophe that had been unjustifiably anticipated (Salkovskis, 1998).

The following statement from a patient nicely captures this learning process: "The disaster I attempted to forestall has not come about, which may mean that my neutralization was a reasonable and effective thing to do" (Salkovskis, 1985). We propose that this natural account can arise from the interaction of two mechanisms. First, action values are updated automatically based on action-outcome contiguity, even when this association conflicts with explicit knowledge. Second, the anticipated catastrophic event is rare and therefore usually does not occur following the execution of the ritual.

The suggestion that illusory action-outcome associations can lead to ritualistic behavior was first introduced by animal studies. Classic experiments showed that pigeons developed repetitive and idiosyncratic behaviors such as pecking or jumping when food was delivered at fixed intervals regardless of their actions, leading Skinner (Skinner, 1948) to describe these behaviors as a form of superstition (Reberg et al., 1978; Staddon & Simmelhag, 1971; Timberlake & Lucas, 1985). These findingshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?qlDMeM and their extension to humans (Bloom et al., 2007; Ono, 1987) , suggest that temporal contiguity alone, namely the mere closeness in time between an action and an outcome, may be sufficient to generate false associations and may therefore offer an early model for understanding ritualistic behavior (Beck & Forstmeier, 2007; Vyse, 1997).

However, a critical challenge to the illusory action-outcome associative learning account of compulsive rituals in humans is the fact that, unlike animals, humans often possess explicit knowledge that there is no (or at least very minimal) causal link between the ritualistic action and the negative outcome they are trying to avert (Abramowitz & Jacoby, 2014). Therefore, if such illusory action-outcome associations exist, they should persist in the human mind despite clear contradictory knowledge. Strikingly, recent behavioral evidence has demonstrated that humans automatically assign value to features of their actions despite knowing them to be irrelevant to outcomes (Ben-Artzi, Pereg, et al., 2026; Shahar et al., 2019). Such outcome-irrelevant learning was found to persist despite leading to suboptimal choices, even when individuals possess clear instructed knowledge about the causal structure of the environment and have obtained extensive experience, allowing them to determine that no causal relationship exists (Ben-Artzi, Pereg, et al., 2026). Interestingly, trait compulsivity was found to be linked to a higher propensity for outcome-irrelevant learning, suggesting an increased vulnerability to act based on arbitrary associations that contradict explicit knowledge (Ben-Artzi, Rozenkrantz, et al., 2026; Shahar et al., 2021). Thushttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?sQRCog it seems that while humans possess a remarkable ability to construct complex cognitive models of their environment (Behrens et al., 2018) , they also possess an inherent tendency toward outcome-irrelevant learning, which may play a role in the emergence and maintenance of ritualistic behaviours.

A second central feature of our proposed mechanism is that, because catastrophic events are rare, ritualistic actions will usually be followed by the absence of disaster. In the reinforcement learning literature, the discrepancy between an expected outcome and an experienced outcome is commonly referred to as a prediction error and is considered a primary driver of learning (Bayer & Glimcher, 2005; Schultz et al., 1997). If a ritualistic action marks a precise point in time at which a negative outcome is anticipated, then the nonoccurrence of that outcome may generate a positive surprise (Balsam & Gallistel, 2009; Gallistel, 2025). Thus, the positive prediction error typically experienced after performing the ritualistic action may reinforce it. In this way, ritualistic actions performed under a state of internal threat may be assigned undue credit for the nonoccurrence of disaster, causing them to be experienced as having "neutralized" it. Over time, this repeated reinforcement strengthens the behavior, making the compulsive ritual increasingly resistant to extinction.

Our proposed mechanism is intended to complement existing accounts of compulsive rituals by addressing an important question concerning how a causally irrelevant action comes to acquire value as a harm-preventive response in the first place. Prominent theoretical accounts have emphasized mechanisms such as anxiety reduction and habit formation.

However, as we review below, each captures only part of the phenomenon and leaves some aspects of ritual emergence unexplained. Perhaps the most influential mechanistic account of the persistence of compulsive ritualistic behavior proposes that rituals are negatively reinforced by the reduction in anxiety or distress that follows their performance (Hezel & Simpson, 2019). Experiments demonstrate that engaging in ritualistic behavior reduces physiological markers of anxiety, leading to the suggestion that rituals may function as a form of emotional regulation by increasing a sense of control (Hobson et al., 2018; Karl & Fischer, 2018). However, existing theoretical accounts do not clarify how an explicitly unrelated action becomes acquired as a ritual. (Lang et al., 2020; Sosis & Handwerker, 2011).

Why would performing only that specific action lead to reduced anxiety?

Another widely accepted claim is that compulsive rituals derive from an increased tendency for habit formation (Gillan, 2021; Graybiel & Rauch, 2000; Robbins et al., 2019).

Specifically, this account suggests that compulsions emerge as rigid, stimulus-response associations that become resistant to voluntary control, and that obsessions may arise as retrospective explanations for behaviors already carried out. On this basis, researchers have proposed reconceptualizing obsessive-compulsive disorder as "compulsive-obsessive disorder" (COD), implying that automatic ritualistic behaviors precede and drive obsessive concerns (Gillan & Robbins, 2014; Robbins et al., 2019). However, while this influential model highlights fundamental mechanisms of automatization and behavioral persistence, it struggles to capture the phenomenological reality reported in clinical contexts. Patients frequently describe their compulsions not as mindless habits triggered by environmental cues, but as intentional, goal-directed actions performed with the explicit purpose of preventing harm (Abramowitz & Jacoby, 2015; Rasmussen & Eisen, 1992). For example, compulsive handwashing is seldom experienced merely as a reflexive response to the sight of soap, but rather as a deliberate attempt to avert contamination or illness. Thus, although the habit framework provides a powerful account of ritual maintenance, it risks overlooking the subjective dimension of OCD, where compulsive acts are consciously undertaken to ward off perceived threats (Kobayashi, 2026). A more comprehensive understanding of OCD could therefore integrate both the automaticity emphasized by habit models and the lived experience of compulsions as purposeful harm-prevention (Abramowitz et al., 2009; Robbins et al., 2019).

We thereby offer a complementary account of the processes that may contribute to the onset and maintenance of human compulsive rituals. To illustrate our proposed mechanism, we use an artificial reinforcement learning agent that models compulsive rituals in human behavior. Throughout the paper, we specify the mental and environmental conditions under which individuals may come to develop rituals that are causally unrelated to harm prevention.

We then illustrate how these can be mechanistically connected with stress reduction. We propose that the onset and maintenance of ritualistic behavior can arise from the combination of specific environmental conditions, such as rare negative events and low-cost actions, with automatic action-outcome value learning. This combination can lead an agent to develop ritualistic behavior that is reinforced as if it prevents a perceived catastrophe, despite the absence of any causal relationship in the external environment.

Model overview

To explore and describe how the two components above could lead to the formation and maintenance of compulsive rituals, we implement them in reinforcement learning agent model simulations. We manipulate external (environmental) and internal (within-agent) factors, allowing us to explore the conditions under which actions might be falsely learned as causally linked to harm prevention. We start by providing a general outlook of the mechanistic components that are included in the simulations and follow with a mathematical description of the model.

Dependent measure for ritual-like behavior

One question that arises is how to define compulsive behavior in an artificial agent.

Here, we follow the DSM-5 definition and assess compulsive ritual-like behavior as the repeated selection of an action that the agent associates with harm prevention, even though no such association exists in the simulated environment.

The simulated environment

In computational modeling, the 'environment' refers to the external world and the rules that govern it. Although these rules are not fully observable in real life, in our simulation, they can be artificially manipulated to reveal the conditions under which compulsive rituals may emerge. In line with our theoretical conditions, we start by simulating a reinforcement learning agent operating in an environment where catastrophes rarely occur.

Critically, the occurrence of a catastrophic event is unrelated by design to the agent's actions.

The agent's internal dynamics

The "agent" reflects the internal psychological processes governing behavior. While the agent is not aware of the environment's rules, it tries to adapt its behavior based on perceived events. We simulate "psychological triggers" that induce in the agent the false belief that a catastrophe is highly likely to occur. Based on its current belief about the likelihood of a catastrophe, the agent transitions between two internal mental states, each associated with a distinct action policy. When the agent believes that a catastrophe is highly likely to occur, it enters the "Anxious" mental state, whereas when it believes that a catastrophe is unlikely, it enters the "Calm" mental state.

When acting in the "Anxious" state, where a catastrophe is anticipated, the agent may misattribute the absence of catastrophe to their own behavior, thereby reinforcing arbitrary actions. This mechanism depends on the degree to which the agent tends to automatically assign credit to its actions, making agents with a stronger tendency toward such learning especially prone to developing ritual-like behaviors. In the model, anxiety naturally decreases over time, consistent with the idea of the "anxiety wave" (Pincus et al., 2008). Notably, this natural decrease is enhanced when the agent behaves deterministically, illustrating the idea that a compulsion may provide a go-to response that enhances relief (Bouvard et al., 2020; Bucarelli & Purdon, 2015).

Mathematical description of the computational model

Reward function. In the simulated environments, regardless of the actions performed, the agent experiences a catastrophic negative reward (here, v harm =-100) with probability freq(c), and a neutral zero reward otherwise. We start by describing an environment in which catastrophic events never occur [i.e., freq(c) = 0].

Threat belief dynamics.

We modeled the agent's internal threat belief at time t as p(harm) t , representing the subjective belief that the catastrophe will occur, regardless of its true probability in the environment (i.e., freq(c)). To generate this belief, we used a cyclic triggering event that recurred every 100 time steps. We initialized the harm estimate (i.e., h) to 0 and updated it at each time step based on whether a triggering event occurred. The harm estimate decayed on each time step toward 0 according to a relaxation rate bounded between 0 and 1, reflecting the tendency for stress to subside naturally over time. See Eq. 10 below for a description of how the relaxation rate changed in each timestep t.

We then defined p(harm), the agent's perceived probability that a catastrophe would occur, as a scaled inverse logit transformation of h. This transformation mapped the accumulated harm signal onto a probability while accounting for the strictly positive nature of h.

At each time step, t, the agent's subjective value of the environment, V t (i.e., critic value), is derived from the current estimated probability of catastrophe, p(harm) t , and the internal expected valence of that catastrophe, v(harm). Specifically, the expected value in a given time step, t, is the multiplication of the belief regarding the probability of harm with the belief regarding its negative valence. The critic's environment value update is thus indirectly driven by the spontaneous relaxation rate parameter (see Eq. 2).

We further assumed that the agent could occupy one of two internal mental states, anxious or calm, corresponding respectively to high and low values of p(harm). Specifically, the agent was defined as being in an anxious state when p(harm) at a given time step exceeded a predefined threshold, and in a calm state when it fell below that threshold (see Eq.5) (Engelen et al., 2023; Feldman et al., 2024; Paulus & Stein, 2006 , 2010). As described below, action selection is conditioned on these two mental states (see Eq. 7).

Action selection and cumulative action cost.

We implemented an advantage actor-critic model to simulate an agent that selects actions depending on its current internal state. Actor-critic are a class of models that receives increasing empirical support due to its ability to explain a wide range of behaviors from bandit task choices to emotional response and group behavior (Babitz & Eldar, 2025; Biderman et al., 2023; Emanuel & Eldar, 2023).

Actor-critic models are composed of two complementary components that support learning: the critic evaluates the value of the agent's current environment (see Eq. 4), whereas the actor selects actions on the basis of learned action tendencies (Bennett et al., 2022; Sutton & Barto, 1998). Simulation 1: Mechanisms for acquiring ritualistic behavior

We begin by illustrating the emergence of ritualistic tendencies in an artificial agent that acts in an environment where actions are objectively unrelated to outcomes. We measure whether the agent will demonstrate consistent "ritualistic-like" behavior when it falsely predicts harm (i.e., p(harm) Eq. 3), observes no adverse events (i.e., reward = 0, Eq. 1), and holds strong automatic credit assignment tendencies (i.e., η, Eq. 9). To illustrate this mechanism, we simulated two types of agents: one with a low propensity for automatic credit assignment, rarely attributing neutral outcomes to its own actions, and another with a high propensity for automatic credit assignment, erroneously attributing neutral outcomes to its actions. We measure the tendency of each agent to repeat, in a "ritualistic-like manner", a specific action, as if it can influence the environment.

Methods

We simulated 500 timesteps for each agent. The environment reward was set to 0 at all timesteps, representing an objectively safe environment. We set mental triggering events that occurred at the first time step and then every 100 time steps, each with a fixed and arbitrary intensity (trigger intensity = 5). The relaxation rate was set to 0.95, allowing a mild relaxation each time step. A threshold for transitioning from a calm to an anxious state was set to 0.05. For the low automatic credit assignment agent group (N=100), η was set to 0.01, while for the high automatic credit assignment agent group (N=100), η was set to 0.1.

Defining the dependent measure of "ritualistic-like behavior"

At each time step, agents select one action out of ten alternatives, based on latent action propensities (Eq. 9). In the simulation, all aspects of the environment were independent of the selected action, and the agent had no influence on the reward. Therefore, any tendency to repeat a specific action in a certain state is driven solely by automatic credit assignment and could be defined as "ritualistic-like". To obtain an empirical measure of such ritualistic-like behaviors of the agent, we defined "ritualistic-like behavior" as excessive repetition of one particular action. Specifically, we identified for each agent and state (calm vs. anxious) the most frequently chosen action and calculated the percentage of time steps in which this action was selected. The frequency of the most selected action in a specific state was considered a dependent measure of ritualistic-like behavior.

Discussion

Simulation 1 demonstrates how a reinforcement learning agent might acquire ritualistic-like behavior. Specifically, we estimated the frequency of the RL agent repeating a particular action when harm was highly expected. In our model, this behavior emerges through three interacting mechanisms. First, action values (i.e., propensities) are updated according to an advantage estimate, A t (Eq. 8), defined as the difference between the observed reward and the current state value. Specifically, when the agent's internal state value is negative (reflecting the belief that harm is highly probable), the estimated state value becomes negative. Because all outcomes in this simulation are neutral (no harm is ever observed), the agent experiences a positive advantage, A t . For the agent with high automatic credit assignment, this positive advantage increases the value of the recently selected action, as though that action had caused safety (Eq. 9). In this way, the absence of harm functions as positive reinforcement under elevated threat, consistent with cognitive accounts of compulsive behavior (Salkovskis, 1985). Thus, the simulation shows how, in an anxious state, an agent can quickly learn to repeat the action that happened to precede a neutral outcome, despite no causal control. Second, low values of η, reflecting the agent's ability to refrain from assigning undue credit, protect against the acquisition of these nonveridical action values. This finding highlights how automatic credit assignment can lead to the emergence of ritualistic-like behavior in a noncontrollable environment. Third, the model links confidence in action selection to an increased rate of relaxation. As a result, once a ritualized response is acquired, performing it accelerates the reduction of perceived threat.

Simulation 2: Factors supporting the maintenance of ritualistic behavior

Simulation 1 demonstrated that an agent can acquire ritualistic-like behavior following a gap between a negative expectation and a neutral outcome. A significant question that follows is to what extent the features of the feared event can influence the acquisition of ritualistic behaviors. In the clinical realm, compulsive ritualistic human behavior is usually characterized as an action driven by an intense fear of a catastrophic event (i.e., death, serious illness, etc.; Abramowitz & Jacoby, 2014). However, catastrophic events are typically very rare. We propose that this rarity supports the maintenance of ritualistic behavior. If the feared catastrophe occurred frequently, then, according to our theoretical framework, the ritualistic action would be expected to extinguish, because negative value would be assigned to the action that preceded the catastrophe. In the current experiment, we therefore examine whether the reinforcement learning mechanism described in Simulation 1 promotes a higher incidence of ritual-like behavior, particularly when the feared event is rare in the external environment, and its internal valence is catastrophic (i.e., very negative valence). To this end, we used the same actor-critic model as before (Eqs. 1 to 10) and generated three groups of agents with 100 agents in each group (total N=300). The first group was simulated with parameters similar to those of the high automatic credit assignment agent in Simulation 1, with a minimal probability for the occurrence of a catastrophe (see Methods). This allowed us to re-examine the frequency of ritualistic-like behaviors across multiple agents, while also serving as a baseline comparison (i.e., baseline group). The second group of agents was a high catastrophe frequency group, where the frequency with which the harmful event occurred in the environment (i.e., freq(c)) was higher compared with the baseline group. The third group of agents was a low catastrophe valence group, where we reduced the extent to which the predicted negative outcome was experienced as catastrophic (i.e., v harm ).

Methods

The baseline group was assigned parameters reflecting high automatic credit assignment, low frequency of the negative event and a high negative valence for that event (500 timesteps with a trigger every 100 time steps and a trigger intensity of 5 points; relax rate of 0.95, threshold calm to anxious state transition = 0.05; automatic credit assignment of η=0.1; frequency of catastrophic outcome of freq(c) ~ Beta(1,1000); valence of catastrophic event of v harm ~ N(-100, 5); see Eq 1 to 10). The high catastrophe frequency group had the same parameterization, only that the frequency of catastrophic events was increased across the whole group (freq(c) ~ Beta(1,1)). The low Catastrophe Valence group had the same parameterization as the baseline group, only that the catastrophic event appeared to be less negative (v harm ~ N(-50, 30); see Table 1). We estimated for each agent the average repetitive behavior rate in each state, the percentage with which the most common action was selected (see Methods of Simulation 1 for details). Averaging these proportions across agents within each group provided an estimate of ritualistic-like behavior following the two manipulations.

Results

Ritualistic-like behavior was estimated as the average repetitive behavior rate for each group of agents in each state (calm vs. anxious; see Methods of Simulation 1 for details).

Replicating the pattern observed in Simulation 1, the baseline group showed a high ritual frequency in the anxious state (77%, HDI 95% between 74% and 80%), but not in the calm state (13%, HDI 95% between 8% and 17%). In contrast, both the high catastrophe frequency group and the low catastrophe valence group showed a reduction in ritualistic-like behavior in the anxious state. Specifically, ritual frequency was reduced to 42% in the high catastrophe frequency group (HDI 95% between 37% and 46%; see Figure 2A) and to 53% in the low catastrophe valence group (HDI 95% between 49% and 57%; see Figure 2A). Similar to the baseline group, both manipulation groups showed no ritualistic-like behavior in the calm state (high catastrophe frequency -13%, HDI 95% between 6% and 19%; low catastrophe valence -13%, HDI 95% between 7% and 19%).

Next, we examined the continuous effects of catastrophe frequency (i.e., freq(c)) within the high catastrophe frequency group and the continuous effects of catastrophe valence (i.e., v harm ) within the low catastrophe valence group.

Both variables showed a linear relationship with ritual frequency in the anxious state. Specifically, a 10% increase in catastrophe frequency was associated with an 8.4% decrease in ritual frequency (HDI 95% between 7.4% and 9.4%; see Figure 2B), and a 10-unit shift towards a less negative valence of the catastrophic outcome was associated with a 6.5% decrease in ritual frequency (HDI 95% between 5.8% and 7.2%; see Figure 2C).

Discussion

In Simulation 2, we described two manipulations that can alter the agent's tendency toward repetitive behavior when in the anxious state. First, we increased the frequency of catastrophic outcomes in the environment. In our model, repetitive behavior is sustained because a neutral and safe outcome tends to follow the execution of the repeated action.

Increasing the frequency of catastrophes disrupts this mechanism and prevents the maintenance of the ritual, because the repeated action is then more often followed by the very outcome it is meant to prevent. This highlights a central implication of the model, namely that an objectively safe environment is an important condition for the emergence of rituals.

Second, reducing the subjective valence assigned to the harmful event weakened the development of ritualistic behavior. When the feared negative event is represented as less severe, the omission of a negative outcome following action execution provides weaker reinforcement. This suggests that when an actor is able to appraise the anticipated harm as relatively small, repetitive ritual-like behavior tendencies may diminish.

Simulation 3: Divergent acquisition of rituals across actions

In previous experiments, we proposed a computational mechanism for the emergence and maintenance of ritualistic-like behavior, whereby agents with elevated anticipated harm falsely attributed the non-occurrence of feared outcomes to their own actions. These simulations showed that such behavior is strengthened when the environment is objectively safe, and the anticipated negative outcome is subjectively severe. Yet, a central assumption in these experiments was that all actions were identical in their propensity to be acquired as rituals. However, from a clinical perspective, it is clear that some actions are more prone to becoming ritualistic (Pinciotti et al., 2023). In Simulation 3, we therefore examine the factors that make certain actions more likely to become ritualized.

Compulsive rituals often build on relatively simple actions, such as washing, checking, ordering, repeating, or counting, whose clinical cost only emerges as they become excessive, repetitive, and time-consuming (Pinciotti et al., 2023). Although clinically significant compulsions can become highly burdensome, this burden may arise through repetition and escalation rather than from the intrinsic cost of the action itself (Bouvard et al., 2020; Bucarelli & Purdon, 2015; Wairauch et al., 2024). For example, hand washing is initially a simple and low-cost action, but when repeated excessively, it can consume substantial time, impair functioning, or cause physical harm. We therefore examine how the cost associated with different actions influences which action is more likely to become ritualized.

A second factor that may distinguish actions that become ritualistic from those that do not is the level of associability of each action. Clinically, compulsions are often contextually linked to the feared outcome. For example, contamination-related obsessions are commonly accompanied by washing rituals, because washing is perceived as capable of reducing contamination-related harm. More generally, when a feared catastrophe does not occur, the resulting relief may be more readily attributed to actions that are contextually associated with the negative outcome. To capture these aspects, we manipulate the associability parameter, β a , which reflects the degree to which a given action can be associated with harm prevention.

Methods

We simulated a group of 200 agents while varying the repetition cost parameter of each action (pr a ) across agents. Specifically, the repetition cost for each action was sampled from a wide distribution, pr a ~|N(0.5,0.5)|, and all actions had a fixed cost decay, f p =0.01. All other parameters were identical to those of the high automatic credit assignment agent in Simulation 1. We estimated the association between the action's cost and its probability of becoming ritualistic.

In a second sample, we simulated a group of 200 agents and varied the associability parameter, which captures, for each action, the degree to which the agent would potentially associate it with harm prevention. Specifically, the associability parameters were sampled from a uniform distribution between 0 and 1 (i.e.,β a ~Beta(1,1)).

Not all actions are equally likely to be acquired as rituals. For example, hand washing is a more common ritual compared to jumping. To illustrate that, in Simulation 3, we separated the ten actions based on two action-specific parameters: repetition cost (pr a ) and associability (β a ). The repetition cost parameter (pr a ) reflects the cost accumulated for each time a given action is done. For example, the cost of a single instance of hand washing is expected to be lower than the cost of a single instance of jumping. Second, we introduced an associability parameter that captures for each action how likely it is to be assigned credit for harm prevention. Discussion

In this experiment, we demonstrate how different action properties can affect ritualistic-like behavior. First, we introduce the notion of a repetition cost that accumulates as the agent excessively repeats an action, highlighting that ritualistic behavior often involves relatively low-cost actions that become debilitating only following their excessive repetition.

Second, we differentiate between actions in terms of how likely they are to be assigned credit for the nonoccurrence of an anticipated catastrophe. For example, a "washing" ritual compared to a "jumping" ritual is more likely to develop in reaction to a contamination fear, as washing is both comparatively less costly and more typically associated with preventing contamination.

General discussion

This work provides a theoretical computational account of how compulsive ritualistic behavior may be acquired and maintained despite having no causal influence on a feared outcome. For this purpose, we modeled an actor-critic reinforcement learning agent operating in an environment in which catastrophic outcomes were rare and causally unrelated to the agent's actions. Yet, recurring "triggers" wrongly increased the agent's subjective belief that a catastrophe would occur, as is commonly seen in OCD patients (Hunt et al., 2022; Moritz & Jelinek, 2009; Moritz & Pohl, 2009; Zetsche et al., 2015). The central mechanism of the model is that, when harm is expected, the nonoccurrence of the anticipated catastrophe can generate a positive advantage signal (Bennett et al., 2022; Emanuel & Eldar, 2023). If the agent then falsely attributes the relief from nonoccurrence to the action that preceded the neutral outcome, that action may be reinforced as though it had prevented the negative event, despite having no actual influence on the environment.

Simulation 1 demonstrated this basic mechanism by comparing two agents that differed solely in their automatic credit assignment parameter -that is, the degree to which they treated outcomes as contingent on their own actions and updated action propensities accordingly (Emanuel & Eldar, 2023; Ligneul, 2021; Ligneul et al., 2022). Whereas the low automatic credit assignment agent did not develop ritualistic behavior and was equally likely to choose any possible action, the high automatic credit assignment agent repeatedly selected one arbitrary action. To capture the clinical phenomenology where compulsions are mostly performed in response to a subjective sense of threat (Ecker & Gönnerhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?JNuKkr 2008; Kobayashi, 2026; Sookman & Pinard, 2002) , we modeled two internal states that reflected the agent's current subjective belief that a catastrophe may occur and were thus termed a "calm" and an "anxious" state.

Ritualistic-like behavior emerged solely in the anxious state, when harm was anticipated, but not in the calm state, where it was not expected. This illustrates the main mechanism suggested by the model, showing how the absence of an anticipated threat can reinforce causally unrelated actions.

Having established in Simulation 1 how a ritualistic-like behavior can emerge in our model, Simulation 2 addressed the question of what factors support its maintenance.

Interestingly, ritualistic-like behavior was more likely to be maintained when the feared event was objectively very rare and subjectively highly aversive. Specifically, increasing the frequency of the negative event or reducing its subjective negative valence decreased ritualistic-like behavior. A main driving force of compulsive behavior, according to our model, is that from the agent's perspective, the ritual always works. As the feared catastrophe becomes more common, the ritual is increasingly followed by the very outcome the agent seeks to prevent, causing its acquired value to extinguish over time. Thus, somewhat counterintuitively, a largely safe environment, coupled with the expectation of a highly aversive outcome, may provide particularly favorable conditions for the maintenance of ritualistic behavior (Gillan & Robbins, 2014).

Notably, not all actions are equally susceptible to becoming ritualistic (Eilam et al., 2006). Compulsions tend to cluster around particular classes of behavior, such as washing, checking, ordering, counting, or repeating (Bloch et al., 2008). One notable commonality is that many such actions are relatively easy to perform repeatedly. This suggests that the emergence of compulsive behavior adheres to a cost-benefit tradeoff, whereby the potential for preventing harm is weighed against the cost incurred by performing the action (Abbott & Sherratt, 2011).

Another notable aspect is that compulsions tend to be conceptually related to the harm they are meant to prevent (Rachmanhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1ah7ZJ 2002) , for instance, washing in response to contamination threats.

To incorporate this phenomenology into our model, in Simulation 3 we demonstrated that actions with lower repetition costs were more likely to be acquired as rituals, as were actions with higher associability (i.e., actions to which the agent more readily assigned credit for harm prevention) (Emanuel & Eldar, 2023). This suggests that the content of a ritual may not be entirely arbitrary, such that even in the absence of a genuine causal relationship, some actions may be more likely than others to acquire harm-prevention value.

The present framework is intended to provide a computational formalization that could complement existing theories of compulsive rituals. A highly influential theory of compulsivity is based on the habit theory that emphasizes the rigidity and persistence of compulsive behavior (Gillan, 2021; Gillan & Robbins, 2014; Robbins et al., 2019). While we do not question the habitual nature of compulsive behaviorhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?JsTg4m we suggest that while these habitual features are highly typical of compulsivity by the time patients seek treatment (Belloch et al., 2009) , such accounts may overlook the initial phase in which compulsions emerge, when their role as goal-directed attempts to prevent harm may be more central. Another proposed mechanism underlying compulsive behavior is that performing the ritual reduces anxiety, although the processes responsible for this enhanced relief remain unclear (Swisher & Newman, 2026). Here we tried to address this by providing the agent with a relaxation mechanism whereby it naturally relaxes following triggers. Importantly, we modulated the natural relaxation rate by the confidence with which the current action was performed. As a result, when an action policy is highly deterministic, the agent relaxes faster. The ritualistic action then becomes a go-to safety action leading to a faster decrease in the subjective belief that harm would occur (Rachman, 2002).

Finally, superstitious-learning accounts highlight the role of accidental action-outcome contiguity, but struggle to explain why such associations persist despite explicit knowledge that the action is causally ineffective (Abbott & Sherratt, 2011; Aeschleman et al., 2003; Skinner, 1948). The idea behind our model is supported by empirical findings showing that humans automatically assign credit to features of their behavior that they know are causally unrelated to outcomes, and that this basic cognitive mechanism is stronger in individuals reporting higher compulsivity (Ben-Artzi, Pereg, et al., 2026; Ben-Artzi, Rozenkrantz, et al., 2026; Shahar, Hauser, Moran, Moutoussis, Dolan, et al., 2021). Thus, rather than replacing existing accounts, the current model specifies a computational pathway through which these processes may interact.

Clinical implications

The model highlights several potential implications for better understanding the mechanism behind exposure and response prevention (ERP), a first-line psychological treatment for OCD (Song et al., 2022). During ERP, patients are exposed to situations that evoke obsessional threat while being encouraged to refrain from performing their ritual. From the perspective of our model, exposure can be seen as increasing the frequency of triggers, thereby repeatedly bringing the patient back into the anxious internal state in which the ritualistic action was reinforced. Response prevention may then allow the patient to experience the nonoccurrence of harm in the absence of the ritual, thereby providing evidence that safety is not contingent on ritual performance (Jacoby & Abramowitz, 2016). The model also offers a perspective on why therapeutic gains often do not last. Preventing a ritual from being performed does not necessarily imply that its previously acquired value has been overridden (Bouton, 2002 (Bouton, , 2004)). Inhibitory learning accounts of exposure propose that exposure establishes new learning rather than eliminating the original threat association, leaving previously learned responses susceptible to later recovery under certain conditions (Craske et al., 2008 (Craske et al., , 2014)). Thus, following the termination of ERP treatment, when the therapist no longer imposes an "external cost" on performing the ritual, the previously learned ritual response may compete with newly acquired learning, particularly when threat is encountered again outside the treatment context.

An important implication of such response prevention treatment concerns the possibility of ritual substitution. Because the mechanism proposed here can, in principle, assign harm-prevention value to alternative actions performed under threat, preventing one specific ritual may leave open the possibility that another overt or covert response would be reinforced instead. If the anticipated threat remains high and alternative rituals are not restricted by the therapist, the same learning mechanism may allow these alternative actions to acquire harm-prevention value and become ritualized. This possibility is consistent with clinical accounts emphasizing that avoidance, reassurance, and mental compulsions may interfere with ERP, and in some cases, replace the overt ritual prevented during treatment (Gillihan et al., 2012). From this perspective, effective response prevention may require addressing not only the primary ritual, but also attending to other responses that may receive credit for the nonoccurrence of the feared outcome during treatment.

Rituals around positive events

Notably, our simulations were limited to actions performed in the context of an anticipated negative outcome, whereas superstitious behaviors may also arise when seeking to promote desirable outcomes, for example in sports or gambling (Dömötör et al., 2016). First, the distinction between these two types of motivations may be less clear-cut in practice. Many situations in which positive-outcome rituals occur can be equally well characterized as an attempt to prevent failure. For example, a ritual performed by an athlete before taking a penalty kick may be driven either by the hope of scoring or by the fear of missing. Second, a crucial aspect unique to harm-prevention rituals is their potential stability over time when the environment is objectively safe. When a ritual is intended to prevent dire harm (e.g., a plane crash), the rarity of the catastrophe provides repeated apparent confirmation that the ritual has "worked." In contrast, when a ritual is intended to produce a positive outcome, the frequent failure of that outcome to occur provides more direct evidence contradicting the ritual's effectiveness, which would reduce the value assigned to the preceding action. Rituals aimed at producing positive outcomes may therefore be less stable, with individuals more likely to abandon or replace an action after an unsuccessful outcome. Evidence supporting this explanation comes from sports, where superstitious rituals that developed after apparent action-success pairings were reported to be abandoned when they were no longer followed by successful outcomes (Morris et al., 2025). This prediction was not tested in the present model and provides an interesting direction for future work examining whether rituals aimed at producing positive outcomes and rituals aimed at preventing harm differ in how they are acquired, maintained, and replaced.

Causal belief and insight

An unresolved question concerns how the learned action values represented in our model relate to explicit beliefs about the causal efficacy of a ritual. OCD is often characterized as an ego-dystonic disorder, yet insight varies considerably, with some individuals recognizing that their rituals are unlikely to influence the feared outcome and others experiencing them as more ego-syntonic and believing that their behavior truly affects the outcome (Jalal et al., 2023). Our model is limited in its ability to represent this level of conscious causal belief. We can speculate, however, that automatically acquired action values interact with, and potentially conflict with, a more explicit cognitive map of the environment. That ishttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?T5IsOU humans may represent a cognitive map of the causal structure of the environment (Moran et al., 2021) , while at the same time acquiring outcome-irrelevant action values despite explicit knowledge that those actions do not predict outcomes (Ben-Artzi, Pereg, et al., 2026).

Such competing sources of information may offer one way of thinking about the pronounced experience of doubt in OCD. An automatically learned action value may provide an implicit signal that the ritual "works," which conflicts with an explicit cognitive representation of the environment that suggests that it should not. Resolving this conflict may be particularly difficult because causal inference about the absence of an effect requires counterfactual reasoning. After performing the ritual and observing that the feared event did not occur, the individual can only imagine what would have happened had the ritual not been performed.

Counterfactual comparisons are thought to play a central role in causal judgment (Byrnehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ZedJlq 2016; Gerstenberg, 2024) , while doubt, reduced confidence, and difficulty reaching subjective certainty are prominent features of OCD (Dar et al., 2021; Marton et al., 2019; Samuels et al., 2017).

Thus, the balance in guiding behavior between automatic credit assignment and an explicit cognitive model of the environment may vary across individuals and circumstances, potentially shaping both the tendency to engage in ritualistic behavior and the degree of causal belief, doubt, and insight associated with it. This dimension of causal belief was not accounted for in the present simulations and remains an important realm for future work.

Limitations

Several limitations of the current study should be noted. First, the present work uses an artificial RL agent and therefore does not provide evidence that the same mechanism operates in human compulsive behavior. The simulations shown in the manuscript are intended to be a mechanistic illustration of the theoretical account, rather than as a model that should be fitted to clinical data. Second, the model necessarily simplifies the psychological structure of compulsive rituals. Internal states are reduced to a binary distinction between anxious and calm states, and agents select among a small number of discrete actions, whereas human threat processing is multidimensional and rituals are often hierarchical, temporally extended, symbolically meaningful, and shaped by verbal rules. Accordingly, the model should be viewed as a simplified computational account of one potential learning mechanism rather than as an attempt to capture the full complexity of human compulsive behavior. Finally, the model treats rituals as temporally distinct events to which subsequent outcomes can be assigned. In everyday life, however, threat expectations are often continuous and temporally diffuse rather than tied to a clearly defined moment. In particular, the nonoccurrence of a disaster has no physical presence and does not occur at a single identifiable point in time (Balsam & Gallistel, 2009; Gallistel, 2025). We propose that the structured and well-defined nature of rituals may itself create such a temporal anchor, allowing the subsequent occurrence or nonoccurrence of harm to be attributed to the ritual (Hobson et al., 2018; Kurby & Zacks, 2008). This assumption, however, was not directly examined in the present model. Bearing all this in mind, the model should be interpreted as identifying candidate computational principles that may contribute to ritual acquisition and maintenance, rather than as providing a complete account of OCD.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present model proposes a learning mechanism that may help explain a central puzzle of compulsive ritualistic behavior: how an objectively ineffective action can acquire value as a means of preventing harm (Salkovskis, 1985). When an anticipated catastrophe fails to occur, the resulting positive advantage may be automatically credited to the preceding action, allowing an initially neutral behavior to be seen as neutralizing the threat (O'Connor et al., 2012). By formalizing this process, the model complements habitual, anxiety-reducing, and superstitious-learning accounts by specifying how relief and repetition may become attached to one particular action. Thus, ritualistic behavior may emerge not simply from irrational repetition, but from ordinary reinforcement learning processes that mistakenly assign credit for safety to actions performed under threat.
