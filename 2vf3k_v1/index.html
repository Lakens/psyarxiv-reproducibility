What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

This cross-sectional study examined post-pandemic levels of meaning in life and its associated factors among Hispanic adults (N = 10,142) aged 18 to 78 years old, residing across 16 countries. Analysis revealed that 50.2% of the participants experienced low levels of meaning in life. Controlling for fixed country effects, mental health, occupational purpose, gratitude, friendships, and engagement in meaningful activities emerged as variables associated with higher meaning in life. Conversely, low perceived workplace success, absence of gratitude, and heavy social media consumption were linked to lower meaning. Key sociodemographic markers, including income, education, and gender, showed no significant associations with meaning in life. This non-significance suggests that meaning in life functions as an accessible resource available to individuals regardless of their sociodemographic factors. These findings indicate that post-pandemic recovery across Hispanic populations must extend beyond economic indicators to prioritize accessible community-based mental health integration and social infrastructure, offering an evidencebased starting point for designing culturally grounded interventions to promote meaning in life.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic represented an unprecedented disruption to global public health, imposing severe psychological distress by exacerbating mental health challenges, and destabilizing social structures by fracturing daily routines, relational networks, and occupational stability. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) (2022) there was a 25% increase in prevalence of anxiety and depression worldwide after lockdown.

Specifically, in Latin America, studies show that around 20% to 70% of the population continues to suffer from depression and anxiety after the pandemic (Pan American Health Organization, 2021). In this post-pandemic context, restoring psychological well-being has emerged as a central priority for behavioral health. Among psychological resources, meaning in life, understood as the affective and cognitive perception of values that guide individuals to act in a particular way, fostering coherence and personal identity (Martínez, 2007), functions as a vital buffer against psychological challenges (Dulaney et al., 2018).

From Viktor Frankl's logotherapy to contemporary models of well-being, meaning in life has been consistently included as a central component of psychological health (Disabato et al., 2025; Ryff, 1989; Seligman, 2011). As higher levels of meaning are associated with lower levels of anxiety and depression, fewer ruminative thoughts, reduced risk of illness, and a lower likelihood of engaging in risky behaviors (Aftab et al., 2019; Brassai et al., 2011; Dulaney et al., 2018; Shek et al., 2022). In contrast, lower levels of meaning frequently accompany psychological distress, alienation, greater risk of suicidal ideation, and reduced life satisfaction (Edwards & Holden 2001; Shek et al., 2022).

The available evidence shows that individuals reporting higher levels of meaning exhibit greater positive affect, self-esteem, effective coping strategies, and resilience (Demir et al., 2023; Krok, 2008). Similarly, higher meaning in life is associated with increased motivation, better work performance, greater productivity, and creativity (Medina et al., 2024; Su et al., 2025). Overall, the evidence supporting the benefits of living a meaningful life is substantial, for example, meaning is significantly related to perceived health, generativity, achievements, work, economic situation, and family situation, with the latter showing the strongest correlation (Schnell, 2009; Tuz-Sierra et al., 2021).

Within Hispanic psychological frameworks, well-being is deeply embedded in social connectedness, interpersonal warmth, and daily engagement (Cavazos Vela et al., 2018; Ortiz, 2020). Relational factors such as close social support networks and community participation provide vital scaffolding for psychological resilience and well-being (Amati et al., 2018; Grouden & Jose, 2015). Similarly, attitudinal dispositions like gratitude and religious commitment foster interconnectedness. Specially, in strained contexts, gratitude has shown associations with meaning in life, as it enhances a sense of belonging (McGee et al., 2024). Likewise, occupational factors, such as meaningful occupational involvement, foster personal coherence and personal agency (Eakman & Eklund, 2012; Lasota, 2023).

Prior research has identified a consistent set of correlates across diverse samples (Karwetzky et al., 2021; Silver et al., 2021). However, the relative importance of these factors, and how they operate in Hispanic communities remains largely understudied.

Particularly in Latin America, empirical research aimed at better understanding the specific affective experiences and motivational cues associated with meaning in life remains scarce, as research has predominantly focused on psychometric studies to validate different instruments, leaving its associated variables comparatively understudied (Caycho-Rodríguez et al., 2023; Simkin et al., 2018; Steger & Samman, 2012).

To address this limitation, we aim to provide the first large-sample descriptive account of meaning in life and its associated factors in a Hispanic sample. Findings are intended to provide an evidence base for culturally grounded programs aimed at promoting meaning in life among Hispanic communities, which remain markedly underrepresented in the well-being literature relative to its global size. The present study offers the potential to generate an empirical foundation for targeted behavioral health strategies, and key insights intended to inform future interventions for restoring psychological well-being in the postpandemic context.

Methods

This study employs a non-experimental cross-sectional design, since no intentional modifications of the variables were conducted to assess their effect on another; rather, the phenomena are observed as they occur in their natural context at a specific point in time (Mousalli-Kayat, 2015).

Participants

The sample was selected using a non-probabilistic convenience sampling method as participants were recruited via dissemination on social media. The final sample comprised N = 10,142 individuals from 16 countries to ensure greater demographic diversity: Argentina (n = 366), Chile (n = 167), Colombia (n = 5,959), Costa Rica (n = 98), Ecuador (n = 163), El Salvador (n = 37), Spain (n = 124), United States (n = 89), Guatemala (n = 133), Honduras (n = 41), Mexico (n = 2,211), Panama (n = 30), Peru (n = 101), Dominican Republic (n = 60), Uruguay (n = 43), and Venezuela (n = 520).

Participants range in age from 18 to 78 years (M =42.3, SD =9.9). Approximately 85.9% were women. Their demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. As inclusion criteria participants were required to be over 18 years of age and be Hispanic. It is important to emphasize that all participants took part in the study voluntarily and were informed of its research purpose through the informed consent process.

Instruments

Instruments (EDSV) (Martínez et al., 2011).

Dimensional Scale of Meaning in Life.

This scale assesses meaning in life, understood as the affective-cognitive perception of referential values that drive a person and prompt them to act in a particular way, providing personal identity and coherence with oneself. It comprises 18 items rated on a four-point Likert scale; the final score is obtained by calculating the average score. Lower scores reflect a lower perceived meaning in life, labelled as "Search for meaning," whereas higher scores indicate a stronger level of meaning, labelled and interpreted as "Meaning fulfillment levels". Psychometric evaluation shows that the scale has optimal internal consistency (α = 0.94).

An ad hoc questionnaire, specifically designed for this study in Spanish, was administered. It comprised 19 questions addressing various aspects of daily life, including friendships, hobbies, gratitude, engagement with material possessions, romantic relationships, religious practices, health perception, social media use, purpose in the occupation, and perceived work success, alongside sociodemographic variables (Dulaney et al., 2018; Grouden & Jose, 2015; Hadden & Knee, 2016; Hooker et al., 2019; Silver et al., 2021). To optimize protocol length and reduce response fatigue in a large sample, specific constructs were assessed using single-item indicators. This approach is consistent with previous research showing that single-item measures can provide useful and valid assessments when the variable represents a global evaluation, a concrete behavior, or a straightforward aspect of respondents' lives (Bowling, 2005; Elo et al., 2003; Spörrle & Bekk, 2014).

Procedure

The ad hoc questionnaire and the EDSV were uploaded to a platform that enabled large-scale distribution. Participants were recruited through dissemination on social media through non-paid advertisements posted on Instagram between August and October 2024.

Subsequently, the dataset was downloaded, and an initial analysis was conducted to remove inconsistent responses as part of a quality control procedure. Responses were screened for eligibility and completeness, a total of 10,280 individuals accessed the survey, after excluding participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria and those with incomplete responses, the final sample consisted of 10,142 participants. All statistical analyses were performed in R using the RStudio environment.

Ethics Declaration

In accordance with the guidelines established by the Ministry of Health and Social Protection (1993), which sets forth the scientific, technical, and administrative standards for health research, this study adhered to criteria related to informed consent, confidentiality of information, and protection of participants' privacy, with prior authorization obtained from all individuals. Both the study protocol and the informed consent form were developed according to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and were submitted to and approved by the Institution's Ethics Committee in session 02 which took place in July 2024. The study was classified as a no-risk investigation given its evaluative nature, as no interventions were performed on the participants.

Data Analysis

To assess the suitability of a multilevel model in the analysis of meaning in life, the proportion of variability explained by the group level (country) was examined using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC was calculated from a null model in which the dependent variable (meaning in life) was modeled with a random intercept at the country level. The result obtained was ICC = 0.0011, indicating that approximately 0.11% of the variance in meaning in life is due to differences between countries. Given that ICC values above 0.05 (5%) typically justify the need for a multilevel model (Hox et al., 2017), the result suggests that variability in meaning in life across countries is minimal. This indicates that the country does not introduce substantial variability in the dependent variable, thus limiting the usefulness of a random-effects model. Therefore, a linear regression model with fixed country effects was estimated, using ordinary least squares to control for systematic differences between countries without assuming an unnecessary multilevel structure.

Regarding the variable of interest, the original distribution of meaning in life exhibited a tendency toward negative skewness. To address this issue, a logarithmic transformation was applied to reduce skewness and improve the approximation to normality.

The transformation produced a more symmetric distribution, closer to normality, thereby enhancing variance stability and facilitating the interpretation of model coefficients. This suggests that the model estimated with the transformed variable provides greater statistical robustness and stability.

Regarding, the associated variables; they were classified into three descriptive groupings for presentational purposes: social factors (Table 3), understood as variables explicitly describing relationships or social interaction with other people, animals, or online contacts; occupational factors (Table 4), referring to variables explicitly describing work role or occupational context; psychological factors (Table 5), understood as variables reflecting internal states, attitudes, or self-evaluations not defined by a specific relationship or role; and sociodemographic variables, including gender, age, education, and income. A greater number of friends is associated with increased meaning in life, highlighting the role of social relationships in fostering meaning. Children also emerged as a relevant variable, with individuals reporting a higher number of children experiencing greater meaning. In contrast, extensive use of social media appears to have negative effects, which become noticeable when usage exceeds three hours per day. Perceived romantic involvement also shows nuanced associations: individuals who are not in love, or who feel very slightly in love, report lower levels of perceived meaning in life. Individuals who reported doing a higher number of meaningful activities exhibited higher meaning in life, as did those who perceived their occupation to be highly meaningful. Conversely, individuals reporting low levels of purpose in their occupation and low levels of perceived success at the workplace presented lower levels of meaning in life. This highlights the role of occupational factors in fostering meaning. Individuals reporting a more positive perception of their mental health exhibited higher meaning in life. Frequent and consistent use of material possessions is positively associated with a greater sense of meaning in life, indicating that the capacity to enjoy personal resources may shape perceptions of subjective well-being. In a similar vein, belief in God and perceptions of better physical health appear to exert a modest positive effect.

Sociodemographic Variables

Sociodemographic variables did not exhibit statistically significant effects in the model, which suggests that their relationship with meaning in life is inconclusive within this sample. These variables include gender (p = .101), level of education (p = .302), income (p = .289), and occupation: employee (p = .118), student (p = .822), or independent worker (p =.147).

Discussion

This study provides, to our knowledge, the largest descriptive account to date of meaning in life and its associated variables among Hispanic communities, drawing on a sample of 10,142 participants across 16 countries in the post-pandemic period. The primary objective of this study was to characterize the state of meaning in life and identify its associated factors in the post-pandemic era. Our findings indicate that half of the sample (50.2%) reported low levels of meaning in life, underscoring that diminished meaning is not a marginal experience within this sample but affects a substantial portion of respondents. This finding is noteworthy as, according to Heintzelman and King (2014), meaning in life is apparently typical and the average life is very meaningful. However, these results could be interpreted as reflecting the effects of the Covid-19 pandemic on meaning, as it has been shown in previous studies that the pandemic took a toll on psychological well-being (Baños et al., 2023; López et al., 2022; Rodríguez-Rey et al., 2020; Schnell & Krampe, 2020). The disruption of long-term life plans, economic shifts, and altered social dynamics appear to coincide with an active state of searching for purpose among an important proportion of Hispanic adults within this sample.

Regarding specific associated factors, variables reflecting social support, occupational purpose, and attitudinal choices emerged as primary elements tied to meaning, being consistent with Viktor Frankl's (1967 , 1991) theories regarding the possible pathways to discovering meaning.

Concerning relational support, our results indicate that friends, children, and romantic involvement were associated with meaning. This pattern of findings could be understood in the light of collectivistic cultures, as Hispanic communities tend to gravitate toward more collectivist tendencies. In these cultures, the sources of meaning may differ from individualistic societies as they place less emphasis on the personal self, which could include personality and strengths, and more on the collective self, which could include social roles (Ortiz, 2020). In a similar vein, personalismo, understood as the preference for connectedness with others and for interpersonal relationships based on trust, motivates individuals to engage in behaviors that promote meaningful relationships (Ortiz, 2020). Therefore, the experience of meaning in Hispanic samples could be rooted in a collectivist culture that values relationships.

In contrast, heavy social media consumption was associated with lower meaning, suggesting that the hours spent in these platforms may disrupt authentic social connection and well-being, possibly through passive social comparison (Valerio & Serna, 2018; World Happiness Report, 2026).

In relation to occupational factors, our findings emphasize that a lack of perceived purpose in one's occupation is linked to lower levels of meaning in life, whereas perceiving a significantly elevated level of purpose is related to higher levels of meaning. This outcome aligns with the evidence reported by Eakman and Eklund (2012), who identified occupational purpose and occupational value as the primary variables of life satisfaction and meaning in life in regression models, suggesting that participation in work, study, caregiving, and other meaningful activities is a fundamental aspect of human life. Specially, in Hispanic cultural contexts, daily occupation represents a domain for personal agency, contribution, and family support; as it provides stability, structure, belonging, identity, and meaning (Leufstadius et al., 2024).

Similarly, low levels of perceived success at the workplace were associated with meaning, which shows the importance of the self-evaluation of one's performance at work for well-being. This could be interpreted in the light of self-efficacy, as individuals who perceive themselves as less successful may also experience weaker beliefs in their ability to achieve valued goals and effectively influence major events. Lower self-efficacy, in turn, may be associated with a diminished sense of meaning and purpose (Cavazos Vela et al., 2018).

Consistent with previous studies, our results indicated that positive mental health stood out as a strong positive element associated with meaning in life (Shek et al., 2022).

Positive perceptions of mental health may provide individuals with psychological resources that facilitate the construction and maintenance of meaning in life (Hinojosa & Vela, 2019).

Thus, individuals who perceive themselves as psychologically healthier may be better positioned to engage with meaningful relationships, pursue valued goals, and experience a sense of purpose. However, it is important to note that these processes may also operate bidirectionally, because social relationships, goal attainment, and perceived meaning can contribute to subsequent psychological well-being (Jiang et al., 2026; Steptoe & Fancourt, 2020).

Our results highlight the negative association observed between low levels of gratitude and meaning in life; raising the possibility that gratitude deficit could be a critical point of existential vulnerability among Hispanic adults, as gratitude fosters resilience and life satisfaction (Lasota, 2023). In Hispanic cultural contexts, where gratitude is one of the ways that individuals experience connection, support, and belonging (Unanue et al., 2019), a lack of gratitude may reflect feelings of social disconnection, alienation, or an inability to identify relational resources in the post-pandemic context.

Among the variables that did not reach statistical significance, religious commitment was one of the most notable given its history of a positive association with meaning (Grouden & Jose, 2015; King & Hicks, 2021; Oñate et al., 2018). In contrast, belief in God did show a significant association, although with a small effect size. Taken together, these findings are consistent with the results reported by Silver et al. (2021) who found that religion was one of the least mentioned sources of meaning across 17 countries, which suggests that the role of religion in meaning has been decreasing in recent years. In the light of Hispanic communities, religion and spirituality may be an important cultural context for meaning, but religious commitment per se may not necessarily be the mechanism through which meaning in life is experienced, specially as spirituality may be embedded in relationships with family and community, not necessarily in institutional religious participation (Campesino & Schwartz, 2006).

Notably, income, educational attainment, employment status, and gender did not show an association with meaning in life. This finding holds substantial social and clinical importance: it demonstrates that meaning in life functions as an accessible resource, regardless of the sociodemographic characteristics of each individual. This is consistent with Frankl's (1991) theory of meaning as a fundamental human motivation, as the capacity to search for and discover meaning is inherent to human existence. Although gender, educational level, and socioeconomic status can influence life experiences, they do not necessarily determine whether individuals perceive their lives as meaningful (King & Hicks, 2021). This pattern is consistent with the possibility that, within collectivist cultural contexts, meaning is substantially co-constructed through relational and social-role fulfillment rather than derived primarily from personal attainment like educational level or income (Ortiz, 2020).

A key finding of this study is that country-level variation accounted for a minimal proportion of the overall variance in meaning in life. This suggests that rather than being dictated by macro-level national differences, meaning in life is rooted in shared individual and relational processes. This interpretation is consistent with cross-national research in Latin America showing substantial comparability in the underlying construct of purpose in life across countries (Caycho-Rodríguez et al., 2023). While Hispanic populations may differ in their sociocultural and national contexts, the experience of meaning may be relatively consistent within this sample, which has implications for the development of regional public policies and behavioral health interventions. Consequently, these findings offer a starting point for designing interventions to promote meaning in life among Hispanic samples that are centered in relational and culturally salient pathways, rather than defaulting to intervention models developed primarily in individualist cultural contexts. Programs that strengthen relational support networks, foster a sense of purpose within occupational and social roles, and cultivate gratitude may be particularly well-aligned with how meaning is constructed within these cultural frameworks, though this remains to be assessed directly through intervention research. Thus, clinical and community programs should incorporate these strategies in their interventions in order to promote meaning in life. Similarly, organizations employing Hispanic workforces should prioritize employee agency, task significance, and alignment between organizational goals and individual values to avoid quiet quitting and foster meaning (Magrizos et al., 2026).

Future research should prioritize probabilistic sampling designs to establish representative estimates, incorporate longitudinal designs capable of testing directional and reciprocal relationships between these variables, and examine whether collectivism operates as an explicit mediator of the associations identified here, rather than as an inferred contextual explanation.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the recruitment procedure may have introduced selection bias, as participants with a greater pre-existing interest in meaning in life may have been more likely to volunteer for the study, similarly, individuals without internet access, with lower social media engagement, or from groups less likely to encounter recruitment materials could be underrepresented. This also resulted in a sample predominantly female and concentrated in Colombia and Mexico, limiting population-wide generalizability. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; therefore, the identified relationships reflect concurrent associations. Third, references to post-pandemic increases in mental health difficulties draw on prior longitudinal literature rather than on data collected in the present study, which captured a single time point. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to observe the dynamic relationships between mental health, occupational purpose, social relationships, and meaning over time.

Conclusion

This study provides broad empirical evidence regarding the factors associated with meaning in life among Hispanic adults in the post-pandemic period. By analyzing data from 10,142 individuals across 16 nations, our results demonstrate that meaning in life is strongly tied to mental health, occupational purpose, gratitude, and social support networks. In the wake of the global disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, understanding these mechanisms has become an essential priority for Hispanic behavioral health. These findings offer practical insights for behavioral health professionals, researchers, and policy-makers seeking to design interventions that enhance meaning in life throughout Hispanic populations.

However, those interventions should move beyond broad economic metrics, and instead, prioritize culturally tailored, community-based strategies that foster social connectedness, workplace agency, and cultivate psychological resources like gratitude. Strengthening these actionable domains offers a promising, accessible pathway to promote psychological wellbeing and meaning in life across Hispanic communities.
