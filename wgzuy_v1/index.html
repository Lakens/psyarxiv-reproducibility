Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The role of social media in misinformation consumption remains contested. This disagreement might arise from the failure to distinguish incidental exposure to misinformation from deliberate consumption. We address this gap by analyzing incoming traffic data, collected in November 2020, to more than 2,000 websites that vary in misinformation and type of bias (liberal, conservative, and conspiracy). We compare the traffic modalities through which users reach those sites: direct traffic, such as typing a URL or using a bookmark (mostly associated with self-directed information seeking); social traffic, from links clicked on social media; and search traffic, from search engines (mostly associated with incidental exposure). We found that reliable, low-misinformation websites are reached mainly via search traffic, whereas unreliable ones are mostly reached via direct traffic; this pattern is stronger for conspiracy websites. Breaking down social traffic by platform reveals that Facebook was the main driver of traffic towards unreliable websites, mostly towards politically biased and entry-level conspiracy websites. These findings suggest that structural interventions such as algorithmic adjustments and content moderation may reduce incidental exposure among users with low belief commitment, but are unlikely to deter highly committed information seekers, who require targeted, ideologically informed strategies.

1 Introduction

Misinformation undermines citizens' ability to make informed decisions, posing a significant threat to democratic processes [1, 2]. Among the drivers of its diffusion, social media have often been blamed [3, 4]. Features such as algorithmic curation (e.g., YouTube's ranked recommendations) and platform designs that favor like-minded communities (e.g., Facebook groups) can reinforce users' existing beliefs while filtering out contradictory information, facilitating the spread of misinformation [5] [6] [7] [8]. Combined with deeper engagement in echo chambers, this can draw users into "rabbit holes" [9, 10] that amplify extreme viewpoints [11] and reinforce ideological segregation and polarization [12, 13].

There is an active debate about how pervasive misinformation actually is in the digital information landscape [14, 15] and whether exposure to misinformation does [16, 17] or does not [18, 19] translate into active engagement, belief uptake, and behavioral change. But social media can also function as intermediaries [20] whose influence depends on users' predispositions, cognitive biases, and environment [21] [22] [23]. For instance, in autocracies and emerging democracies, social media use can be beneficial, increasing political participation and information consumption, whereas in established democracies it can be detrimental, associated with declining political trust, populism, and polarization [24].

A critical point concerns the distinction between exposure and consumption. Because much social media content is processed only superficially [25], researchers have moved beyond mere exposure, analyzing patterns of active consumption such as web browsing activity [26]. However, browsing data have their own limitations. Most tracking tools do not capture in-platform actions, making it difficult to determine whether a visit originated from social media or via other routes [27] (but see [28]). Moreover, such data are difficult to collect from users with extreme ideologies, who are often mistrustful of research institutions [29].

These gaps can be addressed by shifting the focus from individual browsing behavior to website-level incoming traffic patterns. Rather than following individuals across the web, this approach decomposes a website's incoming traffic into the traffic modalities through which its visits arrive, such as search traffic (from search engines), social traffic (from links clicked on social media), or direct traffic (typing a URL or using a bookmark), among others. Traffic is aggregated over a website's entire audience rather than over a recruited panel, so it also captures visits from users unwilling to be tracked, such as those most likely to consume extreme content. Because -in principle-a website's content attracts users seeking that content, systematic differences in traffic modality between types of websites are informative about the information-seeking behavior patterns of the users who reach them.

An earlier analysis of incoming traffic data from 91 websites [30] showed that conspiracy websites were accessed primarily via direct traffic (58% of visits), compared with 18% referred by social media and 14% arriving from search engines. In contrast, pro-science and least-biased websites showed the opposite pattern: they were accessed predominantly via search engines (71% of visits), had little direct traffic (13%), and even less social traffic (5%). A partial replication analyzing 56 Italian misinformation websites found that websites' unreliability correlated negatively with search traffic and positively with direct traffic [31].

These analyses suggest that website characteristics are associated with specific patterns of information seeking: reliable websites tend to be reached via search engines, whereas unreliable websites are more often reached via direct traffic. Yet, given their exploratory nature, limited sample sizes, and lack of key control variables (e.g., website popularity), conclusions drawn from these studies remain tentative. The studies fail to account for differences between misinformation types (e.g., conspiracy vs politically biased) and social media platforms (e.g., Facebook vs Reddit), and do not offer a comprehensive, comparative analysis of the contribution of each traffic modality.

Here, we analyze patterns of incoming traffic for a large set of websites that vary in unreliability and in the type and strength of their bias. We first characterize how traffic modality varies with website unreliability, and whether this relationship differs between politically biased and conspiracy websites; we then break down social traffic by social media platforms to assess the contribution of each. Second, we compare traffic modalities with each other and within each website group. This comparison informs about the relative contribution of self-directed information seeking (mostly reflected in direct traffic), incidental exposure (mostly reflected in search traffic), and the role of social media as intermediaries (social traffic).

2 Methods

2.1 Website characteristics

In November 2020, we manually compiled a list of websites (N = 2,734) by surveying the entire content of MediaBiasFactCheck (MBFC, http://www.mediabiasfactcheck.com), a widely used ground-truth dataset in misinformation research (see e.g., [12, 30]).

For most websites (98.39%, N = 2,690), we mapped their editorial tendency to deliver misinformation using the reliability score developed by Lin et al. [32]. This score was compiled for 11,520 websites by aggregating ratings from six independent expert sources-including professional fact-checkers, MBFC, and NewsGuard (one of the most widely used and comprehensive databases for website trustworthiness ratings [33])-each of which rated websites on dimensions such as trust, reliability, bias, credibility, and transparency. Scores range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating more reliable websites, and show high correspondence with other indices of website quality, which are themselves highly correlated with one another. To keep its polarity commensurate with our other website indicators-MBFC bias strength, for which higher values indicate more extreme political or conspiratorial content-we inverted this score (1 -reliability) to obtain a measure of unreliability. Higher values thus consistently indicate more problematic websites across all indicators (e.g., nasa.gov = .043; infowars.com = .954), and positive slopes indicate traffic that increases with misinformation.

We use MBFC categories to differentiate websites by their bias, in order to investigate differences across groups of unreliable websites. According to MBFC's descriptions, Politically biased websites (N = 1826 in our sample) are moderately to strongly biased toward either liberal (N = 913; e.g., dailykos.com) or conservative (N = 913; e.g., breitbart.com) ideologies, often reflected in topic selection and use of emotionally charged language. These websites may publish misleading reports or omit ideologically unfavorable information, and some of these websites are considered less trustworthy. Conspiracy websites (N = 294; e.g., infowars.com) frequently publish unverifiable claims tied to conspiracy theories and often lack credible evidence and are generally unreliable for factual information. Finally, our sample includes minimally biased (MB) websites (e.g., reuters.com), which are those labelled by MBFC as pro-science and least-biased (N = 614 in our sample). These websites remain committed to scientific integrity, avoid emotionally charged or misleading language, and are considered among the most credible media outlets. Although our focus is on biased websites that vary in unreliability, we retain minimally biased websites whenever a full spectrum of unreliability is needed or when specific ideological biases are irrelevant.

MBFC provides coarse-grained bias-strength ratings for each category (e.g., the degree of conspiratorial content on a 1-5 scale), which are highly correlated with unreliability (r2688 = .84, p < .001; expectedly because MBFC is one of the indices used to compute the unreliability score). Throughout, we refer to MBFC's five labels (pro-science, least-biased, liberal, conservative, and conspiracy) as categories, to the groupings used in our models (liberal, conservative, conspiracy, and minimally biased) as groups, and to each combination of category and bias strength (e.g., CT01, R05) as a bias level. We refer to conspiracy websites with the lowest bias strength (CT01-CT02) as entry-level conspiracy websites.

Note that website group and unreliability are measured independently: group membership reflects MBFC's categorical classification of content, whereas unreliability is a continuous score aggregated across six expert sources [32]. As a result, unreliability varies within each group. For example, conspiracy websites are on average less reliable than minimally biased ones, but the two distributions overlap. A description of MBFC categories and unreliability is given in the Supplemental Material (SM; Appendix SM1).

2.2 Traffic modality

Measures of incoming traffic were collected in November 2020 from SimilarWeb (http://www.similarweb.com), a web analytics platform that provides traffic data for approximately 100 million websites (10 billion content pages). SimilarWeb's data, expressed as monthly averages, correlate highly with Google Analytics [34]. As in previous work [30, 31], we focus on three primary traffic modalities through which users access websites, namely search, direct, and social traffic.

Search traffic is the percentage of total visits originating from search engine queries. Search traffic has been associated with greater source diversity in web-tracking data: users who reach news through search engines visit a more ideologically diverse set of outlets, including outlets that do not match their political preferences [35], and have more varied news diets and higher overall news exposure [20]; moreover, search engines direct users to a more diverse set of sources than social media do [36]. In our sample, the proportion of search traffic correlates positively with bounce rate (the percentage of single-page visits; see below; r1907 = .24, p < .001) and negatively with visit duration (r1907 = -.21, p < .001) and pages per visit (r1908 = -.11, p < .001), suggesting that search visits tend to be short and mostly focused on a specific page.

Direct traffic is the percentage of total visits that SimilarWeb attributes to no referring source, that is, visits in which users type the URL into their browser or use a bookmark. It is therefore commonly taken to reflect self-directed, preference-driven information selection: studies using web-tracking data have linked direct traffic to more congenial, ideologically aligned engagement [35]. In our sample, direct traffic correlates negatively with bounce rate (r1907 = -.51, p < .001) and positively with visit duration (r1907 = .45, p < .001) and pages per visit (r1908 = .36, p < .001), suggesting that users who reach a website via direct traffic show greater on-site engagement with its content.

Social traffic is the percentage of total visits originating from links shared on social media platforms. It captures the extent to which social media function as intermediaries routing users to external websites [20]. Social traffic correlates positively with bounce rate (r1906 = .26, p < .001), and negatively with visit duration (r1906 = -.26, p < .001) and pages per visit (r1907 = -.27, p < .001), suggesting that social visits tend to be short and mostly focused on that specific page. SimilarWeb breaks down social traffic by platform. Here, we collected traffic from Facebook, YouTube, Twitter, and Reddit, which together account for approximately 80% of social traffic in our sample (M = 79.92, SD = 15.27, range: 18.21-99.97; these four platforms were chosen for their popularity at the time, confirmed with a reanalysis of 96 websites two years later; see Appendix SM2).

We included two other measures to account for websites' popularity and single-page visits. A website's popularity was quantified via total visits, measured by SimilarWeb as the monthly number of visits. We reasoned that an unpopular website is more likely to be accessed via direct traffic because it is likely to not appear within the first pages of a search engine; therefore, we controlled for popularity to exclude this artifact. However, note that in our data, total visits did not correlate with direct traffic (r1908 = -.004, p = .875), but correlated positively with search traffic (r1908 = .10, p < .001) and negatively with social traffic (r1907 = -.12, p < .001), which led to its inclusion in all models. Total visits were log-transformed to better align with website rankings (from r1889 = -.19, p < .001 to r1889 = -.99, p < .001). The other measure, bounce rate, represents the percentage of single-page visits, in which users leave a website after viewing only one page. Bounce rate was included as a covariate to control for low-engagement visits (e.g., accidental or uninterested ones), which could otherwise skew the interpretation of traffic modality contributions.

In November 2022, we re-collected traffic measures for a subset of our dataset (see Appendix SM2 for details) and show they are rather stable over a two-year time.

3.3 Relative contributions of traffic modalities

To better understand how traffic modalities contributed within each website group, we directly compared pairs of traffic modalities by fitting, for each pair, a regression model predicting the percentage of traffic from the three-way interaction between unreliability, traffic modality (direct vs search, social vs search, or direct vs social), and website group. Positive values indicate that the first modality in each pair is stronger relative to the second as unreliability increases.

Figure 1, panel I, reports these results. When comparing social vs search traffic, social traffic increased relative to search traffic for liberal (β = 2.88, p < .001) and conservative (β = 2.92, p < .001) websites, but not for conspiracy websites (β = 1.62, p = .066). When the contrast was defined as direct vs search, direct traffic increased relative to search traffic as a function of unreliability in all three groups (liberal: β = 2.62, p < .001; conservative: β = 3.11, p < .001), but most steeply for conspiracy websites (β = 5.48, p < .001). The direct vs social comparison yielded the clearest difference between conspiracy and politically biased websites: in conspiracy websites, direct traffic increased most steeply relative to social traffic (β = 3.63, p < .001), whereas liberal (β = -.56, p = .055) and conservative websites (β = -.13, p = .511) showed no change (Appendix SM15).

4 Discussion

We analyzed incoming traffic to more than 2,000 websites that differed in unreliability-a proxy for misinformation content-and in the type and strength of their bias. As website unreliability increased, the shares of direct and social traffic increased, while the share of search traffic declined. Traffic patterns were broadly similar across website groups, with one notable exception: as unreliability increased, conspiracy websites showed the steepest increase in direct traffic relative to search traffic. By contrast, the balance between social and search traffic shifted toward social traffic for politically biased websites, but not for conspiracy websites. Among the social media platforms examined, Facebook traffic was most strongly associated with website unreliability. This association differed by website group: it was positive for politically biased websites but not significant for conspiracy websites. Twitter, YouTube, and Reddit each accounted for a smaller share of incoming traffic than Facebook, on average. The most consistent pattern across our results was a decline in search traffic with unreliability. This decline, however, does not necessarily mean that search engines actively prioritize reliable sources. Search rankings rely in part on links among websites, which carry information about popularity and, by extension, about the consensus on a website's credibility. Consistent with this, unreliability correlated negatively with total visits, and total visits correlated positively, albeit weakly, with search traffic (see Appendix SM4). Because our data are observational and aggregated at the website level, the association between unreliability and search traffic may therefore partly be a by-product of web interconnectivity and popularity, rather than evidence that search engines assess credibility directly.

Direct traffic exceeds social traffic, particularly for websites with the greatest bias. Direct traffic, which includes also bookmarking favorite websites, was associated with greater engagement than search and social traffic, as reflected in longer visits, more pages viewed per session, and lower bounce rates. Such patterns are consistent with more self-directed, preference-driven visits, but note that our observational data cannot allow us to distinguish the preference for belief-congruent information from other forms of self-directed information seeking (more on this later). Selective exposure has been linked to stronger motivation [37], which may be particularly relevant when beliefs are closely tied to identity and worldviews [38]. People strongly committed to an ideology tend to exhibit rigid thinking, dogmatic beliefs, and strong belief commitment [39] [40] [41], and they may therefore seek information that aligns with their preexisting beliefs and avoid conflicting information. To the extent that direct traffic reflects selective exposure, our findings are consistent with prior research linking selective exposure to both political [42, 43] and conspiratorial worldviews [44].

Note that search and social traffic should not automatically be treated as incidental: these modalities can also include self-directed information seeking. Search traffic, for example, is not always informational: people may search for a topic without a preferred destination. Approximately 10% of Google queries are navigational, meaning that users are seeking a specific website or page (e.g., "covid symptoms wiki" vs "covid symptoms"; see [45]). Most search traffic to unreliable websites has been found to be navigational [46]. 1 Even when Google presents users with a diverse range of results, users tend to engage more with identity-congruent content, including unreliable content, and this tendency is stronger among Republicans than Democrats [47]. Thus, particularly for highly committed information seekers, some search visits may be self-directed and may resemble direct visits in this respect. If anything, direct traffic may underestimate the extent of self-directed information seeking.

Similarly, social traffic can arise in different contexts, such as links shared in ideologically aligned communities (e.g., Facebook groups), algorithmic recommendations (e.g., YouTube videos suggested on the basis of viewing histories), or posts from acquaintances. Although both algorithmic ranking and engagement-based distribution on social media can reinforce ideological clustering and echo chambers [12, 13], they might be involving different degrees of self-directed selection. Clicks from within a chosen, like-minded community may reflect selective exposure, whereas links encountered in broader feeds may be more incidental. Unfortunately, our data cannot distinguish between these contexts. Selection may also operate before the click: users' browsing histories can shape algorithmic recommendations, and their choice of communities may itself reflect selective exposure [48, 49].

4.1 Commitment-driven information seeking

Our results are consistent with behavioral patterns of commitment-driven information seeking. In the following, we provide a tentative explanation for the underlying psychological mechanisms.

The increase in direct (over search) traffic with unreliability was strongest for conspiracy websites. This pattern is consistent with a commitment-driven account of information seeking, in which initial exposure can be incidental but may gradually become more systematic as belief commitment deepens. A person may first encounter a conspiratorial claim through a social media feed or by searching for a specific concern, such as whether COVID-19 statistics are exaggerated, without yet being strongly committed to conspiratorial thinking. At this stage, a link to an entry-level conspiracy website may appear relevant and compelling, and a single search or click can trigger a broader process of information gathering. As more "evidence" accumulates, an initial incidental encounter can build into a more coherent narrative that "something is wrong with COVID", and the person may then move across an interconnected system of webpages that reinforce the same worldview.

Entry-level conspiracy websites can therefore function as entry points to deeper conspiratorial engagement, reinforcing belief systems and potentially drawing users into "rabbit holes" of conspiracy theories [9, 10]. This process aligns with longitudinal psychological research on the monological nature of conspiracy thinking, which suggests that belief in one conspiracy theory often leads to belief in others over time [50]. A network analysis of conspiracy websites reveals a high degree of thematic and narrative interconnectedness [51], and browsing data indicate that unreliable websites are highly interconnected through internal links, which may increase user engagement and contribute to the rabbit hole phenomenon [52].

As belief commitment strengthens, users may increasingly bypass intermediaries and engage in more self-directed information seeking. This shift from social to direct traffic along the unreliability continuum may resemble the solitary "epistemic journey" and "spiritual awakening" characterizing conspiracy believers [29, 53], who often present themselves as independent reasoners and portray their research as autonomous [54]. However, this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence that believers are less susceptible to social influence. On the contrary, it suggests that once belief commitment is established, information seeking becomes less dependent on external intermediaries and more self-directed. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that social influence remains relevant even when users increasingly seek information on their own [54].

4.2 Traffic from social media platforms

Our findings also show distinct differences in how social media platforms route traffic to unreliable websites.

Facebook emerged as the platform delivering the largest share of social traffic to unreliable websites, aligning with evidence that its engagement-driven ranking algorithms and group-based ecosystem foster ideological homogeneity and misinformation amplification [12]. However, as websites' unreliability increased so did the share of Facebook traffic towards politically biased, but not conspiracy websites. One tentative explanation is that temporary efforts by Facebook to reduce the spread of misinformation around the 2020 election may have disproportionately limited referrals to the most unreliable websites starting already with A/B testing in the summer of 2020 [55]. These measures, however, were not specific to conspiracy websites and our data cannot establish whether they explain this pattern.

YouTube contributed less to overall traffic towards unreliable websites: we found that link only for conservative websites. This seems in contrast with evidence for its role in self-driven radicalization and to dissemination of conspiracy theories and extreme content [56]. Our findings are not in contradiction because YouTube might push towards extreme and conspiratorial content within the platform itself, rather than delivering traffic towards external websites.

Twitter was more strongly associated with traffic towards politically biased websites than with conspiracy websites, consistent with studies indicating that its fact-checking interventions (at least in 2020) reduced prolonged exposure to conspiracy theories [57], although misinformation persists [58]. But note that our data was collected before Elon Musk acquired Twitter, reducing content moderation and propagating misinformation, raising concerns for democracy [59].

Among the social media platforms, Reddit traffic was not associated with website unreliability, likely due to its community-driven moderation system. Unlike algorithmically curated platforms such as Facebook and YouTube, Reddit's voting mechanism enables collective content regulation, reducing misinformation visibility [60].

4.3 Limitations and future work

Our data were collected in November 2020. Since then, the online information landscape has changed. Although we demonstrate that traffic patterns remained relatively stable over a two-year period, changes in website and social media ownership, misinformation policies, and editorial practices [33] can lead to changes in misinformation dissemination and traffic modalities. Since 2020, new social media platforms have gained prominence in spreading misinformation, such as TikTok but also more specialized environments such as Stormer, Parler, Truth Social, and Voat, to cite a few, along with messaging platforms such as Telegram, WhatsApp, and even webmail [3] Since our dataset is publicly available, a replication could offer valuable insights into the evolution of incoming traffic patterns over time (provided that SimilarWeb's methodology remains consistent). When we recollected data in 2022 to assess the stability of traffic measures, we found that social traffic included additional platforms. Future research could use the SimilarWeb API to scrape more platforms and track the evolving landscape of social traffic.

Automated traffic, such as that generated by bot visits, may skew direct traffic measures, thus influencing our estimates. Although this effect is likely evenly distributed over website categories, some conspiracy and other unreliable websites (e.g., file://localhost/opt/grobid/grobid-home/tmp/infowars.com) rely heavily on revenue from branded products [61], making bot-generated traffic counterproductive for their business models. Although we did not have data to explicitly control for bot activity, a substantial impact of bots on our direct traffic measures for unreliable websites appears unlikely.

During data collection in 2020, some metrics available on SimilarWeb were not included in our dataset, such as the distinction between organic search results and paid advertisements, because we specifically focused on traffic modalities. Future work could gather more data relying on the SimilarWeb API (as of September 2026, the service is accessible only via a paid subscription). Also, while we collected data on the percentage of referral traffic (i.e., traffic originating from other websites; see Appendix SM3), we did not map specific website referrals. In this regard, the recently developed DONALD corpus, built using the list of websites included in this study [62], offers a valuable resource for investigating referral patterns. This dataset contains over two million documents, each with a list of URLs present on the webpage, allowing researchers to analyze interconnections between unreliable websites. It would not be surprising to find a highly interconnected network of unreliable websites reinforcing each other, as observed in previous research [51, 52].

Finally, a cautionary note is warranted. Our results should not be interpreted as downplaying the role of social media in the dissemination of misinformation. Although this study focused on assessing the extent to which social media drive traffic to external websites, it is crucial to recognize that misinformation can also cause harm within social media platforms themselves, without redirecting users to external websites (e.g., YouTube). The absence of website clicks from social media posts does not necessarily imply a lack of exposure to misinformation. Unfortunately, this type of data cannot capture the consumption of misinformation that occurs entirely within social media ecosystems. Capturing such exposure would require browsing data or platform-specific social media data.

5 Conclusions

Our analysis of incoming traffic across a large sample of websites with varying degrees of unreliability and bias reveals two distinct patterns in misinformation consumption. While incidental exposure, mostly via search traffic, introduces users with low belief commitment to unreliable content, self-directed information seeking appears to drive consumption of websites with the highest bias strength, particularly conspiracy websites. Our interpretation is that unlike politically biased websites, which rely more on social traffic, consumption of conspiracy content tends to follow solitary epistemic journeys, where individuals rely less on social media and more on self-reinforcing discovery mechanisms.

Our results carry important implications for intervention strategies. While structural interventions such as content moderation and deplatforming may help limit incidental exposure to misinformation, they are unlikely to deter users with conspiratorial worldviews, who often migrate to alternative platforms [63] or perceive moderation efforts as censorship, further reinforcing their worldview [17]. Additionally, the growing reluctance of major platforms to implement misinformation countermeasures raises concerns about the longterm sustainability of structural interventions [1].

Given these challenges, individual-level approaches, including psychological inoculation, emerge as more viable solutions, with large-scale prebunking initiatives holding promise as scalable interventions [64]. Ultimately, while no single approach can fully counter misinformation, a comprehensive strategy that assesses costs and benefits while integrating targeted platform policies with prebunking campaigns offers the most effective path forward in mitigating the societal impact of misinformation in both the short and long term.
