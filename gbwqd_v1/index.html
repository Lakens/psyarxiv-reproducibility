Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Full text

Abstract

Random-effect predictions from multilevel models are often used to relate individual differences in processes, such as growth or stress reactivity, to other variables. Yet, regressions using these predictions can distort regression coefficients and standard errors because the predictions combine shrinkage with sampling error. Here, we introduce and evaluate Random-Effect Prediction Adjustment for Inference in Regression (REPAIR), a two-step framework for linear regressions involving random effects. For linear Gaussian multilevel models, REPAIR obtains likelihood-only predictions by generalized least squares. A regularized method-of-moments estimator then uses estimated cluster-specific sampling-error covariances to correct structural moments. Four simulation studies examined random effects as predictors, outcomes, or both, including unequal cluster sizes. REPAIR generally showed small bias at medium to high predictor reliability and often had lower root mean squared error than alternative methods. Low predictor reliability remained challenging, particularly for confidence interval coverage in small samples. These findings support REPAIR as a practical framework for estimating structural relationships involving random effects while accounting for their measurement uncertainty.

1. Introduction

Many psychological studies concern individual differences in processes that unfold over time or across situations (Hamaker & Wichers, 2017). Researchers may ask whether people who improve more rapidly during treatment experience better subsequent outcomes, whether long-term personality or behavior change predicts life outcomes, or how cognitive processes elicited by an experimental task relate to mental health. Multilevel models (MLMs) are natural tools for such questions because they distinguish population-average effects from person-specific deviations representing individual levels or within-person associations (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2010). A common analytic strategy is to fit an MLM, extract its random-effects predictions, and use those predictions in a second analysis (Liu & Rhemtulla, 2022).

The scientific goal, however, is typically to understand relationships among the underlying person-specific processes, rather than merely among their model-predicted values.

This distinction matters in a range of applications. For example, in a randomized meditation trial, Webb et al. (2024) predicted person-specific rates of change in mindfulness, then examined whether these mediated the overall effect of the intervention on subsequent distress. Other researchers have considered whether random intercepts and slopes from MLMs capture meaningful psychological attributes. For example, drawing on five ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies, Kuper and colleagues 2022 predicted within-person relationships between Big Five personality states and eight situation-specific characteristics (e.g., whether the situation was experienced as pleasant). To better understand personality-situation contingencies, they then examined the reliability, short-term stability, external correlates, and latent structure of the 40 random slopes extracted from the MLMs. In both of these examples, as well as numerous others (e.g., Hallquist and Lenzenweger, 2013; McCormick et al., 2026; Willroth et al., 2025), the substantive goal is to characterize individual differences in within-person processes, such as change or decision-making. The shared statistical strategy is to predict person-specific values of those processes from repeated observations and then use them as variables in a downstream analysis.

A standard approach for predicting person-specific random effects is Empirical Bayes (EB) (Robinson, 1991). EB predictions borrow information from the full sample, shrinking uncertain individual values toward the population mean. This adaptive shrinkage can substantially improve the predictions of individual values (Katahira, 2016). When the goal is to estimate relationships among underlying random effects, however, shrinkage complicates the use of EB predictions as observed variables (Liu & Rhemtulla, 2022; Liu et al., 2021). Shrinkage changes the variances and covariances of EB predictions, so downstream analyses using these as outcomes or predictors may not recover the true relationships involving the underlying random effects. The predictions also retain sampling error; treating them as perfectly measured can distort structural estimates and their standard errors (Bollen, 1989; Cohen et al., 2015; Fuller, 1987).

Here, we develop a two-step framework-Random-Effect Prediction Adjustment for Inference in Regression (REPAIR)-that accounts for shrinkage and uncertainty in random-effect predictions. The first step produces likelihoodonly predictions and their cluster/person-specific sampling error covariances within an MLM framework. The second step uses those covariances in a modified method-of-moments errors-in-variables regression that extends established measurement error models (Fuller, 1987). This framework offers a practical way to investigate relationships among person-specific processes measured across different tasks, settings, or timescales. Although our examples emphasize within-person processes arising from repeated observations, we note that the methods presented here apply equally to other forms of clustering such as students nested within classrooms.

2. Existing Approaches

>Existing Approaches

2.1. Joint modeling vs. two-step approaches

The most direct solution to avoiding the problems of treating EB predictions as ordinary variables is not to rely on random-effect predictions at all. A joint model can simultaneously specify an MLM linking observed data to underlying random effects and a structural model describing how those effects relate to one another or to other variables, accounting for uncertainty in the random effects through the full likelihood. Joint modeling might involve simply incorporating person-level variables into an MLM through a cross-level interaction, or fitting latent curve models (Bollen & Curran, 2006), multilevel structural equation models, or other joint latent variable models when a random effect predicts a distal outcome or participates in a more complex path model. Joint modeling is not always practical, however. Researchers often collect repeated measures from several tasks or assessments within the same study, yet these observations may not have a natural occasion-level correspondence. This can lead to difficulties in specification and estimation when model complexity grows (Hickey et al., 2016; Rustand et al., 2024), or loss of information about uncertainty when "shortcuts" such as taking averages or percentages are used (Haines et al., 2025). Two-step approaches offer a practical alternative by representing each latent process or trait with a separately fitted MLM. Information from these models is then carried into a second-step structural analysis estimating relationships among the processes or traits while accounting for measurement error (Lai & Liu, 2026; Rosseel & Loh, 2024).

A separate motivation for two-step estimation is to hold a vetted step 1 model fixed while comparing structural models. Estimating the step 1 model separately can prevent misspecification in the structural model from biasing step 1 model parameter estimates, a phenomenon known as interpretational confounding (Bollen et al., 2007; Burt, 1973 Burt, , 1976)). Similar motivations underlie structural-after-measurement and other multi-step latent-variable procedures (Bollen, 1996; Kuha & Bakk, 2024; Levy, 2023; Rosseel & Loh, 2024). These advantages depend on an appropriately specified first-step model and a second-step procedure that accounts for how the latent quantities were measured.

2.2. Factor score regression and corrected moments

Modularized estimation procedures in structural equation modeling (SEM) address how to estimate relationships among latent traits while accounting for measurement error using models that map those traits to observed indicators. Examples of modularized estimation frameworks include factor score regression (Skrondal & Laake, 2001), the structural-aftermeasurement approach (Rosseel & Loh, 2024), measurement and uncertainty preserving parametric modeling (Levy, 2023), and model-implied instrumental variable, two-stage least squares estimation (Bollen, 1996).

Factor score regression provides a particularly direct parallel to downstream analyses of random-effect predictions.

Factor scores predict individuals' values on latent factors, much as random-effect predictions do for person-specific intercepts or slopes. This connection also has a formal basis: random effects are latent variables, and MLMs can be represented as SEMs (Bauer, 2003; Curran, 2003). Using either type of prediction in a subsequent regression raises the same essential question: how do the prediction method and its corresponding error affect estimates of structural relationships among the latent variables? Skrondal and Laake (2001) showed that the appropriate factor scoring method can depend on the score's role in the structural model. Under their model assumptions, pairing regression-method scores for predictors with Bartlett scores for outcomes yields consistent estimates of unstandardized regression coefficients. Regression-method scores are analogous to EB predictions, whereas Bartlett scores are analogous to likelihood-only predictions. This result shows that shrinkage does not necessarily bias a structural regression coefficient. However, consistency of these coefficients does not imply that the scores' variances and covariances are equal to those of the underlying latent variables (McDonald, 1981; ten Berge et al., 1999). Consequently, standardizing the regression coefficients using the score variance can induce bias (Devlieger et al., 2016). The measurement error structure can also affect standard errors, so coefficient consistency alone does not establish the validity of conventional inference (Saxonhouse, 1976). These considerations motivate the complementary approach of correcting score variances and covariances before estimating structural relationships. Croon (2002) developed such a correction that accounts for both the transformation of the latent variables and the error introduced by scoring. The central logic follows classical errors-in-variables: for scores already expressed as a latent value plus sampling error, their observed covariance contains separable latent and error components. Thus, subtracting the expected error covariance recovers the moment matrix relevant to the latent structural relation. Croon's method additionally generalizes to factor scores without a clean "latent value plus error" structure. For scoring methods that also transform the latent variables-e.g., via shrinkage, such as regression-method scores-the moment correction must account for both the transformation and the error covariance. Devlieger and Rosseel (2017) found that using Croon's correction in a factor score path analysis achieved higher convergence rates than simultaneous SEM estimation, and Devlieger et al. (2016) introduced an accompanying inferential adjustment. Importantly, however, this approach cannot be applied directly to random-effects predictions because the transformations and error covariances can differ across clusters.

2.2.2. Corrected moments: Croon

Devlieger and Rosseel (2020) extended Croon's correction to multilevel factor-score regression, estimating structural relationships from corrected within-and between-cluster covariance matrices. Their between-level correction accounts for sampling variability in cluster means, and their simulations include unequal cluster sizes. However, their proposed procedure does not accommodate random slopes, which they identify as an important direction for further development.

For these effects, measurement precision depends not only on observation counts but also on cluster-specific predictor designs and residual covariance structures.

2.2.3. Latent measurement modeling: Lai & Liu

In standard linear factor models with common measurement parameters, a given scoring method produces a common score-error covariance across individuals. Random-effect predictions from MLMs, however, can have cluster-specific error covariances, and thus varying levels of precision. To address this, Lai and Liu (2026), building on Lai and Hsiao (2022), provide a method for using random-effect predictions from MLMs as predictors and/or outcomes in structural models. They represent each BLUP vector as an indicator of the latent random-effect vector, with person-specific loadings and error covariance carried into a second-step SEM. This "measurement" model accounts for multivariate shrinkage, correlated errors, and differences in precision across individuals. Structural parameters are then estimated using an iterative method such as maximum likelihood. Their approach parallels the "global structural-after-measurement" approach for structural equation models (Rosseel & Loh, 2024).

As detailed below, our approach shares this measurement model foundation, but transforms the predictions to an additive-error scale, allowing linear structural regressions to be estimated using corrected moments rather than iterative second-step likelihood estimation. Numerical stability provides an additional motivation for considering a non-iterative estimator: Lai and Liu (2026) reported limited convergence in some conditions with low intraclass correlations (ICCs) and small cluster sizes, although penalized first-step estimation improved performance.

3. Current Work

This paper makes three contributions. First, we show that when the shrinkage map is nonsingular, the prior-induced shrinkage in a Gaussian MLM's BLUP can be removed exactly. The inverse-shrinkage transformation recovers the cluster-specific generalized least-squares, or likelihood-only, prediction. Crucially, this prediction, as opposed to the BLUP, equals the underlying random effect plus sampling error uncorrelated with that effect, which provides the basis for classical errors-in-variables correction.

Second, we adapt and evaluate the modified method-of-moments (M-MOM) estimator of Fuller (1987) for the second-step structural model involving these predictions. This estimator accommodates measurement error in predictors and outcomes, including correlated errors and cluster-specific error covariance matrices. The resulting structural calculations avoid iterative likelihood optimization, although they still require sufficient information, nonsingular corrected matrices, and explicit diagnostics in sparse settings.

Third, we use simulation studies to clarify when the role and precision of random-effect predictions affect structural estimation. We compare the M-MOM estimator to naive plug-in analyses, simple corrected-moment estimators, Lai and Liu's (2026) two-stage path analysis, and direct joint models when available. The simulations distinguish random effects used as predictors, outcomes, or both, while varying first-step reliability, random intercept-slope covariance, and cluster-size heterogeneity. We evaluate bias, precision, inferential performance, and convergence to determine how well each approach recovers relationships among the underlying person-specific processes.

4. Statistical Methods

In a Gaussian MLM with known parameters, the conditional mean of a cluster's random effect given its observations is the best linear unbiased predictor (BLUP) (Goldberger, 1962; Henderson, 1973). In practice, replacing the population parameters with their fitted values yields EB predictions, also known as empirical BLUPs (EBLUPs). These predictions balance information from each person's observations with information from the population distribution based on the reliability of a person's observations. While helpful in some contexts, the shrinkage induced to achieve this balance poses problems for downstream structural analyses.

In regression models, accurate coefficients for target effects depend on their underlying variances and covariances, which may not be preserved in EB predictions. Classical measurement error models represent an observed value as a sum of its true value and uncorrelated random error (Fuller, 1987). Instead, EB predictions contain a shrunken version of the true random effect, so their prediction errors generally covary with the effects being predicted. Additionally, shrinkage can differ across individuals because of unequal observation counts, different predictor distributions, or different residual covariances, and can be multivariate when a model includes correlated random effects. These hurdles preclude straightforward strategies for recovering the latent variance, such as subtracting the prediction-error variance from the EB prediction variance. Instead, our approach first removes this prior-induced shrinkage, then corrects the second-step regression for the remaining sampling error. The four sections below develop this approach from a scalar intuition to the general multivariate case. We call our approach Random-Effect Prediction Adjustment for Inference in Regression, or REPAIR.

5.1. Study Design

We validated REPAIR in four simulation studies. The first two studies used a random-effects-as-predictors structural model. We see this as the most straightforward application of our approach since psychological data are often longitudinal, e.g., predicting a distal outcome from earlier trends (McCormick et al., 2026). The studies differed in homogeneity of cluster size: when cluster size is homogeneous (Study 1), our approach mimics a classic moment correction estimator like that of Croon (2002) which assumes homoskedasticity in errors; it is when cluster sizes differ (Study 2) that we hypothesized our approach would show an advantage, since the M-MOM procedure in Fuller (1987) is equipped for heteroskedasticity. Lai and Liu (2026) took a similar approach and observed a similar advantage.

These studies were followed by the random-effect-as-outcome case (Study 3). We see two use-cases for this model structure. First, one may wish to analyze the effect of multiple variables on a single random effect (rather than just a bivariate correlation), which a multiple-regression structural model is equipped to estimate. Second, many linear MLMs with level-two predictors can be estimated under this framework (see Appendix B). Thus, the random-effect-asoutcome case can be considered an approach to MLM estimation that reaps the benefits of other two-step procedures (e.g., isolating misspecification error) laid out in the introduction.

Finally, Study 4 combined the predictor and outcome cases by placing random effects on both sides of the structural equation. The design represents two growth processes, each with its own MLM, in which the random intercept and slope of an earlier process predict the random slope of a later one. See Table 1 for each study's data-generating model.

Across studies, we hypothesized that naively using OLS with predictions would achieve minimal bias when the EB prediction (which we refer to as the "BLUP" for simplicity) is used as the predictor and the likelihood-only prediction is used as the outcome, following from Skrondal and Laake (2001). However, minimal coefficient bias does not itself guarantee well-calibrated standard errors, coverage, or Type I error rates, particularly when precision varies across clusters or first-step parameters are estimated. Thus, we investigated whether measurement error methods (e.g., M-MOM and 2S-PA) would improve both coefficient estimation and inference.

Additionally, in the case of heterogeneous cluster sizes (Study 2), we hypothesized that the measurement error methods would fare far better in terms of bias and other properties than simplified versions that do not account for heterogeneity (e.g., averaging measurement errors over people). The divergence in performance would increase as heterogeneity became more severe. In all cases, we posited that joint modeling would be the best benchmark for performance under adequate sample size, and would agree with the true target asymptotically. Finally, we hypothesized that low-reliability conditions would lead to the largest advantage for methods that account for measurement error over those that do not and the worst cross-method performance. As a corollary, we also expected large method differences for the dual process model (Study 4) when one process had low reliability while the other had high.

5.2. Performance Metrics

We evaluated estimator performance via five primary metrics. First, we computed per-replication relative bias by dividing the raw bias by the true effect. We report mean relative bias values as a percentage for interpretability. Null conditions are excluded from relative bias summaries and instead inform Type I error. Second, we computed the root mean square error (RMSE) of estimates across replications to investigate how well the estimator balances bias and variance simultaneously (James et al., 2013). Third, we investigated 95% coverage and standard error (SE) ratios (estimated SE divided by empirical SE) as measures of inferential performance. The SE ratio is the mean reported standard error divided by the empirical standard deviation of the coefficient estimates from the same replications.

Fourth, we calculated Type I error rates in null (i.e. 𝛾 = 0) conditions. Finally, we looked at convergence, defining converged replication as those producing a finite point estimate and a finite, positive standard error. Model fitting programs often issue warnings in non-convergent cases.

Performance metrics are reported either on (a) the subset of replications for which all methods converged in step two ("shared") or (b) the subset of replications for which the method of interest converged in step two ("method-specific").

The former allows for comparisons across methods on identical datasets, while the latter allows for evaluation of each method's standalone performance conditional on convergence. Metrics reported on scheme (b) also include convergence metrics for complete interpretation. Coverage is conditional on convergence, rather than the probability of obtaining a covering interval across all attempted replications. Additional simulation specifications are given in Appendix E.

Bias/RMSE in error correction methods

Among the shared convergence subset, the error correction methods showed low bias across conditions for high slope reliability, with bias becoming minimal as sample size increased (Figure 2a). For medium slope reliability, both M-MOM variants showed the smallest bias, over most other condition factors, with absolute mean bias generally decreasing over the evaluated sample sizes, although improvement varied by method and intercept-slope correlation.

For low slope reliability, the M-MOM variants tended to produce attenuated coefficient estimates, but improved with larger samples. Conversely, 2S-PA was generally positively biased, marginally improving with sample size (except for -0.5 intercept-slope correlation). MSEM showed smaller absolute bias than M-MOM for small samples, but showed a similar positive bias pattern to 2S-PA for greater ones.

The full distribution of bias metrics qualifies these results: under low reliability, estimates show positive skew that shifts bias means more positive than their median counterparts (Figure 3). Median bias indicates that M-MOM has more negative bias across sample sizes, while 2S-PA and MSEM have less absolute bias with larger sample sizes.

However, IQRs and tail behavior show 2S-PA and MSEM producing much more variable estimates and more extreme outliers. RMSE results match this behavior (see below).

Compared to the shared subset, the method-specific convergence subset revealed two primary differences. First, MSEM showed greater positive bias for -0.5 intercept-slope correlation; second, the 𝛼 step-up variant of M-MOM produced generally lower bias, primarily for larger samples under a -0.5 intercept-slope correlation.

On the shared convergence subset, both M-MOM variants performed similarly in terms of RMSE. M-MOM tended to have lower RMSE than 2S-PA and MSEM across most conditions. In some cases (e.g., small effect sizes), naive OLS with likelihood-only predictors had lower RMSE than M-MOM. Its attenuation reduced variability sufficiently to improve squared-error loss in those conditions, despite biased estimation of the latent coefficient. RMSE tended to decrease with larger samples, particularly for 2S-PA and to a lesser degree MSEM. The presence of outliers in naive OLS with BLUPs led to occasionally extreme RMSE values. In their absence, it showed similar RMSE values to 2S-PA and MSEM. The RMdSE (median) was smaller than RMSE for the M-MOM variants, 2S-PA, and MSEM, indicating that large squared errors contributed disproportionately to RMSE. This was not true for naive OLS with likelihood-only predictions.

Coverage behavior was heterogeneous across methods and conditions (Figure 2b). Naive OLS with likelihood-only predictions showed severe undercoverage across the board, while naive OLS with BLUPs showed undercoverage only under low to medium slope reliability. On the shared convergence subset, low slope reliability generally produced the worst coverage in the focal methods (M-MOM, 2S-PA, and MSEM), especially in M-MOM which showed extreme undercoverage for small samples and large effect sizes. Coverage generally improved with increased slope reliability with rates reaching nominal for M-MOM and 2S-PA.

Naive OLS with BLUPs underestimated standard errors. With likelihood-only predictions, SE ratios were near one despite severe undercoverage, consistent with intervals centered on attenuated coefficients. On the method-specific convergence subset, low slope reliability generated miscalibrated standard errors in the focal methods, while greater reliability produced very good standard errors. Sample size had a notable effect on 2S-PA, especially under low slope reliability, with more clusters leading to better standard errors.

Naive OLS with BLUPs showed systematically inflated Type I error rates which improved with slope reliability.

Naive OLS with likelihood-only predictions showed increasingly inflated Type I error rates with larger samples, but only for non-zero intercept-slope correlations. With correlated predictors, measurement error can induce a nonzero coefficient for a predictor whose true structural effect is zero (Fuller, 1987, p. 55).

On the method-specific convergence subset, both M-MOM variants and 2S-PA showed Type I error rates below the nominal 5% for low reliability, but approached the nominal rate with increasing reliability. For MSEM, Type I error rates were inflated when slope reliability was low and the number of clusters was small, but approached 5% as reliability and the number of clusters increased.

Convergence

Both M-MOM variants converged 100% of the time across all conditions. In contrast, 2S-PA and MSEM had 100% convergence in fewer than half the conditions. 2S-PA especially suffered in low-reliability conditions, though improved with larger samples.

Step 1 MLM singularity was a major delineator of convergence: while M-MOM converged in 100% of singular cases (since likelihood-only predictions do not involve the 𝑮 matrix), 2S-PA converged in only 1% and MSEM converged in 95% of these cases. Overall, 12% of replications produced singularity in step 1, with the majority of those replications being in the low reliability conditions.

6.2. Study 2: Random Effects as Predictors-Heterogeneous Cluster Sizes

For Study 2, we compared the same seven estimators from Study 1, along with two additional methods: an "average measurement" version of the M-MOM procedure (M-MOMA), and Lai and Liu's (2026) 2S-PA with averaging (2S-PAA). Both average-measurement variants replace the cluster-specific BLUP "loading" matrices and sampling-error covariance matrices by their averages. In M-MOMA, predictions are transformed using the inverse average loading matrix, and the average BLUP error covariance is transformed by the same matrix. Study 2 evaluates the commonmeasurement approximation rather than averaging additive-error covariances alone. Summary results are reported in Appendix, Table 4.

Standard vs. "average measurement" methods

The "average measurement" versions of M-MOM and 2S-PA showed differential performance as a function of cluster size distribution, while the non-average variants generally did not. For homogeneous cluster sizes, M-MOMA and 2S-PAA performed nearly identically to their standard counterparts (mean/median relative absolute difference was less than 1% for both). As cluster size heterogeneity increased, however, slope estimates attained via M-MOMA and 2S-PAA became more attenuated. The same pattern manifested across sample sizes.

Among the non-average methods, the bias results for both M-MOM variants, 2S-PA, and MSEM replicated those from Study 1 for homogeneous cluster size distribution conditions. On the shared convergence subset, high slope reliability led to minimal bias across conditions and methods, medium slope reliability revealed an advantage for M-MOM, especially for small samples, and low slope reliability showed the most bias across methods, with M-MOM showing an advantage for larger samples (Figure 4a).

In terms of coverage, the standard and average measurement variants of both M-MOM and 2S-PA diverged the most over differing cluster size distributions and slope reliabilities (Figure 4b). Specifically, average measurement methods showed severe undercoverage for low/medium reliabilities and severely heterogeneous cluster sizes compared to their standard counterparts. The effect worsened with larger samples, with both methods dipping below 80% at 300 clusters on the shared convergence subset.

For homogeneous cluster size distributions, the results of the previous study were replicated among the focal methods. Under heterogeneous cluster sizes, M-MOM and M-MOMS showed undercoverage with low slope reliability and few clusters. Coverage improved with higher reliability and more clusters. At low reliability, 2S-PA generally showed overcoverage across the evaluated cluster counts.

Naive methods

Similar to the homogeneous cluster size study, both naive OLS methods performed poorly in terms of bias. Naive OLS with likelihood-only predictions consistently produced attenuation of the slope coefficient across conditions including slope reliabilities and cluster size distributions. Excluding outliers (which, again, were astronomical in some cases), naive OLS with BLUPs did show minimal bias, even in the severely heterogeneous cluster size conditions. However, naive OLS with BLUPs underestimated standard errors for low slope reliability while naive OLS with likelihood-only predictions generally produced very good standard errors across the board.

Convergence

M-MOM demonstrated 100% convergence across all conditions and regardless of step 1 singularity. At low reliability, 2S-PA showed a median condition convergence rate of 85% and a range of 45% to ∼100% across conditions; MSEM showed a median condition convergence rate of 99% and a range of 96%-100%. At medium slope reliability, both methods had a median condition rate of 100%, though some conditions still showed less than 100%. At high slope reliability, both had perfect convergence.

Other metrics

On the shared convergence subset, cluster size distribution had little effect on RMSE. M-MOM (and the similarly performing 𝛼 step-up M-MOM) demonstrated the lowest RMSE across conditions among the focal methods. 2S-PA and MSEM benefited the most from both increasing sample size and increasing slope reliability. Interestingly, the average measurement versions of M-MOM and 2S-PA sometimes had lower RMSE than their individual measurement counterparts, despite greater attenuation, perhaps reflecting a favorable bias-variance tradeoff under squared-error loss in those conditions. All methods approached the oracle for high slope reliability.

Standard error ratios tended towards one for all standard correction methods and conditions at medium and high slope reliabilities on method-specific convergence subsets. Low slope reliability tended to produce overestimated standard errors. Standard and average measurement variants of M-MOM showed ratios closer to one than 2S-PA, 2S-PAA and MSEM for small samples, but the latter performed well with larger samples.

Cluster size distribution had a noticeable effect on Type I error rates on the method-specific subset. Specifically, cluster size heterogeneity had a noticeable effect on standard M-MOM, especially in low slope reliability where the error rate was inflated under severe heterogeneity and small cluster numbers, and deflated for large sample sizes. M-MOMS improved upon standard M-MOM in some of these conditions (these are comparable since their converged subsets are the same). Conversely, 2S-PA and both average measurement methods demonstrated very low Type I error rates across all conditions when slope reliability was low. Performance mostly improved with greater slope reliability for all methods. Summary results are reported in Appendix, Table 5.

Bias

As hypothesized, naively using OLS with BLUPs led to severe attenuation of the structural coefficient, whereas using likelihood-only predictions led to minimal bias, in line with the results of Skrondal and Laake (2001). Increasing reliability reduced the attenuation effect of using BLUPs, but even for high reliability, there was negative bias (condition means ranged from ∼-20 to -25% for high reliability, compared to ∼-73 to -77% for low reliability).

On the shared convergence subset, the error correction methods showed positive bias for low and medium reliability that reduced with increasing sample size and minimal bias for high reliability. Bias means were nearly identical across methods. On the method-specific subset, both M-MOM variants and direct MLM yielded minimal bias even for low reliability and small samples.

Inference

On the method-specific convergence subset, standard error ratios hovered around one across nearly all methods and conditions. Naive OLS with BLUPs and 2S-PA both showed standard error attenuation for low reliability and small samples. Coverage behavior mostly matched standard error behavior in that methods and conditions with attenuated standard errors also showed slight undercoverage. Naive BLUP showed severe undercoverage across the board, likely due in part to its severe bias.

Convergence

M-MOM and direct MLM had a 100% convergence rate across the board, whereas 2S-PA dipped in convergence for low reliability conditions, and small sample conditions at medium reliability. Naive BLUP sometimes produced an eligible point estimate but not an eligible standard error.

Other metrics

On the shared convergence subset, RMSE was virtually identical across methods using likelihood-only predictions and direct MLM. RMSE tended to decrease with both larger samples and slope reliability, approaching the oracle benchmark. On the method-specific convergence subset, all methods showed slightly inflated Type I error rates for small samples, but approached nominal with larger samples.

7. Discussion

Random-effect predictions enable researchers to explore individual differences in within-person processes, yet treating these predictions as directly observed variables can bias estimates of relationships involving those processes and misrepresent their uncertainty. In this work, we developed and evaluated Random-Effect Prediction Adjustment for Inference in Regression (REPAIR), a two-step method for estimating relationships among underlying person-specific processes and other variables. REPAIR inverts the shrinkage in random-effect predictions to recover likelihoodonly predictions that represent the underlying random effects plus sampling error. It then accounts for this error by incorporating person-specific sampling error covariances into a modified method-of-moments (M-MOM) structural regression. Across four simulation studies, REPAIR generally recovered structural relationships well at medium to high predictor reliability, often had lower RMSE than alternative methods, and converged in every evaluated simulated dataset.

7.1. Key Findings

Our simulations illustrate why shrinkage and sampling error pose distinct challenges. When a random slope was the structural outcome (Study 3), likelihood-only predictions removed the attenuation associated with using the BLUPs directly, and OLS regression yielded favorable estimates and standard errors in the conditions tested. This result is consistent with additive outcome error contributing to residual variation without biasing coefficients when it is uncorrelated with the predictors (Skrondal & Laake, 2001); heterogeneous outcome precision may still require appropriate weighting or standard error adjustments to achieve accurate inference. When random effects were predictors (Studies 1 and 2), however, removing shrinkage alone left substantial measurement-error-related attenuation.

Study 4 reinforced this conclusion: predictor-side reliability largely determined the difficulty of structural estimation when random effects appeared on both sides of the equation.

These findings also qualify the role-specific strategy of using BLUPs as predictors and likelihood-only predictions as outcomes (Skrondal & Laake, 2001). In Study 4, this combination showed favorable median bias in some conditions, but low predictor reliability produced highly skewed estimates and extreme outliers. A nearly unbiased median therefore did not establish stable estimation or accurate inference. The implication is that the suitability of extracted predictions depends on their structural role and precision. REPAIR provides a common additive-error representation and an explicit correction for those properties that addresses measurement error when random effects serve as predictors, outcomes, or both in a structural regression.

Across studies, predictor-side reliability was the most consistent determinant of REPAIR's performance. With random-effect predictors (Studies 1 and 2), medium to high reliability generally yielded small bias, although small samples and correlated random intercepts and slopes remained challenging; at low reliability, bias was larger but generally decreased with sample size. When a random slope served only as the outcome (Study 3), REPAIR showed minimal bias at every reliability level on its method-specific convergence subset. With random effects on both sides of the equation (Study 4), high predictor reliability supported small bias regardless of outcome reliability.

Inferential performance generally followed a similar pattern. Medium to high predictor reliability usually supported well-calibrated standard errors and approximately nominal confidence-interval coverage, whereas low reliability produced less consistent results, particularly in small samples. However, coverage and Type I error did not always deteriorate together. In Study 1 (predictor, homogeneous cluster sizes), for example, REPAIR's undercoverage for nonzero effects coexisted with conservative null rejection rates at low reliability. This distinction matters because intervals centered on biased estimates can miss the true effect even when standard errors are not underestimated.

Study 2 (predictor, heterogeneous cluster sizes) further showed that preserving differences in precision helped REPAIR accommodate unequal observation counts. With person-specific sampling error covariances, bias and coverage were generally less sensitive to cluster size heterogeneity than with the average measurement variants. Those variants became increasingly attenuated as heterogeneity increased: under severe heterogeneity and low or medium reliability, their coverage fell below 80% at a sample size of 300. Increasing sample size did not compensate for the averaging approximation in these conditions.

These findings support retaining person-specific shrinkage information when observation counts differ, consistent with Lai and Liu (2026). However, they do not imply that every correction using an average error covariance is inadequate. M-MOMA used the inverse average BLUP loading matrix to transform each prediction and the average BLUP sampling-error covariance (as in (Lai & Liu, 2026)). This differs from first obtaining individual likelihood-only predictions and then averaging their error covariances, an alternative not evaluated here. Person-specific correction also did not eliminate all difficulties under low reliability and small samples but did mitigate the deterioration associated with heterogeneous observation counts.

7.2. Comparison to Alternative Methods

When the structural relationship could be represented by a conventional cross-level interaction, REPAIR performed comparably to direct MLM estimation. In Study 3 (outcome), both approaches achieved complete convergence, yielded minimal bias on their method-specific convergence subsets, and showed comparable standard errors, coverage, and RMSE. REPAIR also compared favorably with MSEM, a joint-model benchmark for relationships involving latent random effects. In several low-and medium-reliability settings, REPAIR produced less variable estimates and lower RMSE, despite MSEM sometimes showing smaller absolute bias. These findings demonstrate that a regularized two-step method can offer favorable finite-sample performance relative to a correctly specified joint model.

Both REPAIR and 2S-PA offer two-step approaches that account for shrinkage and person-specific measurement error. Whereas 2S-PA represents EB predictions as indicators of latent random effects and estimates their structural relationships through iterative likelihood estimation, REPAIR transforms the predictions to an additive-error scale and uses regularized moment calculations to estimate the structural regression. This difference translated into substantial advantages for REPAIR in difficult conditions. For example, in the low-reliability conditions of Study 1 (predictor, homogeneous cluster sizes), REPAIR achieved 100% convergence compared with 65.5% for 2S-PA. Moreover, even on shared convergence subsets, REPAIR often had lower RMSE. REPAIR therefore offers an alternative that combines the flexibility of two-step estimation with favorable numerical stability and estimation accuracy.

These advantages nevertheless require qualification when predictor reliability is low and samples are small. REPAIR's greatest difficulties with bias and confidence interval coverage generally occurred in the same conditions that caused the most convergence failures among competing methods. Although REPAIR continued to produce estimates, they could remain biased and their confidence intervals could miss the true effect more frequently than desired. These problems generally diminished as sample size increased, although larger samples did not ensure nominal coverage in every condition. Thus, REPAIR's numerical stability and frequently lower RMSE are useful advantages, but neither alone establishes that its confidence intervals are well calibrated.

7.3. Practical Recommendations

REPAIR is a useful option when researchers wish to estimate structural relationships using predictions from separately fitted multilevel models, or to examine several structural models while holding the level 1 models fixed. The latter is a motivation shared with other two-step latent-variable methods (Levy, 2023; Rosseel & Loh, 2024). A direct MLM or other joint model remains an attractive option when it readily represents the scientific question. The choice should therefore reflect how readily each approach accommodates the intended MLM and structural model.

For any structural model involving random effects, whether joint or two-step, we recommend checking the adequacy of the first-step MLM and the reliability of the individual effects (e.g., via the posterior reliability in Appendix A; see also Liu et al., 2021; Neubauer et al., 2020). Our simulations offer initial planning benchmarks for REPAIR. With randomeffect predictors, posterior slope reliabilities of .50 and .80 generally supported favorable performance, although small samples and correlated random effects remained challenging in some conditions. At a reliability of .25, analyses with 30-50 clusters were particularly vulnerable to bias and poor coverage, and performance improved with more clusters.

In Study 4, for example, REPAIR generally showed minimal bias with 100 or more clusters, though this depended on reliability and effect size and is not a universal threshold. Researchers should use these benchmarks to select candidate designs and then evaluate expected bias, coverage, and power through simulations tailored to their analysis.

The number of observations needed within each person depends on how much information those observations provide about the effects used as predictors. Our simulations varied cluster size and calibrated reliability separately, so they do not identify a universal minimum number of repeated observations. Researchers should instead assess whether their planned design is likely to yield adequately reliable effects. When predictor reliability is low, collecting more observations per person or increasing the informative variation in the within-person predictor may improve precision.

For example, with the same number of observations, a person's linear rate of change can be estimated more precisely when assessments span the period of interest than when they are concentrated near a single time point.

We have also developed the repair R package, which fits step 1 models with common R backends (e.g., lme4; et al., 2015) and step 2 models with our M-MOM implementation, using standard formula syntax and C++ for computation. The package has been submitted to CRAN and will be available publicly prior to publication of this manuscript.

7.4. Limitations and Future Directions

Several questions remain for future research. Our simulations evaluated correctly specified Gaussian MLMs and linear structural regressions. Although robustness to misspecification is an important motivation for two-step methods (Bollen et al., 2007; Robitzsch, 2022; Rosseel & Loh, 2024), our studies did not examine this. Future work should test misspecified mean and covariance structures, informative observation patterns, and departures from distributional assumptions.

We also focused on MLMs having homoskedastic, uncorrelated level 1 residuals. The REPAIR equations already accommodate more general residual covariance structures through 𝑹 𝑗 , which enters both the likelihood-only prediction and its sampling-error covariance (Equations 4.6 and 4.7). Evaluating performance with heteroskedastic or serially correlated residuals would therefore be a natural extension supporting longitudinal applications.

The choice of regularization for the second-step moment matrices warrants further study. Our M-MOM implementation used the regularization proposed by Fuller (1987) with the minimum constant, 𝑝 + 1, keeping the correction close to its unregularized form. We also developed and evaluated a step-up procedure that chose the smallest constant that yielded a well-conditioned estimating matrix. The variants generally performed similarly, with selected improvements for the step-up variant, including bias in some Study 1 conditions. Alternative choices proposed (e.g., Bogaert et al., 2023; Wall & Amemiya, 2000) could be evaluated for their effects on bias, RMSE, and confidence-interval coverage.

We further note that in its current implementation, REPAIR does not account for "error in the errors": Our secondstep covariance calculation treats the supplied measurement-error covariances and selected regularization constants as fixed. Related two-step methods account for first-step estimation uncertainty through corrected standard errors (e.g., Bogaert et al., 2023; Wall & Amemiya, 2000). Future research should examine when these adjustments materially improve inference using REPAIR.

Finally, REPAIR's prediction-level correction requires each within-person random-effects design to have full column rank. This is a limitation relative to joint models that can combine partial individual information when the overall model remains identified. It is distinct from a singular estimated random-effect covariance, which does not prevent direct likelihood-only calculation. Future extensions could retain estimable combinations of effects from incomplete individual records and recover structural moments by combining information across people (e.g., Botosaru & Powell, 2026).

8. Conclusion

REPAIR provides a two-step framework for structural regression with random effects as predictors, outcomes, or both.

Separating multilevel models from structural estimation allows researchers to retain models suited to their clustered data and use the resulting predictions to address relationships among underlying person-or cluster-specific effects and other variables. This flexibility may be particularly useful for understanding relationships among person-specific processes that are measured across different tasks, settings, or timescales. REPAIR enables researchers to use random-effect predictions in structural regression while explicitly accounting for their shrinkage and sampling error.
