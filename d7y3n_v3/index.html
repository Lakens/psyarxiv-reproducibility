Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. Abstract

A century from now, the computational framework at the center of psychology will no longer organize the discipline. As used here, "computational" names a broad information-processing ontology: meaning is specified in advance, rather than generated through the activity, history, and situation of living beings. This assumption fits engineered systems, but it misdescribes living ones. This article argues that three converging shifts are destabilizing that framework. The first undermines the computational account of inheritance: organisms develop through activity within configurations of constraints reconstituted across generations, not through the readout of genetic programs. The second displaces its account of psychological structure: psychological phenomena unfold as trajectories of coordination rather than as stable traits, with reliability and variability arising from the same generative process. The third sharpens the first two: artificial intelligence systems produce fluent performance without embodiment, developmental history, or any stake in the world. This contrast reveals what living organization contributes. The psychology implied by these shifts treats development as foundational, not as a subfield. It understands psychological constructs as patterns of activity that persons sustain over time, and is better positioned to address a century of changing human ecologies. This future psychology will be a developmental science of living sense-making.

[div-01]

article responds to the special issue's invitation not by predicting which specific ideas will prevail, but by identifying the structural conditions under which the currently dominant framework gives way. The computational framework that organizes contemporary psychology rests on assumptions that are under increasing empirical and conceptual pressure, and the forces driving its reorganization are already operating, whether or not the discipline has registered them.

What follows traces what these pressures contest, what they make available, and what kind of psychology they imply.

Referring to the current framework as "computational" is meant to designate a broad information-processing ontology, not the use of formal or mathematical tools. Its core commitment is that meaning is specified in advance, rather than generated through the activity, history, and situation of living beings. This ontology has shaped theorizing and empirical work across much of the discipline. In the study of cognition, it appeared as the framing of thought as the processing of information, the content of which is settled in advance. The same architecture has been extended to social and emotional life, where cues and stimuli are treated as already meaningful inputs to be decoded or appraised by internal mechanisms. In developmental research, related assumptions appear in models that treat genes and environments as separable sources of information whose significance is given independently of the individual's activity.

These are not separate commitments but facets of a single ontology. On this view, signals with fixed meanings are already present in the world, available to be received; genes or other purported codes carry preassigned significance; and internal mechanisms operate on inputs whose identity is defined independently of the organism's history.

Although it has been productive, the computational picture is increasingly difficult to sustain as an account of psychological life. In the laboratory, what a response will be taken to mean is fixed in advance by the experiment, not by what the organism's own activity makes matter. A similar move runs through developmental explanation. We begin with a robust outcome and then reconstruct the path that leads toward it. Reconstructing a path is not itself the problem; the trouble begins when the endpoint, once named, is treated as though it had been present from the start, directing the process toward itself. Development is then made to look like the delivery of something already specified, not the generative, historically situated activity of a living system. The problem, then, is not confined to one theoretical vocabulary. It is the same problem in different forms: the meaning of behavior and the form of a developmental outcome are both treated as if they preceded the living processes through which they are generated. The analysis that follows centers on development, because developmental explanation makes especially clear what is obscured when meanings, forms, and psychological phenomena are treated as prior to the processes that generate them.

Three empirical and conceptual shifts now contest the computational commitment and illustrate the shape of the alternative. First, evolutionary and developmental biology have challenged the view of development as the execution of a genetic program. Developmental systems theory and the Extended Evolutionary Synthesis have recast inheritance and development as the patterned activity of organisms, not the readout of preformed instructions.

Second, idiographic and within-person methods have revealed that psychological phenomena unfold as trajectories of coordination rather than as stable traits that exist outside the activity that sustains them. Third, advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have demonstrated that fluent behavioral performance can be generated without a living being to produce it, exposing the assumption that the surface form of behavior carries its meaning in the performance itself. The last shift plays a different role from the first two: rather than setting a further boundary, it sharpens the distinction between performance that can be simulated and sense-making that belongs to living systems.

Conceptual resources for moving beyond the computational framework exist, although they have been articulated in partially separate literatures. Developmental systems theory locates causation in the configurations of constraints through which organism-environment systems are reconstructed across generations, dissolving any partition between genetic and environmental contributions (Lickliter & Moore, 2023; Oyama et al., 2001). Process-relational developmental science highlights the inseparability of person and context and emphasizes circular over linear causality (Overton & Lerner, 2014; Overton, 2015; Witherington, 2011). Developmental research within the sociocultural tradition has shown that human cognition is not formed within the individual before meeting culture but is instead constituted through participation in shared practices (Cole, 1996; Rogoff, 2003; Vygotsky, 1978). Enactive approaches frame cognition as sense-making: the activity through which a living being, acting out of its own conditions of viability, brings forth a world that matters to it (Thompson, 2007; Varela et al., 1991). These perspectives differ in emphasis and explanatory ambition, but each contests the assumption that psychological life can be specified apart from the historical, relational, and embodied processes through which it is sustained. They do not resolve into a tidier replacement for the currently dominant ontology, but they provide resources for an account of mind and development that is more biologically coherent and more exacting about what counts as explanation. Mind is not a system processing information about a world it stands apart from; psychological life is the sense-making activity of living beings within conditions of life that are historically contingent and continually reconstructed.

Basis of the Forecast

This forecast rests on theoretical extrapolation from established empirical and conceptual work. Findings and frameworks from developmental biology and developmental systems theory, from idiographic methods, and from the distinction between living and artificial systems are treated as convergent evidence that narrows the range of coherent ontological positions the discipline can adopt. Two of these set boundary conditions on psychology's viable futures, identifying empirical constraints that any coherent successor framework must respect; the third, the contrast between living and artificial systems, serves as a diagnostic that sharpens what those conditions require.

The forecast is further grounded in Jablonka's (2023) extension of developmental systems theory and Waddington's epigenetic landscape metaphor to sociocultural systems, including scientific thought styles. On this account, theoretical persistence and change can be understood as products of self-sustaining networks of assumptions, practices, methods, training, and institutional arrangements. The century-scale horizon suits the analysis because the argument is not about the specific path psychology will take but about the conditions under which a disciplinary attractor becomes unstable. The forces identified here are already operating, and a century is sufficient time for their effects to alter the landscape of psychological science.

Rethinking Development

The first boundary condition concerns the causal vocabulary of development. For much of the twentieth century, both biology and psychology saw development as the sum of separate internal and external influences, with genes specifying form and the environment supplying modification. Developmental psychology adopted this framing, treating innate tendencies and environmental inputs as separable contributions. This approach came to dominate the field, even though it was not the only available position. Piaget (1971) had argued that the structures of cognition are actively constructed through the organism's continuous interaction with its environment, although mainstream developmental psychology moved away from even this moderate commitment.

The assumption of separability is now being contested with new force, as developments in evolutionary and developmental biology give older critiques of genetic determinism and additive causation new empirical traction (Gottlieb, 2007; Lickliter & Moore, 2023). Taken together, the work reviewed below supports a different characterization of development: not as the execution of a genetic program or as an additive combination of separate internal and external causes, but as the activity of a developing organism within a structured field of constraints operating at multiple scales. The term "constraints" is used here to mean not external restrictions or determining causes, but enabling and limiting conditions that shape a field of possible activity, making some forms of organization possible, probable, or stable, while making others less likely (Juarrero, 2023).

Developmental biology provides a foundation for this shift. Morphogenesis involves the coupled activity of gene regulatory networks, tissue mechanics, chemical gradients, cellular architecture, and bioelectrical signaling, rather than proceeding through genetic specification alone (Forgacs & Newman, 2005; Newman & Bhat, 2009; Whited & Levin, 2019). Nor is this activity reducible to physical self-organization alone: cells sense, move, adhere, signal, and modify their surroundings, so that morphogenesis depends on agent-like cellular activity as well as on material and biochemical constraints. Gene expression itself is context-sensitive and relational, shaped by cellular, physiological, epigenetic, and broader developmental conditions (Jablonka & Noble, 2019; E. F. Keller, 2011). On this view, DNA is not an instruction manual for development but one developmental resource among others, available only within the organized activity and conditions through which living form is produced (Nijhout, 1990; Noble, 2015; Oyama, 2000).

The reframing of DNA as a developmental resource sits within a broader transformation in the life sciences. The Modern Synthesis, with its emphasis on gene-centered accounts of evolutionary change, continues to organize much of evolutionary thought. But across multiple fronts, including developmental systems theory, ecological developmental biology, developmental plasticity research, and the Extended Evolutionary Synthesis, biologists and philosophers of biology increasingly treat gene-centered accounts as incomplete, emphasizing the developmental and ecological processes that such models leave out (Jablonka & Lamb, 2014; Laland et al., 2015; West-Eberhard, 2003). Psychology often inherited its assumptions about development and evolution from twentieth-century evolutionary theory, without tracking how contested those assumptions have since become.

Developmental systems theory provides a broader vocabulary for these insights, situating them within an integrated account of inheritance and development. Organisms inherit not only DNA but a suite of developmental resources, from cellular organization and ecological conditions to, in some lineages, social arrangements and cultural practices. What persists across generations is not a set of instructions but the life cycle itself, reconstituted each time from reliably available resources (Oyama et al., 2001). A stable developmental outcome can therefore recur without being specified in advance. It recurs because living systems actively maintain and rebuild forms of organization from one moment to the next and from one generation to the next.

A reliable outcome can be described once it has stabilized, but that description is not the process that produced it. A developmental account explains how forms of organization come to hold, not by assuming they were already encoded, but by tracing the activity through which they are assembled, sustained, and sometimes remade. That activity is never confined to a single phase of life or explanatory frame: cellular, bodily, ecological, and social constraints bear on development simultaneously, but their significance depends on how the organism's activity brings them into relation. In acting, the organism alters bodily dynamics, reorganizes local ecological arrangements, and reshapes patterns of interaction, so that each emerging form of coordination becomes part of the constraint structure that shifts the space of possibility for subsequent development, including what can be reconstructed across generations (Gilbert & Epel, 2015; Lewontin, 2000; Oyama et al., 2001).

In the human case, what is reconstructed is not a biological substrate to which culture is then added: the two have no prior existence apart to be combined. The slow maturation of the human cortex presupposes the cooperative caregiving arrangements that sustain a long childhood, and the human gut has been shaped by the food-preparation practices that altered its evolutionary trajectory. Cultural practices, social routines, and institutional arrangements are therefore developmental resources in the same sense as cellular organization and ecological conditions: they are reliably present across generations, they constrain what is possible without determining what occurs, and the child's growing participation in them reshapes the very constraints that shaped that participation (Cole, 1996; Rogoff, 2003; Vygotsky, 1978).

Psychological Phenomena as Trajectories

A second boundary condition follows from this reframing of development as a central form of explanation rather than a specialized domain within psychology: psychological phenomena unfold as trajectories rather than as stable attributes that the individual possesses apart from the activity that sustains them. Yet for decades, psychological constructs have been treated as enduring properties, whether framed as traits, mechanisms, or latent structures. These assumptions were built into the field's dominant methods, organized around cross-sectional designs, group comparisons, and variable-centered modeling, which obscure the temporal organization of psychological life. The same habit splits what is in fact a single process into separate sources: species-typical reliability is explained through evolutionary or genetic programs and variability through individual differences, when reliability and variability instead arise together, through histories of activity within changing configurations of constraints. Crosscultural developmental research makes this double aspect empirically visible (Henrich et al., 2010; H. Keller, 2007). Human experience, behavior, and cognition are sustained and reorganized through activity, not possessed as fixed properties apart from it.

If psychological phenomena are sustained through activity rather than possessed as stable attributes, then methods that compare fixed quantities across persons will misrepresent them.

Within-person processes often differ fundamentally from between-person associations, and group-level regularities cannot be assumed to describe any actual individual (Molenaar, 2004; Nesselroade & Molenaar, 2010). Ergodic assumptions, long taken for granted in psychological modeling, do not hold for developing organisms whose trajectories depend on changing constraints, histories of interaction, and variable responses to perturbations. Recent work in longitudinal methodology makes this problem concrete: different statistical representations of change applied to the same data can produce contradictory inferences, revealing that the field's modeling choices embed unexamined assumptions about what developmental change is (McCormick & Bauer, 2024).

Intensive longitudinal and person-specific approaches show how patterns of activity develop and stabilize. As an example, Ram and Gerstorf (2009) developed a framework for treating within-person variability as developmentally informative rather than as noise. They showed how fluctuations on short timescales (minutes, days, weeks) carry information about individuals' capacities for change and about the regulatory processes through which those capacities are exercised. In turn, these short-timescale dynamics are coupled with long-term developmental change rather than separate from it. Periods of instability often precede transitions, while moments of stability reflect temporary alignments between the individual and the constraints shaping their activity.

A related finding from research on motor development illustrates this trajectory-based view, building on work that sees skills as emerging from the coupling of body, task, and environment (Thelen & Smith, 1994). The onset of walking is often treated as the maturational unfolding of an internal timetable, yet walking emerges through changing relations among bodily capacities, surfaces, opportunities for movement, and caregiver practices. Once walking stabilizes, it reorganizes the child's relation to the environment: objects, distances, people, and risks become available in new ways. The same posture-dependence shows in what fails to carry over: infants who have learned to avoid a drop-off while crawling do not simply transfer that avoidance when they begin to walk (Adolph & Hoch, 2019). An information-processing interpretation would expect transfer, since the infant has acquired knowledge about drop-offs. A constraint-based account instead makes sense of the lack of transfer: what was organized was not a stored representation of danger but a mode of coordination between perception and action within a specific postural configuration. On a new postural base, that perception-action relation must be assembled again. This boundary condition undermines the idea that psychological constructs can be understood as stable traits or mechanisms (Richters, 2021). A trait-like measure captures only a statistical summary, often averaging over heterogeneous processes, situational contingencies, and developmental dynamics. Such measures may aid prediction under restricted circumstances, but they do not reveal the organization through which psychological life unfolds; they treat abstraction as if it were explanation. A trajectory perspective instead highlights how persons sustain coherence over time through bodily, behavioral, interpersonal, and cultural activity. Some patterns become more stable than others; when constraints shift or new possibilities arise, persons reorganize their activity.

Living and Artificial Systems

The two boundary conditions considered so far clarify what a science of living organization must explain. The rise of artificial intelligence (AI) systems capable of reproducing the surface forms of human behavior, without possessing the underlying organization that generated them, sharpens this clarification. The example of AI is not the deepest source of the reorientation proposed here; the deeper sources lie in development, living organization, and trajectories of activity. AI instead functions as a diagnostic pressure that makes precise what the two preceding boundary conditions require.

The distinction at issue is not between humans and a particular technology but between systems whose activity is grounded in their own conditions of viability and systems whose activity is not. Enactive cognitive science has theorized it in general terms, framing living systems as self-producing and self-maintaining, their activity answerable to their own continued existence rather than to externally imposed design objectives (Di Paolo, 2005; Maturana & Varela, 1980; Thompson, 2007; Varela et al., 1991). On this framing, the distinction does not depend on how a specific engineered system is built or on how capable it becomes. Current large language models (LLMs) are a case in point: they reproduce fluent surface forms of language without the organization that supports reasoning, world knowledge, and functional language use in humans (Mahowald et al., 2024). For a discipline that has long treated behavioral and linguistic performance as its primary evidence for cognitive structure, this dissociation forces a choice. If fluent, contextually appropriate, apparently meaningful output can be generated without embodiment, developmental history, or any stake in the world, then either such outputs are sufficient evidence for mind, or psychology must distinguish behavioral form from sensemaking as the activity of a living being grounded in its own stake in continuing to exist.

The present case of LLMs shows where the resemblance comes from. The training corpora on which these models have been built are not neutral data: they are the accumulated external traces of embodied, culturally situated, developmentally shaped human activity. The statistical regularities that these systems learn to reproduce are the shadows cast by living organization onto the surface of language. The system captures the shadows, but the substance remains outside its reach, because meaning arises in what living beings do as they act within the histories that shape them. Even as artificial systems change in ways that cannot be foreseen, the structural distinction holds. Engineered systems are not the kind of entities whose activity is grounded in their own continued existence, and they remain categorically different from living beings, regardless of their behavioral sophistication.

A field that models cognition on engineered performance rather than on the living organization that gives behavior its meaning will continue to lose its grip on its own object of study. The pressure toward this trajectory will grow as AI systems become more capable and more deeply woven into the environments in which human development unfolds. Meeting this issue will be a major disciplinary challenge of the coming century: psychology must make the distinction between living sense-making and engineered performance central to its explanatory program, rather than treating it as a philosophical aside.

A Psychology Grounded in Living Organization: Implications for the Century Ahead

The two boundary conditions, together with the diagnostic contrast between living and artificial systems, converge on a shared conclusion: psychological life arises through the organization of a living system acting in a temporally extended world. Each challenges the computational ontology in a different register: that the form of a developmental outcome, the stability of a psychological trait, or the meaning of behavior could be specified without regard to the activity that generates it. They constrain the form a scientifically coherent discipline can take, and they clarify that psychological explanation must begin from the embodied, developmental, and relational processes through which activity becomes meaningful for living beings. This places meaning in the activity of living beings rather than in anything given in advance, and a psychology adequate to that claim needs to explain how such activity constitutes meaning.

The persistence of psychology's computational framework is not simply a matter of intellectual inertia. Dominant theoretical commitments persist through alignments among assumptions, methods, training programs, and empirical questions, producing a robust attractor. Jablonka (2023) applies a developmental systems analysis to the gene-centered Modern Synthesis and its resistance to the Extended Evolutionary Synthesis, drawing on Waddington's epigenetic landscape metaphor to characterize the dominant framework as a canalized attractor sustained by mutually reinforcing assumptions, methods, and institutional incentives. The computational framework in psychology presents a structurally parallel case: its attractor is robust because theoretical challenges can be incorporated without altering the assumptions, methods, and institutional arrangements that sustain it. This is why earlier challenges to the computational framework did not fundamentally reorganize psychology. The resources reviewed at the outset of this article could be deflected or absorbed in weakened form: embodiment became an influence on cognition, context became a moderator of behavior, sociocultural participation became an external setting for development, and development itself became a background condition. Jablonka's (2023) analysis frames shifts in attractors as long-running processes, beginning when the supports that sustain a framework start to change and unfolding over decades. The premise of the current paper is that the present pressures differ from earlier challenges because they are altering those supports. Shifts in developmental and evolutionary biology are eroding the biological credibility of instruction-based accounts of development and inheritance, and with it the broader assumption that living organization can be explained by prespecified information. The emergence of practical idiographic methods is weakening the methodological reliance on pre-given traits, mechanisms, and group-level abstractions by making trajectories of activity increasingly tractable. The maturation of AI systems that generate fluent performance without living organization creates an existential disciplinary pressure that no amount of theoretical accommodation can resolve. Together, these forces are not simply arguments for change; they alter the conditions under which arguments operate. Over a centuryscale horizon, alongside generational turnover, these are the kinds of structural forces under which a canalized attractor can shift.

A psychology reshaped by these pressures would look quite different from its current form. Rather than beginning from a taxonomy of cognitive mechanisms and the experimental designs that isolate them, the field would characterize developmental trajectories and ask how patterns of coordination form, stabilize, and reorganize across the lifespan within particular cultural and ecological niches. Students entering the field would be trained in dense longitudinal and person-specific methods; their models would formalize constraint structures, not main effects; and their explanatory targets would be the conditions that sustain or disrupt human meaning-making. Memory, emotion, intelligence, and identity would be studied less as internal structures to be measured than as patterns of activity that persons sustain across time.

Neuroscience would be transformed correspondingly. The brain would be no less central, though what it means to study it would have changed. Rather than measuring neural responses to stimuli the experimenter has selected, neuroscientists would study ongoing neural activity as part of a developing organism acting in the world (Buzsáki, 2019). This would require methods suited to measuring such activity in freely moving individuals who are actively structuring their own environments and to studying how the brain's intrinsic dynamics are coupled with the dynamics of body and environment. The conventional partition between intrinsically driven and experience-driven aspects of brain development would give way to an account of neural organization as one strand of a developing organism-environment system (Marshall, 2024).

The commitments outlined here also shape what psychology can offer in a century of changing human ecologies. Climate disruption, mass migration, demographic aging, and digital technologies are altering the settings in which people sustain and remake their lives across generations. Such a discipline would be positioned to take this seriously in a way the current one is not. It would allow psychologists to study how lives remain coherent while being transformed by changing conditions, rather than treating stability and change as separate problems. Its trajectory-based methods would make actual lives, unfolding under altered ecological and social arrangements, a tractable object of study. And its grounding in the distinction between living and engineered systems would keep psychological science answerable to its subject matter.

The future envisioned here is not one in which psychology adds development, embodiment, culture, or biology to its existing framework. It is one in which psychology redefines its object, and therefore the questions it can ask. The central question would no longer be where a capacity is located, which factor predicts an outcome, or whether an engineered performance matches a human one. The field would ask how ways of perceiving, acting, remembering, feeling, and relating become organized through development, and how they are sustained or remade as conditions change. Psychological phenomena are not informationprocessing operations inside individuals, stable attributes abstracted from groups, or surface performances detached from the processes that make them matter. They are patterns of activity through which persons with histories, dependencies, and stakes in what happens to them sustain meaning over time. A psychology adequate to the next century will be a developmental science of living sense-making.
