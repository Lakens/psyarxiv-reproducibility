How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Debates about reform in sport and exercise psychology research largely center on the shortcomings of null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) and the binary conclusions it produces. We argue that significance testing is not the main culprit. NHST matches our theories, which typically use boxes and arrows to state that an effect exists and in which direction. So changing the statistical tool we use does not change much of what we learn, because it does not alter the questions we pursue or the specificity of the predictions we make: the answers we seek and settle for remain yes or no. The good news is that sport and exercise psychology is in a strong position to tackle more informative questions: We have established that many effects exist, collected a wealth of quantitatively rich data, and have sophisticated methods at hand. What is missing is a place in our theories to put these numbers. We outline three increasingly ambitious levers to transform this foundation into a more quantitative body of knowledge: (1) treating effect sizes and their precision as primary targets of study planning and inference, rather than as decorative statistics reported alongside the p-value; (2) synthesizing evidence cumulatively, so that established findings such as expected effect sizes constrain the next study; and (3) formulating theories themselves more quantitatively, for instance regarding the shape of a relationship or how quickly an effect unfolds, so that they make more precise predictions. Doing so benefits scientists and practitioners alike. Theories that make precise predictions can actually be wrong, which is what makes confirmations meaningful. A focus on cumulative effect size estimation also means that every well-conducted study teaches us something irrespective of its p-value, including the questions practitioners care about most: how much an intervention is likely to help, for whom, and when.

Quantitative data, qualitative answers "[T]he so-called test of statistical significance does not in fact answer a quantitative, scientific question. … [B]y using Fisherian methods some of the putatively quantitative sciences have slipped into asking qualitatively whether there exists an effect…. They have ceased asking the scientific question of 'How much is the effect?'" -Ziliak & McCloskey, The Cult of Statistical Significance (2008 p. 4f) Sport and exercise psychology is predominantly a quantitative science. Its research operationalizes psychological processes as measurable variables and collects rich numerical data through psychometric instruments (e.g., questionnaires, rating scales), behavioral and performance metrics (e.g., reaction time, accuracy), and physiological and neuronal recordings (e.g., heart rate, brain activity). These data are then analyzed with sophisticated statistical models (Biddle et al., 2001; Myers et al., 2018). This profile matches the conventional definition of the quantitative paradigm, which focuses on methodological and analytical properties of research (Creswell & Creswell, 2023).

As the opening quote highlights, using quantitative machinery does not by itself make science quantitative. What matters is whether the questions we ask, and the answers we seek, are quantitative too. In sport and exercise psychology, our research questions and the conclusions we draw from our data are often merely qualitative, however, revolving around whether an effect exists. The result is a large body of yesor-no answers that do not yet reflect the quantitative nuance achievable by numerical measurement and analysis. Establishing whether effects exist is an important first step, and one the field has taken successfully in many areas. This puts us in an excellent position to take the next one: asking how large these effects are and how precisely we can estimate them, and feeding these estimates into theoretical foundations that match the quantitative nature of our methods.

Significance testing: A binary gate on inference

To take this next step, we first need to understand where the gap between our quantitative methods and our qualitative conclusions comes from. One important factor is the field's reliance on statistical significance as its primary tool for scientific inquiry. Under the dominant framework of null hypothesis significance testing (NHST), numerical data are used to compute a p-value: the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. If this probability falls below a conventional threshold, the null hypothesis is rejected; if it does not, the null hypothesis is not rejected.

Criticism of this procedure as a "statistical ritual" is almost as old as the framework itself (for reviews, see Cohen, 1994; Gigerenzer et al., 1989). Even the architects of the frameworks that were later conflated into NHST would likely have objected to its contemporary application, in particular the rigid adherence to conventional thresholds and the idea that a single procedure suits every inferential goal (Fisher, 1956; Pearson, 1962). In the words of Fisher (1956, p. 42), the pioneer of statistical significance testing, "no scientific worker has a fixed level of significance at which from year to year, and in all circumstances, he rejects hypotheses; he rather gives his mind to each particular case in the light of his evidence and his ideas." More recent work has broadened this critique by emphasizing that p-values offer limited information on their own (Cumming, 2014; Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016), are widely misunderstood even by researchers with extensive statistical training (Goodman, 2008; Greenland, 2017; McShane & Gal, 2016 , 2017), provide fertile ground for questionable research practices (Simmons et al., 2011), and vary so strongly across exact replications that a single p-value is a poor guide to what a repetition would yield (Halsey et al., 2015).

These criticisms have resonated within sport and exercise psychology for quite some time (e.g., Andersen et al., 2007; Bernards et al., 2017; Büsch & Strauß, 2016; Thomas et al., 1991), and several problems that may partly stem from an overreliance on NHST have been documented, including p-hacking (D. N. Borg et al., 2023), unrealistically high rates of hypothesis confirmation (Büttner et al., 2020; Twomey et al., 2021), and disappointingly low replication rates (Mesquida et al., 2022 (Mesquida et al., , 2023;; Murphy et al., 2025).

As important and worth discussing as these concerns are, they tend to draw our attention away from a much more fundamental problem. When computed and reported properly, p-values behave exactly as they were designed to (Greenland, 2019; Lakens, 2021). The problem lies instead in their ritualistic use, in particular the widespread tendency to dichotomize substantive conclusions based on statistical significance (Amrhein et al., 2017; Hoekstra et al., 2006; McShane & Gal, 2016).

The distinction between p-values and their use matters because dichotomization would persist even if all the criticisms mentioned above were addressed. Suppose every researcher understood p-values correctly, chose thresholds deliberately and in advance, and avoided questionable practices thanks to well-designed incentives. The output of the NHST procedure would still be binary. However extensive the dataset and however sophisticated the model, the analysis culminates in a decision about whether the result is statistically significant. The information loss is considerable:

Whatever precision a study achieves, whether through hundreds, thousands, or millions of observations, its central conclusion reduces to a single bit of information.

To be clear, such a formal decision procedure serves a legitimate and important function, as it allows scientific claims to be scrutinized and adjudicated (Tunç et al., 2023). But if a binary decision were all we took away from our data, the whole enterprise would be remarkably inefficient.

Recognizing this, statisticians (McShane et al., 2019) and researchers across applied disciplines (Amrhein et al., 2019) have called for statistical significance to be abandoned as the primary criterion for inference and for evidence to be interpreted on a continuum, by estimating effect sizes and their uncertainty. The primary inferential goal then shifts from hypothesis testing to estimation, a shift that has gained traction across sport science (Abt et al., 2020; Elkins et al., 2022; Schinke et al., 2021; Winter et al., 2014) and is equally warranted in sport and exercise psychology. Moving toward estimation is an important step. On its own, however, it is unlikely to close the gap between our quantitative methods and our qualitative conclusions, for reasons we turn to next.

Estimates end up as decorative statistics

Consider a typical sport and exercise psychology study. An investigator compares a motivational intervention with a control condition on a cycling time-to-exhaustion task.

An a priori power analysis indicates that a sample of 128 participants is required to achieve 80% power to detect a statistically significant difference in time to exhaustion between conditions in an independent-samples t-test, using the default medium effect size of d = 0.5 and the assumption of equal allocation of participants to the groups.

Despite the considerable effort of running that many cycling trials to exhaustion, the study is conducted as planned, and the data analysis yields a statistically significant result, t(126) = 3.03, p = 0.003, d = 0.54, 95% CI [0.18, 0.89]. The investigator reports this together with a graph showing the raw time-to-exhaustion values in both groups, the group means, and their confidence intervals. The discussion concludes that the findings support the hypothesis and that the observed effect is of medium magnitude according to conventional benchmarks. Does this sound familiar? It certainly does to us, as several of our own papers follow this or a similar template (e.g., Bieleke & Wolff, 2017; Wolff et al., 2018). And measured against current calls for estimation, such a study appears to heed them. But does it really?

Already at the design stage of the study, however, templates like this focus on maximizing the probability of obtaining a statistically significant result rather than on obtaining a precise estimate of the effect, reflecting a preference for hypothesis testing over estimation. In line with this, the central conclusion is qualitative and rests entirely on a binary decision based on significance, while the estimates are reported but contribute little to the inference. The effect size is merely assigned to a conventional category, a classification that Cohen himself considered a last resort (Cohen, 1988) and that ignores the specific outcome and research design (A. Caldwell & Vigotsky, 2020). Beyond offering a familiar label, the effect size has no bearing on the conclusion: had it been 0.4 or 0.7 and statistically significant, the hypothesis would have been considered supported all the same. Similarly, the confidence interval is reported and plotted but not interpreted. Whether it excludes zero is already implied by the p-value, so just presenting it adds nothing to the inference that the significance test has not already supplied. Its more informative property typically goes unmentioned (Fidler et al., 2004): the interval shows the range of effects compatible with the data, which here spans from small to large. Put bluntly, most of the statistics we report are merely decorative. They appear in the results but get lost on the way to the conclusions. Now consider a follow-up study. Typically, it tests the null hypothesis again, asking whether the effect differs from zero. At best, the estimate from the first study informs the power analysis; it does not enter the inference itself. Inference thus starts over with the same yes-or-no question rather than accumulating quantitative knowledge about the phenomenon.

The problem runs deeper than statistics

The tendency to draw qualitative conclusions is closely tied to the use of statistical significance as a general-purpose instrument of inference, although it can extend to other inferential quantities as well (Muradchanian et al., 2024). For instance, Bayes factors are often sorted into discrete categories (e.g., Jeffreys, 1961) despite having an "unambiguous and continuous scale" (Lee & Wagenmakers, 2013, p. 105).

Remarkably, the reliance on statistical significance persists despite decades of attempts at statistical reform and forceful calls to abandon it as the primary criterion for evaluating evidence. Explanations for the slow pace of reform point to inadequate statistical training (Greenland, 2017), publication incentives that reward significant results (A. R. Caldwell et al., 2020; Franco et al., 2014; Tiller & Ekkekakis, 2023), or simply the inertia of established practice (Gigerenzer, 2018). A binary decision rule may also be easier to apply than interpreting evidence on a continuum, and it offers an illusory sense of certainty (Gelman, 2016; Gigerenzer, 2004). Each of these likely contributes, but all of them locate the problem outside the science itself, in researchers' training, incentives, habits, or preferences. We argue that another reason lies within it: we settle for simple yes-or-no conclusions because most of our theories make equally simple yes-or-no predictions about whether an effect exists, not how large it should be. The second part of this paper develops that claim.

Our theories generate qualitative predictions

"All models are wrong but some are useful." -based on Box (1976) In sport and exercise psychology, we routinely ask whether an effect exists. Does motivational self-talk improve endurance performance? Does mental fatigue impair subsequent performance? Are people who are bored by sports less physically active?

These questions all share an important feature: they ask whether one variable affects or is associated with another. This can be an important starting point for any research area (Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019). But imagine we would be asking instead how large an effect is, how quickly it unfolds, under which conditions it occurs, and how consistently it appears. This might seem a trivial shift at first sight, but it opens up a substantially richer scientific approach. Once the accumulated evidence allows, the field could move beyond the "whether" to refine its theories and make more specific claims. In many areas, we have reached that point.

What an arrow is, and what it is not

In psychology, a lively debate about a "theory crisis" has drawn attention to how theories are formulated and tested (Borsboom et al., 2021; Eronen & Bringmann, 2021; Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019; van Dongen et al., 2025). This debate has yet to gain much traction in sport and exercise psychology, although our theories share many of the features under discussion. Most of them are verbal, often depicted as boxand-arrow diagrams. The boxes represent psychological constructs and the arrows represent the assumed relations between them, usually intended as causal effects (Rohrer et al., 2022). Such diagrams specify which constructs are related and in which direction, and these relations are typically tested with NHST. The theories remain silent, however, about the functional form of those relations or their temporal dynamics. Consequently, a theory's evidential standing often depends only on whether an estimated effect has the predicted sign and differs from zero, not on its magnitude.

Testing such theories yields limited insights because they count a broad range of possible outcomes, namely every effect size in the predicted direction, as supportive evidence. Paradoxically, when a theory predicts only whether and in which direction an effect occurs, greater precision through larger samples can make it harder, rather than easier, to refute it (Meehl, 1967 (Meehl, , 1978)): with sufficient power, even trivially small effects can be detected as statistically significant while remaining within the theory's predicted outcome space. Precision pays off only when a theory specifies what to expect, an issue that becomes ever more pertinent in the era of big data (Lin et al., 2013). Clearly, such theories provide limited practical guidance, too. Practitioners need information about the expected magnitude, uncertainty, and time course of an effect to make informed cost-benefit decisions, not merely to know whether an intervention "works" in principle (Lininger & Riemann, 2016), a conclusion sometimes based on the statistical significance of trivially small effects.

A similar logic applies to the relations a theory leaves out. An absent path is itself a claim: that one construct does not influence another. In a fully specified causal structure such claims are testable, because each omitted relation implies a conditional independence that data can violate (Pearl, 2009). In practice, these implications are rarely examined. Moreover, the structure tends to bend to the data: when a predicted relation fails to appear, or when fit indices suggest that an added path or moderator would improve fit, we extend the model rather than treat the result as evidence against the theory. Neither the arrows a theory draws nor those it omits therefore carry much commitment, and its structure resists refutation even before the question of functional form arises.

We cannot not specify functional form

One might argue that quantifying the functional form of relations in sport and exercise psychology is not necessary, desirable, or even feasible, pointing to the complexity of human behavior and the limited precision of psychological measurement (e.g., Sanbonmatsu et al., 2026). Although human behavior is undoubtedly complex (Balagué et al., 2020; Schüler et al., 2025), whether it is more complex than the systems studied in other quantitative sciences remains an open question, and one we do not pursue here. Our concern is with what the objection presupposes, namely that functional form can be left unspecified in principle. Once quantitative methods are used, it cannot: every fitted model commits to a functional form, and even if the theory does not make that commitment, the analytic choice of model does so nevertheless.

The choice is therefore not between specifying a functional form and leaving it open, but between specifying it deliberately and specifying it by default.

Consider the linear model, which underlies most of the statistical procedures commonly used in sport and exercise psychology (Lindeløv, 2019). Its estimated coefficient is read as the effect of X on Y:

This specification defines, by default, several aspects theories commonly leave open.

Each of them represents an opportunity for learning and advancing the field: a point at which a theory could become more explicit, and against which the data we collect could then be evaluated. The first concerns the shape of the dose-response relationship. The model assumes that each unit increase in X is assumed to be associated with the same expected change in Y across the entire range of X. This rules out all kinds of nonlinear relations, such as higher-order relations, thresholds, diminishing returns, or saturation. Constant increments are an unlikely default for the processes we study. Affective responses to exercise, for instance, do not decline smoothly with intensity but shift markedly around physiological breakpoints, the ventilatory threshold and the respiratory compensation point (Ekkekakis, 2003). As another example, effort and performance can decouple (Wolff, Hack, et al., 2026), and increasing levels of effort produce a convex rather than linear increase in performance, with markedly larger increments at high than at low or moderate levels of effort (Rewitz et al., 2024; Weilharter et al., 2024). Such examples illustrate how theories would benefit from being specific about the shape of the dose-response relations they propose.

The second concerns temporal dynamics. The linear model carries no time index and therefore describes a state that does not evolve. The estimated coefficient is assumed to hold regardless of when measurement occurs, and effects that emerge only after a delay, accumulate, decay, or depend on prior states are not represented (Ryo et al., 2019). Research on boredom provides an example for this. Boredom arises from a misfit between task demands and an individual's abilities, preferences, and energy level (Wolff et al., 2024). As task demands change over the course of a performance, the degree of misfit changes with them, producing boredom trajectories that rise and fall within a single task episode (Rewitz et al., 2026). Capturing these trajectories requires more than the two moments a typical pre-post design records. Boredom is not unique in showing such dynamics; comparable within-person fluctuations have been documented for other subjective experiences as well (Pérez-Díaz et al., 2026).

Theories that specify when and how quickly an effect unfolds would bring these dynamics into view and also tell us when and how often to measure or intervene.

The third concerns differences between persons. The linear model estimates a single slope assumed to hold for everyone, which is at odds with evidence that causal effects in behavioral sciences are typically heterogeneous (Bolger et al., 2019; Bryan et al., 2021). An average slope may therefore describe no individual in the sample, so a coefficient that is informative about a population can be uninformative about the person a practitioner is working with. Averaging can even distort the functional form itself, as when individual learning curves that follow exponential functions average into a power function that describes none of them (Heathcote et al., 2000). Moreover, the coefficient is estimated from variation between people but interpreted as the relation the theory posits within people. This inference holds only if all individuals follow the same process and that process is stable over time, a challenging condition for psychological processes (Molenaar, 2004). A theory that specifies not only the expected effect but also how much it varies across persons and conditions would turn this heterogeneity from noise into information (Bolger et al., 2019; Bryan et al., 2021) and make its generalizability explicit and testable (Yarkoni, 2022).

The fourth concerns everything the model leaves out. Any other influence on Y is assumed simply to add to the effect of X, not to strengthen or weaken it, and the spread of Y around the predicted values is assumed to be the same at all levels of X. Most importantly, the model assumes that nothing it leaves out affects both X and Y, a crucial assumption for causal inference (Rohrer, 2018; Ruissen, 2024). This is the same commitment that the absent arrows in a path diagram make. Many of us are aware of this limitation and avoid causal language especially in correlational work, sometimes at a reviewer's request, yet we often still draw causal inferences implicitly (Grosz et al., 2020). A theory that explicitly names the plausible common causes of X and Y would tell us which variables to measure or control, and when the estimated relation can be read as a causal effect.

Every analytic choice is a potential theoretical claim

The four commitments can be relaxed, for instance, by elaborating the model (e.g., random intercepts or slopes) or by adding variables (e.g., covariates or moderators).

Each such step, however, requires a decision that a theory could inform but currently rarely does. Contemporary theories predict that Y increases with X and leave everything else open. As a result, the specification is settled at the analysis stage, on technical rather than conceptual grounds, and the resulting empirical claim is more specific than anything the theory asserts. Every analytic choice we already make is thus a claim that a theory could make deliberately, and then test empirically.

As with effect sizes, theories that make qualitative predictions are indifferent to whether these assumptions hold. Whether they are met is treated as a technical issue internal to the analysis: a violation prompts a different model, not a revision of the theory. Effect sizes and findings about functional form therefore rarely make their way back into the theory, because a theory whose predictions are restricted to qualitative yes-or-no answers has no parameter to revise and no place to record a magnitude, a shape, a rate, or a degree of variability. Estimates accumulate across studies, and meta-analysis pools them (Hagger, 2022), yet they rarely accumulate into theory, because the pooled estimate has nothing in the theory to update. Encouragingly, this means that the field already generates much of the quantitative information that more specific theories would require. What we need are robust ways of feeding this information back into theory, and theories that can put it to good use. We turn to both in the next part.

Asking better questions to find more relevant answers "Far better an approximate answer to the right question, which is often vague, than an exact answer to the wrong question, which can always be made precise."

-John W. Tukey (1962, p. 13, emphasis in original) All of this raises the question of how sport and exercise psychology can make fuller use of its quantitative potential to address more interesting and relevant questions.

We believe the field is in an excellent position to do so, and we see three levers, in increasing order of ambition: making better use of what we already have, consolidating the existing evidence and feeding it back into theory development, and making our theories' predictions more specific. Figure 1 illustrates these levers and where they lead: the more specific a theory becomes, the fewer outcomes it can get away with, and the more it delivers to scientists and practitioners alike. Each lever moves theories further along this continuum, as we discuss in the following sections.

Lever 1: Making better use of what we already have

As outlined above, leading statistical associations (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016) and researchers across disciplines (Amrhein et al., 2019) have long called for abandoning statistical significance as the dominant, and often sole, criterion for evaluating evidence. The aim is not to discard p-values or significance tests, but to stop reducing evidence to a binary decision by default. What takes its place is an honest and comprehensive account of what the data show, to which p-values, effect sizes, confidence intervals, measures of variability, and the data themselves each contribute.

Read as a continuous measure of how compatible the data are with a statistical model, a p-value remains informative (Greenland, 2019), and so do effect sizes, their confidence intervals, measures of variability, and the data themselves. Effect sizes and their confidence intervals, in turn, become primary targets of inference, telling us how large an effect is and how precisely it has been estimated (Cumming, 2014).

Binary decisions keep their place where a decision must actually be made (Tunç et al., 2023), but they need not be the default endpoint of every analysis. In this sense, the first lever requires no new data and no new methods, only fuller use of the statistics we already compute. As theories become more specific, they prompt better questions, rule out more wrong answers, and yield more useful predictions. This shifts the focus from piling up statistically significant results to building genuinely quantitative theories, an evolution that characterizes maturing scientific disciplines.

Effect sizes become most informative when they are interpreted in practical terms rather than sorted reflexively into conventional categories (Andersen et al., 2007; A. Caldwell & Vigotsky, 2020). Measures with a natural metric, such as minutes of activity or repetitions completed, are often clearest in their original units (Baguley, 2009; A. Caldwell & Vigotsky, 2020). Psychological scales, by contrast, often use arbitrary metrics (Andersen et al., 2007; Blanton & Jaccard, 2006) and are usually standardized, which expresses an effect relative to how much people in a sample differ rather than how much it matters (Baguley, 2009). It is often more informative to relate an effect to what people notice and care about, that is, to how large a difference must be before individuals perceive it and before they judge it as meaningful. Both can be established psychometrically with anchor-based methods (Anvari & Lakens, 2021; for an example, see Späth & Brand, 2026). Where a decision is required, these differences provide a more relevant value to test against than zero (Lakens et al., 2018). Beyond such decisions, they provide a metric with substantive meaning.

Reporting that an intervention shifts an outcome by about twice the difference people begin to notice, but by less than half of what they consider meaningful, says far more than a standardized effect size labeled with Cohen's (1988)'s benchmarks, and it is easier to communicate to athletes, coaches, and the public. The confidence interval, which shows the range of effects that fit the data reasonably well (Amrhein & Greenland, 2022), can be expressed in the same metric. Stating that the data are compatible with effects from about half to three times the difference people begin to notice tells readers directly how the plausible effects would be experienced by those affected. Because what people notice and value differs between, say, rehabilitation patients and elite athletes, determining it for a given population and context becomes an interesting research question in its own right.

Effect sizes and confidence intervals are summaries, and quite different datasets can produce identical summary statistics (Anscombe, 1973; Matejka & Fitzmaurice, 2017).

Showing the data behind the estimates therefore completes the picture. Bar and line graphs, still common in the literature, display summary statistics while hiding the individual observations (Weissgerber et al., 2015). Showing those observations is especially valuable in sport and exercise psychology, where samples are often small (Pindl et al., 2026; Schweizer & Furley, 2016) and skew, clustering, or a single outlying participant can shape an aggregate result. It also reveals how individual changes are spread, which is informative in its own right, since genuine differences between people are worth understanding rather than averaging away (Atkinson & Batterham, 2015; Bryan et al., 2021). Contemporary tools make this easier than ever. Raincloud plots, for example, combine a distribution, a boxplot, and the individual observations, keeping clustering, skew, and floor or ceiling effects visible (Allen et al., 2021).

Estimation plots place the raw data alongside the effect estimate and its interval in a single figure (Ho et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2026). Such displays let readers judge whether a summary fairly describes what happened. Plotting the raw data also gives a first look at the shape of relationships between variables, such as thresholds or diminishing returns, which our theories have yet to specify.

Shifting the emphasis toward estimation also changes how we value empirical work.

A study's worth lies not in its p-value but in how robustly it answers the question it set out to address. This makes research considerably less precarious, especially early in one's career. A doctoral project designed to estimate an effect precisely contributes something whatever the estimate turns out to be and whether or not it is statistically significant. It is poorly designed studies and a lack of honest consideration of data that need an apology, not null results, because they matter in at least two ways. First, data from a well-conducted study add to the accumulated evidence irrespective of their pvalue (Amrhein et al., 2019), which is exactly the premise of meta-analysis (Hagger, 2022). The more studies are published on the basis of their design and robustness rather than their outcome, the more useful these syntheses become, because fewer informative null results remain unreported (Franco et al., 2014). Second, when a study tests against a meaningful difference rather than zero, a null result counts as evidence against the hypothesis and the theory it was derived from. In practice, we often attribute such results to flaws in the sample, the manipulation, or the measure, and rarely consider that the theory itself might be wrong. This is sometimes justified, but as a default it shields theories from conflicting evidence, much like the "'patching-up' procedures with the invention of ad hoc hypotheses" that Hassmén et al. (2016, p. 94) see as one sign of what they call degenerating research programs in sport and exercise psychology. Studies designed from the outset to be robust and interpretable leave little leeway for such explanations and help us decide whether a theory needs refinement or retirement.

Estimation also offers concrete tools for designing such studies. Instead of powering a study merely to reject a null hypothesis, researchers can plan for a target precision (Lakens, 2022; Maxwell et al., 2008) around a meaningful difference (Riesthuis, 2024).

When the goal is a narrow interval rather than crossing a threshold, there is less to gain from flexible analysis, which may also reduce questionable research practices such as p-hacking. Most importantly, the questions themselves change. Asking how much, how uncertain, and what else is known invites more interesting answers than asking whether an effect differs from zero (Calin-Jageman & Cumming, 2019; Ziliak & McCloskey, 2008). Making these questions answerable directs attention to sharpening constructs, validating measures, and mapping functional relationships (Scheel et al., 2021) Making better use of our data does not by itself strengthen the theoretical foundation of sport and exercise psychology, but it prepares the ground. An estimated magnitude has the greatest impact when a theory has a place to put it, something directional theories do not yet provide. Estimation supplies the quantities that the next two levers can accumulate and build into theory.

Lever 2: Adopting a cumulative approach to evidence synthesis

Every well-conducted study adds to what we know, and synthesizing these contributions shows how much has already been established. Corcoran & Steele (2025) provide a worked example. Updating an earlier meta-analysis of the self-talk literature (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011) with Bayesian methods, they show that the average effect of self-talk on sport performance is now estimated with considerable precision. This is a genuine achievement of the field, and it has a practical implication: further primary studies of the basic effect add little information and produce what has been referred to as "research waste" (Chalmers & Glasziou, 2009). Why do such studies continue to be conducted? We suggest that the answer lies in the theories that motivate them. A theory asserting only that self-talk improves performance has no place to record how large the effect has turned out to be. Because our theories currently do not register what the evidence has established, each new study starts from the same open question as the first.

Building the estimate into the theory would change this. If replications are treated as contributions to theory development rather than as repeated tests of the same claim (Irvine, 2021), the theory could have predicted an effect of approximately 0.48 ± 0.10 on the basis of the initial meta-analysis. It could also have specified how much this effect should vary across contexts. Subsequent studies would then test that prediction rather than testing again against zero. An observed effect of 0.10, for instance, would fall well outside the predicted range and call for either a revision of the theory or a restriction of its scope. Velicer et al. (2008) demonstrated this cycle for the transtheoretical model of behavior change: they translated the model's verbal claims into numerical effect size predictions, compared these with observed effects and their confidence intervals, and used the discrepancies to refine both the theory and its predictions. Encouragingly, the raw material for such predictions is already at hand, as meta-analytic estimates like the one for self-talk are available for a range of sport psychological interventions (Lochbaum et al., 2022). Research programs comprising several hundred published studies that have established an effect are therefore well placed to turn this accumulated knowledge into more specific theories that can be wrong in many informative ways (Muthukrishna & Henrich, 2019).

The field is in a good position to build such a cumulative practice. Meta-analyses on sport psychology and performance become increasingly frequent (Lochbaum & Lane, 2025), and there are established ways to keep them up to date. Living systematic reviews are updated by dedicated teams whenever new studies appear (Elliott et al., 2017; Iversen & Quintana, 2026), and community-augmented meta-analyses make the underlying data openly available, so that researchers can add their own results and use the current estimates when planning a new study (Bucher et al., 2023; Tsuji et al., 2014). More generally, shared repositories make data findable and reusable (Wilkinson et al., 2016), and sport science already has discipline-specific examples that could serve as a template (Klemm et al., 2024). Artificial intelligence makes it easier than ever to handle the large amounts of data and continuous updating this requires (Bieleke et al., 2026). Data collection can be pooled as well. Multi-lab studies yield precise estimates and show how much an effect varies between sites (Coles et al., 2022). Megastudies compare many interventions on the same outcome in the same large sample, shifting the question from whether an intervention works to how much it achieves relative to others (Milkman et al., 2021). Pooled evidence also shows how much results vary across studies, which indicates how well a phenomenon is understood (Bryan et al., 2021; Linden & Hönekopp, 2021). The payoff for sport and exercise psychology for embracing such a culture would be considerable. A researcher planning a study could start from the current best estimate instead of from scratch. A null result could sharpen that estimate instead of ending up in a drawer. A coach or athlete could ask not only whether a technique works, but how much it is likely to help them. And differences between studies and between people would become some of the many interesting questions the field can pursue.

Lever 3: Making the theories themselves more quantitative

A theory that predicts only the direction of an effect has nothing to revise when estimates come in. Magnitudes, functional forms, and rates of change can be reported and meta-analyzed without changing the theory itself. This changes once a theory makes quantitative commitments, and these can be modest. A theory can state a plausible range for an effect, as in the self-talk example above. It can specify the functional form of a relation, or how an effect unfolds over time. At the far end are formal models, which state a theory so explicitly that its predictions follow from it without further interpretation, whether as equations, as algorithms explored through simulation, or as causal structures (Haslbeck et al., 2022; Robinaugh et al., 2021; Smaldino, 2017; van Dongen et al., 2025). Each of these commitments gives the estimates produced by the first two levers a place in the theory.

The further a theory moves along this continuum, the more it gains in terms of specificity, and formal models show these gains most clearly. First, they are more restrictive than verbal theories and fail in identifiable ways. A verbal theory can accommodate most patterns of results, whereas a model that cannot reproduce a specific set of observed data is shown to be wrong in a specifiable respect (Guest & Martin, 2021). van Dongen et al. (2025) make this explicit: a theory provides a productive explanation of a phenomenon (Bogen & Woodward, 1988) only to the extent that a formal model of it produces the statistical pattern that represents the phenomenon. By this account, a verbal theory that predicts only the sign of a relation is compatible with many patterns but produces none of them. Second, the attempt to formalize exposes gaps before any data are collected, because constructs that seem well defined in prose often turn out to be underspecified once a functional relation has to be written down (Borsboom et al., 2021; van Rooij & Baggio, 2021). Third, an explicit formulation can be checked in its own right, for instance for internal consistency and for whether its parameters can be recovered from the kind of data a study would yield (Wilson & Collins, 2019). Fourth, formal models yield predictions their authors did not anticipate, because consequences follow from the structure of the model rather than from the theorist's intentions. This is what makes a prediction risky in the sense described by Meehl (1978), and it is widely regarded as a hallmark of a good theory.

Revisiting the four commitments that a linear model makes by default shows what precise theorizing can offer. For two of them, sport and exercise science already has well-developed examples. The first is functional form. Psychophysics specifies that perceived intensity grows as a power function of stimulus intensity (J. C. Stevens & Mack, 1959; S. S. Stevens, 1957), and Borg's rating scales build on this function (G. Borg, 1982 Borg, , 1998)). Ratings can therefore be mapped onto intensities and prescribed as training targets, which a directional claim could not support. Committing to a form also made theoretical disagreement productive. Because Fechner's logarithmic law and Stevens' power function made different predictions, they could be tested against each other and were later reconciled within a single Bayesian account (Petzschner et al., 2015). The second is temporal dynamics. Impulse-response models describe how fitness and fatigue build up and decay after each training load (Banister et al., 1975), and their original linear form was replaced by a nonlinear one as evidence accumulated (Busso, 2003). On a shorter time scale, computational models track how fatigue and the willingness to exert effort change from moment to moment (Matthews et al., 2023). Each of these models can be revised as data accumulate, and some are already used in training practice.

For the other two commitments, differences between persons and common causes left out of a model, comparable models in sport and exercise psychology are still rare, but starting points exist. The expected value of control theory describes effort investment as a weighing of expected payoffs against effort costs (Shenhav et al., 2013 (Shenhav et al., , 2017)). Applications to sport have so far used its equations as a conceptual guide rather than as a fully specified model fitted to data. Even in this form, the theory breaks effort decisions into components that can be manipulated or held constant, distinguishes different functions of effort, suggests how costs and values may differ between groups of people, and yields testable predictions about effort, task termination, and boredom in sport (Bieleke et al., 2024; Stähler et al., 2025; Wolff et al., 2021). Causal diagrams provide a similar starting point for causal structure. They make explicit which variables are assumed to influence which, and thus which common causes must be accounted for before an association can be interpreted as an effect. They have recently been introduced to sport and exercise psychology (Ruissen, 2024) but are not yet widely used. Developing these approaches further is a promising direction for the field. Theories that specify how much an effect varies across people and which causal structure underlies it would address the questions raised above, and the intensive longitudinal and pooled data needed to test them are increasingly available.

Formalization has two limitations that should be kept in mind. First, it does not by itself make findings robust and replicable. Experimental economics has a long tradition of formal modeling but its replication rate is still well below what this tradition might suggest (Camerer et al., 2016). Formalization determines whether a theory is specific enough to be wrong, whereas replicability depends on statistical power, analytic flexibility, and publication practices. Second, models built on poorly defined constructs or unvalidated measures can be precise without being accurate (Eronen & Bringmann, 2021; Scheel et al., 2021). A model may even fit aggregate data well while describing no individual, as when averaged exponential learning curves yield a power function that holds for none of the learners (Heathcote et al., 2000). Where measurement is still contested, formalization can nevertheless help, because specifying what a construct is supposed to do clarifies what a valid measure would have to capture.

Doing so requires distinguishing the constructs or phenomena a theory is about from the physiological or behavioral subsystems that serve as its proxies and from the numbers an instrument records, and stating how they map onto each other (Wolff, Gluth, et al., 2026).

Quantitative formulation is therefore best understood as a direction for theory development, not as a prerequisite or the ultimate goal for every research question.

Each step along the continuum described above makes a theory more informative.

Sport and exercise psychology has established that many of its effects exist and has collected data that allow more specific questions: how large these effects are, what form they take, for whom they hold, and when they unfold. Developing theories that can be tested against such data and revised as evidence accumulates will allow us to address these questions.

Conclusion

We began this paper by revisiting a debate about science reform that most of us have heard many times. Rather than adding yet another argument to the same pile of legitimate arguments, we took a step back and asked why the debate persists in the first place. Our perspective is that our theories are not yet equipped to make full use of the improvements proposed in methodological reforms. The field collects quantitatively rich data and analyzes them with sophisticated methods, but the questions we derive from our theories lack a matching level of specificity and invite yes-or-no answers. As a consequence, all kinds of statistics that are computed, reported, and pooled, often end up as decoration because our theories offer no way to put them to use. We see this less as a dead end than as a starting point, because sport and exercise psychology rests on a strong empirical foundation, and in many areas it is ready to take the steps we laid out above: from theories that tell us whether an effect exists toward theories that tell us what to expect in a truly quantitative manner. Taking these steps would make not only our questions more interesting but also our answers more useful for scientists and practitioners alike.
