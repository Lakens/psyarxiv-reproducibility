Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Stress is an umbrella concept that lacks a universally agreed upon definition. The ambiguous conceptualization of stress undermines theoretical integration, construct validity, and cumulative knowledge development. In our critical review, we delineate sources of conceptual ambiguity, describe obstacles in the operationalization and conceptualization of stress, and evaluate whether the term stress itself is necessary for scientific exploration. We show that we do not need the term stress to describe the phenomena studied in stress research.

However, the term 'stress' may still be useful to refer to a field of research and to integrate research findings. We demonstrate that phenomena related to stress can be described using more precise constructs: stressors, stress responses, and health outcomes. To facilitate conceptual clarity, we synthesize umbrella definitions for these constructs. Because these umbrella definitions remain necessarily broad, we emphasize that specifying the conceptualization and operationalization of the stressor, stress response, and health outcomes is indispensable for empirical rigor. To that end, we introduce the Stress Conceptualization Checklist for Empirical Studies (SuCCES), a structured reporting framework. The checklist covers formulation of the conceptualization, operationalization, differentiation, life period, assessment window, and context. This reporting standard aims to strengthen construct validity, improve measurement coherence, and promote cumulative progress in research on stress-related processes.

"Stress, in addition to being itself and the result of itself, is also the cause of itself" Stress is an umbrella term that is used for a plethora of phenomena. Almost forty years ago Pollock (1988) noted that "stress itself eludes definition, and there is no consensus as to what it encompasses". The status quo is not very different today. Stress describes a variety of distinct components of a sequential process, with both internal and external causes of a stress response including physiological and psychological elements (Monroe, 2008; Monroe & Slavich, 2020). Accordingly, stress theories have different foci, and conceptualized stress either as a stimulus, a response process, or as the interaction between the environment and a person (Aldwin, 2012; Hobfoll, 1989; Hutmacher, 2021; Mulhall, 1996), as we discuss below.

This ambiguity of the stress conceptualization and operationalization is problematic for theory formulation and empirical science (Bringmann et al., 2022; Myroniuk et al., 2026).

Stress conceptualizations influence how we understand the stress phenomenon and our surroundings (Taylor & Dewsbury, 2018), such as what we infer from research (Richter-Levin & Sandi, 2021; Taylor & Dewsbury, 2018). Conceptual imprecision complicates the boundary between stress and related concepts, which reduces discriminant validity (Podsakoff et al., 2016). A solid stress definition is also crucial to develop evidence based clinical interventions (Walker & Avant, 2019).

The conceptual clarity and usefulness of the term "stress" has been discussed since the 1970s (Del Giudice et al., 2018; Goldstein & Kopin, 2007; Harkness & Monroe, 2016; Hinkle, 1974 Hinkle, , 1987;; Hutmacher, 2021; Kagan, 2016; Kasl, 1995; Koolhaas et al., 2011; Monroe, 2008; Pollock, 1988; Rushen, 1986; Steinberg & Ritzmann, 1990; Young, 1980).

Several authors tried to improve the conceptualization of stress (Del Giudice et al., 2018; Goodnite, 2014; Hobfoll, 1989; Kagan, 2016; Koolhaas et al., 2011; Rees, 1976; Steinberg & Ritzmann, 1990), or to provide new or integrative theories of stress (Del Giudice et al., 2018; Epel et al., 2018; McEwen & Akil, 2020; Rees, 1976; Slavich, 2020). We build on these prior articles in six steps: we (a) review dominant stress conceptualizations, (b) analyze sources of conceptual ambiguity, (c) evaluate obstacles in the conceptualization and operationalization of stress, (d) examine the necessity of the term 'stress' for describing stress research, (e) unify definitions of stressors, stress responses, and health outcomes, and (f) provide a checklist to improve reporting of stress conceptualization and operationalization in empirical research.

Stress conceptualizations

Stimulus models of stress assume that certain adverse environmental conditions are more likely to be stressful than others, and can therefore be regarded as "stress" (Monroe, 2008). Internal or external 1 demands that may elicit a stress response have been termed "stressors" (Epel et al., 2018; Selye, 1976). Many stressor types fall under the umbrella "stress as a stimulus", including life events, chronic difficulties, traumatic events, acute adverse events, and daily hassles (Harkness & Hayden, 2020). Stimulus models also include diathesis stress models, which describe how stressors may result in adverse stress responses and health outcomes if people have certain characteristics, such as high neuroticism scores or specific genetic factors, that exacerbate the effects of stressors (Jeronimus et al., 2025).

The term stress also describes a stress response; the state of the organism after the exposure to a stressor (Selye, 1976). The stress response can contain physiological (Epel et al., 2018; McEwen & Akil, 2020), emotional (Epel et al., 2018; Mechanic & Volkart, 1961), behavioral (Alberdi et al., 2016; Epel et al., 2018), and cognitive elements (Myroniuk et al., 2026; Selye, 1976). Theorists argued that environmental challenges are necessary to elicit stress responses (Monroe & Slavich, 2020), and studied stress responses using laboratory stressors such as social evaluation (e.g., Heleniak et al., 2016; Sep et al., 2019), or in response 1 There are conceptualizations that explicitly include internal stressors and others that focus on environmental stressors exclusively.

to stressful events in daily life (Myroniuk et al., 2026). Other stress response theories do not demand specific stressors, such as the "safety signal paradigm" in which a lack of safety signals suffices to elicit a stress response (Christianson et al., 2012; Seligman & Binik, 2021).

Accordingly, stress responses have also been studied in isolation, thus without exposure to a stressor.

Stress may as well refer to person-environment transactions and describe processes that influence how we respond to environmental conditions (Somerfield & McCrae, 2000).

Such transactional theories typically build on the theory of stress and coping (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), which assumed that relationships between people and their environment are bidirectional and dynamic. Stress became defined as a person's cognitive appraisal of the person-environment transaction, by weighting their resources to cope against the situational demands and the threat to wellbeing (Folkman et al., 1986; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984).

Psychological processes such as appraisals and coping may also reflect a stress response instead of a person-environment transaction, and some theorists therefore do not distinguish between stress responses and person-environment transactions (e.g., Epel et al., 2018; Myroniuk et al., 2026).

Building on transactional theories, scientists focused more on long term consequences and defined stress as the ongoing "adaptation to challenging environmental conditions over time" (Monroe, 2008), in keeping with research on conditional adaptation after the experience of stressors (Ellis & Del Giudice, 2019; Ellis, 2023). The adaptive calibration model (Del Giudice et al., 2011), for example, assumes that stressful environments drive the development of adaptive strategies (Ellis & Del Giudice, 2019). People are thought to experience stress when their phenotype does not match the environment in which they live now, compared to the environment they grew up in (i.e., low person-environment fit). The concept of allostatic load similarly describes how identical external environments can produce highly heterogeneous biological configurations in different people based on their history, coping mechanisms, and genetic variations (McEwen, 2000).

These aforementioned conceptualizations illustrate that stress may refer to different phenomena and components of a process, in which a person is exposed to conditions that elicit a particular reaction (Monroe & Slavich, 2020). To delineate how to deal with this conceptual ambiguity, it may be useful to reflect on plausible reasons for the unclear conceptualization of stress.

Explanations for ambiguous conceptualization and operationalization of stress

One historical reason for the lack of clarity of the stress concept is the problematic transfer of the term stress from the field of physics to the medical and social sciences. The earliest scientifically rigorous mention of the term "stress" in physics defined stress as the directed reactive force distribution per unit area inside a material as a reaction to applied external force (Azadi, 2017; Cauchy, 1821). In this physics context, the other important measure is strain, which describes the amount of deformation as a result of stress (Kopin, 1995). When stress vanishes, strain may also return to zero because of the elastic behavior of the material, and the object's shape restores completely once the source of deformation is removed. However, when stress crosses a material-specific elasticity limit, the object may be permanently deformed, which indicates strain. Because physicists view stress as a theoretical variable that is not directly measurable, they assess stress not by direct measurement but infer it from measurements of external force applied and strain.

The terms 'stress' and 'strain' were introduced into the medical sciences by Walter Cannon (1871 Cannon ( -1945) ) to explain the effects of stressors on human homeostasis; a regulatory mechanism to maintain bodily stability. Similar to physicists, Cannon (1935) used the term stress mainly to explain external force to the body (i.e., the stimulus or "load"), such as change in temperature in the atmosphere, which could lead to strain in the biological system, and thus a failure to maintain homeostasis. However, as described below, the term 'stress' was kept while the terms 'external force' (load) and 'strain' were often lost over the course of time. An exception are theories in the occupational health literature in which strains have been defined as the outcomes of a stress process that was triggered by a stressor (Lepine et al., 2005).

Hans Selye (1907 Selye ( -1982) ) popularized the stress concept in the medical and social sciences, to explain the maladaptive effects of stimuli on the human body (for a more in depth discussion of the work of Hans Selye and Walter Cannon, see Cooper & Dewe, 2004; Hinkle, 1974; Kopin, 1995; Viner, 1999). Selye (1956) used the term 'stress response' to explain the state of the body after exposure to external stimuli. He also coined 'stressor' to describe the agent or demand that evokes a stress response, which corresponds with the external force in physics. Following Selye, researchers started to use the term stress to describe both the causal factors leading to a response in human beings as well as this non-specific stress response itself.

Selye's definition of stress can also explain why there are so many different operationalizations of stress (Kagan, 2016). Although Selye used different definitions during his career, his most commonly used definition of stress is "the nonspecific response of the body to any demand", which captures a broad variety of variables (Selye, 1976). In fact, Selye (1976) enumerated >150 stressors and ~100 manifestations of stress in his book Stress in Health and Disease. Selye's (1976) stressors and conditioning agents included exogenous stimuli (such as drugs, pollution, isolation, parachute jumping, shift work) and endogenous stimuli (such as grief, anxiety, cognition, sleep deprivation, and corticoids). Moreover, Selye listed both physiological and psychological manifestations of stress, for example, alterations in the liver, learning, cognitions, memory, emotions, vitamin c levels, tumors, and appetite. Interestingly, some hormones were recorded as both stressors and manifestation of stress (e.g., glucagon, insulin and corticoids). Hence, Selye's stress definition makes almost any measure tapping into exposure, perception, emotion, or biological response a measurement of stress.

The term 'stress' may also be ambiguous because the use of the term has evolved over time in science and society. Lay people's perceptions of stress may differ from how clinicians and scientist define stress (Wezyk et al., 2024). For example, Selye (1976) noted that stress is not the same as emotional arousal or nervous tension, whereas lay people's perceptions of stress typically do include arousal (e.g., agitation, restlessness) and negative emotions (anxiety, sadness; Wezyk et al., 2024). These lay' perceptions of stress are also reflected in more recent scientific definitions of stress that have included arousal (Bystritsky & Kronemyer, 2014).

Another explanation for the many different conceptualizations and operationalizations of stress lies in the many disciplines that studied stress, each with their particular theories, operationalizations, methods, and study objectives (Cohen et al., 2016; Mulhall, 1996; Pearlin, 1989; Steinberg & Ritzmann, 1990). As outlined, medical sciences often rely on the theory of allostasis and allostatic load for stress physiology (Ganster & Rosen, 2013; McEwen, 2000; McEwen & Akil, 2020), whereas the social sciences tend to build on the theory of stress and coping (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Because science is the result of complex social and historical processes (Hutmacher, 2021), and there has been little integration between research fields, both the conceptualization and operationalization of stress followed their own trajectory within each research field over time.

A more philosophical reason for the lack of conceptual clarity is that the concept of stress is a human social construction (Pollock, 1988; Young, 1980). Scientific concepts are constructed to "help organize the phenomena in the world around us into meaningful categories and describe their attributes" (p. 165, Podsakoff et al., 2016). Manufactured concepts, especially those aimed to explain psychological or physical processes, often do not have clear boundaries (Gerring, 1999). Kagan (2016) noted that this lack of conceptual clarity is especially present among psychologists, who tend to "lump" abstract concepts without specifying the characteristics or participant responses. This conceptual unclarity is a generic problem in the social sciences (Bringmann et al., 2022; Podsakoff et al., 2016) and exemplified by the addressed problems with concepts such as friendship (Block & Elmer, 2025), coping (Keil, 2004) and resilience (Den Hartigh & Hill, 2022; Yoon et al., 2021).

Obstacles in the conceptualization and operationalization of stress

Current stress conceptualizations do not meet all criteria for a good concept such as 'coherence' (a bounded set of phenomena) and 'differentiation' (from neighbouring concepts; see Gerring, 1999; MacKenzie, 2003; Suddaby, 2010). Stress operationalizations across scientific disciplines, such early childhood events, cognitive appraisals, corticosteroids, neural regulation, and momentary emotional states, exemplify that there is a lack of coherence.

Furthermore, the lack of differentiation is exemplified by the fact that stress responses and the pathogenic effects of stress are difficult to distinguish (Tennant et al., 1985). For example, heightened stress levels may be inferred from heightened cortisol levels, rumination, and restlessness, but these concepts are also elements of umbrella concepts like anxiety and depression. Similarly, it may be hard to draw a conceptual line between some perceived stressful daily hassles, such as sleep problems and symptoms of psychopathology (Wright et al., 2020). A related critique of some conceptualizations of stress is that they are tautological (MacKenzie, 2003; Podsakoff et al., 2016; Suddaby, 2010), such as Selye's (1976) definition of stressors and stress (Kagan, 2016; Rushen, 1986); a stress response is a response that is caused by a stressor, which is defined as something that is able to elicit a stress response. Furthermore, when stressors are conceptually conflated with the stress response, they risk being confounded in measurement, and thereby threaten the validity of the research (Harkness & Monroe, 2016).

A more fundamental problem with the many conceptualizations of stress is exactly the diversity in phenomena and entities that have been labelled as stress. A concise concept definition demands a specification of the exact properties of a phenomenon (e.g., whether these properties include perceptions or feelings), and the entity, that is the, the person, object, or context to which these properties apply (Podsakoff et al., 2016). However, conceptual definitions of stress (see supplementary Table S1) typically include a mixture of entities (e.g., stimuli and responses) and phenomena (e.g., physiological responses or appraisals of stressors). These obstacles in stress conceptualization stimulated some authors to redefine stress by formulating more narrow boundaries to counter the overly permissive use of the term stress (see e.g. Del Giudice et al., 2018; Kagan, 2016; Koolhaas et al., 2011). For example, Kagan (2016) suggested limiting stress to events that "pose a serious threat to an organism's well-being". Koolhaas and colleagues (2011) proposed to restrict the use of stress to stressors that are uncontrollable and unpredictable, in order to distinguish these stressors from demands that fall within the adaptive capacity and normal physiological responses. Contrarily, Romero and colleagues (2015) argued for a more permissive use of the concept stress, including normal physiological stress responses in healthy individuals. These examples illustrate that theories either strive to include or exclude 'normal' event or responses and that there, evidently, may be no consensus on a single universal definition of stress.

Can we describe stress research without using the term stress?

One radical way to deal with the ambiguity of the concept stress is to drop it, as many have suggested (e.g. Crosswell et al., 2022; Hinkle, 1974; Kagan, 2016; Rushen, 1986). The question is whether we even need the term stress to describe the phenomena of interest in empirical stress research. The answer is no because more specific terminology can be used to describe stress. To illustrate, stressors be described as life-events, long-term difficulties, childhood trauma, or daily hassles. Examples of more specific terms to describe work stress are performance constraints, conflicts with colleagues, interruptions at work, illegitimate tasks, and daily negative work events. In the medical field, physiological indicators of functioning of the stress system have been examined, such as circulating plasma levels of Creactive protein (CRP) interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha as markers of the immune-inflammatory system, cortisol measures as markers of functioning of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis (HPA-axis), and indicators of heart rate, pre-ejection period, and respiratory sinus arrhythmia as markers of functioning of the autonomic nervous system (Black et al., 2017). Evidently, the term stress is not needed to describe the aforementioned phenomena, that is, authors could directly describe the constructs they are measuring. The only construct that is hard to measure without the word stress is the feeling of stress. Feelings of stress have been measured by asking about how stressed someone has felt (e.g., Snippe et al., 2023) or how often someone felt stressed (Kuckuck et al., 2025), which is hard to measure in another way than by using the word 'stress'.

Unifying and specifying conceptual definitions of stress

The provided examples of stress studies illustrate that the use of the term "stress" is not crucial to describe the phenomena we study. Yet, stress became so deeply ingrained in everyday language and the scientific discourse that the term "stress" seems impossible to "undo." Stress may best be used to describe an interdisciplinary research field that focuses on the explanation of individual and group differences in stress related processes, often with the aim to predict health outcomes. The phenomena that have been studied in the name of 'stress' operate on multiple levels (e.g., physiological, emotional, societal, person, environment) and interact to produce the key features of what we may call "stress". Cross-level findings may complement one another (Eronen, 2021), and help identify similarities (Epel et al., 2018).

Researchers may examine to what extent the properties of "stress" concepts at different levels overlap (i.e., the mechanistic property cluster of 'stress'), just as how biologists started to sort species, and psychiatry strives to unravel clusters of psychopathology (Kendler et al., 2011).

Second, researchers must identify the entity (i.e., object or event) to which these properties apply, such as an individual, a relationship, or a broader social system. Stress conceptualization checklist for empirical studies

Even though the formulated umbrella definitions distinguish different phases of the stress process, the definitions lack the specificity needed to adequately define a phenomenon in empirical studies. Therefore, researchers should use these umbrella definitions to clarify the part of the 'stress' process that they are investigating but should also further specify the foci of the stressor, stress response, or health outcomes in their empirical study. To guide researchers in doing so, we drafted the Stress Conceptualization Checklist for Empirical Studies (SuCCES) (see Table 2), which empirical researchers could use to be more transparent about their conceptualization and operationalization of stress. For an example illustrating how the SuCCES can be used, see the supplementary material.

As a first step described in the SuCCES, the study should describe the conceptual framework and/or conceptual definition of the stressors, stress response, or health outcomes that underpin the study. Second, researchers could use specifiers for the type of stressor, stress response, or health outcome that is studied. How to do so, is exemplified by the field of research on 'love'. Similar to stress, love is studied interdisciplinary (e.g., in the fields of history, theology, philosophy, and psychology). Researchers have narrowed their conceptualizations of love by addressing, for example, Christian love (e.g., Sullivan-Dunbar, 2009), Arendt's conception of love (e.g., Gregory, 2001), or obsessive desire or Limerence (Bradbury et al., 2025). Likewise, they have used specifiers of the type of love, such as romantic, religious, parental, and obsessive love. Similarly, specifiers for the type of stressor, stress response and health outcomes can be used, for example, financial stressors, interpersonal stressors (see Meyer et al. (in prep) for classifications of stressor types), physiological stress responses, and emotional stress responses. Next, it is pivotal to specify the exact operationalization of the construct under study and describe how the chosen operationalization is linked to the provided stress definition and constructs being measured (Bringmann et al., 2022). Furthermore, it needs to be clarified how the studied phenomenon differs from their outcomes (i.e., differentiation). In addition, researchers are advised to follow the stress measurement guidelines by Crosswell et al. (2022) and Epel et al. (2018) which encourages researches to specify, amongst others, the life period to which the measurement applies and the assessment window. Finally, researchers have come to acknowledge that stress responses do not occur in isolation from the context in which stressors are experienced, and it is thus advised to take factors such as individual, genetic, environmental, socio-economic, and cultural contextual factors into account (Epel et al., 2018) and to clarify how these factors are expected to influence the stress process.

Limitations and future directions

A limitation of our critical review is that the suggested umbrella definitions of stressors, stress responses, and health outcomes are not based on existing theoretical stress models. Theoretical models of 'stress' often go beyond an abstract 'stress' conceptualization and additionally specify concrete phenomena or processes that lead to stress. A systematic examination of theoretical models on stress can provide a comprehensive overview of the real-life phenomena that have been studied in the name of stressors, stress responses, or health outcomes. In addition, we acknowledge that using our umbrella definitions and SuCCES checklist is only a first step to improve agreement on stress concepts. Further theoretical integration across disciplines, pre-registration, and inclusion of the SuCCES checklist within reporting guidelines would be important next steps.

Conclusion

Stress may refer to a multitude of stimuli, responses and consequences thereof. Stress may reflect different phenomena, from life events to heart rate variability. There are different historical and philosophical reasons for the ambiguous conceptualization of stress. Even though we do not need the term stress to describe the phenomena studied in stress research, it may still be useful to use the term stress to refer to a field of research. By unifying existing conceptual definitions of the stress process, we have provided broad conceptualizations of the three core stress components: stressor, stress response, and health outcomes. We recommend that researchers follow our proposed checklist for empirical studies (SuCCES) and describe the conceptual framework, the conceptualization and type of stressor, stress response and health
