Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Objectives: Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) is a cyclical hormone-related condition, causing significant mood symptoms in the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle. Despite the substantial impact it can have on daily life, little is known about how it shapes reproductive decision-making. This study explored how people with PMDD experience and reflect on reproductive decisions, and the role of healthcare support in this.

Design: Semi-structured qualitative interviews, informed by a feminist critical realist approach.

Methods: Twenty UK-based adults who had received a diagnosis of, or self-identified as having, PMDD took part. Semi-structured interviews explored experiences of how, if at all, PMDD had impacted thoughts and decisions around pregnancy or parenthood. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, supported by a lived experience advisory group.

Results: Four themes were developed: PMDD as a whole-family experience; intergenerational awareness and responsibility; assessing readiness and capacity for parenthood; and self-advocacy and negotiating an unsupportive system. Participants not only spoke about how PMDD symptoms shaped their decisionmaking, but also considered relational impact, the potential heritability of PMDD, and the limited healthcare support available. Support from loved ones, familiarity with their own symptom patterns and establishing strategies for managing PMDD were often described as facilitators of decision-making.

Across themes, findings were interpreted within a "good mother" ideal, helping to explain why reproductive decisions often carried emotional and moral weight.

Conclusions: PMDD can shape reproductive decision-making in complex ways. Findings demonstrate the need for PMDD-specific support that helps people make informed decisions aligned with their circumstances and needs.

Introduction

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) is a cyclical hormone-related condition, characterised by severe emotional and physical symptoms in the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Common symptoms of PMDD include depression, anxiety and irritability (Halbreich et al., 2003), with significantly heightened risk of suicidal ideation and behaviour (Prasad et al., 2021).

The estimated pooled prevalence of PMDD is 3.2% for confirmed diagnosis, rising to around 7% for provisional diagnoses (Reilly et al., 2024). PMDD is commonly conceptualised as a disorder of hormonal sensitivity, although its aetiology is likely underpinned by a range of biopsychosocial mechanisms. The DASH-MC Framework (Dimensional Affective Sensitivity to Hormones across the Menstrual Cycle (Peters et al., 2025)) suggests that symptoms occur as a result of hypersensitivity to fluctuations in oestradiol (E2) and progesterone (P4), with the most severe symptoms arising from surges in allopregnanolone, a metabolite of P4. Greater prevalence of early life trauma has also been observed in individuals with PMDD (Kulkarni et al., 2022), and new research also implicates neuroinflammatory pathways (Cheng et al., 2025), indicating that the mechanisms remain complex and not fully understood.

PMDD is associated with significant social and occupational impairment, contributing an estimated 3,000 days of severe symptoms across the reproductive lifespan (Rapkin & Winer, 2009), impacting relationships (Hodgetts & Kinghorn, 2025), work and education (Brown et al., 2024a) and health-related quality of life (Yang et al., 2008). Despite this, research into the experience and impact of PMDD remains limited, with many individuals facing inadequate support (Islas-Preciado et al., 2025; Osborn et al., 2020).

The cyclical nature of PMDD can lead to identity disruption, losing a sense of self during the symptomatic period, and feeling a need to "overcompensate" once the luteal phase is over (Buys, 2024; Ussher, 2004).

There is little evidence on how PMDD affects broader life decisions, particularly those relating to reproduction. This lack of PMDD-specific knowledge may create uncertainty for individuals making decisions about pregnancy and parenthood. Reproductive decision-making is complex and shaped by emotional, sociocultural, economic and health-related factors (Hashemzadeh et al., 2021). Both physical health (Alderotti & Trappolini, 2022; Phillips et al., 2018) and mental health (Krumm & Becker, 2006; Lawley et al., 2022) conditions have been shown to impact people's decisions surrounding pregnancy and parenthood. Factors such as medication use, ongoing support needs and risk of worsening mental health have all been reported as reasons for delaying pregnancy or not having children (Lazzari & Beaujouan, 2025).

For PMDD, given the hormonal nature of the condition, there are other aspects that may be relevant to reproductive decision-making. Firstly, it is known that PMDD significantly increases risk of perinatal depression (PND), with a Swedish population-cohort study finding that premenstrual disorders were associated with increased risk of both pre-and postnatal depression, with the association slightly stronger for postnatal depression (Yang et al., 2024). This could impact reproductive decision-making for either a first pregnancy or subsequent pregnancies if PND was previously experienced. Additionally, because hormonal contraceptives are a recommended treatment for PMDD (Carlini et al., 2022), those whose symptoms are effectively managed by them may feel reluctant to stop treatment to conceive because of concerns that symptoms will return (Osborn et al., 2020). A small proportion of people with PMDD have a total hysterectomy with bilateral oophorectomy to offer symptom relief by stopping menstruation (Carlini et al., 2022). However, the decision to have this procedure is coupled with feelings of sadness and grief over infertility (Osborn et al., 2020). A mixed methods UK-based study explored how parents felt about their ability to care for a child, including concerns about parenting during the luteal phase (Brown et al., 2024b). Despite this, little in-depth work has been done to investigate how and why people with PMDD make these decisions about parenting and what support, if any, they receive from healthcare professionals during this time.

Participants were eligible if they were 18+, lived in the UK and had experience of PMDD. This included individuals who either self-identified with, or were diagnosed with, PMDD. This decision was taken due to both the length of time taken to obtain a PMDD diagnosis, and the lack of awareness of PMDD amongst healthcare professionals (Nayak et al., 2025). This allowed a greater variety of individuals experiencing PMDD to take part in the study. Individuals were not required to have experienced pregnancy or thought about reproductive decisions to take part.

Participants were recruited through King's College London Research Recruitment Circular, social media platforms and lived experience networks, allowing for a diverse range of participants to take part.

The concept of information power (Malterud et al., 2016) was followed as an alternative to data saturation. Information power focuses on study characteristics (i.e. aim, specificity, analytic method, participant knowledge) to determine dataset size, with regular reflection on this during the data collection period.

The sample comprised twenty participants, including both parents and non-parents, which was relevant to exploring current and anticipated experiences of reproductive decision-making. Participant characteristics are reported in Table 1. Conversations with healthcare providers were also discussed, as well as opinions on availability of support. The full topic guide for the broader study was developed in collaboration with a lived experience advisory group. A pilot interview was conducted to assess length and suitability of the topic guide.

Demographic and background information were obtained through an online Qualtrics survey prior to the interview. The participant information sheet and informed consent form were displayed prior to this on Qualtrics, with full consent required before completing the survey. The full interview lasted approximately one hour, and participants were given a £20 voucher as a recognition of their time.

Analysis

Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke's six-step reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006 , 2019). Reflexive thematic analysis offers an interpretative approach for exploring patterns of meaning across participants. This was appropriate for the study aim, which was to understand how people with PMDD made sense of reproductive decision-making. Reflexive thematic analysis was also a suitable method for the feminist critical realist framing of the study, as analysis considered both participants' accounts, as well as how broader norms and systems shaped the meaning of these experiences. This means themes were actively developed through engagement with the data, rather than as pre-existing findings. Inductive analysis was used, promoting a data-driven approach and giving more power to the participants' voices.

Analysis began with repeated reading of the transcripts and note-taking to support familiarisation with the data. JD then coded the sections of the dataset relevant to reproductive decision-making. This included semantic codes, such as "concerns about parenting while symptomatic" or "lack of information", as well as latent codes, for example "negotiating the good mother ideal" or "weighing desire against risk". Codes were reviewed and developed into initial themes, which were discussed with the research team (CJ, CAW, HZ). A PMDD lived experience advisory group of five individuals also contributed to theme development and refinement. The group met at three points during the project, including an analysisfocused session after initial coding and theme development. During this collaborative online session, thematic maps and selected anonymised quotes were shared and discussed. Reflections from the group shaped several aspects of the analysis, such as highlighting the focus on PMDD as both relational and isolating and reframing intergenerational concerns to better reflect the more positive aspects discussed by some participants. Following these discussions, the themes were refined and four final themes were developed and defined.

Positionality statement

The first author (JD), who conducted 8 (40%) of the interviews and led the thematic analysis, is a cisgender, heterosexual, White woman. This positioned her as both an insider and outsider in relation to participants' accounts and therefore this may have impacted how interviews were perceived or interpreted. The first author discussed reflexivity with a supervisory team (CAW, CJ, HZ), who bring multidisciplinary clinical and academic perspectives, including clinical experience of caring for women with PMDD in the perinatal period, and research expertise in both PMDD and choices around parenthood.

Ethics

Ethical approval was granted by King's College London Health Faculties (Blue) Research Ethics Subcommittee (reference: HR/DP-24/25-46572). Participants were provided with information signposting to sources of support, and a distress protocol was also developed in case additional support was required.

Analysis

Overview of themes Four themes were developed: 1.

PMDD as a whole-family experience 2. Intergenerational awareness and responsibility 3. Assessing readiness and capacity for parenthood 4. Self-advocacy and negotiating an unsupportive system Together, these themes suggest that PMDD shaped reproductive decision-making not only through symptoms themselves, but through how participants imagined the relational, intergenerational and personal consequences of pregnancy and parenthood.

Across the analysis, there was frequent reference towards a "good mother" ideal, which has been used here to reflect gendered expectations of what motherhood "should" look like, situating participants' decisions within societal norms and narratives.

This ideal helped explain why reproductive decisionmaking often carried significant moral weight, with participants considering whether they could be the kind of parent they felt they should be while living with PMDD. The "good mother" ideal connected the four themes in different ways. In theme 1, it appeared through concerns about the possible impact of PMDD on partners and family life. In theme 2, it was reflected in participants' sense of responsibility towards future children. In theme 3, it shaped how participants assessed their own capacity for parenthood, while in theme 4, it was complicated by the lack of specific information and support.

Theme 1: PMDD as a whole-family experience This theme explores how PMDD shaped reproductive decision-making due to the ways in which participants thought about the possible impact of symptoms on partners, children and family life.

Decisions were often made within a whole-family lens, with considerations as to how PMDD affected those around them. For example, whether they would be emotionally available, whether they perceived Reproductive Decision-Making in PMDD 11 that they would place too much pressure on their partner, and the possible impact of their symptoms on children. One participant shared that they were concerned that: "I might just take it out on my child a bit much"

Another described concern that the anger they currently directed towards their partner during symptomatic phases could also affect future children:

"I directly put that rage towards my partner, I don't want to do that to children"

Participants who were already parents spoke about their current experiences. Some spoke about becoming more irritable or overwhelmed during symptomatic phases and described feeling guilty about how this affected their children: "I like snap at the children, speak to them more harshly"

Symptoms of PMDD which made participants want to withdraw from social situations sometimes also had knock-on effects for the child's participation in social activities: "I just don't want to socialise with the other parents… that affects him because he wants to go out with his friends"

This appeared to create a gap between the kind of parent they described wanting to be and the way they sometimes felt when experiencing PMDD symptoms. These accounts reflected the weight of expectations associated with the "good mother" ideal, particularly the idea that mothers should be consistently emotionally available, patient and able to protect children from distress: "I just get very hopeless...that then feeds into how you feel about family life and about your parenting"

Participants who were not parents spoke about similar worries they associated with parenting and how this contributed to reproductive decision-making:

"And what if I rage and I have to look after them at the same time?"

However, family was also a crucial support system for many participants. Supportive partners, and effective communication, reconnection and repair after symptomatic periods were described to be important, and some participants discussed how this made the idea of parenthood feel possible. Being able to survive challenging experiences as a family was seen to strengthen relationships and support for one another, and this was reflected in how participants spoke about hope in reproductive decisionmaking: "Connection is one of the things that helps me" "There would also be somebody else taking care of the child...

I think it's manageable" Therefore, while participants spoke about the difficulties in parenting with PMDD, and how this impacted reproductive decisions, it was often a matter of weighing up difficulties against a system of whole-family support.

A tension within this theme was that PMDD was often described relationally but also experienced as isolating. Participants recognised that PMDD affected those around them, but also described withdrawing from others, feeling alone with their symptoms, or struggling to explain what they were experiencing: "It impacts my relationships…I just feel like…everybody's out to kind of get me…I broke up with my boyfriend in that kind of period but we're back together because a week later I was like, I didn't need to do that"

This could mean that at times, it was harder to access support from family, and it was in these moments of isolation where complex and emotional reproductive decisions felt much more challenging.

This theme focuses on how reproductive decision-making was viewed in relation to own childhood experiences, as well as experiences for future potential children. Considering their own childhood, some participants reflected that they thought their own mothers experienced PMDD, and spoke about the impact this had on their childhood, seeing their mother withdraw for periods of time or having their other parent "shield" them from her during symptomatic periods. Some participants were anxious of repeating patterns; sharing that: "I was like a bit scared of my mum...

I just wouldn't have seen her for like days... I don't want my children to have that experience"

However, others also discussed their childhood experiences as reassuring. Participants who felt their mother had experienced symptoms of PMDD spoke about how they had a childhood filled with love and care, and participants were confident they would be able to provide a similar nurturing environment if they chose to have a child: "My mum has had it and she managed to raise me...not really that much of a worry"

Looking toward the future, some participants spoke about feeling a sense of moral responsibility to avoid passing PMDD on to future children. They also mentioned how difficult they found experiencing symptoms of PMDD, and with uncertainty as to the genetic nature of the condition, did not want to risk passing PMDD onto a future child. This was a significant worry for some and was sometimes the catalyst for reproductive decisions: "Passing on what I described as debilitating...genes...

I did not wish to do that" "If I have...a female child, then they might get PMDD, and that makes it quite sad"

However, positives of such awareness were also shared. Some participants felt that awareness of PMDD could be protective. Knowing about PMDD, having a name for it and understanding symptoms could make it possible to respond differently to a future child's needs. Participants discussed hope for the future, with more research hopefully bringing better treatment or support, making the possibility of their child experiencing PMDD less worrisome:

"I would have all this knowledge about [PMDD]…and I think science will…progress"

This theme links to the "good mother" ideal through expectations of maternal protection and responsibility. Participants' concerns about inheritance or repeating difficult family patterns demonstrated a sense of responsibility towards a future child. However, participants did not only imagine protection through avoiding parenthood. For some, their own awareness of PMDD offered the possibility of creating a different family environment or responding differently to symptoms.

Theme 3: Assessing readiness and capacity for parenthood This theme explores how participants assessed whether, when and how parenthood might feel possible while living with PMDD. PMDD did not lead to one clear reproductive pathway. Instead, it shaped how participants weighed up risk, desire, uncertainty, loss and hope when imagining their future. For some, it was a major factor in deciding not to have children, or in delaying parenthood.

"I decided not to have children. That was a sobering decision…a lot of my aggression stemmed from this particular condition…it has nothing to do with the maternal instinct" For others, it was one consideration among many: "[PMDD] definitely didn't put me off...we just waited a little longer" While for some participants, regardless of whether they wanted children or not, it didn't feel like a factor in the decision: "I'm not sure I can see a way that PMDD has influenced my thinking around that" "I'm not sure that [PMDD] would influence the process of having children"

Participants discussed how the intensity of symptoms, as well as the cross-cycle symptom variation, might impact their capacity to cope with the demands of parenting. Participants expressed that they would often revisit reproductive decisions and would assess how stable they currently felt, as well as how they envisaged future treatment and greater awareness shaping their experiences: "I just feel like I'll be able to grow by then and I'll be able to manage a way more…I'm gonna be hopefully way more equipped"

Medication and hormonal treatment also shaped how participants thought about reproductive decisionmaking. For some, symptom management of PMDD relied largely on hormonal contraception, with the prospect of pausing this for a potential pregnancy feeling like an impossibility: "I am like at a period in my life where I feel like I'm scared to not be on the pill" Many participants experienced Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS, previously PCOS) as a co-occurring condition, which is known to have implications for fertility, thus contributing to decision-making. For some of these participants, questions about whether, when and how to have children were not only shaped by PMDD, but also by concerns about fertility. This could create an additional sense of pressure, as participants felt they may need to make decisions about parenthood before feeling emotionally ready:

"I don't know if it's the PMDD or the mix of the PMDD and the PCOS…and I don't know what my fertility will be like because of my PCOS…it's like…an added anxiety and pressure"

Participants also raised concerns about the postpartum period, with worries as to how their mental health would be impacted by such a significant hormonal transition. Some participants were aware of the increased risk of postpartum depression in PMDD: "PMDD like really has just made me very fearful of having children...made me decide not to have children...

I worry that I would be more predisposed to having some form of postnatal condition"; while others feared significant PMDD symptom exacerbation following childbirth: "I don't know if pregnancy can make it worse or not...like after pregnancy it can get like 100 times worse"

These concerns did not always lead participants away from parenthood, but they did shape how they imagined the timing and conditions under which parenthood might be possible. On the other hand, a few of the participants who were mothers described their experiences of pregnancy as providing peace and stability, viewing pregnancy and the immediate postpartum period as a positive period with no regular menstrual cycle to produce PMDD symptoms: "My mood was really stable and I felt kind of really calm throughout the pregnancies"

Having to consider one's own readiness and capacity for parenthood can be an emotional subject, with participants discussing feelings of sadness and grief in relation to decision-making. For some, decisions about delaying or not pursuing parenthood were framed through concern for a future child's wellbeing: "I still feel it would have been so unfair to the child, first of all.

And yeah, this was a dilemma that I weighed on for years. This wasn't some sudden decision…Why have a child to satisfy myself?"

A sense of hope was also noticeable, with participants feeling that with more time to learn about PMDD and how to best manage it, their readiness or capacity for parenthood would change.

"In trying to manage it and control it right now, when I'm younger, in the hope that when I actually do…think about pregnancy and having children, hopefully it will be better managed"

Overall, the "good mother" ideal was particularly visible in this theme. Participants evaluated whether they felt able to meet a child's needs while living with PMDD. For some, delaying parenthood or deciding not to have children was framed by participants not as a lack of desire, but as an attempt to make a responsible decision in relation to a future child.

"I think I would not be a good enough mother, having to deal with these issues"

Theme 4: Self-advocacy and negotiating an unsupportive system This theme focuses on the role of healthcare, information and support in reproductive decision-making.

Participants discussed the difficulty of making reproductive decisions without adequate PMDD-specific guidance. Participants sought information about symptom changes in pregnancy and the postpartum period, whether PMDD introduced specific susceptibility to any perinatal conditions, and medication usage during the perinatal period: "If I start medication, and like if I plan to, I don't know, get pregnant next year or the year after like would that be an issue or if you're like breastfeeding?"

Most participants were unsure, but doubtful, about the existence of any specific guidance on PMDD in the perinatal period: "There's not a leaflet about PMDD let alone family planning about it"

Participants often reported that the only information they had seen was anecdotal, in community groups or online forums: "I have also read sort of anecdotally about people whose PMDD has become worse since they have had children"

Some participants had actively sought advice or guidance from a healthcare professional. Participants often described healthcare professionals as lacking knowledge about PMDD, dismissing concerns or offering advice that felt overly simplistic and unprofessional. For example, discussing pregnancy as a route to symptom relief, without attention given to postpartum mental health or the practical realities of caring for a child: "The gynaecologist...was almost encouraging me to have a child because she was saying your symptoms improved during pregnancy...but what about afterwards?"

These encounters left some participants feeling unsupported, or as though they had to understand the mechanisms of PMDD for themselves. Other participants discussed negative experiences with healthcare professionals when seeking information about more permanent PMDD treatment options, such as a hysterectomy. They were made to feel this was not an appropriate treatment, as they were yet to have a child: "The most discussion I've had is with a gynaecologist and saying…can I get a hysterectomy like is that possible?

And they said to me well, you haven't had children yet"

In order to obtain appropriate care or onward referral, participants had to endure constant self-advocacy, educating healthcare professionals and negotiating between services. Most participants did not feel that they had access to informative healthcare support, however one participant did describe an appointment with a specialist who provided some PMDD-specific information relevant to the perinatal period.

Reproductive decision-making was therefore not only shaped by PMDD symptoms, but also by the difficulty of navigating systems that did not understand the relevance of PMDD to these conversations.

When it came to what more they wanted from healthcare professionals, participants were clear: they wanted information and time to discuss their concerns with knowledgeable professionals: "There needs to be a lot of information to support women making this decision because it is a huge deal…so I think, you know, whoever it may be, GP, gynaecologist, psychiatrist, you know, should be giving the pros and cons" Without this, they were left to make uncertain decisions.

Participants highlighted research as a beneficial tool for informing healthcare professionals' knowledge: "Well, it would be helpful to start with some research in the area to gather the thoughts of women like myself"

This theme extends the "good mother" ideal into the healthcare context. Participants described uncertainty around pregnancy, postpartum mental health and family life, alongside limited PMDD-specific guidance from healthcare professionals. This placed responsibility heavily on the individual, as participants were often left to search for information, educate professionals and advocate for their own care.

Some participants discussed healthcare encounters that centred future fertility or motherhood, for example questioning treatment preferences because a participant had not yet had children, which is reflective of the "good mother" ideal.

Discussion

This study explored how people with PMDD experience reproductive decision-making. Four themes were constructed: PMDD as a whole-family experience; intergenerational awareness and responsibility; assessing readiness and capacity for parenthood; and self-advocacy and negotiating an unsupportive system. The findings suggest that while PMDD can shape reproductive decisions in complex ways, it does not lead towards one single outcome. Across interviews, decision-making was shaped not only by symptoms themselves, but by relational impact, feelings of responsibility towards future children, assessments of own parenting capacity and experiences of limited healthcare support.

The moral weight of reproductive decision-making expressed by participants in this study can be understood in relation to good mother ideology and the wider concept of intensive mothering. Good mother ideology positions mothers as responsible for prioritising children's needs and child-rearing above Reproductive Decision-Making in PMDD 20 all else (Williamson et al., 2023), with intensive mothering further emphasising self-sacrifice, emotional availability and responsibility for children's wellbeing (Hays, 1996). While these concepts have been widely used to understand experiences of motherhood, they have received limited attention in relation to PMDD. The present study therefore extends this literature by showing how expectations of emotionally available, protective and child-focused motherhood may shape decision-making while living with PMDD.

This was evident in concerns about the impact of symptoms on children and partners, fears about passing PMDD on and assessments of whether participants felt stable or prepared enough to parent in the way they wanted.

These findings also have implications for how voluntary childlessness is understood in the context of PMDD. Motherhood is often assumed as expected, natural or desirable (Ussher, 2005), however in this study, some participants were choosing not to have children or to delay parenthood. This echoes decisions seen in other health conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease (Mountifield et al., 2009), rheumatic diseases (Manouchehri et al., 2024) and bipolar disorder (Dolman et al., 2016), where decisions to delay or avoid pregnancy were linked to concerns about medication, parenting capacity, stigma and relapse risk.

Interestingly, research has also shown that individuals with endometriosis are regularly encouraged to conceive as a means of symptom reduction or relief (Sirohi et al., 2023), despite this not being backed by scientific evidence. This was echoed in the present study, with one participant's account of being encouraged to consider pregnancy because PMDD symptoms may improve during this period. Although cyclical hormonal fluctuations remit during pregnancy, given the increased risk of perinatal depression in individuals with PMDD (Yang et al., 2024), healthcare professionals should avoid framing pregnancy as symptom management. Another participant described a healthcare professional dismissing her desire for a hysterectomy, one of the surgical options recognised as a final treatment option for PMDD (Carlini et al., 2022), due to her not yet having children. This suggests that some healthcare professionals may be prioritising potential future motherhood over patient preferences, with an assumption that fertility preservation should take precedence.

Another key finding of this study is that reproductive decision-making in PMDD was often relational rather than purely individual. Participants did not only consider whether they personally wanted children, but also how PMDD in this context may impact partners and other family members. For example, some expressed concern that during their luteal phase, they would be placing too much parenting burden on their partners. However, some participants also spoke about how important support from their partner or wider family was, and how this made the potential of parenthood feel possible, as they would not be managing alone. In previous work, supportive partners, open communication and a team-based approach to child-rearing helped some mothers navigate the pressures of good mother ideology (Williamson et al., 2023). Similarly, here, reproductive decision-making was partially shaped by the presence or absence of relational support. Given this, support around PMDD and reproductive decision-making may therefore need to include discussion of partners and wider family support, rather than focusing only on individual symptom management.

Strengths and limitations

The interviews involved both parents and non-parents, allowing the analysis to explore current or past decision-making experiences alongside imagined futures. The involvement of a lived experience advisory group also supported reflexive engagement with the developing analysis, particularly around language, sensitivity and avoiding an overly deterministic account of PMDD and parenthood.

However, the findings should be interpreted in relation to the study sample and context. Participants were predominantly highly educated, and all described their gender identity as female. The analysis may therefore not fully explore how PMDD and reproductive decision-making is experienced by different sociocultural contexts. In addition, the use of the "good mother" ideal as an interpretative lens largely reflects a Western view of motherhood and may not be applicable across different cultures.

Recommendations for research and clinical practice Future research should explore healthcare professionals' knowledge and experiences of supporting people with PMDD around the preconception and perinatal periods. This would help identify training needs and opportunities for clinical guidance. Further work should also include more diverse participant groups, including people from a wider range of socioeconomic backgrounds, people living in non-Western contexts, and trans and non-binary people who experience PMDD. Finally, given the relational nature of the findings, research with partners and family members may also help explore how PMDD is understood and supported within family systems.

These findings have several implications for clinical practice. Importantly, PMDD should be recognised as relevant to preconception care, the perinatal period and reproductive decision-making. People with PMDD need non-judgemental support which acknowledges one's own reproductive autonomy. This should include opportunities to discuss symptom management, medication, pregnancy, postpartum mental health, parenting concerns and family support. Accessible PMDD-specific patient information or decision aids may also help to reduce uncertainty and support more informed decision-making.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study found that PMDD can shape reproductive decision-making in complex and varied ways. Participants' decisions were not only influenced by symptoms, but by concerns about family life, parenting capacity and the availability of support. The findings demonstrate the need for PMDD-specific, non-judgemental support, so that people with PMDD can make informed decisions that align with their own values and circumstances.
