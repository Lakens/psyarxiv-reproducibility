Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Introduction and Overview

Nature, red in tooth and claw, but also warm in heart and paw, has built within the human brain the capacity to feel emotions (from movere: to move). When they're working adaptively, they impel us to move, to act. But it has also built within us a capacity to reason (from reri: to reckon), which provides us with more behavioral options. This dichotomy was recognized by ancient thinkers across civilizations, long before the Greeks formalized it as a conflict between the irrational and rational parts of the soul. Since then, philosophers have debated whether our moral sense arises from emotion, reason, or some interaction of the two. This was all a matter of philosophy until the sciences started shining their light on these questions -beginning with Darwin, but particularly in the 20th century, when we began locating emotion and cognition in the brain. In recent decades, with advances in evolutionary theory, neuroimaging, and the psychological and cognitive sciences, the debate has continued (e.g., Greene et al., 2001; Greene, 2008 Greene, , 2013;; Haidt, 2001).

Moral philosophy has also wrestled with another tension, though not for nearly as long. From Kant to Rawls and Bentham to Singer, the field has struggled with an apparent incompatibility between two of its three central theories: consequentialism and deontology. Crudely, what 3 makes an act or decision right can be answered in terms of its outcomes (consequentialism) or the duties it upholds or violates (deontology). While the reasons for this tension are overdetermined, one, perhaps, is the constraining nature of moral philosophy's inherited constructs and disciplinary boundaries. 4 Integrating insights from neuroscience, cognitive science, and the evolutionary sciences with 5 new theoretical frameworks for understanding the underlying structure of our moral sense (and morality more broadly), adaptive moral theory (AMT) reinterprets these longstanding tensions. Neural Basis for the CARE/Duty (C/D) Framework

AMT treats the CARE system and the duty network as functionally distinct neural networksdistributed rather than localized in discrete brain regions (cf. Moll et al., 2005). The neural underpinnings proposed for each are provisional and open to revision.

Despite longstanding concerns about oversimplifying hemispheric specialization (Nielsen et al., 2013), research continues to support broad differences in processing style between the hemispheres, while acknowledging their deeply interconnected nature (Gazzaniga, 2000 (Gazzaniga, , 2005;; Goldberg & Costa, 1981; McGilchrist, 2019). Evidence of functional lateralization suggests the right hemisphere is more specialized for affective, contextual, holistic, and socially attuned processing, and the left for analytical, abstract, sequential, and symbolic processing, including language (Gazzaniga, 2005; McGilchrist, 2019; Schore, 2019). Self-awareness, empathy, and identification with others appear to depend substantially on right-hemisphere resources (Decety & Chaminade, 2003). Thus, AMT hypothesizes that the CARE system is relatively rightlateralized, and the duty network relatively left-lateralized -a matter of relative emphasis rather than strict division.

The CARE system proposed here draws on convergent findings from affective neuroscience, attachment research, and social bonding literatures (e.g., Panksepp, 2011; Fisher et al., 2006; Feldman, 2017; Churchland, 2011; Schore, 2022). Candidate core regions include the ventral tegmental area (VTA), nucleus accumbens (NAcc), medial preoptic area (MPOA), bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), periaqueductal gray (PAG), rostral anterior cingulate cortex (rACC), amygdala, and temporoparietal junction (TPJ). The VTA, NAcc, MPOA, BNST, and PAG support mammalian caregiving through reward, nurturance, attachment, and protective responses. The rACC contributes to affective processing and responses to distress, while the amygdala and TPJ contribute social salience and attunement to others.

Modulated by oxytocin, prolactin, dopamine, endogenous opioids, and vagal tone (Porges, 2011), these circuits support empathy, attachment, and caregiving behavior. Oxytocin, the most studied of these, has been found to increase empathy (Hurlemann et al., 2010; for a review, see Barchi-Ferreira & Osório, 2021), which is associated with prosocial behaviors and bonding with ingroup members (Feldman, 2017). But oxytocin also promotes defensive responses to perceived threats against in-group members (De Dreu et al., 2010) -a "tend-and-defend" pattern consistent with the CARE system's partiality.

The Duty Network

The duty network circuits proposed here draw on convergent findings from moral cognition, norm processing, and cognitive control literatures (e.g., Moll et al., 2005; Greene et al., 2004; Buckholtz & Marois, 2012).

Candidate core regions include the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), anterior and frontopolar PFC, superior temporal sulcus (STS), and temporoparietal junction (TPJ), modulated by dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems. The dlPFC and anterior PFC support cognitive control, working memory, and the representation of abstract rules. The STS and TPJ support the reading of social information and others' intentions. These regions are plausible candidates for representing, maintaining, applying, and detecting violations of social rules.

As a distributed functional network, the DN draws on semantic and memory systems, interfaces with language-related circuitry, and recruits affective systems in response to rule violations. Both systems draw on the TPJ for information about others' minds, recruiting it bilaterally, with a right-hemisphere emphasis for CARE and a left-hemisphere emphasis for the duty network. AMT further hypothesizes that conflict between the two systems could engage conflict-monitoring circuitry, such as the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC; Greene et al., 2004).

The Evolutionary Turn

There is a long tradition of viewing morality through a Darwinian lens (E.O. Wilson, D.S. Wilson, de Waal, Rée, among others) -one that naturally started with Darwin himself. In The Descent of Man, he argued that moral faculties evolved from animal social instincts (1871/2004), an idea developed in his private notebooks: "Looking at Man, as a Naturalist would at any other Mammiferous animal, it may be concluded that he has parental, conjugal and social instincts, and perhaps others . . . These instincts consist of a feeling of love & sympathy or benevolence to the object in question"; and observed that in other animals these instincts could lead individuals to "forget" themselves -aiding and defending others at their own expense (Barrett et al., 1987, p. 619). And he expressed sympathy for the notion that our moral intuition was the result of our ancestors' accumulated experiences of utility (1871/2004, pp. 101-102).

Darwin also observed that a high standard of morality gives little or no advantage to an individual over other members of his tribe, yet "an advancement in the standard of morality . . . will certainly give an immense advantage to one tribe over another" (1871/2004, p. 166). This perspective -group selection -is a minority view in the evolutionary human sciences, though multilevel selection theory, which builds on it, has a growing number of proponents (D.S. Wilson & Wilson, 2007; Sober & Wilson, 1998). With these group-level lenses, prosocial emotions, altruism, and moral norms can be understood as adaptations shaped by selection among groups, rather than solely as strategies serving individual genetic fitness.

The most influential recent evolutionary accounts, however, build on the dominant view in evolutionary theory -inclusive fitness and kin selection -and increasingly center on cooperation. Evolutionary moral psychologist Oliver Scott Curry states that "morality is a collection of biological and cultural solutions to the problems of cooperation and conflict recurrent in human social life; and game theory reveals what those problems and solutions are" (Curry, 2016, p. 44). His theory of Morality as Cooperation (MAC) identifies at least seven distinct types of cooperation: allocation of resources to kin, coordination to mutual advantage, social exchange, dominance, submission, division of disputed resources, and recognition of prior possession. Similarly, Greene asserts that "morality is a set of psychological adaptations that allow otherwise selfish individuals to reap the benefits of cooperation" (2013, p. 23).

In contrast, I define morality as follows:

Morality is the realm of affairs involved in ensuring, benefitting, increasing, and sustaining the survival and well-being of other living entities. Evolved adaptations such as the emotions and the mammalian CARE system, as well as higher-order cognitive rules (implicit and explicit), motivate, regulate, constrain, and govern behavioral efforts within this realm.

While the two theories -MAC and AMT -are not incompatible, they focus on different parts of the moral landscape. AMT agrees that MAC's seven types of cooperation are part of the picture; they can be understood through CARE, the duty network, or their interaction. But, while Curry acknowledges that moral motivation, values, and emotion are relevant to morality, he argues that "'care' -like 'altruism' -is a generic term that does not distinguish between forms of prosocial behavior with different ultimate and proximate roots" (Curry et al., 2019, p. 50). 'Care' is generic. But the CARE/Duty framework is not. It can make those distinctions. For example, kin care evolved via inclusive fitness -direct fitness through parental investment, indirect fitness through kin selection -proximately mediated by the CARE system, and, later, by the DN's filial obligation. Reciprocal altruism is ultimately an exchange strategy among nonkin. Its rules, such as promise-keeping and debt repayment, are proximately implemented by the duty network (with its origins in CARE).

Curry further explains that care is not related to any specific type of cooperation and therefore predicts that it will not constitute one of the seven corresponding moral domains: family values, group loyalty, reciprocity, heroism, deference, fairness, and property rights (Curry et al., 2019). For AMT, CARE is not one of seven domains; it is one of morality's two fundamental systems -and it's the foundational one. Each of our theories identifies a different organizing feature of morality. For MAC, it's cooperation, so care is easy to write off; for AMT, it's care and concern for the well-being of others, mediated by the C/D framework, with cooperation among the norms the duty network codifies.

MAC's emphasis on cooperation and AMT's emphasis on care recall psychologist and ethicist Carol Gilligan's seminal challenge to moral psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg's theory of moral development, which placed reasoning about justice and rights at the highest stage, while treating care-based reasoning as less morally advanced. In her book In a Different Voice (1982), Gilligan wrote: "The moral imperative that emerges repeatedly in interviews with women is an injunction to care, a responsibility to discern and alleviate the 'real and recognizable trouble' of this world. For men, the moral imperative appears rather as an injunction to respect the rights of others and thus to protect from interference the rights to life and self-fulfillment" (p. 100). Just as Gilligan challenged a framework that privileged rules and fairness and undervalued care, AMT challenges evolutionary approaches that privilege cooperation and undervalue care. Not only is AMT a Kantian-Schopenhauerian synthesis, it is a Gilligan-Kohlberg synthesis.

These first principles -the duty to extend care (beneficence) and the duty to do no harm (nonmaleficence) -were codified in our earliest moral codes, from ancient Egyptian teachings like Ma'at, to African ubuntu philosophy, to Confucian ethics, as well as Judeo-Christian, Buddhist, Islamic, and Native American traditions -all centering love, compassion, and care. These traditions provide instructions on how to care for oneself, how to care for and treat others, and generally how to live a 'good' life: Don't harm another (by taking their life, wife, cow, or by deception). Of course, morality-as-cooperation proponents could argue that 17 cooperation is in here, too -and in places it is. But AMT predicts this; the duty network codifies cooperative norms, among many others.

While religious teachings explicitly codified care into moral principles, morality is deeply embedded in other cultural products, where moral themes arise organically and unconsciously (as evolutionary psychologists might predict of any evolved disposition). Storytelling -from ancient myths to modern films -has both unconscious and conscious forms. Indeed, evolutionary approaches to literature suggest that one adaptive function of stories is to expand and refine our capacity to process social information while safely exploring social conflicts and ethical dilemmas (Boyd, 2009). Stories are seldom about cooperation or coordination, but about love, sex, compassion, loyalty, sacrifice, betrayal, duty, honor, promises, and revenge. Fiction, Boyd argues, uniquely cultivates a moral sense.

Moral themes may be explicit and purposeful -as in fables, folk wisdom, protest art, and philosophy -aimed at shaping moral virtue and sensibility. Kant's categorical imperative (CI) is a later and more abstract derivation of CARE: "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end" (Kant, 1785 (Kant, /1998, p. 38), p. 38). The rule is, essentially, do not treat others merely as instruments, but rather as having worth in themselves. It makes care a duty.

The duty to not lie/tell the truth is likewise plausibly derived from CARE. Deception prevents access to accurate information, potentially exposing someone to harm. Central to many of these codes and stories are injunctions against boundary transgression -CARE formalized by Duty: justice. While likely all ancient religious texts codified CARE into 18 duties and responsibilities, some texts also laid down laws and rights. The ancient Babylonian Code of Hammurabi exemplifies this explicitly with 282 specific laws that aim to redress harms done (retributive justice) with punishment to the violators. The text explicitly says that prince Hammurabi, having been given the power to do so by the gods, shall "bring about the rule of righteousness in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil-doers; so that the strong should not harm the weak; so that [he] should rule . . . the land, to further the well-being of mankind" (King translation, emphasis added). 19 Here, justice and fairness are DN-based rules derived from CARE. Schopenhauer recognized this. Both "'the duties of law' [do no harm] and the 'duties of love' [helping] have their root in natural compassion. . . . It is original and immediate, and lies in human nature itself" (1903, p. 177). Philosopher-theologian Joseph Fletcher, whose situation ethics is taken up below, went further: "Love and justice are the same, for justice is love distributed, nothing else" (1966, p. 87).

Indeed, CARE's affiliative impulse -empathy and concern for others -is not only at the root of the DN's justice principles, it also functions as a proximate mechanism motivating (and often regulating) fairness. As noted earlier, concern for others appears early, before rules: toddlers help others achieve their goals without prompting or reward (Warneken & Tomasello, 2009). Young children likewise show sensitivity to distributions that disadvantage them as early as age two (proto-CARE); by age four, they will forgo personal gain to protest unfair treatment before they can articulate principles of justice (Sánchez Hernández & Hyde, 2026). As philosopher John 20 Rawls observes, "the acquisition of the sense of justice takes place in stages connected with the growth of knowledge and understanding" (1971, p. 433). It takes time to grow a prefrontal cortex and to learn cultural rules. Future work will offer a fuller treatment of justice -and address rights, autonomy, and boundaries.

Philosophical Resonances with AMT

Perhaps AMT's closest philosophical resonance is with situation ethics. Developed by Fletcher, its underlying structure was the Christian ethic of agape (unconditional love). Fletcher argued 21 that moral rules ('illuminators') should generally guide behavior, but always yield when the situation warrants it. AMT essentially naturalizes Fletcher's religious agape as our evolved CARE system, and his flexible illuminators as the DN (Fletcher, 1966). A related position in contemporary analytic philosophy is threshold deontology: the view that deontological duties are binding but may be overridden when sufficiently weighty moral considerations arise.

Imagine someone gives heroin to a person about to be executed. The moral evaluation involves weighing immediate suffering (registered by CARE) against breaking the law (registered by the DN) -in this case, designed to protect long-term well-being and the broader social good. Since the temporal context eliminates future considerations, CARE overrides the rule meant to protect future well-being. Indeed, primal, instinctual CARE often overrides the duty network: a healthcare worker breaching confidentiality to protect safety, a parent stealing food for their hungry child, lying to protect a loved one from danger -ad infinitum. In AMT's terms, what we experience as moral intuition -simply feeling or knowing what is right -can arise from CARE, Duty, or their interaction. 22 Eastern traditions also reveal this dual structure. Buddhism distinguishes karuna (compassion) and metta (loving-kindness) -care-based responses to suffering -from sila, its codified rules of conduct and obligation (Harvey, 2000). And, like situation ethics and threshold deontology, Buddhism (particularly Mahayana) allows overriding rule-following when it better serves the alleviation of suffering.

Hindu philosophy offers perhaps the clearest pre-theoretical expression of AMT: karuna (CARE) expresses the affective desire to reduce suffering, while dharma (Duty) represents an extensive system of role-based obligations. In The Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna's love and compassion for his kin conflict directly with Krishna's injunction to go into battle and slay them. Arjuna says to Krishna: "My senses are overcome by the weakness of pity, and my mind is confused about duty. Please tell me what is better for me" (2. 7, trans. Prasad, 1988).

Fletcher's theological view may explain why situation ethics never gained substantial traction in secular moral philosophy, 21 despite its intuitive appeal. AMT proposes that a similar structure emerges naturalistically from our evolved neurobiology. Confucian philosophy also reveals a parallel C/D duality: ren -benevolence, empathy, and concern for others' well-being; li -norms, roles, rituals. But li requires ren lest it be hollow (Confucius, 1998, 3.3).

Across time and cultures, the C/D moral structure has been intuited and expressed, though an extensive search (beyond the scope of this paper) would likely reveal further parallels.

Moral Decision-Making

Our everyday lives are filled with moral decisions which often involve weighing self-interest (proto-CARE) against rules and obligations (Duty) and care for others (CARE) -as in deciding whether to break a law, keep a promise, help a friend, betray a spouse, or tell the truth.

Moral decision-making also varies considerably across individuals, reflecting among other factors, differences in cognitive processing and norm sensitivity, as well as in affective sensitivity, regulation, and orientation. For instance, some individuals, whether by nature, nurture, or both, are more empathic -weighted more toward others than self. At the other end of the spectrum are those with a self-centered orientation with little or no care for others. In AMT's terms, someone with this disposition would be operating from a One Life Matters (only) moral frame. Moral frames can be used for any moral dilemma, real or abstract, but become particularly salient in abstract and hypothetical dilemmas, discussed below.

The CARE system responds directly to welfare in the here and now. Seeing someone drowning, for example, may produce an immediate impulse to rush in to save them -or inhibit us from pushing someone from a footbridge, even if it saves many lives. But what about the future? Prospection -the capacity to mentally simulate possible futures (Suddendorf & Corballis, 2007; Seligman et al., 2013) -enables us to think about outcomes, whether they be of a moral or nonmoral kind. In the moral realm, CARE can extend forward in time. When deciding whether to keep a secret, for example, we may weigh our duty to honor confidentiality against the potential future harm that could come from not sharing the information. AMT calls this projection of concern for well-being into the future CARE prospection -consequentialist reasoning.

Moral Frames

When subjects wrestle with moral dilemmas, they may do so from one or more of eight moral frames (cognitive schemas), which map a space of moral orientations toward life. In everyday 23 moral life, the particularist frame Some Lives Matter (more than others) often predominates, 24 especially its relationship-based arm where valuation may arise from CARE-based bonds or Duty-based obligations. In abstract dilemmas, however, the absence of individuating information may favor a shift toward the universalist All Lives Matter (equally) frame, in which every life counts equally. ALM is not confined to abstract dilemmas, however; its impartiality principle can also inform everyday judgments of fairness, such as the equal distribution of resources, the domain of the shared-resources rule (Fig. 1), from which ALM could have derived.

Some frames happen to reflect stable orientations or psychological dispositions -as in the case of One Life Matters (only) -but here, the frames are perspectives or stances one might adopt when reasoning through a particular dilemma. Therefore, someone could be a universalist -All Lives Matter (equally) -by dint of religion, yet operate from a different frame when confronted with moral decisions.

In abstract sacrificial dilemmas, CARE's merit-based arm of Some Lives Matter (more than others) may favor saving a doctor over an anonymous bystander because of the greater good she may go on to do. This valuation may arise intuitively, through learned associations between doctors and the well-being of others, or through CARE prospection -thinking about the lives she might save. The duty network can produce similar consequentialist outcomes with an established rule such as a protocol prioritizing healthcare workers. Such rules are efficient -CARE prospection codified -producing well-being benefits without case-by-case consequentialist reasoning.

The moral frame All Lives Matter (equally), however, is instantiated in the duty network by a rule: count every life equally. The save more lives algorithm follows: five outweigh one. The result is a consequentialist outcome reached without consequentialist reasoning. The Trouble with Trolleys

Life Matters (but it's not in one's control) is placed in the nonmoral sphere because moral agency is heteronomous -deferred Philosopher Philippa Foot (1967) introduced the trolley (tram) problem to explore the principle of double effect: that causing harm as a side effect of good intentions and actions differs morally from causing harm as a means to good ends. Judith Jarvis Thomson (1985) later examined trolley problems and other sacrificial dilemmas in her classic paper "The Trolley Problem," including bystander at the switch and fat man (also known as large man/footbridge). These gedanken experiments, and the many variations that followed, have become central to moral philosophy and moral psychology, particularly in research on the apparent divide between deontological and consequentialist -and more specifically utilitarian -judgments.

In the standard switch dilemma, a trolley is barreling toward five people, but a bystander can pull a switch diverting it onto a side track where only one person stands. Remarkably, one study that included 42 countries and 70,000 participants found that 81% of respondents would pull the switch (Awad et al., 2020). Such responses are conventionally classified as utilitarian and often understood as reflective of consequentialist reasoning -weighing outcomes to bring about the greater good. AMT hypothesizes instead that some, perhaps many, of these respondents have shifted to the ALM frame and are running the duty network's save more lives algorithm. If so, choosing the switch would be consequentialist in outcome but not in cognitive process. (Which process predominates is an empirical question.) 29 Greene's dual-process theory proposes that social-emotional and cognitive processes both contribute to moral judgment and can come into conflict (Greene et al., 2001 (Greene et al., , 2004;; Greene, 2008). He distinguishes personal dilemmas, such as footbridge, in which one directly causes serious harm to an identifiable person, from impersonal ones, such as switch, which lack one or more of these features. For Greene, personal dilemmas elicit emotional responses that drive the deontological judgment (don't push); impersonal ones engage controlled cognition and yield the utilitarian judgment (pull the switch). This makes intuitive sense when using the emotion/reason dichotomy. But as AMT argues, morality should be carved at a different joint.

For AMT, the aversion to pushing in footbridge reflects the CARE system, potentially reinforced by the duty network's prohibition against harm. In switch, the 'personal' features that strongly recruit CARE are diminished, allowing rule-based judgment to play a greater role. One such rule is ALM's count every life equally, which yields the familiar five-over-one response. Thus, what looks like a utilitarian response need not arise from consequentialist reasoning.

Prospection is associated with default mode network activation (Buckner & Carroll, 2007; Spreng et al., 2009; Szpunar et al., 29 2007). Consequentialist judgments arising from CARE prospection should therefore recruit the DMN; identical judgments driven by the ALM rule should show less DMN engagement.

Neuroimaging findings are often taken to support Greene's interpretation. Greene and colleagues (2004) found increased dACC activity during difficult personal moral judgments and greater dlPFC activity associated with utilitarian judgments, interpreting these findings in terms of conflict and cognitive control. But perhaps dACC engagement reflects conflict between CARE and Duty -and the dlPFC activity, duty network rule processing. Further support for this possibility comes from Bago and De Neys (2019), who found that 'utilitarian' responses in sacrificial dilemmas can appear as the initial intuitive response rather than the product of slow deliberation. Thus, the fast response here may reflect rapid DN rule applicationconsequentialist in outcome, but not necessarily involving prospective evaluation.

Trolley problems chiefly bear on the contrast between ALM and SLM, but other moral frames may also shape responses. One is Life Matters (but it's not in one's control), the fatalist frame. A respondent who declines to pull the switch may not be following a prohibition against killing, as researchers might infer, but deferring moral agency to fate, the cosmos, or God. On this view, it is not one's place to determine who lives and who dies. Thus, the fatalist frame offers an interpretation of inaction that differs from those ordinarily considered in trolley research. The remaining frames may also illuminate sacrificial dilemmas and moral decision-making more broadly, but exploring their applications lies beyond the scope of this paper.

Conclusion

Adaptive moral theory integrates evolutionary science, neuroscience, and cognitive science to offer a new lens on our evolved moral sense, reframing enduring debates in moral philosophy. It posits two functional moral systems in humans -the CARE system and the duty network. The ancient CARE system is oriented toward the well-being of others; the phylogenetically newer duty network, toward rules, commitments, and obligations. Both the CARE system and the duty network can extend beyond their evolutionary origins. CARE reaches beyond its original relational targets (e.g., saving a stranger), the DN beyond the well-being it originally served (e.g., blunt truth where a white lie would be kinder). Both can also extend beyond the present: CARE through prospection, the DN through commitments. And each system's strength is also its liability: CARE's partiality can narrow into tribalism; the DN's rules can be applied harshly. When the systems conflict, situation ethics can help recalibrate.

The inherited philosophical debates about the binaries (consequentialism/deontology and emotion/cognition) have shaped what moral psychologists ask -and what they look for in the brain. Indeed, these framings led Greene (2008) to identify four empirical possibilities by crossing the two binaries -as if these were the only axes available -roughly associating cognition with consequentialist judgments and emotional responses with deontological judgments. AMT argues that this framing carves the moral mind and brain at the wrong joints. A division that better reflects our moral sense is functional: the CARE system and the duty network. Cognition and emotion are necessary for a moral sense, but they are not its operating systems. CARE is affective-motivational but recruits cognition, as in prospection; the duty network is rule-based, but its violation detection elicits emotions.

For AMT, although we may at times weigh well-being outcomes against duties, the two do not have parallel bases in the brain. Capacities central to deontology have a neural basis in the duty network, whereas consequentialist reasoning is a cognitive operation recruited by the CARE system (CARE prospection). Consequentialist outcomes need not involve consequentialist 30 reasoning: duty network rules can produce them as well.

AMT is also empirically testable. When Curry and colleagues conducted a large neuroimaging study, they found no associations between his seven moral domains and regional grey matter volume, leading them to call for theory-driven, a priori predictions -and functional rather than structural measures (Wilkinson et al., 2024). AMT offers exactly such predictions. It also advances a set of hypotheses and opens new lines of inquiry across philosophy, neuroscience, cognitive science, and psychology. Among them: 1.

Care, not cooperation, is foundational and fundamental to morality.

2. The duty network provides a fundamental neural basis for the human capacity to represent and apply moral duties and obligations -capacities central to deontology.

3. Consequentialist reasoning is CARE prospection -CARE's concern for well-being projected forward in time.

Compressed into duty network rules, that concern can yield consequentialist outcomes without consequentialist reasoning.

Just as consequentialist reasoning, on AMT's account, involves general cognitive capacities recruited by the CARE system for 30 prospection, what we call "moral cognition" may be better understood as general cognitive capacities engaged by the CARE system and duty network rather than a faculty in its own right. A fuller treatment awaits future work.
