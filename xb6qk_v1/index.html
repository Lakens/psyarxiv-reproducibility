To fix Exact P-Values

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

p > .05 In a first study, Zendle et al. (2021) surveyed international physical card game players recruited from English-language Subreddits and initially dismissed the relationship between purchasing physical card packs and problem gambling as not significant (r = 0.07, p > .05; this result is derived from a reanalysis of their publicly available data).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p > .05 In a first study, Zendle et al. (2021) surveyed international physical card game players recruited from English-language Subreddits and initially dismissed the relationship between purchasing physical card packs and problem gambling as not significant (r = 0.07, p > .05; this result is derived from a reanalysis of their publicly available data).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Gambling-like products share structural and psychological characteristics with legally regulated traditional gambling but are generally not regulated as gambling.

Virtual gambling-like products include video game loot boxes, whilst physical gambling-like products include blind boxes and card packs. Stakeholders are concerned about the potential harms of these globally popular products, including overspending and the normalization of gambling amongst children. We surveyed Mainland Chinese video game players (final N = 913) to investigate whether spending on various gambling-like products is associated with problem gambling severity. Replicating previous results, we found weak-but practically meaningfulassociations between problem gambling severity and both digital loot box and physical card pack spending. In contrast, the association between problem gambling and physical blind box spending was very weak and arguably not practically meaningful. Fundamental differences at the product characteristic-level mean that some gambling-like products, like blind boxes, may be less risky, but further research is warranted. Thus, a cautious and balanced approach with a focus on product transparency and child protection should be taken when regulating gambling-like products. Blind box regulation may justifiably be light-touch, whilst loot box and card pack regulations could be stricter and more restrictive, given a greater potential for harm. 1. Introduction

Gambling-like products share structural and psychological characteristics with legally regulated traditional gambling (e.g., roulette and slot machines) but are generally not regulated as gambling. For example, players can (i) spend money to buy "loot boxes" or engage with "gacha" mechanics inside video games to (ii) obtain random virtual rewards, most of which are worth little, but a few rare rewards are highly valuable. Both elements are characteristics found in traditional gambling activities that create anticipation and excitement and may encourage further participation (Farhat et al., 2021). Nevertheless, most countries do not regulate loot boxes as gambling because they do not satisfy all criteria of strict legal definitions (Xiao et al., 2022). Stakeholders (such as consumers, parents, and policymakers) are concerned about potential harms these products might pose. For example, children may be tempted to overspend to obtain desirable in-game items that make their characters stronger or more attractive, regardless of whether they can convert any winnings into real-world money (Mills et al., 2024a (Mills et al., , 2024b)). Supporting the idea that gambling-like products share psychological characteristics with real gambling games, research has consistently found a positive correlation between loot box spending and problem gambling internationally (Garea et al., 2021; Spicer, Nicklin, et al., 2022; Xiao, Fraser, et al., 2024; Zendle & Cairns, 2018): the highest loot box spenders are not high-income individuals, but those experiencing gambling harm (Close et al., 2021). Because gambling-like products are not regulated as gambling and are not age-restricted to adults only, children and young people are frequently exposed to them, which might normalize gambling engagement (Spicer, Fulwood, et al., 2022). Longitudinal research has found that young people who purchased loot boxes were more likely to participate in and spend more money on traditional gambling six months later (Brooks & Clark, 2022; González-Cabrera et al., 2023; Palmer et al., 2025).

Previous research has generally focused solely on virtual video game loot boxes.

However, physical gambling-like products, such as "blind boxes" containing random toys (Duan et al., 2022; Whyke et al., 2023; Xiao, 2022) and card packs with randomized content (Xiao et al., 2026; Zendle et al., 2021), are also becoming popular across the world. These products are defined as gambling-like products since people can (i) spend money to (ii) obtain random rewards in the form of rare items. The blind box phenomenon is recently exemplified by the popular Labubu dolls sold by the Chinese company Pop Mart. These became a global sensation in 2024 and 2025 after K-pop and Western celebrities shared photos of their Labubu dolls on social media (Wang & Hancock, 2025). With most blind boxes, the exact contents are unknown until after the product is purchased and opened. Furthermore, a "secret" rare toy with a 1/72 chance of being obtained is also included, prompting people to purchase many boxes to "chase" it (Lee et al., 2025, p. 7). Card packs are similar products that have been sold for many decades across the world in the form of baseball or Pokémon cards: the consumer does not know exactly what cards they will receive, and rarer cards may be highly valued on the secondary market, where individuals trade, buy, and sell them between each other (Mudd, 2003). Card packs have seen a notable resurgence in popularity during and following the COVID-19 pandemic (Ang, 2025).

Despite blind boxes' and card packs' popularity amongst both youths and adults, very limited research (in contrast to loot boxes) has examined their psychological aspects, such as potential associations with problem gambling severity. Notably, Duan et al.'s survey of Mainland Chinese young people (2022) found that blind box overengagement is positively associated with suicide risk; however, that study was crosssectional and could not establish causation. Duan et al. (2022) specifically identified problem gambling as likely relevant and hypothesized a positive correlation between blind box engagement and problem gambling (p. 10). However, Duan et al. (2022) did not include a problem gambling measure. Lee et al. (2025), through qualitative focus groups with US adult blind box consumers, identified that participants commonly described the purchasing experience as "addictive"; identified similarities and perceived dissimilarities with gambling; and often viewed the business model as ethically questionable and predatory, but nonetheless continued to purchase blind boxes (pp. 7-8).

In the context of physical card packs, two studies have directly emulated loot box research methods. In a first study, Zendle et al. (2021) surveyed international physical card game players recruited from English-language Subreddits and initially dismissed the relationship between purchasing physical card packs and problem gambling as not significant (r = 0.07, p > .05; this result is derived from a reanalysis of their publicly available data). In a second study, Xiao et al. (2026) surveyed card game players living in English-speaking Western countries and found a weak positive association between spending on physical card packs and problem gambling (r = 0.15). This relationship was markedly weaker than the association between loot box spending and problem gambling in the same sample (r = 0.31). However, that weaker positive correlation remained nonetheless practically meaningful because it was stronger than the conventional smallest correlation of interest or smallest effect size of interest (SESOI; i.e., r ≥ 0.100) (Drummond et al., 2020, p. 11; Ferguson, 2009 Ferguson, , pp. 532-533, 2023, p. 3), p. 3).

In summary, previous research suggests that the relationship between problem gambling and physical gambling-like products is weaker than that between problem gambling and virtual gambling-like products (e.g., video game loot boxes). However, no research on the relationship between blind box purchasing and problem gambling has been conducted, despite an obvious international need due to their recent popularity. Furthermore, no research on the relationship between card pack purchasing and problem gambling has been conducted in non-English-speaking contexts. Therefore, in the present study, we extend on previous research (see Xiao et al., 2026) to better understand the relationships between problem gambling and gambling-like products that are not (yet) regulated as gambling (i.e., (i) video game loot boxes, (ii) physical blind boxes, and (iii) physical card packs) in a sample of people living in Mainland China.

Mainland China is a unique jurisdiction to study in relation to gambling-like products for multiple reasons. Firstly, traditional gambling is strictly prohibited in Mainland China (Xiao et al., 2023, p. 647), in contrast to other countries that have increasingly legalized gambling in recent years (Ukhova et al., 2024; Wardle et al., 2024). People may spend money that would have been spent on traditional gambling on permitted gambling-like products instead for a similar experience. Thus, spending on gamblinglike products is likely higher. Secondly, blind boxes were first popularized in Mainland China, and most of these products are produced and marketed by Chinese companies. For example, Pop Mart, based in Beijing, is one of the most popular blind box producers, and more than 60% of its 2024 revenue came from within Mainland China (Wang & Hancock, 2025). Therefore, we anticipate higher social acceptance (see Sato et al., 2024, p. 11) and a higher rate of engagement among this population compared to global norms, meaning that recruiting relevant participants (those who engaged) would be easier. Thirdly, public health recognizes that in populations with higher overall engagement, the rate of problematic engagement is also higher (Kesaite & Wardle, 2022; Rose & Day, 1990). Therefore, a Mainland Chinese sample should also contain more individuals at risk of harm (see Russell et al., 2022, pp. 108-109).

Fourthly, Mainland China is also the first and presently, only jurisdiction to recognize the potential harms of blind boxes and adopt dedicated regulation: inter alia, requiring companies to inform consumers of their probabilities of obtaining the rare toys; prohibiting the sale of blind boxes to children under eight years of age; and restricting their sale to minors aged between 8-17 unless parental consent has been verified (Xiao,

Policymaking and implementation should be informed by psychological research on the potential underlying harms. Thus, the results of the present study have the potential to inform global policy, as blind boxes and other gambling-like products become increasingly more popular worldwide.

1.1. Research questions and hypotheses

Research question 1: Is spending on gambling-like products (i.e., loot boxes, blind boxes, and card packs) associated with the severity of gambling-related problems?

Research question 2: Is spending on physical gambling-like products (i.e., blind boxes and card packs) associated with the severity of gambling-related problems differently (i.e., to a lesser or greater extent) than spending on digital gambling-like products (i.e., loot boxes) is associated with the severity of gambling-related problems?

2. Method

A paid panel of 1,250 adult participants from Mainland China was recruited through Tencent Survey [腾讯问卷] to answer the survey (which is publicly shared at our data deposit link). Tencent Survey has a filter that allows pre-selecting only participants who tagged themselves as a "video game player." We deemed this to be the most effective filter for recruiting a relevant sample of people who engage with popular culture and may have spent money on loot boxes, blind boxes, and card packs. We excluded participants who did not self-report as a video game player or subsequently failed to reaffirm that they had played video games in the past 12 months, as detailed below. There was no specific filter for loot boxes and blind boxes. There was also no trading or collectible card game filter for card packs (cf. Xiao et al., 2026). Whilst the "tabletop games" filter is partially relevant, most tabletop games (e.g., Wingspan (Stonemaier Games, 2019)) are not monetized through card packs.

To estimate the minimum number of participants to be recruited for data collection, the ratio of the number of cases (N) to the number of model parameters that require statistical estimates (q) was considered. In accordance with Kline (2023), a recommended N:q would be 20:1. As the number of model parameters that require statistical estimates with respect to the structural equation model of interest in the present registered report equals q = 45 (see Footnote 1), the number of cases thus equals N = 20*q = 20*45 = 900. By performing structural equation model power analyses using the R package semPower version 2.1.3 (Moshagen & Bader, 2025), considering N = 900 and a = 0.050, the structural equation model of interest in the present registered report would yield a power 1-b = 0.85 (b = 0.15) to detect modelimplied g standardized estimates g = 0.10. Additionally, we conservatively estimated based on prior research using the same participant recruitment method and source (Xiao, Fraser, et al., 2024) that data collected from approximately 20% of participants would be excluded from analysis because, despite being video game players, they did not play any games containing loot boxes and so were not exposed to nor had any opportunities to purchase that specific form of gambling-like products, and in addition, they also did not report purchasing any physical gambling-like products, such as blind boxes and card packs. This exclusion ensured that the sample consisted of participants who were exposed to and had the opportunity to purchase at least one form of digital gambling-like products (e.g., video game loot boxes) and/or had purchased at least one physical gambling-like product. Further, we anticipated that data collected from approximately 10% of participants would be excluded for satisfying other exclusion criteria detailed below. Accordingly, we preregistered terminating data collection upon reaching N = 900*(1/((100-20)/100))*(1/((100-10)/100)) = 1,250.

We paid Tencent Survey CN¥4,500 ( ≈ US$660) with the aim of recruiting 1,250 participants who provide a valid survey response. The exact amount Tencent Survey paid each participant to compensate for their time and participation varied and was calculated based on their contractual agreement, accounting for the participant's past behavior on the platform. We do not know precisely how much each participant was paid. The average was CN¥3.6 (≈US$0.5) per participant.

Participant eligibility was assessed through initial screening questions. To obtain a sample of Mainland Chinese video game players who played games containing loot boxes, we excluded any participant who self-reported residing in Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan, the Diaoyu Islands, or overseas, as we aimed to recruit a Mainland Chinese sample, as justified above. Regulatory and cultural differences, along with varying product availability, mean that including those other regions may return different and imprecise results. We also excluded any participant who self-reported not having played any video games in the past twelve months because their self-identification on the Tencent Survey platform with the "video game player" tag would have been inaccurate, following the methods of prior loot box studies (González-Cabrera et al., 2024; Kristiansen & Severin, 2019; Xiao, Fraser, et al., 2024). We anticipated that the vast majority of our respondents would be aged 18 or above. However, the platform did allow under-18 users to join and be recruited as potential panel participants for surveys. It was technically possible to pay the platform to exclude under-18s from being recruited. However, the costs associated with doing so were disproportionately high. Therefore, we asked all participants to confirm that they were aged 18 or above and excluded under-18s. Upon satisfying any of the exclusion criteria through the initial screening phase, the respondent was informed that they did not satisfy the eligibility criteria, and their survey was thereupon terminated. Further, we included three interspersed attention checks throughout the survey and excluded the participants who failed two or more of them. Firstly, participants were asked to correctly answer a simple arithmetic question ("3+5=?"). Secondly, participants were asked whether they had played a non-existent, fictional video game in the past 12 months (i.e., a nonsensical item). Thirdly, participants were tested using an instructional manipulation check, in which they were asked to select two specific options. Finally, at the end of the survey, participants were asked whether, in their honest opinion and with no influence on their payment, their data should be used for the researchers' analyses (Meade & Craig, 2012). We informed them that their answer to this question would not affect their compensation. We excluded all participants who told us not to use their data. We excluded data from participants who did not complete the online survey in full, as they may have exercised their right to withdraw consent to participate partway through the survey.

In total, 1,056 respondents met all eligibility criteria, and 914 (86.6%) of which reported exposure to at least one form of digital or physical gambling-like products.

Additionally, departing from our registration, we excluded one participant who initially affirmed that they did not reside overseas but later inconsistently indicated " 海外 [overseas]" as their province of residence and registration when self-reporting their demographics (detailed below), leaving a final N = 913.

4. Discussion

The current study investigated whether spending on various gambling-like products such as blind boxes, card packs, and loot boxes was associated with the severity of gambling-related problems. We also aimed to-but could not-provide evidence on whether spending on physical gambling-like products (such as blind boxes and card packs) is associated with the severity of gambling-related problems differently than spending on digital gambling-like products (such as loot boxes) is associated with the severity of gambling-related problems. To test these, we conducted an online survey of a convenience sample of Mainland Chinese video game players, a demographic we expected to be most likely to engage with gambling-like products.

Our results suggest that engagement with all gambling-like products are positively associated with problem gambling severity. Specifically, spending money on gambling-like physical blind boxes was positively associated with problem gambling.

However, this relationship is very weak and falls below the conventional smallest effect size of interest (SESOI) for research on media effects, i.e., R² ≥ 1% (Drummond et al., 2020, p. 11; Ferguson, 2009 Ferguson, , pp. 532-533, 2023, p. 3, p. 3) and should therefore be interpreted as practically not meaningful. In contrast, spending money on digital loot boxes in video games and on physical card packs were weakly positively associated with problem gambling with effect sizes that are practically meaningful, replicating previous loot box findings in Mainland China (Xiao, Fraser, et al., 2024) and internationally (Garea et al., 2021; González-Cabrera et al., 2023; Palmer et al., 2025; Spicer, Nicklin, et al., 2022; Zendle & Cairns, 2018) and a previous anglophone country physical card pack study (Xiao et al., 2026).

Notably, when we controlled for age, gender identity, and monthly disposable income as preregistered, all effects became slightly stronger. Specifically, the effect size for the relationship between blind box spending and problem gambling crossed the threshold for the conventional SESOI and therefore could also be interpreted as practically meaningful. Given these borderline results, further research is needed as our results suggest limited, weak evidence at best that problem gambling severity may be a very small factor that is relevant to blind box engagement, potentially leading to some harm. This association certainly is not capable on its own to explain any associations previously found between blind box engagement and suicide risk (Duan et al., 2022). The relationships between any one novel technology or popular culture product and psychological risks and practical harm are inherently multifaceted and complex and should not be oversimplified, e.g., between internet use and mental health (Vuorre & Przybylski, 2024). Our study, being cross-sectional in design, cannot make any causal claims about blind box engagement leading to consumers experiencing gambling harm or blind boxes being "addictive" in a scientific sense.

Nevertheless, the lack of strong evidence for population-level risks does not mean that specific consumers, who may be so-called "outliers" (Petrovskaya & Zendle, 2023, p. 12), do not experience individual-level harm and should be especially studied and duly supported. In our sample, some participants (3.3%) reported spending more than 12 times their self-reported monthly disposable income on gambling-like products in the past twelve months, which would, by definition (if we assume their monthly disposable income did not change), be financially harmful as previously observed in loot box surveys (Drummond et al., 2025, p. 7).

Potential explanations for the association between blind box spending and problem gambling being negligible include that even the more desirable "secret rare" toy from blind boxes (e.g., a Labubu with a 1/72 chance of being obtained) could be easily purchased directly on popular and well-stocked secondary markets in Mainland China, like 闲鱼 [Xianyu] (Goofish.com), at a price equivalent to that of merely two or three blind boxes. Therefore, in practice, consumers are unlikely to buy many blind boxes when trying to obtain a secret rare toy, since they have a cheaper, nonrandomized, and not gambling-like alternative acquisition method. In contrast, most video game loot box rewards cannot be transferred between users: players must win them personally (Xiao & Henderson, 2024), forcing them to spend money on the gambling mechanism of opening many loot boxes. Similarly, card packs are also fundamentally different from blind boxes: for example, the rarest prizes from physical card packs, while they can be bought on the secondary market, are often very expensive and can cost as much as dozens of card packs (Kim, 2023).

Another distinction between blind boxes on the one hand and loot boxes and card packs on the other is that many consumers will be satisfied with the blind box opening regardless of its result: they wish merely to obtain a toy and, having already chosen from a series they prefer, any random toy would be acceptable, even if the rarer, specific ones would have been more exciting and desirable (Wei & Yu, 2025, p. 15178).

In contrast, the vast majority of loot box and card pack openings lead to disappointing results that the consumer can derive very little value from: consumers would desire only to open and obtain specific rare rewards, and most often fail, thus encouraging them to try again by spending more money, rendering these products more similar to traditional gambling. Thus, "loss chasing" (Zhang & Clark, 2020) would often be present in loot box and card pack engagement, but may be less relevant to blind boxes.

The nuanced product characteristics of all gambling-like products and consumers' motivations for engaging with them should be further scrutinized and, where appropriate, differentiated.

In light of weak evidence at best linking gambling-like products (particularly, in relation to blind boxes, but also with loot boxes and card packs to a more limited extent) to traditional gambling harms (Sidloski et al., 2022), despite apparent structural and psychological similarities, the appropriate regulatory response should be cautious and balanced whilst the evidence base develops. Many consumers do enjoy these products (Lee et al., 2025), which have cultural and social significance to them (Wei & Yu, 2025). The risks to more vulnerable consumers, including young children and people already experiencing gambling problems, and the unlikelihood of these products causing widespread and substantial harm to adults should both be recognized. Policies that do not overly restrict-particularly adults' engagementand instead seek to provide more transparency so that consumers can make more informed purchasing decisions are more justifiable. Stricter loot box and card pack regulations are more justifiable, given a greater potential for harm.

Mainland China's prohibition on selling blind boxes to young children aged under eight, requiring parental consent before selling them to minors aged between 8-17, and requiring prominent disclosures about the probabilities of obtaining different prizes are appropriate in light of our findings (Ren et al., 2026; Xiao, 2022; 市场监管总 局 [State Administration for Market Regulation] (PRC), 2023). Singapore is expected to soon also require blind box and card pack probability disclosures without banning the products themselves (Sun, 2026). Other jurisdictions may not need to adopt dedicated regulations and could instead enforce pre-existing consumer law to require the same, because these probabilities are important product information that ought to be disclosed to consumers before sale (European Commission, 2021, p. 105; Lo et al., 2026).

As to limitations, this study was conducted in Mainland China's specific cultural and regulatory context. Potential cultural differences in loot box engagement have previously been posited (Gentles et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2023). Our Mainland Chinese participants may have been less willing than Western participants to admit to experiencing and thus underreport gambling problems due to stigma (Dhillon et al., 2011). Mainland China's presently globally unique blind box regulations requiring companies to provide transparency as to the probabilities of obtaining different prizes may also have effectively reduced the gambling-like nature and risks involved with these products (Ren et al., 2026). The lack of relevant regulatory policies in other countries may mean that blind box engagement remains more gambling-like elsewhere, and thus the associations are stronger. Surveys about blind box engagement and problem gambling should be conducted in other cultural contexts, considering that these products are globally popular and may be affecting different populations in varied ways.

In our study, we distinguished between physical and digital gambling-like products based on the definitions we provided to our participants. However, these mediumbased boundaries are increasingly blurring. POP MART's official POP NOW feature allows consumers to purchase physical blind boxes through its online shop, with the unboxing result immediately revealed digitally and the physical product delivered in person only after some time has passed. Clove is a mobile app through which consumers can purchase digital "loot boxes" containing physical trading card products that could later be delivered, whilst Whatnot is a different app through which content creators sell physical card packs that are then opened live on stream. When a consumer spends money through such hybrid product offerings, it becomes more difficult to determine whether they have spent on a physical product, a virtual service, or something else entirely with unique risk factors.

5. Conclusion

Physical blind boxes and card packs and digital loot boxes in video games are internationally popular gambling-like products. They offer random prizes and thus share structural and psychological characteristics with legally regulated traditional gambling. However, they are generally not regulated as such at present. Concerns have been raised about their potential harms, including overspending and underage engagement. We thus conducted an online survey of adult Mainland Chinese video game players (N = 913). We replicated previous research findings and found weak but practically meaningful links between problem gambling and both digital loot box and physical card pack spending. In contrast, we found only a very weak association between problem gambling and blind box spending that was arguably not practically meaningful. Various product characteristics that differentiate various gambling-like products, such as the rare prizes being alternatively acquirable through direct purchase from the secondary market at a reasonable price, which may have reduced the gambling-like nature and potential riskiness and harm of blind boxes. Given these fundamental differences, various gambling-like products should be separately studied. A cautious and balanced approach with a focus on product transparency and child protection should be taken when regulating gambling-like products. Blind box regulation may justifiably be light-touch, whilst loot box and card pack regulations could be stricter and more restrictive, given a great potential for harm.
