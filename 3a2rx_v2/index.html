Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). Abstract

Emotions do not arrive as isolated labels. They unfold, persist, subside and sometimes return, shaped by events, bodily responses and individual experience. Yet two fields that could illuminate this movement have largely progressed separately: affective computing seeks to recognize momentary affect from physiological and behavioural signals, while affect-dynamics research studies temporal patterns using comparatively sparse self-reports. We propose bringing them together. Continuously inferred valencearousal trajectories, combined with information about the events that shape them, could reveal how people respond to emotional perturbations, recover and move between states. We suggest representing intrinsic restoring tendencies through individualized, potentially multistable effective potential landscapes, while modelling event-related influences separately. Such landscapes might eventually offer a quantitative window onto aspects of emotional temperament and regulation; their existence and interpretability are hypotheses, not assumptions. We outline a path from controlled experiments and participant-labelled reference data to wearable sensing and personalization in daily life. Conceptual simulations illustrate how landscapes could differ or change after experience. Crucially, we specify how the proposal could fail: estimated landscapes must survive realistic parameter-recovery tests, comparisons with simpler dynamical models, out-of-sample prediction, and validation of the physiology-to-affect measurement chain. If successful, this approach could connect continuous affective sensing to new questions about emotional self-awareness, resilience, relationships and mental health.

1. Introduction

Emotional life is not a succession of independent snapshots. A distressing event may leave one person unsettled for hours while another soon recovers; a moment of joy may fade quickly or reshape the course of a day. To understand such differences, we need to know not only how someone feels, but how their feelings change, what sets those changes in motion and what follows when a stimulus is gone.

Affect-dynamics research already provides an important foundation. It has described emotional variability, inertia, transitions and recovery and examined their associations with wellbeing and psychopathology (Trull et al., 2015; Wichers et al., 2015; Kuppens & Verduyn, 2017). The DynAffect model represents affect as a stochastic process attracted toward a person-specific home base (Kuppens et al., 2010; Oravecz et al., 2011). Work on state-space grids, complex systems and multistable affect has further explored attractors and transitions (Hollenstein, 2007 (Hollenstein, , 2015;; van de Leemput et al., 2014; Loossens et al., 2020; Olthof et al., 2023). More recently, individual stability landscapes have been inferred from momentary valence data (Goicoechea et al., 2025).

These advances also show why the problem remains open. Most empirical studies sample affect a few times a day, often without detailed records of the events that occurred between reports. Apparently distinct emotional states might reflect multiple internal attractors, changing situations or properties of the rating scale rather than the same mechanism (Haslbeck et al., 2023; Haqiqatkhah et al., 2024; Ryan et al., 2025). Dense observations alone will not resolve this ambiguity; we also need information about the forces acting on the person.

Affective computing offers a complementary possibility. Wearable and other physiological sensors can provide much denser observations, and emotion recognition has made substantial technical progress (Shu et al., 2018; Saganowski et al., 2023). Yet many systems remain trained on short laboratory recordings and evaluated on momentary categories or ratings, not on the course of an emotional episode (Larradet et al., 2020). Continuous monitoring outside the laboratory is increasingly feasible, although its interpretation remains difficult (Saganowski, 2022; Gutierrez Maestro et al., 2023).

Our proposal is to connect these approaches. Physiological measurements could support continuous estimates of valence and arousal, while records of meaningful events and their appraisal provide context for those trajectories. Repeated departures from and returns toward familiar emotional regions could then be used to investigate person-specific response and restoring dynamics. We illustrate one possible mathematical representation-a malleable effective potential landscape-not as an established map of temperament, but as a testable hypothesis.

The framework moves from measurement (Sections 2-3) to dynamical modelling (Section 4), empirical tests (Section 5), research opportunities (Section 6) and ethical safeguards (Section 7).

2. What an emotion recognition system is modelling

Automatic emotion recognition (AER) should not be understood as reading a person's inner life directly from a sensor. Emotion involves external and internal events, appraisal, bodily change and conscious experience, and different theories place different emphasis on those components (Scherer, 2009; Kuppens & Tong, 2010; Barrett, 2017). Figure 1 illustrates the view that motivates our proposal. Physiological activity-including cardiovascular, electrodermal and other bodily responses-both accompanies emotional processing and contributes to the sensations through which feelings become meaningful (Purves et al., 2001; Khalsa et al., 2018; Carvalho & Damasio, 2021). The same bodily pattern can nevertheless be experienced differently depending on the person and context (Moors, 2017). A physiology-based model, f(x), therefore learns an imperfect relationship between measured signals and reported affect, not a universal correspondence between body and emotion. That relationship may change across people, contexts and time; individualized calibration can improve performance in some settings (Han et al., 2024; Li & Washington, 2024). Even an accurate estimate of how someone feels at one instant, however, cannot explain why they arrived there or where they will go next. That is the missing temporal question (Trull et al., 2015).

3. From classification to continuous, personalized recognition in daily life

Figure 2 outlines the transition we envisage: from isolated emotion classification to continuous, personally calibrated affect estimation in daily life. It also places events alongside affective trajectories. Without knowing what happened, a long negative episode could reflect strong reactivity, repeated adversity or slow recovery-very different accounts of the same observations.

3.1 A population-level model for continuous affect

Controlled elicitation studies have enabled progress in physiological affect recognition, but they often use sparse labels and test generalization only within laboratory conditions (Saganowski, 2022; Larradet et al., 2020). Some research now demonstrates the value of personalized models (Han et al., 2024; Li & Washington, 2024). A next step is to design studies expressly for tracking change, rather than merely assigning a label to each clip. We envision first training a population-level model, f(x)g, in a diverse sample with synchronized physiological recordings and continuous or near-continuous reports of valence and arousal. Existing datasets demonstrate that such annotation is feasible (Sharma et al., 2019; Sharma et al., 2020). Crucially, stimulus onset and offset, pre-stimulus reference periods and post-stimulus recovery should all be recorded. Repeated exposures across emotional directions and intensities would help distinguish response sensitivity from subsequent recovery.

There is no perfect way to obtain a continuous record of subjective feeling. Participants can rate affect as it unfolds, but doing so may change the experience. Alternatively, they can revisit a recording and reconstruct what they felt, as in the affect-rating-dial tradition (Gottman & Levenson, 1985; Ruef & Levenson, 2007). This reduces interference during the original episode but adds memory, habituation and timing errors. Because simultaneous continuous ratings have also proved feasible (Sharma et al., 2019), the two approaches should be compared empirically, rather than either being treated as unproblematic ground truth.

3.2 Deployment, prompting and personalization

A population model trained in the laboratory will still face unfamiliar people and situations. We therefore envisage a second stage in which wearable sensing and a smartphone app provide provisional affect estimates during ordinary activities, while users occasionally report their experience and relevant events. Earlier work has already combined mobile sensing with self-reported affect and has explored wearable monitoring outside the laboratory (Asselbergs et al., 2016; Gutierrez Maestro et al., 2023).

Prompts could appear when affect seems to change or predictions are uncertain, supplemented by randomly timed prompts to avoid sampling only detected episodes. The resulting reports could improve the shared model and calibrate it to the individual, yielding f(x)i. Once a robust individualized f(x)i model is developed, it would replace the population-level f(x)g model as the automatic emotion recognition model for the user. Prompt probabilities, missing reports and model updates must be documented: changes in an algorithm should never be mistaken for changes in a person's emotional life. Pilot studies should establish whether such monitoring is accurate, tolerable and useful before wider deployment. We anticipate that, the higher the difference between the individual user and the population used to train f(x)g for relevant factors (such as, for example, sex, age, ethnicity, socioeconomic status etc..), more data will be necessary to develop a robust f(x)i.

3.3 What the user sees

A wearable device might record photoplethysmography, electrodermal activity, movement and skin temperature. A phone could translate these signals into uncertain estimates of affect that are reviewed later, rather than demanding constant attention (Figure 3). Physiological sensing is attractive because it can operate during activities in which recording speech or faces would be impractical or intrusive (Saganowski, 2022; Gutierrez Maestro et al., 2023). A user might see how feelings changed over a day, which events preceded a lasting shift, or how quickly they tended to recover. With further validation, these records could support self-awareness, reflection on triggers, personalized coping, conversations with loved ones or psychotherapy. Their purpose should be to support introspection, not replace it. Estimates should be presented with uncertainty, and users should remain free to disagree with them.

4. Emotional dynamics as movement over a personalized potential landscape

Affect dynamics already includes summary indices and formal time-series models, including autoregressive and stochastic approaches to person-specific restoring tendencies (Lodewyckx et al., 2011; Hamaker et al., 2015; Kuppens et al., 2010; Oravecz et al., 2011). Multistability and attractor landscapes have also appeared in affective science and psychopathology (Loossens et al., 2020; Wichers et al., 2015; Hayes & Andrews, 2020; Olthof et al., 2023). Some landscapes have been derived from the long-run distribution of states, including simulated psychological models and individual experience-sampling series (Cui et al., 2023; Goicoechea et al., 2025).

Our specific proposal is to use densely sampled, physiologyinformed valence-arousal trajectories to estimate restoring dynamics, while separately accounting for measured events. The distinction matters because spending a long time in one affective region does not, by itself, establish that the region is an intrinsic attractor. Repeated perturbations and recovery periods may provide more informative evidence than occupancy alone.

4.1 From a single attractor to a landscape

Imagine an individual's emotional state moving across a landscape. Valleys represent regions toward which the state tends to return; ridges make some transitions less likely; and a change in the landscape alters what is stable. We formalize this intuition with an effective potential U(x, y) over valence and arousal. Its negative gradient describes the restoring tendency in a gradient-driven model. Local minima correspond to attractors, although whether human affect actually obeys such dynamics remains an empirical question. If supported, landscape geometry may offer a way to describe aspects of emotional regulation and temperament.

DynAffect offers a useful point of departure: its personspecific home base and restoring strength correspond, in a simplified first-order formulation, to a single quadratic well (Kuppens et al., 2010; Oravecz et al., 2011). Our hypothesis permits more than one basin. We also use inertia in the illustrations to depict overshoot and settling, but do not assume that inertia is necessary or identifiable in real affective data. The first-order model is our proposed starting point for estimation.

5. What would establish the framework

The idea should be judged by what it predicts, not by how intuitive its visualizations appear. A credible landscape must be recoverable from realistic measurements, outperform simpler explanations and describe something reproducible about a person. Our preliminary simulations are a limited numerical check, not a validation of the framework.

5.1 Identifiability from realistic data

The first test is parameter recovery. Starting with simulated trajectories whose landscape and event inputs are known, we should vary sampling density, sensor-derived measurement error, delay, session duration and the number and type of perturbations. We can then ask which aspects of the landscape can be recovered. Experience-sampling work suggests caution: even simple affect-dynamics summaries can require substantial observation, and nonlinear models may need long series to earn their complexity (Pirla et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2026). Dense wearable recordings do not automatically provide dense *information* about emotion. Repeated, well-characterized event responses may be particularly valuable for identifying restoring tendencies (Oravecz et al., 2011; Friedrich et al., 2011).

An initial exercise fitting one low-noise simulated trajectory recovered some features of the generating landscape and its triggers, but other landscape parameters showed appreciable error; estimates deteriorated when data were made sparse (Supplementary Methods; Figures S1-S4). This is a proof of numerical feasibility under favorable conditions, not evidence of general identifiability. It leaves open the harder problem of simultaneously discovering landscape structure and unknown event inputs, and it does not establish validity for real emotional data.

5.2 Alternative models

A multi-basin model should be adopted only if it explains or predicts trajectories better than credible simpler models. These include a single attractor, as in DynAffect; switching situations that elicit different affective states without intrinsic multistability (Ryan et al., 2025; Hamaker & Grasman, 2012); and a single attractor whose location drifts with context or time (Bringmann et al., 2017). The Affective Ising Model provides another multistable account worth comparing on the same observations (Loossens et al., 2020). Tests should predict heldout responses and recovery periods, keep each episode intact across validation splits, and allow each candidate the same event information. Attractive plots or in-sample fit are not enough.

Claims about temperament require additional evidence. Person-specific features should show meaningful reliability across independent sessions and predict subsequent responses better than population-level models. If landscape features change substantially from week to week without major events, they may describe circumstances rather than dispositions. They must also add information beyond simpler summaries such as mean affect and variability, which already explain much of what affect-dynamics metrics predict about wellbeing (Dejonckheere et al., 2019). Malleability is compatible with reliability, but only if the expected timescales of stability and change are specified and tested.

5.3 The measurement chain

Most importantly, the emotional trajectories would themselves be estimates. Physiological responses lag, and recognition models smooth, filter and shrink predictions toward familiar values. These procedures can create apparent inertia, restoring forces and boundaries even when they do not exist in the underlying experience. Self-report brings its own delays and scale effects (Haslbeck et al., 2023; Haqiqatkhah et al., 2024). Validation should therefore compare physiology-inferred trajectories with high-resolution participant reports, propagate uncertainty into the dynamical model, and examine generalization across people, elicitors and contexts (Sharma et al., 2019; Dollack et al., 2023). Physical activity, posture, sleep and other physiological influences must also be considered. A convincing finding cannot be an artefact of the affectrecognition algorithm.

Finally, timescale matters. Laboratory episodes lasting minutes may tell us about local recovery near a familiar state; establishing multiple attractors, their persistence and their possible plasticity would require much longer naturalistic observations, ideally across days or weeks.

6. Research directions enabled by the framework

If the proposed measurement and modelling pipeline proves reliable, it could open several research directions that are difficult to pursue with occasional affect reports alone (Figure 5).

Individual differences in appraisal and reactivity. People can experience the same event differently, or interpret similar bodily responses in different ways. Pairing personalized affect estimators with measured events could help separate differences in expressed or physiological reactions from differences in reported experience, although a simple exchange of models between individuals would not, by itself, identify appraisal. Controlled comparisons and explicit measurement models would be needed.

Social and interpersonal dynamics. Synchronized trajectories from partners, families or other close relationships could illuminate emotional co-regulation, lagged responses and social synchrony (Mayo & Gordon, 2020). Does another person's presence change the speed of recovery, the likelihood of transition or the apparent stability of an affective region? Such questions require dyadic designs that distinguish shared events from genuine interpersonal influence. Development over the lifespan. Repeated measurements could investigate whether patterns of emotional responsiveness and recovery change with age or major life experiences (Wrzus et al., 2014; Bleidorn et al., 2022). If landscapes are identifiable, their evolving geometry could offer a new description of those changes.

Temperament and its determinants. Reliable person-specific features-such as restoring strength, transition propensity or barrier geometry-could be studied alongside established measures of temperament and personality, and eventually in genetic and environmental research (Rothbart et al., 2000; Kandler & Instinske, 2025). Their value would depend on being stable, interpretable and informative beyond conventional affect summaries.

Interventions and recovery.

Emotion-focused psychotherapy, meditation or other interventions might change not only average distress but also the ease of leaving or recovering from difficult states. The landscape representation offers testable hypotheses about such changes, provided treatment effects on event exposure and appraisal are distinguished from changes in restoring dynamics (Hayes & Andrews, 2020; Olthof et al., 2023).

More broadly, this approach could connect the technological ability to observe affect more frequently with the scientific ambition to understand emotional change-without assuming in advance that landscapes are the right model.

7. Ethical considerations and risks

A system that continuously estimates a person's emotions would be more than a scientific instrument. It could alter how someone interprets themselves and how others treat them. Four concerns deserve attention from the start: the psychological effects of monitoring, privacy and governance, overconfidence in model outputs, and unequal performance (Martinez-Martin et al., 2018; Martinez-Martin et al., 2021). Table 1 summarizes possible harms and safeguards.

Effects of monitoring. Tracking affect may support reflection, but may also intensify rumination or dependence on an app. A graphic labelled as a person's "distressed basin" could be mistaken for an immutable identity rather than a contingent model estimate. These effects should be evaluated prospectively, not assumed beneficial (Faurholt-Jepsen et al., 2019).

Privacy and governance. Emotion-related recordings can expose intimate information about health, relationships and behavior. People must retain meaningful control over collection, access and deletion, and there should be strong protections against unwanted use by employers, educational institutions and insurers. Existing legal restrictions, including the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, reinforce the importance of context-specific safeguards (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024).

Reification. A predicted trajectory is not a direct measurement of inner experience. Clinicians, partners or users themselves may give an appealing visualization more authority than it warrants. Estimates should display uncertainty and permit correction or disagreement, in keeping with critiques of overconfident emotion recognition (Cross et al., 2023; Rad et al., 2025).

Unequal performance. Models may work less well for particular ages, cultural backgrounds or physiological profiles, creating unequal benefits and potential harms. Performance disparities, including those arising from sensor measurement, require direct evaluation rather than assurances of generalizability (Cross et al., 2023; O'Leary et al., 2024).

Ethics, then, is part of the research agenda, not a deployment checklist. The aim is a tool that supports a person's own interpretation of emotion while minimizing avoidable harms (Martinez-Martin et al., 2018; Martinez-Martin et al., 2021).

S3. Likelihood

Velocities are estimated from the observed positions by finite differences. For each interior time step the model predicts the next velocity from the current velocity, the damping, the gradient of the candidate landscape at the current position and the trigger force at the current time; under the model the residual between observed and predicted next velocity is Gaussian noise with variance σₚ²dt in each dimension. The negative log-likelihood is the sum over steps and dimensions of the squared residuals scaled by that variance, plus the log-determinant term that ties σₚ to the residual scale. Steps at which the trajectory touches the boundary of the valence-arousal square are excluded. Quiescent stretches inform estimation of the landscape, damping and noise. The first step after an onset provides information about the net push through its contribution pⱼdt to the expected velocity increment, in addition to landscape, damping and any remaining event forces. Subsequent relaxation provides information about λⱼ jointly with restoring dynamics and damping.

S4. Optimization and model selection

The continuous parameters were estimated by minimizing the negative log-likelihood with the L-BFGS-B quasi-Newton algorithm (SciPy) using forward-difference gradients. Basin centres were initialized at the peaks of a kernel density estimate of the trajectory, hill centres at high-arousal, neutral-valence positions with random jitter, and other parameters at mid-range defaults; five random restarts were run per configuration and the fit with the lowest negative log-likelihood retained. In Regime C the number of basins and hills was selected by an outer search over N_b ∈ {1, …, 4} and N_h ∈ {0, 1, 2}, scoring each configuration by the Bayesian information criterion, BIC = 2•NLL + k•log(n), with k = 5N_b + 5N_h + 8 free parameters and n the number of interior residuals. In Regime B the overdamped reduction of the model was used, because acceleration cannot be estimated from widely spaced samples.

S5. Trigger identification (Regime E)

When nothing about the triggers is given, four steps surround the joint fit. First, candidate onsets are detected as change points in the magnitude of successive velocity differences, computed from the finite-difference velocity: a step is flagged when it exceeds a robust threshold, the median plus 4 × 1.4826 × the median absolute deviation, so that the event spikes themselves do not inflate the threshold; flagged steps closer than 15 time units are merged into one event, because an underdamped kick rings for several steps; the first flagged step of each cluster is taken as the onset; and the first 5 time units are skipped to avoid the start-up transient. Second, each detected trigger's push vector is warm-started from the velocity jump at its onset after subtracting a damping-only prediction, and its decay from a default of 5 time units. Third, landscape, damping, noise and all (pⱼ, λⱼ) are fitted jointly with onsets fixed at the detected times. Fourth, residuals under the fitted model are recomputed with 10 time units masked around each known onset, the change-point rule is rerun on them to catch weak events that the first pass missed, and any new event is added and the model refitted, for up to three passes. Finally, because the detector can over-detect, candidates are ranked by recovered push magnitude and nested subsets (all candidates, drop the weakest, drop the two weakest, and so on) are refitted and scored by BIC with k = 5N_b + 5N_h + 8 + 3N_triggers; the subset with the lowest BIC is retained. A detected change point is a candidate disturbance in the simulated dynamics, not independent verification of a real-world event or its psychological meaning. Independently recorded events and appraisal reports would be needed to establish that interpretation in human data.

S6. Results

Regimes A and C. Regime A fits 23 continuous parameters with the component structure supplied. In the structure-selection run displayed for Regime C, BIC selected the generating configuration (N_b = 2, N_h = 1). Figure S1 shows BIC of approximately -73,461 for the selected model and -73,397 for the nearest competitor, a difference of approximately 64. Trigger sensitivities were recovered within 2%, but recovery of all continuous parameters was not within a few percent: the secondary-basin valence width, hill amplitude and secondary-basin amplitude have errors of approximately 36%, 34% and 13%, respectively. The selected component structure and close contour agreement in much of the visited region should therefore be distinguished from accurate recovery of every coefficient.

Regime B. The 200-and 500-point overdamped fits approximately recover the basin locations in the displayed trajectory, with biased component amplitudes. The 50-point fit distorts both locations and geometry, and the 100-point fit fails, with visited-region surface RMSE of approximately 3.74 (Figure S2). These results do not establish a monotonic sample-size threshold or a guaranteed optimization remedy; additional restarts and replicated trajectories are needed to separate optimization failure from sampling limitations and misspecification of the overdamped approximation.

Regime D. With onsets given, the push vectors and decays of all three triggers were recovered with a largest error of 3.0 percent on any component (the 3-unit decay of the positive-surprise trigger); basin centres and widths to about 1 percent and basin depths to within 6 percent; damping and noise to within 0.4 percent (Figure S3, top).

Regime E.

With nothing about the triggers given, change-point detection found three onsets at 60.0, 95.0 and 150.0, the true times; residual re-detection found no further events; and BIC retained all three, dropping any one of them raising the criterion by thousands of units. Push vectors and decays were recovered with a largest error of 2.3 percent, basin centres and widths to about 1 percent, basin depths to within 3 percent, and damping and noise to within 0.3 percent (Figure S3, bottom; Figure S4). Knowing the onsets (D) gave no measurable advantage over detecting them (E), because detection was exact.

Coverage limits. Hill and confinement parameters are weakly recovered in several fits, and some secondary-basin parameters are also inaccurate in the structure-selection run (Figure S1). Poorly visited regions provide limited information, but a single fit cannot separate this explanation from parameter trade-offs, optimization error or discretization effects. Recovery of basin geometry and event parameters must therefore be assessed separately, with replicated simulations and uncertainty estimates rather than a blanket claim of accurate recovery.

S7. Scope

The checks provide conditional evidence of numerical recovery for selected landscape and event features in one simulated trajectory; they do not establish general structural or practical identifiability. Their scope is bounded in five ways. They concern one simulated person with three sharp, well-separated, exponentially decaying events; overlapping events, ramped onsets and sustained stimuli, which are not represented by the instantaneous-onset exponential forces tested here, have not been tested. The noise level is low relative to the event kicks (spikes 5-15 times the detection threshold) and to what physiology-inferred trajectories will carry; the residual re-detection and BIC steps exist for noisier cases but have not been stress-tested. Onsets are fixed at the detected step rather than refined continuously, which introduces onset uncertainty up to the sampling resolution and may become more consequential under sparse or noisy observation. The landscape family used for fitting is the family that generated the data, so misspecification is not tested. Finally, U is identifiable only up to an additive constant, and its numerical scale depends on the affect coordinates, time units and first-or second-order convention. Occupancy alone identifies a potential relative to noise; drift-based fitting with known time units can estimate restoring coefficients and noise separately under the stated model assumptions.

Ethical considerations, potential harms and mitigation strategiesThe possibility that a person's emotional life has a discernible dynamic structure is both scientifically compelling and personally meaningful. Affect-dynamics research has supplied models of temporal change; affective computing offers increasingly dense observations. Together, they may allow us to ask not only how someone feels, but what moves them, what helps them recover and what patterns persist. We have proposed individualized effective potential landscapes as one way to express-and test-those questions, not as a literal map of the mind. Existing work on attractors and affect dynamics provides the foundation; the proposed advance lies in linking continuous physiology-informed trajectories with measured events and investigating whether restoring forces and multistability can be identified in individual lives. The simulations show what the model can express, not what human emotion necessarily does. Rigorous measurement validation, recovery studies and comparisons with simpler explanations will determine whether the landscape hypothesis survives. Even if it does not, the effort to connect dense, personally meaningful affect measurements with models of emotional change could advance the study of emotion, relationships and mental health.
