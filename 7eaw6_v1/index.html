To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 86 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p >.05 When considering same-day weather, only temperature is a significant predictor of the number of website visits, with higher temperatures associated with fewer visits (β temp = -0.099, p <.001; β rain = -0.005, p >.05; β wind = 0.008, p >.05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To improve Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 31 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Protecting homes from increasing climate hazards depends in part on consumers' understanding and acting on objective climate risk information. Yet such information is not interpreted in isolation. Interpretation may be influenced by immediate contextual cues. This research examines how weather conditions, as one example of relevant contextual cues, influence consumers' responses to property-level climate risk information. Pairing website visit data from an online platform providing flood risk information with the weather data at users' location, we demonstrate that weather is associated with interest in personal climate-hazard risk. Specifically, visits to the website increase following periods of rainfall and decline during extended dry spells at the users' locations. Beyond generating interest in climate-hazard risks, we show in four experiments that weather moderates the effect of objective climate-hazard risk on consumers' judgments. When weather conditions contradict the objective risk, consumers' judgments shift away from the direction implied by the objective risk information. In contrast, when weather aligns with the objective risk, weather effects are mostly absent. These effects also trickle down to consumers' willingness to adopt protective measures. These findings advance our understanding of consumers' evaluation of risks by showing that heterogeneity in risk perception and protection motivation can emerge from an asymmetric integration of objective risk information and immediate contextual cues depending on whether the signals align or not.

INTRODUCTION

Dry weather conditions are deceptive when it comes to flood risk. Drought reduces the soil's capacity to absorb water, so when rain eventually returns, it is more likely to produce sudden, fast-moving flooding rather than being safely absorbed into the ground. Such patterns of extended dry periods followed by heavy rainfall are becoming more frequent under climate change and have contributed to severe flood events (He and Sheffield 2020; Rashid and Wahl 2022). The conditions consumers experience in their immediate context may therefore not always align with, and can even be misleading about, the actual risk they face. Now imagine a consumer buying a new house who receives an official notice, on such a dry day, that the house is at high risk of flooding. There has been no rain for weeks; the ground is visibly dry, and nothing in the surrounding environment suggests that flooding is imminent. Will these conditions influence how the consumer interprets the risk? What if the consumer makes the same evaluation after several consecutive days of rainfall? More broadly, do cues in the consumer's immediate context, such as weather, alter how they interpret and act on objective risk information?

And does the influence of such contextual cues depend on whether they align or conflict with the objective risk?

Consumers do not interpret objective climate risk information in isolation. Instead, they may integrate objective risk with concurrently available contextual cues, such as local weather. We propose that such integration, and thus the influence of weather, depends on the weather's congruence with the objective risk information. We test this across five studies. Using secondary field data, we first show that weather is associated with interest in specific climate-hazard risks. Across four experimental studies, we then demonstrate that weather systematically moderates how consumers interpret objective risk, and examine whether the effects extend to their willingness to implement or pay for protective measures.

We make several contributions. First, we show that objective risk information and contextual cues jointly shape consumers' risk perceptions. Rather than exerting independent effects, con-sumers' responses to objective risk information depend on the contextual cue. Previous research has shown that risk perceptions can vary across individuals and situations due to differences in prior beliefs, experiences, emotions, and the way risk information is communicated (Slovic et al. 2004; Lechowska 2018; Loewenstein et al. 2001; Siegrist and Árvai 2020). We extend this work by showing that weather, as a contextual cue, does not uniformly increase or decrease perceived climate risk; rather, its influence depends on the objective risk being evaluated. Thus, the heterogeneity in risk perception can arise from the interaction between objective risk and the immediate context in which that risk is evaluated.

Second, we show that this integration is asymmetric. When weather conflicts with the objective risk information, consumers' risk perception shifts away from the direction implied by the risk information. However, when weather and objective risk information are aligned, weather produces little additional change in perceived risk. Thus, congruence does not produce a mirror-image amplification of the effects observed under incongruence. This asymmetric pattern suggests that congruent and incongruent contextual cues are not integrated the same way. Rather, contextual information appears to exert a stronger influence when it competes than when it corroborates objective risk.

Third, we demonstrate how these asymmetric contextual effects on risk perception influence consumers' protection motivation. Incongruent weather primarily shifts consumers' judgments of the underlying threat away from the direction implied by objective risk information, while it has weaker effects on their assessment of their ability to cope with the risk. We demonstrate that these shifts in perceived threat are consequential as they extend to consumers' willingness to engage in or pay for protection measures.

THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

Consumers often receive objective risk information when making major purchases. An investment fund may display a risk rating, an insurance premium may reflect actuarial assessments of future losses, or a homebuyer may receive information about the property's exposure to climate hazards. However, consumers rarely interpret such information in isolation. Objective risk information is often encountered alongside contextual cues that provide an additional signal about the same risk. For example, a high-risk investment may be evaluated during a prolonged market rally, an insurance premium may indicate elevated risk despite a consumer's personal history of claimsfree use, or a property may be classified as highly exposed to flooding after weeks of dry weather.

In such situations, consumers are confronted with two potentially informative signals: objective risk information derived from statistical assessments and historical data, as well as contextual information derived from immediate experience or the surrounding environment. Consequently, how the consumer interprets the objective risk information that is communicated may depend on the contextual cue with which it is encountered. We examine this interplay in the context of propertylevel climate risk, where consumers may evaluate objective risk information alongside immediate weather conditions.

Objective Climate Risk Information Climate-related hazards, such as floods and wildfires, can cause substantial damage to residential and commercial property, with serious financial consequences for households and communities. Global, annual economic losses from climate-related hazards reached approximately $277 billion in 2025, with direct property damage accounting for a significant portion of these costs (Gallagher Re 2026). For example, the wildfire disaster that occurred in Los Angeles in 2025 caused an estimated $65 billion in total losses; primarily through the destruction of over 16,000 structures (Gallagher Re 2026). Similarly, in the 2021 Central European floods, a major economic impact was incurred through damage to infrastructure and residential property (Tradowsky et al. 2023). For consumers who own or are considering purchasing a property, these potentially severe consequences make estimations of a property's exposure to climate-related hazards particularly important.

Climate-related hazards are characterized by uncertainty regarding whether, when, and with what severity they will occur (Rougier, Sparks, and Hill 2013). Assessing climate risk of a property therefore requires synthesizing multiple complex sources of information, including historical hazard observations, characteristics of the location, model-based estimates of hazard occurrence and intensity, and projections of future climate conditions (e.g., Bates et al. 2021; Kearns et al. 2022). The resulting assessments estimate a property's exposure to potential hazard events often probabilistically and over long time horizons. In this research, we refer to such systematically derived and standardized estimates of property-level hazard risk as objective risk information.

Because the assessments underlying objective risk synthesize complex probabilistic and modelbased information, communicating this information to consumers requires translating that complexity into formats that are relatively simple and intuitive to interpret. Local and national governments, non-profit organizations, and other information providers increasingly do so through various tools including targeted messaging, risk maps, labeling systems, and educational materials (Moser 2010; Graf, Renner, and Klebel 2026; Massa and Comunello 2024).

More recently, property climate risk information has also been incorporated into real estate platforms (e.g., Zillow, Redfin, Zoopla) in the form of climate risk labels to inform decisions even before buying a property. Using numerical or color-coded categories (e.g., green for low risk and red for high risk), these labels translate probabilistic risk information into a simple and accessible format. Visual labeling systems of this kind have already been proven effective in other consumer decision domains, including Nutri-Score nutrition labels (Dubois et al. 2021) and household energy-efficiency ratings (Newell and Siikamäki 2014). Recent findings suggest that similar labeling approaches can also be effective in communicating climate risk of properties.

For instance, disclosures of property climate risk via such labels have been shown to influence property search and bidding behavior on real estate platforms (Fairweather et al. 2024), affect willingness to purchase a property (Ghasemi and Newell 2025) as well as willingness to engage in protection behaviors (Krefeld-Schwalb, Wilson, and Van Den Bergh 2026). Indeed, these labels appear to have real marketplace relevance. In late 2025, just over a year after introducing property climate risk scores, Zillow stopped displaying them directly on its listings following complaints from stakeholders that the information hurt sales (The New York Times 2025), and in 2026 the state of Florida initiated a lawsuit against one of the platforms providing climate risk score for real estate (Kaufman and Roston 2026). Together, these findings and developments suggest that consumers consider objective risk information when evaluating properties. In our experiments, we use a similar labeling system to communicate and manipulate objective risk (see Figure 1).

While objective risk assessment provides information on a property's climate hazard risk, consumers rarely process this information in isolation. Risk perception is subjective and constructed and therefore hardly reflects a direct readout of objective risk information (Slovic 1987; Slovic et al. 2004). Identical objective risk information can give rise to different judgments depending on characteristics of the individual, the way the information is communicated, and the context in which it is encountered (Maibach et al. 2023; Lechowska 2018; Loewenstein et al. 2001; Siegrist et al. 2020; Jenkins et al. 2024). Indeed, research suggests that subjective perceptions of flood risk can differ substantially from expert assessments and publicly available risk information (Botzen, Kunreuther, and Michel-Kerjan 2015). In particular, consumers evaluate risk information within an immediate decision context that may provide additional cues about the same risk. These contextual cues can affect how objective risk information is interpreted and thereby contribute to heterogeneity in consumers' risk perceptions and responses. For climate hazards, local weather is a particularly prominent contextual cue.

Weather as a Contextual Cue for Climate Risk A growing body of research shows that local weather influences climate-related judgments, concerns, and behavioral intentions. Abnormally warm temperatures increase belief in, and concern about climate change as well as support for climate action, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the "local warming" effect (Sugerman, Li, and Johnson 2021; Li, Johnson, and Zaval 2011).

This effect emerges for both subjectively perceived and objectively measured temperature anomalies. For example, one study found that individuals' thoughts about whether a day felt warmer or cooler than usual influenced their belief in climate change and relevant donation behavior (Li et al. 2011). Another study demonstrated that objective temperature deviations alone are predictive of people's online searches about climate change and climate-related voting behavior (Sisco and Weber 2022).

Beyond temperature, individuals also rely on other weather cues such as precipitation anomalies, drought conditions, strong winds, or heavy snow, while forming climate-relevant judgments and behaviors (Sisco, Bosetti, and Weber 2017; Taylor, de Bruin, and Dessai 2014; Ghasemi et al. 2026). For example, deviations from average rainfall patterns positively predicted support for environmentally oriented political parties and the adoption of solar panels (Ghasemi et al. 2026).

Similar effects of precipitation-related weather experiences on climate-related beliefs have been documented in other contexts, (Taylor et al. 2014; Alexander et al. 2023). Zaval et al. (2014) found that even explaining the limited relevance of short-term weather fluctuations to long-term climate change did not eliminate the local warming effect.

One explanation for these effects is that local weather offers a simple and cognitively accessible cue for judgments that would otherwise be abstract and hard to make (Sugerman et al. 2021; Li et al. 2011; Sisco 2021). Specifically, because weather is immediate and directly observable, it may make relevant climate related thoughts easier to bring to mind (Tversky and Kahneman 1973). Unusually warm weather, for example, may make heat and related concepts more available, thereby increasing their influence on judgment. This heightened accessibility may, in turn, encourage attribute substitution when people evaluate broader climate questions (Sugerman et al. 2021; Li et al. 2011; Kahneman et al. 2002; Zaval et al. 2014). Rather than answering a complex question such as whether the climate is changing, individuals may substitute it with a more readily answerable question, that is, whether the weather currently feels unusually hot or cold. Together, these heuristic processes help explain how immediate weather conditions can influence judgments about a phenomenon like climate change, which are otherwise abstract, unfold over long periods, and are difficult to assess directly.

However, most existing evidence on weather effects concerns broad beliefs and concerns about climate change. Far less is known about what the local weather does to consumers' interest in and perceptions of their own, personal exposure to climate-hazard risk, for example, their home's flood or wildfire risk specifically. This distinction is important because perceptions of specific and personally relevant climate consequences appear to be more proximal to how people respond to climate-related risks than broader beliefs about climate change (van Valkengoed, Perlaviciute, and Steg 2024; Haden et al. 2012; Blennow et al. 2012). For example, in one study, although farmers' general beliefs about climate change predicted their mitigation behavior, their adaptation actions were more strongly associated with beliefs about the local impacts of climate change (Haden et al. 2012). Similarly, perceptions of specific climate hazards have been shown to mediate the relationship between general belief in climate change and intentions to adopt protective measures (van Valkengoed et al. 2024).

Another limitation of existing research is that local weather has largely been examined as directly influencing climate-related judgments. However, consumers evaluating property-level climate risk may encounter local weather while simultaneously receiving objective information about the same hazard. Our main question is therefore not simply whether weather increases or decreases perceived risk, but whether the effect of objective risk information on consumers' climate hazardrelated judgments depends on the weather context in which that information is encountered.

Integrating Objective Risk Information and Contextual Weather Cues

When objective risk information and local weather are available simultaneously, consumers encounter two cues that may appear relevant to the same hazard but differ markedly in how they represent it (Weber 2006). Objective climate-risk labels summarize assessments of hazard exposure over extended time horizons, making the underlying risk difficult to observe directly. Local weather, by contrast, is immediate, concrete, and directly observable. This saliency of weather can make hazard-related associations easier to bring to mind (Tversky and Kahneman 1973; Sisco 2021). Rainfall, for example, may make flooding and its consequences more readily accessible, whereas hot and dry conditions may evoke associations with wildfire risk.

Similar to the attribute substitution explanation in the case of local warming effects (Kahneman and Frederick 2002; Zaval et al. 2014; Sugerman et al. 2021), consumers may rely on weatherbased associations when evaluating objective risk labels, even when these impressions suggest a different level of hazard exposure. Moreover, given that the influence of immediate contextual cues on climate-related judgments has been shown to be quite persistent (Zaval et al. 2014), we propose that weather-based impressions may even carry particular weight when they conflict with objective risk information. When the two sources are congruent, weather largely reinforces the interpretation already conveyed by the risk label. When they are incongruent, however, weather introduces a competing interpretation of the hazard that, owing to its immediacy and cognitive accessibility, may take precedence over the communicated objective assessment. Accordingly, rainfall accompanying a low flood-risk label may increase perceived risk, whereas dry conditions accompanying a high flood-risk label may reduce it. By contrast, rainfall accompanying a highrisk label or dry conditions accompanying a low-risk label may contribute relatively little beyond the objective risk already communicated. Thus, we expect an asymmetric influence of contextual weather, whereby weather cues exert a stronger effect on consumers' risk judgments when they conflict with objective risk information than when they reinforce it.

Risk Appraisals and Protection Motivation

To specify these risk judgments more precisely, we draw on theories of protective and riskrelated behavior. Across several such prominent frameworks, responses to risk mainly depend on two types of appraisals: i) how threatening the hazard appears, and ii) whether an effective response to that threat appears available and feasible. For example, the Protective Action Decision Model distinguishes threat perceptions from perceptions of protective actions (Lindell and Perry 2012), the Health Belief Model distinguishes perceived susceptibility and severity from perceived benefits, barriers, and self-efficacy (Rosenstock, Strecher, and Becker 1988), and the Extended Parallel Process Model distinguishes perceived threat from perceived efficacy (Witte 1992). The Protection Motivation Theory provides a particularly parsimonious formulation of this broader distinction in terms of threat appraisal and coping appraisal (Rogers 1975).

Threat appraisal refers to consumers' evaluation of the hazard itself, including perceptions of the hazard's severity, likelihood, and how personally vulnerable they perceive themselves to be (Rogers 1975). Coping appraisal, by contrast, captures confidence in one's ability to perform a protective action (self-efficacy), belief in that action's effectiveness (response efficacy), and the perceived costs of protection (Rogers 1975). Thus, threat appraisal captures how threatening the climate hazard appears, whereas coping appraisal captures whether responding to that threat appears feasible. These evaluation processes have been examined in the context of climate-related hazards, such as floods and wildfires, in terms of their necessity and importance in giving rise to protection motivation (e.g., Villamor et al. 2023; Martin, Bender, and Raish 2007; Martin, Bender, and Raish 2010; Babcicky and Seebauer 2019; Grothmann and Reusswig 2006; Zaalberg et al. 2009; Bamberg et al. 2017; Bubeck, Botzen, and Aerts 2012), which refers to the intention to take action in response to a perceived risk (Rogers 1975). In the context of property-level climate risk, protection motivation can be expressed in consumers' willingness to implement or pay for adaptation measures to reduce their exposure to climate hazards and protect the properties they own or may acquire. These actions can range from relatively low-cost measures such as purchasing sandbags and clearing drains in flood-prone areas, or removing dry vegetation and maintaining defensible space in wildfire-prone areas, to more extensive and costly interventions, including major property modifications such as installing flood barriers, elevating parts of the home, or using fireresistant building materials, and purchasing disaster insurance (Lee, Ma, and Li 2022; Kreibich et al. 2015; van Valkengoed and Steg 2019). Taken together, this literature suggests that consumers' motivation to respond to climate hazards depends not only on how much threat they perceive, but also on whether they believe that effective and feasible means of responding are available.

The preceding integration argument has implications for both threat and coping appraisal. If consumers form these appraisals by drawing jointly on objective risk information and contextual weather cues, then the effect of objective risk information on threat and coping appraisal should vary with the weather context in which the risk information is received. Because threat and coping appraisals, in turn, contribute to protection motivation, contextual effects on these appraisals may ultimately extend to consumers' willingness to implement or pay for protective measures (see Figure 2). Present Research Across five studies, we examine the integration of object risk information and contextual cues.

We study how local weather functions as a contextual cue that shapes how individuals perceive and respond to communicated climate risks for residential properties. We focus primarily on rainfall as the weather cue and flood risk as the focal climate hazard because floods are among the most widespread and economically consequential climate hazards globally. For example, in the European Union, floods account for the largest share of economic losses from weather-and climate-related extremes, representing nearly half of total losses between 1980 and 2024 (European Environment Agency 2025).

Study 1 examines whether weather conditions influence engagement with hazard-specific risk information. Using secondary field data, we test whether periods of prolonged rainfall are associated with increased interest in personal flood risk, as reflected in visits to a platform providing property-level flood risk information. Across the remaining four studies, we experimentally test whether weather moderates the effect of objective risk on threat and coping appraisal and, in turn, protection motivation, following the conceptual framework shown in figure 2.

In a series of vignette studies (Studies 2-5), participants evaluated residential properties while imagining that they were searching for a new home to purchase. Each property received a randomly assigned objective climate-hazard risk-level (high vs. low risk), communicated through visual risk labels (see figure 1). Participants evaluated each property under one of three weather conditions, manipulated via descriptions and visual stimuli: heavy rainfall (Rain), dry conditions (No-Rain), or a control condition in which no salient weather information is provided. For each property, participants assessed three outcomes: threat appraisal (the extent to which the property's flood risk is perceived as threatening), coping appraisal (the perceived ability to effectively manage or protect against that risk), and protection motivation (willingness to adopt or pay for protective measures).

We test whether objective flood-risk information and weather conditions interact in affecting threat and coping appraisal. For flood risk, rainfall is congruent with high objective risk and in-congruent with low objective risk, whereas dry conditions are congruent with low objective risk and incongruent with high objective risk. Consistent with our integration argument, we expect incongruent weather to shift appraisals away from the direction implied by the objective risk information, while congruent weather should produce little or no change relative to a control condition in which no salient weather information is provided. Accordingly, the interaction should shift threat and coping appraisal in opposite directions. H 1. Relative to the same properties presented in the control condition, incongruent weather decreases perceived threat for high-risk properties (i.e., high-risk properties under dry conditions) and increases perceived threat for low-risk properties (i.e., low-risk properties under rainfall conditions), whereas congruent weather produces negligible changes in threat appraisal (i.e., highrisk properties under rainfall and low-risk properties in dry conditions).

H 2. Relative to the same properties presented in the control condition, incongruent weather increases perceived coping ability for high-risk properties (i.e., high-risk properties under dry conditions) and decreases perceived coping ability for low-risk properties (i.e., low-risk properties under rainfall conditions), whereas congruent weather produces negligible changes in coping appraisal (i.e., high-risk properties under rainfall and low-risk properties in dry conditions).

Because protection motivation depends on both threat appraisal and coping appraisal, changes in either appraisal process can influence motivation to protect. However, objective risk can affect these pathways in opposing ways: higher risk should increase threat appraisal by highlighting hazard severity and vulnerability, but may decrease coping appraisal by making the hazard seem less manageable. Therefore, we do not derive a directional prediction for the net effect of interaction on protection motivation. Instead, we examine whether the changes in threat and coping appraisal produced by the interaction between objective risk information and weather context extend to consumers' willingness to implement or pay for protective measures.

Studies 2 and 3 test these hypotheses in a flood-risk context using flood-related appraisals and protection measures. Study 4 examines whether the same congruence logic generalizes to wildfire risk, where dry conditions rather than rainfall constitute the congruent weather cue for high risk. Finally, Study 5 tests the robustness of the moderation effect in a more complex decision environment involving another competing, non-focal climate risk. Except for Study 1, all studies were preregistered; questionnaires, pseudonymized data, analysis codes, as well as preregistrations can be accessed at https://researchbox.org/8285 (CODE: NNONJR).

STUDY 1

As a preliminary proof of concept for the relevance of local weather, Study 1 examined whether rainfall (or its absence) is associated with people's interest in their flood risk. As a proxy for this interest, we used the daily number of visitors to a website that reports flood risk for individual addresses, operated by a non-profit organization in the Netherlands https://www.klimaateffectatlas.nl/. Although the website reports on a range of climate-related risks, it is primarily consulted for flood risk information.

We examined whether the number of website visitors on a given day was associated with local weather conditions preceding that day. We expected that, as rainfall accumulates, people would be increasingly likely to inquire about their flood risk (i.e., an increase in website traffic), and conversely, that an increasing number of consecutive dry days (i.e., days without rainfall) would be associated with lower levels of interest (i.e., a decrease in website traffic).

Method

For the analyses, we combined two independent datasets: (1) website traffic data from the Klimaateffectatlas, and (2) meteorological data from the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute (KNMI).

The Klimaateffectatlas data consist of the daily number of website visitors per city in the Netherlands between 7 February 2025 and 20 April 2026. Due to privacy restrictions, more gran-ular location information about users was not available. Since the dataset only included city-day observations with at least one visitor, we expanded it to include all possible combinations of cities and dates in the given period by adding zero-visitor observations.

We downloaded freely available historical weather data from 48 weather stations spread throughout the Netherlands. For each weather station and date, we gathered the daily amount of rain (in 0.1 mm)foot_0 , average daily temperature (0.1°C) and average wind speed (in 0.1 m/s). We constructed measures of the number of consecutive days with and without rain preceding each observation.

Additionally, we computed rolling averages of rainfall, temperature and wind speed over the preceding three, seven and 14 days for each station-day observation in the data. Rainfall was the focal weather variable, while temperature and wind speed were also incorporated because they are key meteorological indicators associated with other major climate-related hazards, including climatological hazards (e.g., heatwaves and droughts) and meteorological hazards (e.g., storms).

We merged the two datasets by matching each city to its nearest weather station based on the latitude and longitude of city centroids and weather stations. The average distance between the city centroids and their matched weather stations was 14.27 km, and the largest distance was 35.61 km.

Discussion

Results provide empirical support for the relationship between local weather conditions and individuals' interest in their objective flood risk, a precedent to their motivation to take a protective action against flood risk. Importantly, we found that both consecutive days with and without rain significantly predicted website visits, but in opposite directions: as the number of rainy days increased, website visits also increased, whereas longer dry periods were associated with reduced website visits. Notably, the effect of consecutive days without rain was larger in magnitude than that of rainy days. This may be due to the fact that, in the Netherlands, rainfall is largely the meteorological norm, such that its prolonged absence may register as a more salient cue than rain itself. Importantly, the effect of rainfall remained largely robust after controlling for season and other weather conditions such as temperature and wind.

Study 1 suggests that local weather, particularly rainfall and its absence, is related to respondents' interest in their personal flooding risk. Building on this correlational evidence, the following studies experimentally test whether weather conditions also affect how individuals interpret and respond to objective risk communication. Specifically, we examine whether weather functions as a contextual cue that alters individuals' evaluations of objective risk information.

STUDY 2

Study 2 investigates whether and how weather influences the responses of people to objective flood risk. We tested the influence of the weather conditions under which one evaluates an objectively high or low flood risk on threat and coping appraisal, and whether these appraisals subsequently affect willingness to implement flood protection measures. To examine this, we conducted a vignette study in which participants evaluated properties with high and low flood risk under different weather conditions (Control, Rain, and No-rain conditions).

We expected participants to respond to flood risk information by reporting higher perceived threat and lower perceived coping ability for high-risk properties compared to low-risk properties.

Our primary interest, however, was to test the moderating effect of weather on the interpretation of objective risk information. We specifically tested how weather influences responses when congruent with the objective flood risk (rain with high risk; no rain with low risk) versus incongruent (rain with low risk; no rain with high risk). Finally, we examined whether any differences in threat and coping appraisal would propagate to willingness to implement protection measures.

Method

Procedure. Participants completed a hypothetical vignette task in which they imagined visiting a neighborhood to view houses for sale with the intention of purchasing one. Before the vignette task, participants were informed that each house would be presented with information on price, number of bedrooms and bathrooms, and a flood risk label (see figure 1), ranging from A (minimal risk) to E (extreme risk). They received instructions on how to interpret these labels (e.g., Label A indicates minimal flood risk over a 30-year period in terms of both likelihood and severity) and information about potential flood protection measures and their approximate costs.

A manipulation check assessed comprehension of this information.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three weather conditions: Control, Rain, or No-rain. All participants imagined waking up on the morning of their house-shopping trip (Day 1). In the experimental conditions, participants received daily weather forecasts (rainy or clear sky; see figure 4A) and images of the neighborhood to enhance vividness. Participants in the Control condition received no weather-related information. Participants then evaluated two houses in randomized order, each presented with its flood risk label -one with a high flood risk (Label D) and one with a low flood risk (Label B) -together with price, number of bedrooms and bathrooms, and an image depicting the assigned weather condition (see figure 5). Images in the control condition matched those of the No-rain condition. House pictures were randomly assigned to low and high risk label conditions.

As visiting a house more than once is typical before purchasing, participants then imagined revisiting the same two houses ten days later (Day 10). We informed participants in the Rain condition that it had rained continuously for the preceding ten days, and those in the No-rain condition that it had been sunny with no rainfall. We supported weather manipulations with 10-day forecast images (see figure 4B) and told both experimental groups that this consecutive weather pattern was unusual for the season (i.e., unusually wet and unusually dry). Control participants again received no weather information.

Participants re-evaluated the same two houses in the same order. House images were adjusted to reflect the cumulative impact of the weather (e.g., muddier or drier grass). All other property information remained identical. We used a two-wave design to increase the manipulation intensity by amplifying the contrast between a single day of rainfall and prolonged rainfall, in line with the stronger rainfall effects observed over longer time windows in Study 1.

For both house visit rounds, participants rated the severity, likelihood, and vulnerability of flooding as measures of threat appraisal (TA), and their perceived ability to protect the house, the effectiveness of flood protection measures, and the cost of protection as measures of coping appraisal (CA). They also indicated their willingness to implement flood protection measures (WTI) as a measure of protection motivation (see table 3 for details of the measures used). Finally, participants completed two manipulation checks assessing their recall of the weather conditions and the time elapsed between visits, and demographic questions.

Willingness to Implement.

We next estimated a structural equation model (SEM) to examine whether differences in TA and CA translated into willingness to implement flood protection measures (WTI). TA and CA were specified as correlated latent factors in the SEM. TA was measured with perceived severity, vulnerability, and likelihood and CA by self-efficacy, response efficacy, and perceived costs. WTI was regressed on both appraisals, flood risk, weather condition and their interaction, as well as the same property-level covariates and participants' SES used in the mixedmodels. The model also included paths from the experimental predictors to TA and CA to estimate indirect effects. To account for within-subjects dependency, we estimated robust standard errors clustered at the participant level. Model fit for SEM was good, with robust fit indices indicating a close representation of the data (CFI = .981, TLI = .966, RMSEA = .055, 90% CI [.047, .063], SRMR = .021).

Thus, the stronger influence of weather on perceived threat when it conflicted with communicated risk also carried through to willingness to implement protective measures.

Discussion

Study 2 showed that weather context influences the interpretation of objective flood risk. Weather, particularly rain, moderated the effect of risk on threat appraisal, but had no effect on coping appraisal, suggesting that threat perceptions are more responsive to contextual cues than copingrelated evaluations. The interaction between weather and risk labels on threat appraisal further suggests that weather does not simply increase or decrease perceived threat. Instead, its effect depends on congruency with objective risk information. Weather cues attenuate the influence of risk when the two sources are incongruent, whereas no reliable additional change in threat appraisal is observed when they are congruent. Only variation in threat appraisal predicted downstream pro- tection motivation; coping related appraisals were unrelated to WTI. Thus, although weather only moderated the effect of risk on threat perceptions, changes in perceived threat were sufficient to shape willingness to implement protection measures.

STUDY 3

Study 3 aims to replicate the findings of Study 2 with minor methodological modifications.

Study 2 focused on willingness to implement (WTI) flood protection measures captures the behav-ioral intention but do not necessarily reflect the economic tradeoffs involved in real-world purchasing decisions. Study 3 therefore used willingness to pay (WTP) as the outcome measure, which captures protection motivation in terms of monetary valuation and financial commitment.

In addition, prior research identified coping appraisal as a key predictor of protection motivation (Babcicky and Seebauer 2019; Poussin, Botzen, and Aerts 2014), in contrast we observed in Study 2 that coping appraisal did not predict protection motivation. This may be partially due to the vague nature of the adaptation measures. In Study 2, participants were only informed about floodproofing options in general. This limited specificity may have been sufficient for risk information to influence coping appraisal, but insufficient for coping beliefs to translate into concrete behavioral intentions. To address this, Study 3 introduced a more concrete adaptation option in the form of a defined "Floodproofing Service Package," with specified contents and a maximum cost.

Additionally, because weather can influence affect producing a potential experimental confound (Denissen et al. 2008), we measured and controlled for participants' positive and negative mood to determine whether the effects of weather on risk perception and protection motivation operated beyond mood-related influences.

Method

Procedure. The methods of Study 3 were largely identical to Study 2. Before starting the vignette task, we introduced participants to a Floodproofing Service Package. This package involved an expert visit to the property to implement flood protection measures such as sealing doors and windows or installing temporary or permanent flood barriers. The package was clearly defined in terms of its contents and maximum costs ($10,000). A hover-over summary of the package details was also available during the house evaluation phase to minimize potential recall issues.

We then measured, together with threat and coping appraisal, protection motivation as participants' willingness to pay (WTP) for the service package using two items: one assessing the likelihood of purchasing the package (assuming they had decided to purchase the house) and one assessing the maximum amount they would be willing to pay for it. We also adapted the response efficacy component of coping appraisal to assess the perceived effectiveness of the service package. Previous studies focused on isolated risk dimensions, either flooding or wildfire risk. In reality, however, properties are not characterized by a single risk. Discussion

However, wildfire risk label did directly influence willingness to pay for flood protection measures in interaction with the presented flood risk. Specifically, under control weather and low flood risk, exposure to a high wildfire risk label reduced willingness to pay for flood protection. This suggests that a competing hazard risk information may influence protection decisions even when it has little detectable effect on appraisal of the focal risk.

Weather moderated the effect of flood risk labels on threat appraisal and ultimately influenced protection motivation, even in the presence of another competing risk label. These findings suggest that the weather moderation effect is robust even when multiple risk signals are simultaneously present. Replicating previous studies, incongruent weather conditions weakened the directional effect of flood risk labels on perceived threat. Results for congruent weather conditions were more mixed. Consistent with earlier findings, dry weather did not significantly alter responses under low flood risk. However, under high flood risk, rain unexpectedly reduced threat appraisal. This finding contrasts with both intuition and prior studies, which generally found that congruent weather cues did not affect responses to risk labels. One possible explanation is that as the present study used the extreme endpoints of the flood risk scale ("A" vs. "E"), in this context, prolonged rainfall combined with an extreme flood-risk label may have reduced the label's credibility: participants may have inferred that if flooding was not visibly occurring despite persistent rain, the "E" designation overstated the actual threat and was therefore unreliable. However, because this explanation was not directly tested, the unexpected Rain effect should be interpreted cautiously.

GENERAL DISCUSSION

Weather conditions systematically alter the interpretation of objective climate risk information.

Across five studies, we show that weather conditions are associated with engagement with climate risk information, interact with objective risk information to influence consumers' threat perceptions and, ultimately, their intentions to protect themselves against flooding and wildfire.

A central finding is that weather primarily influences the interpretation of objective risk through incongruence attenuation rather than congruence amplification. When weather conditions conflict with objective risk (e.g., high flood risk during drought or low flood risk during rainfall), individuals systematically adjust their judgments, attenuating the directional influence of the objective risk information. When weather conditions align with objective risk (e.g., high flood risk during rainfall or low flood risk during dry conditions), however, the effect of objective risk information on threat perceptions is generally smaller and less consistent. Thus, the influence of immediate weather context is not simply additive. Rather, it depends on its relationship with the objective risk, with conflicting contextual information exerting the more pronounced influence.

Theoretical Implications

Our findings contribute to the literature reporting effects of local weather on general climate judgments. Prior work has established that weather influences climate-related beliefs and concerns (Sugerman et al. 2021; Sisco 2021; Li et al. 2011). However, there is less research on whether and how weather influences the subjective perception of specific climate risks, such as floods and wildfires. This question is important because perceptions of personal climate-hazard risk are a key antecedent of engagement in adaptive behaviors (van Valkengoed et al. 2024; Haden et al. 2012). In our secondary data analysis, we examine whether objectively measured weather conditions predict consumers' interest in information about their personal climate-hazard risk. Although periods of heavy rainfall may temporarily increase the immediate likelihood of flooding, they do not alter the underlying long-term risk profile of a location. Nevertheless, we find that prolonged rainy periods increase interest in personal flood risk information, whereas prolonged dry periods have the opposite effect. This pattern is consistent with immediate weather conditions making hazard-related information more salient or accessible, although further research is needed to directly identify the underlying psychological process. Notably, the weather here is objectively measured rather than subjectively perceived which is consistent with prior research showing that both subjective perceptions of weather and actual weather conditions can shape climate-related beliefs and judgments (Ghasemi et al. 2026; Sisco et al. 2022).

Beyond demonstrating that weather influences interest in personal climate-hazard risk, our experiments identify the interaction between contextual weather and objective risk information as a source of heterogeneity in consumers' risk judgments. Although objective risk labels communicate different levels of hazard exposure, their influence on consumers' judgments depends on the immediate weather context. This relationship can also be framed as a varying influence of weather across different levels of objective risk. Rainfall increased perceived threat when objective flood risk was low, while it had no influence on perceived threat when the objective risk was high. These findings suggest that the influence of contextual weather is particularly pronounced when it conflicts with the communicated risk, rather than when it corroborates it. This extends research showing that risk judgments are constructed from multiple sources of information (Maibach et al. 2023; Lechowska 2018; Loewenstein et al. 2001; Siegrist et al. 2020; Jenkins et al. 2024) by showing that consumers' interpretations of personally relevant climate risk depend on the interaction between the level of objective risk and the immediate weather context. More broadly, these findings suggest that differences in consumers' risk perceptions arise not only from variation in their objective risk levels, but also from how these risk levels interact with contextual cues. Considering other contextual factors beyond weather may therefore reveal additional sources of heterogeneity, as objective risk may also interact with these factors to influence how consumers perceive and respond to climate risk.

Importantly, this interaction was asymmetric. Conflicting weather produced the clearest shifts in risk perception, whereas corroborating weather generally produced smaller and less consistent changes. This pattern suggests that contextual cues do not simply add to objective risk. When weather is congruent with the objective information, it largely provides a signal consistent with what is already conveyed by the risk label. When it is incongruent, however, it introduces a competing source of information that creates greater scope for judgment to shift. This interpretation is consistent with research suggesting that incongruent information can attract greater attention and more extensive processing than congruent information (Lee and Schumann 2004). However, our studies do not directly measure attention to, confidence in, or reliance on the competing cues. Future research could therefore examine the processes through which consumers integrate conflicting objective and contextual information.

Our findings also contribute to research applying protection-motivation frameworks to climate risk adaptation. Causal evidence remains relatively limited in this area, because most applications of the theory rely on correlational designs (Villamor et al. 2023; Martin et al. 2007; Martin et al. 2010; Babcicky et al. 2019; Grothmann et al. 2006; Zaalberg et al. 2009; Bamberg et al. 2017; Bubeck et al. 2012). By experimentally manipulating both objective risk and weather context, we demonstrate that these appraisals themselves can be influenced by the information context in which a climate risk is evaluated. Objective risk strongly influenced both threat appraisal and coping appraisal. However, the weather effect emerged more consistently for threat appraisal. This difference is informative because the two appraisals tap into distinct aspects of protective decision making. Threat appraisal is related to how concerned one is about the hazard itself, whereas coping appraisal is related to the feasibility and effectiveness of responding to it. Therefore, our results suggest that how risk information translates into perceived threat appears more open to influence from contextual weather cues, whereas its translation into a sense of one's ability to cope appears comparatively more resistant to such influence.

These appraisal differences also affected protection motivation. Threat appraisal consistently predicted consumers' willingness to implement or pay for adaptive measures across the experimental studies. Moreover, the indirect effects through threat appraisal followed the broader asymmetry observed in risk perception. Thus, variation in how consumers interpret communicated climate risk can ultimately be reflected in their willingness to invest in protection against that risk. In contrast, the relationship of protection motivation with coping appraisal was weaker and less consistent.

However, we do not interpret this as an evidence that coping appraisal is unimportant. Indeed, prior work has found that both appraisals contribute to protection motivation (Bamberg et al. 2017; Grothmann et al. 2006) or coping appraisal emerges as the stronger predictor (Bubeck et al. 2012; Babcicky et al. 2019). Rather, our findings suggest that threat appraisal may be particularly important in the property-level climate-risk decisions examined here. The relative roles of threat and coping appraisal should be tested across other protective measures, hazard contexts, and decisions involving greater personal experience or real financial consequences.

Prior research demonstrates that weather conditions can significantly influence mood; specifically, overcast or rainy conditions often increase negative affect, whereas sunlight and warmer temperatures elevate positive mood (Klimstra et al. 2011; Keller et al. 2005; Kööts, Realo, and Allik 2011, but see Denissen et al, 2008). Under the affect heuristic framework, individuals rely on these incidental emotional states to inform their risk judgments, with positive affect attenuating and negative affect inflating perceived risk (Slovic et al. 2005). However, our findings challenge this mood-driven explanation. If immediate affect were the primary mechanism, rainy conditions would uniformly elevate threat perceptions regardless of communicated risk levels or hazard types.

Instead, we observe a distinct interaction with the underlying risk environment: rain amplifies flood threat only when communicated risk is low, and actually attenuates threat perceptions within a wildfire context. Because these divergent patterns persist in our studies as well as in models controlling for self-reported mood, we argue that weather does not solely operate as a valence-based mood inducer. Rather, it functions as a contextual cue evoking hazard-specific associations.

Practical Implications

Our findings have potential implications for consumers and organizations that provide propertylevel climate-risk information. Climate-risk labels and disclosures typically summarize relatively stable, longer-term hazard exposure, yet consumers may encounter this information under highly variable short-term weather conditions. Our results suggest that these immediate conditions can alter how the same communicated risk is interpreted, particularly when the weather appears inconsistent with the objective risk disclosure. This matters in consequential decisions such as deciding whether to invest in adaptation. For example, when high-risk warnings are encountered during weather conditions that appear "safe," consumers may downplay the threat at exactly the moment when caution and action are most needed. This is especially relevant for flood risk. For example, dry conditions can increase flood risk by altering soil and vegetation in ways that reduce how much water the ground can absorb and increase surface runoff, which makes flooding more likely when rain arrives (Barendrecht et al. 2024). Yet the dry weather itself may give a false sense that flooding is unlikely, which in turn undermines protection.

Conversely, communicating low risk when weather cues signal potential danger may lead to inflated threat perception. This can lead to unnecessary worry or unnecessary use of resources in areas where they are not urgently needed. That said, this kind of over-preparedness is not necessarily harmful in all cases. It may even strengthen overall resilience by maintaining a general state of alertness and readiness to respond. In wildfire-prone areas, for example, fire does not always remain confined to high-risk zones; embers can travel long distances with the wind and ignite new fires kilometers away (Anthenien, Tse, and Carlos Fernandez-Pello 2006). In this sense, a broader distribution of preparedness can sometimes improve community response at a regional or national level.

More broadly, our results suggest that people interpret risk communication in the context of everything happening around them, and weather is a particularly salient part of that context. Communicated risk is not processed in isolation; it is interpreted in light of the visible contextual cues that can either reinforce or contradict its impact. Recognizing this contextual sensitivity may be especially important when risk information is intended to support major financial and protectionrelated decisions.

Limitations and Future Directions

We note that the influence of weather cues may vary across hazard domains. For example, although the wildfire study (Study 4) showed directionally consistent effects, the interaction did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that rainfall may be a weaker or less diagnostic cue in wildfire contexts than in hazards where precipitation is more directly consequential, such as flooding. This highlights the importance of systematically examining multiple weather dimensions (e.g., wind, heat, humidity, and antecedent dryness) and their hazard-specific diagnostic value in future research.

Our findings provide initial evidence for the role of weather cues in shaping risk perceptions, while future field studies could further extend these insights by examining weather-risk relation-ships in naturally occurring hazard contexts. Importantly, Study 1 already provided evidence from a real-world setting, showing that prolonged rainfall (and its absence) was associated with increased (decreased) public interest in flood risk, based on naturally occurring weather conditions and real hazard concerns. Field studies can further build on this evidence by capturing the complexity of real-world hazard environments, where multiple concurrent weather cues (e.g., temperature, wind speed, humidity) and contextual factors (e.g., media coverage) jointly shape risk perceptions.

These cues may interact with, reinforce, or attenuate the influence of any single weather cue as a signal of risk. Study 5 represents a step in this direction by demonstrating that the core effect persists in a more complex risk environment.

Finally, the present studies identify the behavioral pattern produced by congruent and incongruent cues but do not directly observe the cognitive process through which consumers integrate them. One possible explanation could be related to the perceived diagnosticity of contextual cues.

Consumers may regard weather conditions as especially more informative about their personal risk when they contradict with the objective risk as these cues are immediate, more salient, and more concrete.

An alternative explanation, however, is that an incongruent weather may exert its influence by undermining the confidence in the objective risk label, leading consumers to discount the objective assessment rather than necessarily assign greater weight to weather. These mechanisms could operate independently or jointly, producing similar patterns of perceived risk. Future research could directly examine the psychological processes underlying this asymmetry to better understand how consumers integrate communicated risk with contextual weather cues and why conflicting weather exerts a stronger influence than corroborating weather.

In sum, we demonstrate that weather can function as a contextual cue that shapes climate risk perception by moderating threat appraisal processes. These findings provide a foundation for future research on how weather context can be systematically incorporated into models of risk perception and communication, with the aim of improving the timing and effectiveness of interventions designed to enhance adaptive behavior.
