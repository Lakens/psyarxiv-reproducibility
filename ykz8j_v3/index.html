Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

How humans come to acquire language has been a persistent object of philosophical and scientific inquiry, in part because it bears on fundamental questions about the nature of human cognition.

Classically, this field of inquiry has centered innateness as a critical component of language acquisition, traditionally characterizing innate capability in terms of richly specified, biologically endowed linguistic structure, such as Universal Grammar. Contemporary theories have evolved considerably from these earlier formulations and now present several differing candidates for innateness in language. Throughout this evolution, though, questions of innateness have remained central to theories of language acquisition, even as the nature of those questions has shifted in response to new sources of evidence. In this Perspective, we consider evidence from three complementary sources of evidence-linguistic diversity, human social interaction, and language models (LMs)-that together inform the question of what children must bring to the task of language learning. We conclude by identifying desiderata for theories of language acquisition that seek to specify the nature, scope, and explanatory role of innate contributions to language development.

Communication through language is one of the most distinctive features of our species, and how humans acquire this capacity remains among the most enduring questions in the study of human nature. Central to this inquiry is the concept of innateness, which has shaped theories of language acquisition for decades.

More specifically, philosophers, scientists, and educators have long debated whether human language (and human knowledge more broadly) depends on innate concepts or representations, a position historically associated with content nativism 1 (see Box 1). Determining what, if anything, is innate matters not only for theories of language acquisition, but also for broader accounts of human cognition and of how biology and experience jointly shape development and evolution. There is broad agreement that some features of the architecture of learning must themselves be biologically endowed (contra Locke, 1690/1948), such that even "the behaviorist is knowingly and cheerfully up to his neck in innate mechanisms" (Quine, 1969, pp. 95-96). Beyond this minimal commitment, however, consensus quickly breaks down. Yet any complete theory of language acquisition must ultimately specify precisely what is innate, how it is represented, and how it interacts with experience during learning.

Within language acquisition research, innateness has historically catalyzed vigorous scientific debate that focused on whether humans are born with specialized cognitive structures for language (and/or thought), or whether linguistic representations instead emerge from domain-general learning mechanisms, potentially in combination with social interaction. At one pole sit perspectives based on strong nativist frameworks, often based on theories of Chomsky (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hvrMDv Chomsky, 1965) and other scholars from this tradition (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GYvJT8 Jackendoff, 2002 Jackendoff, , 2010;; Pinker, 1994). These (content) nativist approaches propose that humans are born with a specialized mental architecture for language, an internalized generative system that contains domain-specific representations that "subserve knowledge and behavior and are hardwired in advance" (Elman et al., 1996, p. 25). At the other pole sit empiricist and most 1 We use content nativism to refer to theoretical positions that posit innate concepts, representations, or knowledge as foundational to human cognition, including language. By contrast, we use innateness more broadly to refer to the claim that some aspects of the organism's initial endowment are not acquired through postnatal experience, without specifying what those innate contributions consist of. Thus, acknowledging innateness does not in itself entail a commitment to one particular account of nativism.

Historically, these differing perspectives polarized scientific discourse on language acquisition (Allen & Behrens, 2019) such that even the simple choice of terminology, whether to describe a particular process as "acquired" or "learned", signaled allegiance to one theoretical camp or the other. This tension is further complicated by varying definitions of what it means to be innate, which can refer to not-learned, genetically encoded, universal, amongst other traits (see Table 1), leading some to claim that the concept is not 'coherent or theoretically useful' (Mameli & Bateson, 2011, p. 436 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KVNWJP Griffiths, 2002 and Linquist, 2018). Thus, innateness has been both a source of intense controversy and a valued theoretical guide in the study of the origins of language (Blitman, 2014; Elman et al., 1996).

Today, contemporary developmental science provides little support for treating the innate and acquired as opposing causal forces. It is widely acknowledged that development arises through continuous interactions among organismic and environmental processes such that even strongly biologically prepared capacities depend on developmental environments for their realization (Griffiths & Tabery, 2013; Mameli & Bateson, 2011). The traditional nature-nurture opposition is therefore a false dichotomy. Moving away from a historical debate towards contemporary discourse, the literature now encompasses a wide range of alternative accounts to which we return below in considering contemporary proposals (e.g., Aslin & Newport, 2012; Hartshorne & Snedeker, 2026; Lake et al., 2017; Levinson, 2025; see also Box 2). Our goal is not to revive debates around nativism and empiricism, nor to replace this historical opposition with broad claims of interactionism. Instead, we argue that agreement about interactionism does not eliminate the need to specify the interacting components and the nature of interactions. The question of innateness, as we characterize it here, concerns the learner-internal component of how language is learned-an indispensable part of any interactionist account.

By describing innateness as an "orienting principle", we mean that it serves to organize our approach to explanation: what properties of the learner must be in place for language to emerge through interaction and over time? This approach invites theories that specify the learner's contributions to language learning and that explore how these contributions interact with particular developmental environments to produce observed outcomes. Innate specification could take very different forms. They might include a broadly applicable statistical learning architecture, biases in attention or perception, early emerging sociocognitive capacities, conceptual primitives, or specifically linguistic representations (see Box 2). These possibilities correspond to different tiers of explanation, and the evidence reviewed below bears differently on each. Our aim is therefore not to establish that language is "innate" in a unitary sense, but to use contemporary evidence to prioritize precision and specificity in formulating plausible claims about what is developmentally prespecified and what can emerge through learning and interaction.

In this Perspective, we draw on three related but distinct bodies of evidence-linguistic and environmental diversity, language in human interaction, and language learning in artificial language models-to evaluate contemporary claims about innateness in language acquisition (see Figure 1). We focus on these developments because in combination, they constrain three fundamental components of any theory of language acquisition: the diversity of input and developmental outcomes that surface in learners' ecologies, how interactions mediate the relationship between input and outcomes, and the architectural constraints of a learning apparatus that produces human-like learning. The progression of the paper thus moves from ecological descriptors of diverse conditions for learning, through interactional dynamics that structure learning within these diverse conditions, to candidate mechanisms for language learning. Considered together, they invite testable questions about which biases, capacities, or representations must be developmentally prespecified and how the factors may interact with experience. This Perspective is not intended as a comprehensive survey of the vast literature that is relevant to linguistic innateness. Such a survey would encompass, among other topics, a more thorough accounting of comparative cognition (including non-human animals and artificial intelligence), theories and findings concerning language evolution (including homesign and emerging sign systems), the cultural amplification of learning biases, and research on behavioral genetics and heritability. This article is intentionally put forth as a Perspective rather than a review article as its purpose is not to exhaustively catalogue the relevant evidence, but to selectively synthesize interconnected bodies of work that, when considered together, motivate new approaches to contemporary innateness. This Perspective proceeds as follows. First, we describe three complementary constraints on contemporary claims about innateness: Linguistic and environmental diversity establish the range of outcomes and learning environments that any theory must explain; research on human interaction clarifies what information is actually available to children within those environments and what capacities may be required to make use of it; and computational models provide a means of asking which properties of a learning system must be prespecified for human-like acquisition to emerge. We then bring these directions together in the final section, where we consider how contemporary theories of innateness could be formulated and tested, and how the different proposals summarized in Box 2 locate prespecified structure at different levels.

2. Diversity as a test for innateness

Any account of linguistic innateness must grapple with two striking facts about human language. The first is its remarkable consistency: across cultures, contexts, and circumstances, children reliably acquire language. The second is its equally striking diversity: languages and contexts for learning vary profoundly, and children readily adapt to that variation. Questions about the developmental origins of language are therefore inseparable from questions of diversity. We begin with diversity because claims about innateness are often established from evidence of invariance: they propose that some property of the learner produces reliable developmental outcomes despite variation in experience. Evaluating such claims therefore requires specifying which outcomes are consistent versus which vary, and whether apparent regularities survive examination across a sufficiently broad range of languages and learning environments.

We focus here on two forms of diversity: typological diversity, concerning the range of languages studied, and environmental diversity, concerning the range of learning ecologies considered. Whereas typological diversity has long figured prominently in discourse on innateness, the challenges posed by environmental diversity have come into focus more recently.

2.1 Typological Diversity

Accounting for typological diversity has long been integral to any account of innate capacities that children bring to the task of acquiring language and remains a key consideration today. At the outset, we provide a historical perspective with examples not intended to characterize the current state of theorizing which has evolved considerably since these proposals were formulated. Rather, we cite these examples to guard against a historical simplification, the suggestion that traditional nativist accounts were largely indifferent to cross-linguistic diversity or developed without engaging variation across languages.

Linguistic diversity was often central to the nativist theoretical enterprise, both on the grounds that an innate architecture was required to accommodate diversity and as evidence used to distinguish between competing accounts of what that architecture might look like. Theories within the Government and Binding framework, in particular, were designed to explain how children could rapidly acquire grammatical structures that vary across languages while drawing on an innate system (Baker, 2005; Chomsky, 1981a; Gibson & Wexler, 1994). One example is the Maturation Hypothesis (Borer & Wexler, 1987), which proposed that some grammatical principles emerge according to an internally determined developmental timetable, much like other biological functions. Importantly, such proposals drew on cross-linguistic evidence in support of nativist theory. For example, on the basis of the relatively late emergence of passive constructions both in English and Hebrew, Borer and Wexler argued that some principles, such as those underlying the grammatical dependency in passive sentences, mature rather late, around four years of age. This exemplifies a strong form of the innateness claim, according to which aspects of grammatical development are thought to reflect internally guided maturational processes.

Grounded in cross-linguistic evidence, this example demonstrates that typological diversity has been central to questions of innateness.

At the same time, these accounts have often been criticized for relying on too narrow a range of languages (Ambridge & Lieven, 2011; Evans & Levinson, 2009; Tomasello, 2009 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?j3LRT2 Han et al., 2016; Viau & Lidz, 2011). To return to the prior examplehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?9AHH30 although some cross-linguistic data was invoked in positing that passive structures follow an innate maturational schedulehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mqiqsn data from a wider range of languageshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?OOqLzi including Zulu (Suzmanhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TowdGV 1985) , K'iche' (Pye & Quixtan Poz, 1988) , Sesotho (Demuth, 1989) , and Inuktitut (Allen & Crago, 1996) , have showed that passive-like structures do not emerge late across all languages. Instead, their developmental timing appears to depend on properties of the input, including frequency and functional distribution.

Typological diversity also constrains claims about universal innate grammatical structure. Evans & Levinson (2009) argued that some of the categories often treated as fundamental building blocks of language, such as nouns, verbs, adjectives, and prepositions, are language-specific not only in how they are marked, but also in whether they can be straightforwardly identified at all. Some languages lack adverbs, some lack adjectives, and some have been argued even to lack a clear noun-verb distinction, though this remains controversial (Evans & Levinson, 2009). In polysynthetic languages, moreover, forms corresponding to nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs may appear as affixes within highly complex words rather than as independent lexical items (Fortescue et al., 2017).

One dimension of typological diversity is structural diversity-that is, variation across languages in the grammatical categories, organizations, and constructions they instantiate. Such variation places important constraints on claims about the linguistic content that could plausibly be innate. Scholarly positions span a broad range, from proposals that learners are endowed with rich, high-level linguistic universals despite substantial cross-linguistic variation (e.g., Crain & Thornton, 1998; Pinker, 1984) to arguments that there are no true structural universals at all (Evans & Levinson, 2009), and that linguistic structure must instead be derived from variable input (see Ambridge et al., 2014; Braine, 1992; see also Section 3 below). This range of positions remains central to contemporary debates about innateness because claims about innate contributions to language learning must ultimately be evaluated against the diversity of human languages that children learn. The more systematically theories are tested across a broad range of language structures, the more clearly we can determine the extent to which proposed universals reflect species-wide constraints on language learning, distributional properties of particular languages, or interactions between the two.

2.2. Environmental diversity

A second, complementary constraint that has perhaps received less attention until recently comes from environmental diversity: the substantial variation across environments within which language learning outcomes are attained. Children do not just acquire varying languages; they acquire language across contexts and communities that differ markedly in who speaks to children, how caregiving is networked and distributed, and how linguistic interaction is organized. To the extent that language develops reliably across such heterogeneous learning ecologies, theories of language learning must account for both the stability of developmental outcomes across environmental variation and the ways in which learning is shaped by the particulars of that variation.

Paying closer attention to environmental variation complicates attempts to isolate the starting state of particular components of language from the experience through which they are acquired. Because words must be learned from experience via exposure, classical nativist accounts often distinguished the lexicon from an autonomous syntactic system, with innateness claims focused primarily on the latter (e.g., Crain & Thornton, 1998; Pinker, 1984; Valian, 2014). However, recent evidence, though not weakening the inescapable distinction between lexical and grammatical knowledge (speakers can clearly generalize grammatical patterns to novel or meaningless lexical items), presents a challenge to strong claims that lexical and grammatical knowledge are acquired through largely independent systems. Evidence increasingly points to interdependence across these domains during development.

Across children, syntactic and morphological development are closely associated with vocabulary growth (Bates & Goodman, 1997; Frank et al., 2021), although the two remain empirically distinguishable (Donnelly et al., 2025). Similarly, many contemporary computational models acquire lexical and structural regularities through shared learning mechanisms. Neuroimaging studies have also not consistently identified a neural system devoted exclusively to syntax (e.g., Fedorenko, Ivanova, et al., 2024; Fedorenko, Piantadosi, et al., 2024). Together, these findings are compatible with abstract grammatical representations but suggest that such representations may emerge through learning processes that are interdependent with lexical knowledge.

In line with this more integrated perspective, attention has shifted from diversity in syntactic structure alone to how children extract and build language structure, including but not limited to syntax, from the diverse inputs available to them in their environments. Thus, environmental diversity is theoretically and empirically informative from both nativist and non-nativist perspectives. In the search for what is innate, understanding environmental variation is especially important because it sheds light on how learning proceeds under diverse forms of input. If children succeed despite substantially different forms of language input, then the mechanisms that have evolved to support language learning must be flexible enough to operate over such heterogeneous forms of experience. Accordingly, in some communities, children acquire language successfully despite receiving relatively little direct input from adult caregivers (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BCIquK https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?tNfF4l Bunce et al., 2025; Casillas et al., 2020 Casillas et al., , 2021;; Foushee & Srinivasan, 2024). More broadlyhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?DGTJT6 learning appears to be adaptable to input variation : Schröder et al. (2012) , for example, found that input patterns associated with stronger language outcomes in Berlin were associated with poorer outcomes in Delhi. These findings suggest that children's learning mechanisms are well positioned to capitalize on locally available forms of input, highlighting adaptive learning as an important consideration in modern accounts of innateness.

These findings also underscore a methodological point: claims about what counts as language input depend heavily on how input is measured and operationalized. Newer large-scale approaches make it possible to test longstanding assumptions about input and learning across broader and more representative samples (Frank et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?420vYf 2025). Bergelson et al. (2023) , for example, used daylong audio recordings to study language input in socio-demographically diverse communities (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Wo7c1x Cychosz & Cristia, 2022). Importantly, they adopted an inclusive definition of input that included child-available rather than only child-directed speech (Sperry et al., 2019). Their findings partly aligned with prior work -for instance, children who heard more speech also produced more speech-but they did not replicate several factors often treated as universal causal predictors of language development, including child gender, multilingualism, and maternal education. Large-scale sampling across diverse environments helps distinguish robust regularities from patterns that arise only under particular methodological approaches or within specific contexts.

Taken together, typological and environmental diversity define two interacting dimensions of the language-learning problem: children acquire different linguistic systems, and they do so across markedly different learning environments. These dimensions are not independent. The structure of the language being learned shapes which cues are available and informative, while the organization of the learning environment shapes how often those cues occur, how they are distributed, and how accessible they are to the learner (Elouatiq et al., 2026). Systematic investigation of language variation is therefore essential for evaluating claims about what must be innate (see Goldin-Meadow, 2005). At the same time, variation alone does not reveal which aspects of the available information children actually exploit in learning linguistic structure. We therefore turn next to the structure of interaction, asking what information social interactions make available to learners and what learner-internal (or innate) capacities may be required to make use of it.

3. Interaction and language acquisition

There is substantial and converging evidence illustrating that (a) children's social and linguistic input is rich in meaning and interactional in structure (e.g., Cameron-Faulkner, 2014; Chouinard & Clark, 2003; Clark & Kelly, 2022; Yu & Smith, 2017; Yurovsky et al., 2013), and (b) children have early-emerging capacities for social interaction that may help them adapt to and benefit from the interactional language environment in which they are being raised (e.g., Goren et al., 1975; Jaffe et al., 2001; Nguyen et al., 2022; Senju & Csibra, 2008). Characterizing these interactions is important on the grounds that the strength of any argument for innate knowledge depends on knowledge of what information is available to the learner in connection with their emergent capabilities. Any contemporary account of innateness must therefore take seriously what children's interactional environments provide and the internal capacities they bring to those environments.

3.1 Affordances of interaction

The presence of structure in linguistic input does not, on its own, support any particular claim about innateness. All theories of language development must explain how learners exploit the information available in their environments. The relevance to innateness arises when we ask whether that information is sufficient to account for the knowledge children ultimately attain. Traditional arguments for innate linguistic knowledge, such as Poverty of the Stimulus, have relied on claims about a gap between the evidence available in experience and the knowledge children ultimately attain (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FEBJuL Lasnik & Lidz, 2016, https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FEBJuL. Evaluating those claims therefore requires us to characterize the information available to learners in natural interaction. We ask here whether interaction provides cues that narrow that gap and, in turn, what capacities learners must bring to exploit them. To do this, we must first understand whether the affordances of interaction-however they are locally realized-give children access to the key information they need to make the right inductive generalizations about language. The features of the input that matter must be considered given the diversity of socio-cultural environments sketched in section 2 above.

The first task, then, is to identify which aspects of language use help bridge the gap between the linguistic evidence children encounter and the knowledge they ultimately attain. Because children encounter and acquire linguistic structures through communicating with others, audience design, intersubjectivity, and shared social aims are built into the very process of learning a language. Thus, cross-cultural tendencies stemming from interactional needs can help us distinguish between languagespecific, social-specific, and other domains of innate support for robustness in early language learning. It has long been suspected that these aspects of interaction can shed light on the process of learning itself.

For example, infant-directed speech (IDS)-a special case of audience design-has garnered a special explanatory role in theories of early language development as a mechanism for drawing infants' attention to linguistic signals and making the structure and meaning of those signals more salient, resulting in more efficient learning in some contexts (Cooper & Aslin, 1990; Fernald et al., 1989; Frank et al., 2009; Leung et al., 2021; ManyBabies Consortium, 2020; Moerk, 1976). We can leverage the fact that IDS format and rates vary across cultures to better understand how children's learning systems adapt to local patterns in language use (e.g., by triggering a mechanism that shifts greater attention to audible input).

A similar case can be made for the multimodal and first-person perspective of children's input; such work shows how potential speaker meanings and relevant possible responses become helpfully narrowed during interaction (Cameron-Faulkner, 2014; Clark & Kelly, 2022; Reddy et al., 2013; Snow, 1977; VanDam et al., 2016; Yu & Smith, 2017; Yurovsky et al., 2013; Zhang & Yu, 2025). Other research focuses our attention on structural aspects of interaction itselfhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?h8yVBM for examplehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Gu0pHZ that children's input unfolds in the form of turns at talk, which can provide a source of immediate linguistic feedback (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?h8yVBM Chouinard & Clark, 2003; Farrar, 1992; Saxton, 1997) , clues to verb paradigms (Clark & de Marneffe, 2012; Ibbotson et al., 2014) , and tools for avoiding (or repairing) misunderstandings (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Y1ls0j. Clark, 2020; Nikolaus & Fourtassi, 2023). Such findings show how linguistic concepts may naturally arise (and offer themselves for encoding) in the course of dynamic, interactional settings. For example, Clark and de Marneffe (2012) show that adult construals and responses lead children to distinguish infinitival (donner 'give-Inf') and participial (donné 'give-PP') homophonous verb forms in French (e.g., via the addition of auxiliary verbs, given a close temporal pairing with salient events, etc.).

Thus, children encounter language as socially motivated, interactionally structured input that may guide attention to particular cues and engage adaptive learning strategies while also reflecting broader typological regularities in human language.

Sensitivity to these interactional cues, which ultimately arise from the need to understand others and make oneself understood, is built somehow into the learning process-the question is really what might be innate or linguistic about such sensitivities? Computational linguistics research so far has done little to examine these questions -for example, by integrating multi-modal, interactive input and the sociocognitive skills that may support its use into model training. Doing so may, however, shed critical light on key evolved features of human language acquisition, including adaptive learning and interactionready linguistic representations. The next section examines what kinds of social sensitivities children might bring to the language learning task, innate or early emerging, to illustrate the routes by which sociocognitive abilities may shape the acquisition and transmission of linguistic representations.

3.2 Innate or early emerging social cognition

Children appear to have the basic sociocognitive skills needed to coordinate and communicate with others from very early in life. Theories accounting for these traits range from hypotheses about individual, human-specific capacities (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5U9OHC Gergely & Csibra, 2003; Tomasello, 2010 Tomasello, , 2014) ) to more general proposals linking social instincts to the emergence and transmission of linguistic systems (Levinson, 2006 (Levinson, , 2025)). Each perspective points to shared sociocommunicative skills (not language structure) as the unifying feature of human language communities, reframing what is considered innate, and contrasting the lack of universal grammatical forms (Evans & Levinson, 2009 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?DnjvGp) with cross-population consistency in the structure of interaction itself (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?QXrpdE. Clark, 1996; Levinson, 2006). A key implication of this work is that the coordination of cooperative, communicative behavior in real time between two or more human minds relies on cognitive biases that produce these consistent solutions (Levinson, 2006 (Levinson, , 2025 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PropJs. Most evidence put forward for innate (or innate-like) social capacities comes from developmental or evolutionary work.

Evidence for an early emerging capacity for social interaction is supported by young infants' orientation to human social signalshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KyU4JP including faces (Goren et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GnSFZ3 1975; Reid et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BkFSlX 2017) , speech (Dehaene-Lambertz, 2000; Forgács et al., 2022) , gaze following (Çetinçelik et al., 2021; Csibra & Gergely, 2011; Senju & Csibra, 2008) , and multi-modal turn-taking (Jaffe et al., 2001; Nguyen et al., 2022; Snow, 1977). While infants are capable of learning linguistic structure from incidental exposure (Saffranhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?EwYIkf 2020; Saffran et al., 1996) , they are more likely to pick up on phonological and grammatical patterns when the input is socially embedded (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?iMsm0m Ferguson & Lew-Williams, 2016; Kuhl et al., 2003; Rabagliati et al., 2019). Early forms of social engagement are culturally variable-sustained mutual gaze and scaffolded turn taking are common in some communities, but avoided in others (Gaskins, 2006)-so infants must quickly become sensitive to local patterns of social interaction. Indeed, in the first few months, infants start to discover and abstract activity structure (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?cHad3k Reddy et al., 2013 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?cHad3k Bruner, 1985). However, we also see consistencies across culture, for example, spontaneous declarative pointing emerges just before the first birthday across culturally and linguistically diverse sites (Liszkowski et al., 2012 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ZdSwPd Brown, 2011; Salomo & Liszkowski, 2013). Supplementing findings on humans, apparent consistencies between human and non-human interactions have been used to argue for the deep evolutionary origins of coordinated interaction. For example, the basic structure of human turn-taking has similar analogs across the animal kingdom, though the timing and the primary cues used (e.g., gaze, proximity) vary substantially across species (Fröhlich et al., 2016; Pika et al., 2018; Rossano, 2013; Rossano & Liebal, 2014). Great ape communication-like human conversation (Schegloff, 2007)-uses routine action sequences, just encoded in gesture (Fröhlich et al., 2016; Rossano, 2013). That said, there are a few traits that have been argued to be specific to humans (and thus more evolutionarily recent), but still universal, including cooperative pointing (Tomasello, 2010; Tomasello et al., 2005) and complex grammar (Chomsky, 1965; Hockett, 1960 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CvkbN4 Raviv et al., 2022).

In sum, there is substantial evidence that early developing, universal sociocognitive skills combined with interactional linguistic input provides the fundamental context to support language learning across culturally and linguistically diverse developmental contexts. This suggests an important new framing for our conceptions about what might be innate in human language acquisition.

4. Language models and the innateness debate

We turn finally to language models (LMs) -artificial systems that process and produce text, made famous through commercial products such as ChatGPT and Claude. LMs provide a new experimental tool in investigations of innateness, and their linguistic successes and shortcomings inform the identification of biases needed for human-like learning under developmentally realistic conditions. Neural network models -which learn with relatively minimal innate content -have long been a source of challenges to content nativist theories of language acquisition (Elman et al., 1996; Rumelhart & McClelland, 1986). These models were the source of some of the fiercest controversy around the nature of innate constraints (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?uTd2HS Fodor & Pylyshyn, 1988; Marcus, 2001; Pinker & Prince, 1988). However, although these debates were historically important, they proved unsatisfying to many, in part because early models tended to address only restricted learning problems rather than the whole language system. In contrast, the advent -and widespread use -of neural network-based language models trained on vast quantities of data has substantially altered the discourse on innateness in development. Here we use the term LM broadly to describe the class of deep neural networks including smaller encoder-based models such as BERT, primarily but not exclusively based on the transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) that are trained on and used to interact with text in current artificial intelligence applications. The remarkable success of these models in acquiring productive language (when trained on billions or trillions of words of data) challenges in-principle nativist arguments based on the assumption that language cannot be learned purely from data (Piantadosi, 2024). Yet these in-principle successes also pave the way for a much more detailed comparison of how LMs learn, what they learn, and how much data they require (Kodner et al., 2023). This comparison is a rich opportunity to test hypotheses about innateness: in particular, to investigate which components of a model must be innately specified to achieve a good fit to human language learning. Thus, while the successes of LMs may cast doubt on specific in-principle arguments for content nativism, they also allow productive investigation of other nativist proposals.

4.1 Answering broad in-principle arguments for content nativism

Early arguments for strong content nativism with respect to language development were largely built on a set of formal arguments about the learnability of natural language (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?y4lwx7 Clark & Lappin, 2010).

These arguments virtually always focus on syntactic structures, starting from the -perhaps in hindsight questionable -premise that these are the most difficult to learn from data. The first of these historically was the logical argument from the poverty of the stimulus (Chomskyhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?8NtJJo 1965) , supplemented by other arguments for the unlearnability of natural language syntax in general (Gold, 1967; Nowak et al., 2002; Wexler & Culicover, 1980). These arguments were never fully convincing to all researchers focused on human language acquisition. To take one issue, the specific premises of the arguments were controversial -for example, Gold's theorem of unlearnability required positing an adversarial teacher, teaching only through positive examples, and convergence to a discrete and perfectly correct grammar. Such critiques led to a general lack of consensus about the support for strong content nativist positions based on formal learnability arguments (Clark & Lappin, 2010).

In the present moment, whatever else one may think of their technical capacities or social consequences, it is clear that modern language models can generate vast amounts of highly grammatical text (Futrell & Mahowald, 2025). Earlier data-driven arguments for the unlearnability of language often rested on the claim that evidence for particular grammatical constructions was too sparse for those constructions to be learned without innate linguistic scaffolding (e.g., Legate & Yang, 2002). If, however, a learning system exposed only to linguistic data can acquire the relevant structure, then such demonstrations weaken the claim that the structure is unlearnable from experience alone (Perfors et al., 2011). Transformer neural networks are especially informative in this respect: through relatively general mechanisms for allocating attention across sequences, they provide a powerful architecture that can be applied across many different data types. Transformers may instantiate what Elman et al. (1996) termed "architectural nativism"-basic built-in structure that makes learning possible-but, to the extent that they successfully acquire particular aspects of linguistic structure, they provide proof of concept that those structures need not themselves be innately represented as linguistic content (X. Yang et al., 2026). This argument only succeeds, however, if we are convinced that LMs do in fact learn target aspects of language correctly. Despite flawless production, critics have asked whether LMs' underlying grammatical representations match those of humans. Studies of models' internal representations suggest that hierarchical structures recognizable to linguists do in fact emerge from the operation of general learning mechanisms (Manning et al., 2020) -an instance of the general phenomenon of emergence (McClelland, 2010) -contra suggestions that such structures would have to be innately specified (Chomsky, 1981b). Emergence and innateness are therefore not opposed: structure can emerge through learning from a prespecified architecture, so the relevant question is what that architecture contributes, not whether the resulting structure is itself innate. In addition, the grammatical judgments produced by LMs do correspond to those of human learners (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ydZP4R J. Hu, Mahowald, et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2025; Y. S. Wang et al., 2026; cf. Dentella et al., 2023). Zooming in on individual phenomena that have been the focus of interest in prior work on learnability (e.g., syntactic islands), LMs show some evidence of generalizing syntactic details that have been argued to be unlearnable without innate structure (Wilcox et al., 2024). These successes are controversial, however, and appear only when trained on developmentallyunrealistic amounts of data (Lan et al., 2026). Finally, LMs can generalize to grammatical constructions that are unseen in their input, suggesting the generality of their linguistic representations (Misra & Mahowald, 2024).

In sum, LMs change the landscape of arguments about content nativism. It is no longer possible to claim that specific aspects of language could not be learned without innate content on logical grounds; what remains open is whether they are learnable with the quantity and type of input that children receive, and whether children do in fact learn them. Importantly, a model that reaches human-like performance without built-in linguistic content shows what can be learned in principle, not how children actually learn: it does not show that children lack innate structure, and these models carry architectural assumptions of their own. These findings advance the field by focusing inquiry on the similarities and differences between LMs and human learners.

4.2. Towards the productive use of LMs to investigate innateness

LMs provide a host of exciting opportunities to investigate visions of innateness beyond classical content nativism. Modern LMs are "stimulus computable" (Carvalho & Lampinenhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?8Yx4Aa 2025; Frank & Goodman, 2026) , meaning that they often process stimulus information in generic formats such as text, images, waveforms, or even video streams. Thus, these models can be fed with data from human language acquisition and the question then becomes the extent to which the models match human language development. Mismatches -where models and humans diverge -can be clues to (potentially innate) aspects of human language learning (Warstadt & Bowman, 2022).

One key mismatch between LMs and humans is the amount of data required for LMs to learn compared with humans. While children hear tens of millions of words in their language input, models learn much more slowly, requiring billions or even trillions of words of input before they begin showing interesting linguistic and cognitive behaviors (Frank, 2023; Frank, 2026). Several different efforts focus on altering the architecture or training data of LMs to "bridge" this gap in data efficiency. For example, the BabyLM challenge is an attempt by the machine learning community to explore LM architectures and training regimes that produce good performance from developmentally-realistic amounts of data (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?E1Sx7p. Hu, Mueller, et al., 2024; Warstadt et al., 2025). Other studies use visual data along with language in an exploration of whether multimodal information can lead to more robust or faster learning. For example, Vong et al. (2024) trained a multimodal language model on a sample of egocentric videos from a single child, and the model was able to discover a sizable number of word-object mappings.

A second potential mismatch to human learning is in LMs' learning biases. When trained on developmentally-realistic amounts of data, models may show less bias towards hierarchical structure (Yedetore et al., 2023), even if they acquire it with more data. In addition, there has been substantial debate about whether LMs can learn "impossible languages" -languages with structures that are never observed in human communication systems (Chomsky et al., 2023). In an ingenious set of training experiments, Kallini et al. (2024) found that LMs show systematic deficits in learning languages based on linear order and other transformations that do not appear in natural language grammars. Follow-ups have revealed a nuanced picture, however: while LMs do disprefer many impossible languages, their inductive biases are weaker than humans, and they do not perfectly distinguish all attested language structures from unattested ones (X. Yang et al., 2025; Ziv et al., 2026). Comparisons of this sort are illuminating not because they rule out (or "rule in") LMs as models of human language but because they can provide a method for assessing the learning biases of specific LM architectures and how they relate to the distribution of human languages. Indeed, it may be possible to identify the specific architectural features of LMs that lead more human-like structures to emerge (Mueller & Linzen, 2023).

The strategy of comparing models to human learning has the potential to reveal innate aspects of human language learning, but such comparisons require methods for measuring model performance, commonly known as benchmarks. The first step in this project has been the creation of developmentallyinspired grammar benchmarks that allow for measurement of the early emergence of grammatical structure (Warstadt et al., 2020; Huebner et al., 2021; cf. Vázquez Martínez et al., 2023). Beyond simply measuring competence, it is also desirable to measure the alignment between a model's responses and those of children; a number of developmentally-inspired tools now allow for more direct comparison with children's aggregate performance (Tan et al., 2024 (Tan et al., , 2026;; S. Wang et al., 2026). Comparing different models using such benchmarks could help form in-principle arguments for specific varieties of innateness. For example, comparisons of richer and leaner models could test whether specific structural priors are necessary for the efficient acquisition of syntax, or whether particular forms of multimodal input produce a closer fit to children's learning trajectories. Such experiments can help clarify which aspects of the learner must be innately specified and which can emerge through learning from developmentally realistic input.

To succeed, however, cognitive modeling of language acquisition with LMs will require modeling variability, not just the monolingual acquisition of English. Indeed, the newest generation of the BabyLM challenge includes a multilingual track (Choshen et al., 2026) and other benchmarks are increasingly multilingual (Jumelet et al., 2026). In addition, to date, simulations of this sort have rarely investigated environmental variation of the type discussed in section 2 above, in part because of the lack of detailed training data reflecting diverse environments at the scale necessary for LM training (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Qqctil Binyamin & Sulem, 2026; Feng et al., 2026 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Qqctil. In this way, greater efforts to diversify human language acquisition data are analogously needed in developing computational models via LMs.

5. Modern conceptions of innateness

The preceding sections place substantial pressure on classical content nativism, while at the same time reinforcing the value of the fundamental question that motivated it. Any theory in which linguistic structure emerges through learning must still specify which properties of the developing system must be already in place for that learning to occur. Interactionism does not remove this requirement: explaining development requires characterizing not only the information available in the environment, but also which architectures, biases, capacities, or representations are developmentally prespecified and how they shape learning over time. The three bodies of evidence reviewed above help make this question increasingly tractable, albeit in non-exhaustive ways. Linguistic and environmental diversity constrain what prespecified capacities must be capable of accommodating across languages and environments. Research on interaction constrains what information is actually available to, and sampled by, children and therefore what must be attributed to developmentally prespecified capacities rather than to the input alone.

Computational models provide a means of implementing and comparing candidate forms of prespecification and testing which are necessary for human-like learning. Importantly, this does not imply a single form of innateness. As summarized in Box 2, contemporary proposals about innateness locate prespecified structure at different levels: in a basic statistical learning architecture, in sociocognitive capacities, in conceptual primitives, or in specifically linguistic structure. These alternatives make different claims about what must be present prior to or independently of linguistic learning and therefore place different explanatory burdens on experience. Our aim in this section is to clarify how such claims can be made precise, mechanistic, and testable by providing four criteria (desiderata) for evaluating them (see also Figure 1).

5.1. Desiderata

First, modern theories must embrace and explain both typological and environmental variation. Classical nativist theorizing took as its fundamental observations that language acquisition was essentially universal across typically developing children, but that language diversity was bounded. These observations continue to be important guides to theory, but our understanding of both has broadened significantly. The observation that language per se is universal is correct but masks the immense amount of variability between individual children (Frank et al., 2021; Kidd & Donnelly, 2020). This variability provides critical insight into the nature of the language learning process.

In contrast to environmental variation, the boundedness of syntactic variation across languages has been a key explanandum for nativist theories (Baker, 2005; Chomsky, 1981a). Yet this theorizing often started from the idea that alternative (empiricist) models would predict a broad or even uniform distribution of possible languages, as opposed to the restricted set that is observed. As reviewed above, this starting point is open to question: LMs make predictions about the types of languages that should be more and less frequent based on their learnability (Kallini et al., 2024, and subsequent work). Thus, explicit predictions about syntactic diversity must be compared to modern baseline models. Further, all this discussion has focused exclusively on syntactic variation; modern theories must grapple with the much larger range of linguistic -not just syntactic -variation described in Section 2 above.

Second, viable theories must be precise and mechanistic in what they propose is innate, and why.

As argued in Section 4.1, language models weaken in-principle learnability arguments for innate linguistic content: it can no longer be claimed that specific structures could not be learned from data alone. Whether those structures are learnable from the quantity and type of input children actually receive remains open, however, because the most striking model successes depend on far more data than children hear (Frank, 2023; Lan et al., 2026). Proposals about innate universals therefore need motivation beyond in-principle learnability, such as evidence about what children's input and learning actually provide. For example, innate structures -domain-specific or domain general -could privilege certain inputs or bias a learning system towards particular outcomes (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1A2jTY 2 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1A2jTY Rowland et al., 2026). But even then, historical and contemporary discussions of innateness have too often been confused by different definitions and operationalizations of what it means for a property to be innate (Griffiths, 2002; https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?9wQ7iu 1 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?9wQ7iu. Theories that posit innateness -whether innate learning mechanisms, innate social cognition, or innate linguistic content -must be precise about what they are claiming (e.g., whether their claim is that these abilities are unlearned, present at birth, genetically encoded, human-specific, or any of the other potential components of innateness) and why.

Third, theories of innateness should be implemented -or at least implementable -in formal (e.g., computational) models (Ecker & Lewandowsky, 2012; Guest & Martin, 2021; Tauber et al., 2017).

Verbal theories often remain vague and under-specified, which allows researchers a number of degrees of freedom when it comes to interpreting the evidence in the light of theoretical predictions (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BCBjxS Tomasello, 2000; Fisher, 2002; and Tomasello & Abbot-Smith, 2002). Model building, instead, forces theories to be precise and explicit both in their claims and their underlying assumptions.

Most importantly perhaps, a significant advantage of formal models is that they make theories falsifiable.

Insulated from disconfirmation, verbal theories often do not provide the kinds of testable predictions that allow for corroboration or refutation (Pullum & Scholz, 2002). Fourth, and finally, viable theories must explain interactions between learners and their context by being testable on input derived from the rich variety of contexts in which children actually learn.

While classic nativist theories point out the robustness of language acquisition across wide ranges of environmental input (Goldin-Meadowhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?o0xWrl 2005; Landau & Gleitman, 1985) , modern theories must also capture the sensitivity of learners to the local context of acquisition. General linkages between the quantity and quality of children's linguistic input document the impacts of one aspect of interaction (Anderson et al., 2021; Bergelson et al., 2023; Coffey & Snedeker, 2026; Hart & Risley, 1995; Rowe & Snow, 2020 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wawpgx. Yet to go beyond these approacheshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0pNjrt we must be able to connect implemented learning mechanisms to ecologically valid input data -whether they are transcripts of child-directed speech (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Ee66ZD egocentric video recordings of children's experiences (Long et al., 2024; Sullivan et al., 2021) , or immersive recordings of the home environment (Raviv et al., 2026; Roy et al., 2015). The adequacy of theories can only be judged in the context of fully interactional data.

Box 2 outlines some of the current approaches, together with information about which of our desiderata they currently satisfy, and where challenges still remain. Our view, based partly on the evidence we have discussed here, is that future proposals for innateness will need to specify, at the very least, a prescribed basic statistical learning architecture (as proposed by minimal innateness theories), will probably need to include some basic sociocognitive biases to underpin the drive for interaction (sociocognitive innateness), and maybe even some semantic/conceptual primitives (conceptual innateness). We are not yet convinced of the need to posit innate linguistic representations (the syntactic innateness of classical content nativism) to explain the data thus far, but we acknowledge that the evidence is not yet clear-cut and that our own views are affected by the preconceptions that we bring to this perspective. A critical key leverage point for empirically adjudicating between different conceptions of innateness in the future will be the creation of larger, richer, datasets that document how children learn language in naturalistic contexts, and that identify the range of the sociocultural and linguistic contexts in which they learn. Such data will enable us to build more mechanistic, formal models that can then be used to test competing hypotheses more precisely, by evaluating how closely models built with, and without, different sociocognitive, cognitive and syntactic primitives can simulate human language learning -in interaction, across cultures, and across languages.

5.2 What would these desiderata look like in practice?

These requirements are not individually new. As mentioned earlier, generative theories have indeed sought to account for cross-linguistic variation, and computational approaches have long argued for explicit implementation. However, a major challenge is to satisfy these constraints jointly, such that a proposal specifies what the learner contributes, specifies these contributions within a learning system, explicates how the system responds and adapts to developmentally relevant input, and evaluates whether it captures both consistency and variation in human learning.

Existing work provides positive examples of this approach. Pearl and colleagues, for example, have examined whether phenomena traditionally attributed to innate grammatical constraints can be acquired by learners with explicitly specified representations and biases operating over realistic input (Pearl & Sprouse, 2013). Such work localizes questions of innateness to the specific assumptions that must be built into a learner for that constraint to emerge from the evidence available to children.

Conceptual innateness provides a different example; Hartshorne and Snedeker (2026), for example, have explicitly relocated much of the proposed innate contribution from syntax to conceptual structure: learners are hypothesized to begin with rich, combinatorial conceptual representations, while syntactic representations arise through learning. Importantly, their proposal identifies conditions under which it would fail-for example, if infants lack the requisite conceptual primitives or if conceptual structure proves insufficient to constrain syntactic learning. It therefore illustrates the movement from a generic claim that "something must be innate" toward a more precisely articulated hypothesis space.

Analogously, recent computational work demonstrates how putatively innate structure can itself be formalized and manipulated. Shuvaev et al. (2024), for example, encoded an inherited network initialization through a constrained "genomic bottleneck" and distinguished the contribution of this initial structure from subsequent learning. And McCoy and Griffiths (2025) have suggested that specific structural prior information can be introduced into language-learning networks through meta-learning, thus dramatically increasing learning efficiency. Such approaches provide a useful proof of concept for treating innate biases as a quantitatively specified component of a learning system. Taken together, these examples illustrate how contemporary approaches can move beyond broad claims about innateness by specifying, formalizing, and testing the particular learner constraints that shape acquisition in interaction with experience.

6. Conclusion

In this piece we have revisited the question of linguistic innateness in the light of a set of striking empirical developments that have emerged in the last one to two decades. The first is the renewed interest in understanding language acquisition across languages, coupled with renewed appreciation for the extent of variation across individuals and contexts. This development has widened the scope of inquiry beyond a small number of well-studied languages and populations, and with it has come a clearer recognition that language learning unfolds under highly diverse structural and social conditions. The second is the greater emphasis on the interactional richness that underlies linguistic communication and on the study of interaction as an important site of discovery about the origins of language. And the final development is the rise of LMs, which has offered a powerful new perspective on classic questions about the sources of linguistic structure, the relationship between input and learning, and the extent to which complex and seemingly language-like knowledge can emerge from learning algorithms operating over extensive input.

Each of these developments poses a challenge to traditional content nativist views that have argued that the universal acquisition of language must be driven by specific innate syntactic machinery.

As these empirical, technological, and methodological developments reshape our understanding of the origins of language, it is reasonable to query the place of innateness as a core theoretical construct.

We argue that its continuing value lies in directing attention to identifying which particular properties must be developmentally prespecified for language learning to proceed as it does. The evidence reviewed here helps to focus that endeavor. Linguistic and environmental diversity constrain the range of outcomes and environments that candidate forms of pre-specification must accommodate. Research on interaction clarifies what information is actually available for children to sample, thereby refining claims about what must be prespecified rather than learned from experience. Computational models offer an increasingly powerful means of implementing and comparing candidate architectures, biases, capacities, or representations. Innateness therefore remains useful as an orienting principle not because it directly answers how language develops, but because it identifies a set of questions that contemporary theories must address. The future of research on innateness in language lies in formulating and testing increasingly precise hypotheses about what is prespecified, in what form, and how those initial properties interact with experience over development.

Boxes, Tables & Figures Box 1: A historical perspective on innateness

The concept of innateness has a centuries-long history. Classical philosophers such as Plato argued that some knowledge is inborn, while early modern thinkers such as René Descartes also defended the existence of innate ideas. In contrast, seventeenth-and eighteenth-century philosophers such as John Locke and Immanuel Kant famously debated the origins of ideas in different ways, with Locke portraying the mind as a tabula rasa shaped by experience and Kant proposing that the mind contributes to organizing structures that make experience possible. In the nineteenth century, Charles Darwin's theory of evolution reframed these questions by situating human cognition within a biological and comparative framework. In the twentieth century, ethologists such as Niko Tinbergen and Konrad Lorenz, developmental theorists such as Jean Piaget, and linguists such as Noam Chomsky each reshaped debates about what aspects of mind and behavior are biologically prepared versus constructed through experience. Chomsky, in particular, placed human language at the center of the modern debate, proposing that language acquisition rests on innate linguistic knowledge in the form of syntactic structures. Since Chomsky's foundational proposal, debates over the origins and mechanisms of language learning have led to the development of competing frameworks, including parameter-setting theories, minimalist theories, and Words and Rules approaches associated with scholars such as Steven Pinker, on one side, and usage-based approaches, social-interactionist theories, and connectionist models, often associated with theorists such as Michael Tomasello, on the other. More recent work by researchers such as Elizabeth Spelke and Susan Carey has further extended the debate by examining whether infants begin life with core systems of knowledge that structure subsequent learning. These perspectives on what is innate variably align with nativist or empiricist orientations and have generally sought to classify language as a product of nativism, constructivism, or empiricism.

Statistical learning and inductive generalization

Learners extract structure from distributional input and, through constrained induction, generalize beyond the data to discover rules and generate novel forms.

Empirical: Word-boundary learning in minutes; learning of adjacent and non-adjacent dependencies; sensitivity to informativeness gradients; overgeneralization errors as signatures of induction. Theoretical: Poverty of the Stimulus, Plato's problem, and the logical problem. Computational: statistical, probabilistic, and computational models in which priors or inductive biases combine with input to yield generalizations. This account explains how learners can move from finite, underspecified input to abstract and productive linguistic knowledge. It suggests that acquisition is scaffolded by learning biases and inferential constraints without necessarily requiring richly specified innate linguistic content.

Perceptual biases

Early perceptual biases orient attention to socially and linguistically informative input and constrain learning.

Preferences for speech, mother's voice, and native language; phonetic tuning, phonotactics, prosody, and segmentation; face orientation, recognition, and gaze sensitivity.

Provides an attentional and inferential filter that makes structure in the input more discoverable.

Humans, and in some cases other species, are born with primitives that structure their understanding of the world and underlie later learning.
