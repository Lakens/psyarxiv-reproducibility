Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .83 Whether the option’s gain is larger in harder tests depends on the measure and the specification: as declared (ω c without priors) it is, but in bits with priors mean proportion correct was not associated with the gain overall (p = .83), and in grade 8 the association went the other way.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Right/wrong scoring of multiple-choice items discards information about which wrong option was chosen. IRT models that use the choice date from 1972, but evidence on its value has come mostly in sample, one test at a time. We compared option-level and right/wrong models on predicting the option chosen in held-out responses (log-loss, in bits) across 116 Item Response Warehouse tables (110 Brazilian), 469 TIMSS and PIRLS tests and 121 Czech exam forms. In the warehouse, modeling the chosen option gained 15.4 millibits per response over the 2PL, 1.36 times the gain from estimating item discrimination. Of the option's gain, 84% came from which wrong option was chosen, where right/wrong models use only distractor popularity; the rest, on whether the answer was right, was about a fifth of discrimination's gain. The gain was positive in all 116 warehouse tables, in 119 of 121 Czech forms and, with priors on both models, in 463 of 469 TIMSS and PIRLS tests; relative to discrimination, it ranged from 0.8 (Czech exams) to 1.5 (ENEM). By our declared measure, the gain peaked in the lowest ability deciles in most tables; per wrong answer, the choice improved prediction over distractor popularity most in the top decile.

The distractors are part of the item too (Thissen et al., 1989). Operational scoring usually discards the choice: the response is coded 0 or 1 and modeled with a binary item response model, such as the one-, two-or three-parameter logistic model (1PL, 2PL, 3PL). A review by Gierl et al. (2017) found comparatively little research on distractors.

This paper asks what the discarded information is worth. We do not ask whether optionlevel models fit better in sample (with more parameters they usually will), but whether they predict the option chosen in responses they have not seen better than binary models do (Section 1.4).

1.2 • What is known

Item response models that use the choice date from Bock (1972), whose nominal response model gives every option its own response function. Bock reported that scoring all the options was substantially more precise than right/wrong scoring for test-takers below median ability and about as precise above it. Thissen (1976) found more information in the lower half of the scale when wrong responses to the Raven Progressive Matrices were modeled (as summarized by Haberman et al., 2019). Thissen and Steinberg (1984) added a latent "don't know" category whose responses spread over the options in estimated proportions, and Thissen et al. (1989) showed how such models serve item analysis. Suh and Bolt (2010) introduced the nested logit model: a 2PL, 3PL or four-parameter logistic model (4PL) for the correct response and, given an incorrect response, a nominal model among the distractors, a structure that Bolt et al. (2012) present as a natural way to evaluate what distractor selection adds to scoring. De Ayala and Sava-Bolesta (1999) and DeMars (2003) studied how many examinees the nominal model needs.

How much the choice adds has mostly been measured in sample and one test at a time. Haberman et al. (2019) analyzed an international language assessment (12,123 examinees; 29 listening and 39 reading items with four options) against a 2PL whose wrong-answer probability is split over distractors in fixed proportions. Modeling the distractors lowered the in-sample perresponse log-penalty by .004 to .005, nearly twice the .002 gained by estimating item slopes (a ratio of about 1.7 to 1.9), while reliability rose by less than .01. In the Swedish admission test SweSAT, Ramsay et al. (2020) found that optimal scoring of all options improved their measures of score performance by about as much again as the step from sum scoring to optimal binary scoring. In reasoning tests, nested logit models raised reliability over their binary counterparts: by a reported .029 (3PL against 3PL nested; the paper's point estimates imply .021) in the last series of the Standard Progressive Matrices (Myszkowski & Storme, 2018), and by .018 (2PL against 2PL nested) in a 20-item recruitment test, where they also outperformed the nominal model (Storme et al., 2019). In a financial literacy test, Storme et al. (2026) report that a nested logit model gives ability estimates more strongly associated with external validity criteria than conventional binary scoring. De Ayala (1989) compared adaptive tests based on the nominal model and on the 3PL with empirical data and an external criterion. García-Pérez (2014) argued that the higher information of polytomous models for multiple-choice items can be an artifact of estimation error or of inadequate option response functions. Out-of-sample prediction is the natural answer: extra information that is an artifact should not help predict responses the model has not seen. Drasgow et al. (1995) warned early against overfitting artifacts and evaluated four option-level models in five tests with separate calibration and cross-validation samples of about 3,000, but they assessed fit, not the gain over binary models. Wallmark et al. (2024) compared a monotone multiple-choice model with the nominal model by log-likelihood on held-out test-takers of the SweSAT, and Kreitchmann et al. (2018) compared a multiple-choice model with omissions with the 3PL used to score TIMSS Advanced by item fit in a held-out half. Each studied one assessment. Neither measured the predictive gain over a binary model. In a special issue on one Raven dataset (Myszkowski, 2020), Bürkner (2020) compared only binary models by approximate leave-one-out crossvalidation (LOOCV), because his software, brms, did not yet support models with distractors in the required form. Jin et al. (2022) found that a nominal model with a mixture of random guessing was preferred to the nominal model by the widely applicable information criterion (WAIC) and by LOOCV in all five analyses of a single mathematics exam.

Outside psychometrics, Ghosh et al. (2021) extended knowledge-tracing models to predict the option chosen. An et al. (2022) found that training such models to predict the option as well as correctness improved their prediction of correctness in data from Brazil's national high school exam (ENEM 2019) and EdNet. Fei et al. (2026) fitted nominal models to 189 language models answering 31,554 items from 14 benchmarks, with five-fold cross-validation over cells.

There, Bock's nominal model did not improve the prediction of correctness over the 2PL, but it did improve the prediction of the option chosen. None of the studies above with human testtakers measured the option's out-of-sample gain over right/wrong scoring across many tests.

1.3 • The yardstick and the warehouse

The InterModel Vigorish (IMV) expresses the predictive gain of one model over another as the relative change in the weight of a coin whose expected log-likelihood matches each model's mean held-out log-likelihood (Domingue, Rahal, et al., 2025). Applied to dichotomous models in 89 datasets of the Item Response Warehouse (IRW), it gave a mean IMV of 0.006 for 1PL → 2PL and of 0.00016 for 2PL → 3PL (Domingue et al., 2024). The same paper warns that the IMV is biased toward more complex models when evaluated in sample. Kanopka and Domingue (Kanopka, 2023, Ch. 3) extended the IMV to categorical outcomes with ω c , which applies to any model of categorical responses, ordered or not. Domingue, Kanopka, et al. (2025) compared polytomous models on every dichotomization of the response, of which correct against incorrect is one. Stenhaug and Domingue (2022) distinguish two prediction tasks, held-out responses of known persons and held-out persons; we use both.

The IRW (Domingue, Braginsky, et al., 2025) now has a nominal branch that keeps the option each person chose (Domingue, 2026). In its multiple-choice tables with a known chance level, an IRW vignette found that the 3PL lower asymptote improved out-of-sample prediction of correctness (IMV 2PL → 3PL positive in 11 of 11 tables, 8 of them from ENEM, on one heldout split and with fits that predate mirt's fix of the prior (Appendix A); Item Response Warehouse, 2026). Nominal models have been fitted across the warehouse before: Nalbandyan et al. (2026) used them in 245 IRW datasets of polytomous responses to quantify the degree of category ordering. What the wrong options of keyed multiple-choice items add to prediction, the question of this paper, was not part of that work.

1.4 • This study

This study compares option-level and right/wrong models out of sample with one design and the IRW's yardstick across many tests: 116 tables of the warehouse (110 of them Brazilian) and, as replications outside it, 469 Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) and Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) tests and 121 forms of the Czech national exams. It asks:  R1 (how much): How much does modeling the chosen option improve held-out prediction over binary models?  R² (where): Where on the ability scale is that gain largest?  R3 (choice and ability): How does the choice among wrong options relate to ability? Because binary models predict which wrong option is chosen only from distractor popularity (Section 3.2), we answer R1 for the option chosen and for correctness alone. We also compare the gain, in bits, with that from estimating item discrimination, as Haberman et al. (2019) did in sample (exploratory; Section 3.4). We answer R3 with the spread of choices among wrong options by rest-score decile and with an external criterion for ability.

We then ask which model predicts best, whether the gain holds for test-takers unseen in calibration and how many test-takers are needed before it pays. The assumption that random guessers spread evenly over the options is tested by a model comparison (Section 5). The primary measures of R1 to R3 and some secondary analyses were planned before the main run; the rest were added afterwards and are exploratory (Section 3.10).

3 • Method

3.1 • Models and fitting

All models were fitted in mirt 1.47 (Chalmers, 2012), unidimensional, with expected a posteriori (EAP) ability estimates under a standard normal prior: Rasch and 2PL; 3PL, with lognormal(0, 1) and Beta(5, 17) priors on the slope and lower asymptote (Table A1); the nominal response model (Bock, 1972); the nested logit without guessing (2PLNRM; Suh & Bolt, 2010); and, on 5 of the 10 samples per table (all 10 in the 12 ENEM tables of 2013 to 2015) with the same folds, the nested logit with guessing (3PLNRM), with the same priors as the 3PL. The 3PL and the 3PLNRM were fitted with mirt's expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm under those priors, which stops below the posterior maximum while reporting convergence because mirt takes the derivatives of the prior on the lower asymptote with respect to the asymptote rather than its logit (Xiao, 2026; corrected in the development version 1.48.2); we then polished each fit to the posterior mode reached from mirt's solution, and the figures for these two models are at that mode unless marked otherwise (Appendix A). The posterior can have more than one mode.

The chance-filter check (Section S10.5) and the per-database gaps between the 3PLNRM and the nominal model in Section S8 keep mirt's fits.

Nominal models were fitted by marginal maximum likelihood with the category slopes parametrized directly (a1 fixed at 1, all slopes free relative to a reference distractor, the first in text order): mirt's default a1•ak parametrization, the same model, stopped below the maximum while reporting convergence (Appendix A gives the problem and the checks). In the warehouse tables, with between about 1,150 and 2,400 training responses per item, the nominal model was fitted without priors. Where items had fewer than about 1,000 responses (the sample-size curve, TIMSS and PIRLS), maximum likelihood overfitted or did not exist for some items, and we report posterior modes under the priors of Table A1. The threshold between the two regimes was chosen after looking at the results (Section 6). A few secondary checks use the default parametrization (Table S1).

3.2 • Option probabilities from binary models

To compare binary and option-level models on the same outcome, a binary model must predict the option chosen. We split its predicted probability of a wrong answer over the distractors in proportion to their shares among wrong answers in the training data, as in the 2PL with noninformative distributions of Haberman et al. (2019) and in Fei et al. (2026). Binary and option-level models then predict the same outcomes and can be compared with any loss on them, and Rasch → 2PL changes only the probability of the key, so on the option scale it measures the value of discrimination alone.

3.3 • Prediction tasks

Held-out responses. In each sample, every observed person-by-item response (cell) was assigned at random to one of five folds. Each model was fitted on four folds (80% of the cells); ability was estimated by EAP from the person's training cells; and the held-out cells were predicted. This is the missing-responses task of Stenhaug and Domingue (2022).

New test-takers. Persons were split into five folds and models were calibrated on 80% of them. For each held-out person, items were split at random into halves S and T; ability was estimated from S only and used to predict the responses in T, and the reverse. We used 3 samples per table (348 samples).

Ability against an external criterion.

Estimating ability from wrong answers and correlating it with the full-test estimate, which contains those same answers, is circular. In each of 3 samples per table, items were split at random into halves A and B and persons into five folds; models were calibrated on 80% of the persons. Each held-out person was scored on half A by EAP with the sum score, the 2PL, the 3PL, the nominal model, the nested logit without and with guessing, and, using only the items answered incorrectly, the choice among wrong options alone. The criterion was the 2PL ability estimate on half B. As a null, the letters of the wrong options were shuffled. After the first results we added two other criteria with the same scorings of half A: the proportion correct on half B and, in ENEM, the mean 2PL EAP on the two areas the person took on the other test day.

3.4 • Measures

Binary IMV (correctness).

For each pair of models, the IMV (Domingue, Rahal, et al., 2025) on whether each held-out response was correct. Positive values favor the second model. ω c (which option). The extension of the IMV to categorical outcomes (Kanopka, 2023, Ch. 3), computed on each pair of observed options and averaged over pairs, items and folds (Appendix B).

Log-loss in bits.

For each held-out response, a model's log-loss is -log 2 of the probability it gave to the option actually chosen: 1 bit for a probability of 1/2, 2 bits for 1/4 and 0 for 1. The gain of model b over model a is the drop in mean log-loss, in bits per response; we report millibits (thousandths of a bit). The loss splits exactly into two parts (Appendix B): the loss on whether the response was correct, the only part on which binary models differ from one another (Section 3.2), and, for wrong responses, the loss on which wrong option was chosen.

Because the parts add up to the total, the gain from the option can be split into correctness and which wrong option, and compared with the gain from discrimination on one scale. Bits were added after the first results. Domingue, Kanopka, et al. (2025) argue that IMVs computed on dichotomizations of a polytomous response can be interpreted interchangeably with ω c ; correct against incorrect is one such dichotomization. But IMVs do not add up, and ω c averages over pairs of options, among them pairs of distractors that the Rasch → 2PL comparison leaves unchanged (Section 3.2), so a ratio between two such values depends on how the pairs are weighted. We compare the option with discrimination in bits only. These are bits of held-out logloss; the bit scale of Wallmark and Wiberg (2026) uses the same unit to build a score scale from the entropy of item responses.

3.5 • Aggregation and uncertainty

For each table, a measure is the mean over its 10 samples, and its standard error (SE) is the standard deviation over samples divided by √10; a gain is "above two SE" when it is larger than twice that standard error. We pooled tables with a multilevel random-effects meta-analysis (restricted maximum likelihood, REML; metafor; Viechtbauer, 2010), separately by family and for all 116 tables. For the gains in bits we also report the between-table standard deviation, τ, and a 95% prediction interval for a new table, and the ratio of the two gains has its own interval (Appendix B). A meta-regression of ω c 2PL → nominal on the table's mean proportion correct, its number of items and its family asks where the option's gain is larger.

3.6 • Where the information is

By ability. Held-out cells were grouped by decile of the 2PL ability estimate. Our declared measure was ω c 2PL → nominal computed on the held-out cells of each decile. We also computed the bits gained per response and, after the first results, per wrong answer, with the 2PL and the 3PL as baselines. Because that gain measures prediction against the distractors' training shares, not information about ability, we also computed, as exploratory analyses, the Fisher information per wrong answer under the fitted nominal model (below) and the gain per wrong answer that model implies when measured as the empirical one (Section S12).

Fisher information.

In the nominal model, the information that the choice among distractors adds to that of correctness is a1 2 (1-P K ( θ )) Var ( ak | wrong ), where K is the key: the information about which distractor, weighted by the probability of a wrong answer (Appendix B; cf. Fei et al., 2026). For each table we computed the ratio of option information to correctness information, summed over items and averaged over a standard normal θ, from the nominal parameters of the first fold of each sample, and, by decile of θ, the information per wrong answer, a1 2 Var ( ak | wrong , θ ).

By item.

We related each item's ω c to its proportion correct and to its number of "informative distractors": wrong options that are the most probable response at some θ between -3 and 3 (the definition of Fei et al., 2026; the range of θ is ours).

3.7 • Choice among wrong options

Without any model, on the ENEM and ENCCEJA pools, we recorded by item and decile of the rest score the share of wrong answers that went to the most chosen distractor (chance: 1/(m -1)), for all test-takers and for chance-level scorers: persons whose number correct is not above what Binomial(J, 1/m) gives at the .05 level, with J items.

Item-level tests of uniformity are in Section S4.

3.8 • Sample size

In 20 tables (ENEM 2013 (ENEM , 2017 (ENEM , 2021 (ENEM and 2025 in their four areas, and four ENCCEJA tables) we repeated the cell-level design with calibration samples of 250 to 16,000 persons, three times each, and, at N = 250, 500 and 1,000, with priors on both models (Table A1).

3.9 • Outside the warehouse TIMSS and PIRLS.

We kept the four-option multiple-choice items that IEA scales, used the whole country sample without sampling weights and followed the main design, adding the 2PL and the nominal model with priors (Appendix C). Because ω c depends on the number of held-out cells per item and fold, the comparison with Brazil is made in bits only, and against Brazil's figures under the same priors and sample size (500 and 1,000 test-takers in the samplesize curve).

Czech national exams.

The design was that of the main analysis (Appendix C). The headline figures use the nominal model with a1 fixed at 1, as in the warehouse tables; the fit with the default parametrization is in Section S9.

3.10 • Planned and exploratory analyses

Before the main run we planned the primary measures: ω c 2PL → nominal and the binary IMV 2PL → nominal for R1; ω c by ability decile for R²; and, for R3, the spread among wrong options by decile and the recovery of ability from wrong answers, which we changed to the external criterion of Section 3.3 before that step was run, because the planned version was circular. The plan, written down before the run but not registered publicly, also listed secondary analyses and a re-run with another seed on part of the tables, which was not done. 4 • Results

4.1 • R1: how much the chosen option adds to held-out prediction in the warehouse tables

Modeling the option improved the held-out prediction of which option was chosen in all 116 tables (Table 1, Figure 1). In ω c , the pooled 2PL → nominal gain was 0.0133 [0.0114, 0.0153]: averaged over pairs of options, items and folds, the coin equivalent to the nominal model's held-out predictions weighed 1.3% more than the 2PL's (Section 1.3; Appendix B). It was 0.0182 in ENEM, 0.0102 in ENCCEJA and 0.0065 in the other tests, and above two SE in each of the 116 tables. Against discrimination.

Both gains are measured on the option chosen, but discrimination changes only the prediction of correctness; the 2PL predicts which wrong option is chosen from distractor popularity, the same at every ability. In bits, the option gained 15.4 millibits per response against 11.3 for discrimination: 1.36 times as much overall (95% interval 1.24 to 1.48) and 1.53 times in ENEM (1.40 to 1.67). In ENCCEJA the two were comparable (1.12, from 0.97 to 1.30): their difference, +1.3 millibits per response [-0.3, 3.0], is not distinguishable from zero. Table by table, the option exceeded discrimination in 89 of 116 tables (Figure 1a). Of the option's gain, 84% came from predicting, when the answer was wrong, which wrong option was chosen (13.0 millibits per response), and 16% from predicting whether the answer was right (2.5 millibits, about a fifth of the gain from discrimination; the parts are pooled separately, Table 1). For comparison, replacing each item's proportion correct with the 2PL, a step from popularity to ability on correctness, gained 51.5 millibits per response [44.7, 58.3]; the which-wrong-option gain was a quarter of that (0.25 [0.21, 0.29]; exploratory; Section S16).

Heterogeneity and calibration size. The gain varied widely between tables (Table 1): τ was 5.5 millibits for the option, with a 95% prediction interval of 4.4 to 26.3 for a new table, and 3.9 for discrimination (3.6 to 19.0). The option's prediction interval lay above zero overall and in each family, but that of its difference from discrimination crossed zero in all of them (-6.6 to 14.8 overall): in a new table the option could gain more or less than discrimination (table by table, Section S10.1). The ratio is a property of this design, not of the option alone: it compares two pooled estimates at the calibration size of the warehouse samples (a median of 2,382 training responses per item), and it depends on that size. In the 20 tables of the sample-size curve, without priors, it was 1.16 with 500 test-takers, 1.42 with 1,000 and 1.47 with 3,000 (means over tables), and with 250 the option did not pay (Section 4.7). In simulations (Section S10.7), modeling the option lost 0.7 to 1.3 millibits per response when the distractors carried no information and, with 3,000 test-takers, recovered 79% to 93% of the gain it would have with the true item parameters; its ratio to discrimination ranged from 1.27 to 3.18, depending on the generating values.

Difficulty and the 3PL. The gain was positive in every year of each of the 12 series (ENEM's four areas and ENCCEJA's eight level-area combinations), and larger in harder tables: in the meta-regression, ω c 2PL → nominal fell by 0.021 per unit of mean proportion correct (p < .001).

Against the 3PL, the operational model of ENEM (INEP, 2011 (INEP, , 2012)), the option still added information about which option was chosen: ω c 3PL → nominal 0.0112 [0.0097, 0.0127], above two SE in 116 of 116 tables, and 12.4 millibits per response [11.4, 13.5].

On correctness, however, the nominal model no longer gained over the 3PL overall (-0.3 millibits [-0.9, 0.3]) and lost to it in ENEM (-1.6 [-2.3, -1.0]); in ENCCEJA it still gained 0.9 [0.3, 1.5]. The 3PL itself predicted correctness better than the 2PL: by 4.1 millibits per response in ENEM [3.6, 4.6], above two SE in 52 of 52 tables, and by 1.6 in ENCCEJA [1.3, 2.0]. The 3PL carries priors and the 2PL does not, so this gain mixes the lower asymptote with regularization (Table A1). Across booklets, which order the same items differently, this gain grew with an item's position in all 52 ENEM tables (by more than two SE in 47), and the nominal model's standing against the 3PL on correctness fell with position in 51 (by more than two SE in 45), consistent with position effects (Kanopka & Domingue, 2025) that our models do not include. The 3PL already gained in the first 15 items of each test day, where the nominal model trailed it only in natural sciences (Section S14).

4.3 • Which model predicts best

The fair comparison for the nested logit with guessing (3PLNRM), which carries priors, is with the nominal model under priors too. In total log-loss it beat the nominal model with the priors of Table A1 (set B) in 112 of the 116 warehouse tables (111 above two SE; without the fits whose maximum is at infinity, Appendix A, 113 and 111), and the nominal model without priors in 113 (108 above two SE; 109 without those fits). Its whole advantage over the nominal model was in correctness: in which wrong option was chosen the nominal model was better, by more than two SE in 50 of 116 tables. 4.5 • R3: how the choice among wrong options relates to ability

Low scorers' choices are concentrated. Among chance-level scorers (a median of 38% of persons per table), a favorite distractor drew a median of 35% of their wrong answers per item in ENEM (chance: 25%) and 47% in ENCCEJA (chance: 33%). The choice became more concentrated as ability rose: the median share of the favorite distractor went from 34% to 47% in ENEM and from 46% to 57% in ENCCEJA. This says that low scorers' choices are not uniform, not that low scorers do not guess; whether guessers spread evenly over the options is tested in Section 5.

4.6 • Test-takers unseen in calibration

For test-takers unseen in calibration, modeling the option still improved prediction in all 116 tables and in all 348 samples (median ω c 2PL → nominal 0.0175 in ENEM and 0.0091 in ENCCEJA; Figure S3), and the 3PLNRM improved on the 3PL in all 116 tables as well. Their ability came from half of their responses. Compared table by table, the option's gain in bits for unseen test-takers was about 92% of its gain on held-out responses of known test-takers, while its gain on correctness was 1.27 times as large.

4.7 • How many test-takers are needed

Estimated without priors, the option paid in log-loss from about 500 test-takers (19 of 20 tables at N = 500), and on correctness also from about 500 (19 of 20; 20 of 20 at N = 1,000); with 250 and no priors it did not pay, because maximum likelihood overfitted and for some items did not exist (Figure S4). With 250 test-takers and priors on both models, the count depends on the prior set: with set B, the option paid in 14 of 20 tables (not in the 4 ENEM natural sciences tables, nor in 2 of the 4 ENEM mathematics tables); with set A, in 18 of 20 (14 of 16 ENEM and 4 of 4 ENCCEJA tables), with the whole gain in which wrong option was chosen.

4.8 • Group gaps

In the full 2023 ENEM microdata (500,000 test-takers scored with item parameters calibrated on independent samples; criterion: the official score from the other test day), scoring the option (nested logit with guessing) instead of right/wrong (3PL) raised validity in all four areas and left socioeconomic and racial gaps per unit of validity unchanged (ratio 0.98 to 1.02 across 4 areas and 5 group contrasts). Against simulations without option-level differential functioning (differential distractor functioning; Green et al., 1989; Penfield, 2008; Suh & Bolt, 2011) that reproduce the observed validity, the option did not shift disadvantaged groups uniformly: it placed them slightly higher among low scorers and slightly lower among high scorers; the mean shift, within ±0.03 SD, changed sign with the weighting (Section S7).

5 • Robustness

Table D1 (Appendix D) lists the robustness checks, and Section S10 gives their details; in none did a result change direction. The mixture of the nominal model with uniform random guessing (Jin et al., 2022) predicted worse than the nested logit with guessing directed to the key in 20 of 20 tests (median 3.6 to 3.7 millibits); sending the ability-independent mass to the key instead of spreading it evenly over the options accounts for most of its gap to the nested logit with guessing (2.3 millibits).

6 • Limitations

Mostly Brazil. Of the 116 warehouse tables, 110 are Brazilian, and the unit of replication is the program, not the table: outside Brazil the replication rests on TIMSS and PIRLS, which need priors at their sample sizes, and on two Czech exams, where the gain was smaller. We make no claim about multiple-choice tests in general.

Specification of the nominal model.

The threshold of about 1,000 responses per item between maximum likelihood and priors was chosen after looking at the results, from the sample-size curve, which places the crossing between 400 and 800 responses per item, and from TIMSS 2023 grade 8, with about 760. The priors are not neutral: both sets center the distractor slopes where the nominal model reduces to the 2PL with fixed distractor shares, so they shrink toward the binary baseline, and the prior on the key slope also restricts its sign (Table A1); and the two prior sets in use below the threshold give different counts at N = 250 (Section 4.7).

Standard errors.

The table-level SE comes from 10 samples that overlap by chance, so it understates the SE relative to the population; with the correction, ω c stays above two SE in the ENEM and ENCCEJA tables (Appendix B).

Local dependence.

Folds are random over cells, and the halves A and B and S and T are random over items, so a held-out item can share a passage or stimulus with training items of the same person. If the choice of distractor reflects how that person read the shared material, part of the option's gain could come from that dependence rather than from ability. The concern is largest in PIRLS, where every item belongs to a passage; we did not run folds that keep a passage together.

Exploratory comparisons and populations. The 3PL and nested-logit comparisons in the ability analysis are exploratory, our 3PL is not INEP's scoring, and our 3PL and 3PLNRM are at the posterior mode reached from mirt's solution, which need not be the only mode (Section 3.1). New test-takers come from the same table and year as the calibration sample, so that task does not show that the gain generalizes to other populations. The sample-size thresholds are readings of a curve in 20 tables, not tested cut-offs.

Group gaps. The analysis of group gaps covers one exam and one year, and the data do not separate whether the tilt it finds reflects option-level differential functioning or model misfit where guessing is common.

Unidimensional models.

All models are unidimensional. Bolt et al. (2012) raised the possibility that distractor selection reflects a different trait or ability from the one behind the correct response; we did not test it.

7 • Discussion

7.1 • What a wrong answer is worth

In every program we studied, modeling the chosen option improved its prediction in responses the models had not seen. In bits, the gain was of the order of the gain from estimating item discrimination (1.36 times as large in the warehouse tables), but most of it is information about which mistake is made. On whether the answer is right, it was about a fifth of the gain from discrimination. The size is not a constant: it depends on the calibration sample, and in the Czech exams it was about a third of Brazil's and below the gain from discrimination. This is the out-of-sample counterpart of the in-sample comparison that Haberman et al. (2019) made in one language test. It also answers part of the concern that the information of option-level models is an artifact (García-Pérez, 2014): held-out responses confirm that some of it is real, and across tables the promised gain (the Fisher information ratio) and the collected gain (the correlation with the other half of the test) were ordered alike (Spearman .76).

Against the 3PL, the gain on correctness disappears overall and reverses in ENEM, and in ENEM mathematics the nominal model only ties with the 3PL as an ability estimator. Both results are consistent with one mechanism: in ENEM, a large part of what the option adds at the bottom of the scale is structure that a guessing floor also captures (the 3PL itself gained 4.1 millibits over the 2PL there, more at later booklet positions; Section 4.1). A model with both (the nested logit with guessing) predicted best in the Brazilian tables, also against the nominal model with priors, and tracked ability better than right/wrong 3PL scoring in 113 of 116 tables (Section 4.5).

7.2 • Where, and from whom

The classical statement is that option scoring helps most below the median (Bock, 1972).

Our declared measure agrees with it in most tables: ω c by decile peaked in the two lowest deciles in 92 of 116. Per wrong answer, however, the chosen option improved prediction over overall distractor popularity most in the top decile. This is a predictive statement, not Fisher information (Section 4.4).

Low scorers do not spread their wrong answers uniformly. This does not test the assumption that a latent class of random guessers finds all options equally attractive (Jin et al., 2022), or answers correctly with a fixed probability (Xiao et al., 2026): chance-level scorers are a score group, not that class, and a small uniform class mixed with content-driven responses would produce the same non-uniformity. The model comparison does test it (Section 5): the mixture with uniform random guessing predicted worse than the nested logit with guessing directed to the key in all 20 tests, and most of the gap came from where the ability-independent mass goes.

7.3 • For the Item Response Warehouse

The comparison can be run on any table that keeps the chosen option, and its inputs are those of the warehouse's own yardstick: held-out cells, the IMV and ω c . The analysis also produced an item-and option-level layer for the 116 tables (4,492 items and 20,385 options: option parameters, ω c per item and informative distractors), rebuilt with the nominal model at its maximum, to be released with the code upon publication. For users, the model and its fit matter: mirt's default parametrization of the nominal model can stop short of the maximum (Appendix A), and where guessing is common, a model with both a guessing floor and the option predicted better than either alone. These results quantify information; they do not by themselves argue for changing how any exam is scored. Partchev (2020) held that high-stakes scoring rules should be fixed in advance, easy to explain and accepted as fair, and so advised investigating the information in wrong responses in research rather than exploiting it in assessment.

7.5 • What comes next

Two choices in this study call for a specification fixed in advance: a single prior for samples below about 1,000 responses per item, and folds that respect shared passages. Kanopka and Domingue (2025) noted that their position-sensitive mixture used only a 1PL kernel, a limitation of that work. A nominal kernel is one way to lift it.

7.6 • Conclusion

Which wrong option a test-taker chooses carries information that right/wrong scoring discards. Measured on responses the models had not seen, that information improved the prediction of the option chosen in every program we studied. In the warehouse tables the gain was of the order of what estimating item discrimination adds, mostly in predicting which mistake was made rather than whether the answer was right. Bock (1972) gave a model to use it. These results measure what it adds to held-out prediction in these programs.

Data and code availability

The tables are in the nominal branch of the Item Response Warehouse, version 5.0 (Domingue, 2026). The fix. With a1 fixed at 1 and all slopes free except that of the reference distractor, the model is the same (the map between the two sets of slopes is one to one when the key slope is not zero) and mirt reaches the maximum, with the same EM, the same quadrature, the same cap of 2,000 cycles and the same EAP. In the 5,800 folds of the warehouse tables, all fits converged (a median of 63 cycles, at most 841), the training log-likelihood rose by a median of 17 units over the default fit (at most 277), and the 6 folds in which the default fit had degenerated without the convergence flag detecting it disappeared.

Checks.

In 34 folds an independent optimizer checked the maximum. In 11 folds of 11 tables, the refit came within 0.005 log-likelihood units of the maximum found by an independent EM (the default convergence tolerance of mirt), and the default parametrization started from the refitted solution stayed there and predicted the same (largest difference in the probability of the chosen option, 3.4e-4). In 23 other folds of 22 tables, a quasi-Newton optimizer started from two points reached the same solution, between 0.0002 and 0.011 units above the refit. Out of sample, the refitted model predicted better than the default fit in 104 of the 116 tables (100 by more than two SE; median gain 0.39 millibits per response) and worse in 12 (8 by more than two SE), all by 0.08 millibits or less.

With priors.

With the priors of Table A1, the default parametrization reaches the posterior mode: in 12 folds of the TIMSS and PIRLS fits, five versions of the fit (an independent optimizer from two starting points, and mirt by default, with a tighter tolerance and from another start) gave the same log-posterior and the same held-out gain.

The 3PL and the nested logit with guessing. The 3PL and the nested logit with guessing were fitted with mirt's EM under the priors of Table A1 (lognormal(0, 1) on a1, Beta(5, 17) on g, mirt's expbeta prior). These fits stopped below the posterior maximum while reporting convergence (by 0.5 to 102 log-posterior units in 480 fits of 24 tables), and a tighter tolerance did not change this. The cause is not that of the nominal model. In mirt, up to the current release (1.48) and including the version we used, the log-density of the expbeta prior is correct but its first and second derivatives are taken with respect to g rather than its logit, the parameter the EM updates; the prior then pulls g toward its mode more strongly than intended, and the EM settles at a point that is not the posterior mode. Xiao (2026) documented the error, which is corrected in the development version 1.48.2; we observed the stop independently. We therefore polished every fit of the two models in the main analyses (5,800 fits of the 3PL, 3,200 of the 3PLNRM in the comparison of Section 4.3, 11,600 in the analysis against the other half [all 10 samples, for the standard errors of Section S10.2] and 3,480 for new test-takers) to the posterior mode reached from mirt's solution, maximizing mirt's log-posterior with the derivatives of the prior taken with respect to the logit of g, and the figures in the main text are at that mode. The posterior can have more than one mode: from other starting points a higher one exists in some fits. No sign changed, and the counts moved in favor of the 3PL and the 3PLNRM. In 8 fits of the psychTools and vocabulary tables the maximum is at infinity, because a distractor was never chosen in the training data; the two counts that this moves are given both ways. The other analyses that use these two models were polished in the same way: 40 fits in the group-gap analysis, 208 for the short form, 100 in the comparison with the mixture with uniform guessing, 3,025 nested logits in the Czech exams and 1,240 fits in 124 TIMSS and PIRLS tests (all 84 of TIMSS 2019 grade 8 and 10 at random from each other database). In TIMSS and PIRLS, polishing moved the 3PLNRM against the nominal model (Section S8). The chance-filter check (Section S10.5) and the per-database gaps between the 3PLNRM and the nominal model in Section S8 keep mirt's fits. The guessing vignette cited in Section 1.3 fitted its 3PL in mirt with the same prior before the correction; it reports that, without any prior on g, the 2PL → 3PL gain stayed positive in the three tables where it checked this.

Appendix B • Measures, aggregation and Fisher information

ω c . For each item, each observed category k and each other category k′, we keep the heldout responses that fall in {k, k′}, form for each model the normalized probability p k / ( p k + p k ′ ), and compute the binary IMV between the two models on that two-category outcome. Results are averaged over k′ (weighted by the number of responses in each pair) and over k (weighted by the frequency of k) (Kanopka, 2023, Ch. 3). A table's ω c is the mean over items within a fold, averaged over the five folds. On 12 warehouse tables (six comparisons each), pooling the heldout cells of the five folds before averaging over items gave the same sign in all 72 comparisons and, except for the nested logit against the nominal model, whose ω c is close to zero, differed by a median of 2% (at most 18%).

Log-loss in bits.

For a held-out response with chosen option o and key probability p = P(key), the log-loss of the chosen option splits exactly into a correctness part and a "which wrong option" part: where y is 1 if the response is correct.

Meta-analysis.

Tables were nested in family-year clusters (in "other tests", each table is its own cluster), 28 clusters for all 116 tables. The four ENEM areas of a year share test-takers, so the year cluster absorbs their common variation. The gains from the option and from discrimination come from the same tables and samples, so the interval of their ratio comes from a bivariate multilevel meta-analysis of the two (sampling covariance from the 10 samples; unstructured random effects by cluster and table; Fieller's method).

Fisher information.

In the mirt parametrization of the nominal model,

The information of the full option response is I ( θ )= a1 2 Var P ( ak ), and the information of correctness alone is where K is the key.

Their difference is the information added by the choice among distractors: the information about which distractor, weighted by the probability of a wrong answer (cf. Fei et al., 2026).

For each table we computed the ratio of option information to correctness information, summed over items and averaged over a standard normal θ, from the nominal parameters of the first fold of each sample. From the same parameters we computed, by decile of θ, a1 2 Var ( ak | wrong , θ ): the Fisher information about θ that a wrong answer carries through the choice among the distractors.

Standard errors.

The table-level SE comes from 10 samples that overlap by chance in a pool of about 30,000, and relative to the population it is short by a factor of about 1.45 in ENEM (1.25 to 1.73 in ENCCEJA). As with K-fold cross-validation in general, an SE that ignores the overlap between training and test sets is too small (Bengio & Grandvalet, 2004). Even with the SE inflated by that factor, ω c remains above two SE in the 108 ENEM and ENCCEJA tables. In the computer-based residency form the factor is 11.6, and with it ω c stays far from zero (z about 9); in the three small tables the SE only measures the split into folds.

S6 • Short form and items saved

For each of the 52 ENEM tables, item parameters were calibrated on 10,000 test-takers and the test was given, post hoc, to 10,000 others, with no persons or items shared. A short form drew items from a 22-item half of each test and was scored by EAP; the criteria were the 2PL score on the held-out half and the mean 2PL score on the two areas taken on the other test day.

With a bank of 22 items and 10 items given, the item selection is close to a fixed short form, and we call it that; De Ayala (1989) compared adaptive tests based on the nominal model and on the 3PL in the same spirit. We also scored linear tests of increasing length and, as a null, repeated everything with the letters of the distractors shuffled. The categorical information of the 3PLNRM was computed with a closed form checked against numerical derivatives, because mirt does not provide it for that model. The nominal model was fitted to its maximum (Section 3.1 of the main text).

Results. Scored with the 3PLNRM, the short form reached, with a median of 8.4 items, the correlation with the 2PL score on the held-out half that right/wrong 3PL scoring reached with 10 (.696 against .685 after 10 items); with the other test day as criterion, 8.9. The 3PL and the 3PLNRM were fitted with mirt's EM under the priors of

S7 • Group gaps

Data. The full 2023 ENEM microdata: test-takers present in the four areas on both days, the regular booklets of the first administration (2,666,644 persons). Three disjoint calibration samples of 20,000 test-takers and one scoring sample of 500,000 different test-takers. Items: 45 in natural sciences, 45 in human sciences, 40 in languages (without the foreign-language items) and 43 in mathematics. The criterion of each area is the mean official INEP score in the two areas taken on the other test day. Group contrasts (focal against reference): school type, family income, mother's education, race, region and sex.

Models and scoring.

In each area and calibration sample we fitted the 3PL and the nested logit with guessing (3PLNRM) with mirt's EM under the priors of Table A1 of the main text (polished to the posterior mode reached from mirt's solution; Appendix A of the main text), and scored the 500,000 test-takers by EAP on a grid of 61 points from -4 to 4 with a standard normal prior. Our 3PL orders test-takers almost as the official score of the same area does (Spearman .979 to .992).

Measures.

For each area, contrast and score: the standardized gap d, the validity r (the correlation with the criterion) and d/r, the gap per unit of validity, which removes the attenuation that measurement error causes in both; the gap at equal criterion, γ, from a regression of the score on a restricted cubic spline of the criterion (5 knots) and the group, divided by r, with ordinary least squares weighting and with weighting by the focal group; and differential prediction in the sense of Cleary, from a regression of the criterion on a spline of the score and the group. Option-level differential functioning is what the literature calls differential distractor functioning (Green et al., 1989; Penfield, 2008; Suh & Bolt, 2011).

Simulations.

Two simulations without option-level differential functioning that reproduce the observed validity serve as nulls: a generative one, in which ability is drawn from a latent regression on the criterion and the groups and all responses are simulated from the 3PLNRM (validity within -0.004 and +0.003 of the data), and a conditional one, which keeps each response's correctness and redraws only the distractor chosen. A permutation of the group label within each contrast (20 times) gives the noise floor of the change in γ/r (standard deviation at most 0.0034).

Results. Scoring the option raised validity in all four areas (natural sciences .694 to .733, mathematics .694 to .709, human sciences .738 to .741, languages .703 to .708, without the 3,547 test-takers who left a whole area blank). The ratio of d/r under option scoring to d/r under right/wrong scoring was 0.98 to 1.02 across 4 areas and 5 group contrasts (sex is reported separately). Raw gaps widened slightly, as expected from a more valid score (for example, the income gap in natural sciences went from 1.315 to 1.361 standard deviations of the score).

Against the simulations, the option did not shift disadvantaged groups uniformly: in 20 of 20 area-by-contrast cells it placed them higher among low scorers (the three lowest deciles of the criterion) and lower among high scorers (the three highest); the mean shift, within ±0.03 SD, changed sign with the weighting. The 3PL overpredicted the disadvantaged group on the other day (in natural sciences, income: -0.371 SD of the criterion), and the 3PLNRM overpredicted it less in the 20 cells, which is what its higher precision predicts. For sex, the option placed women higher at equal criterion in natural sciences (about 0.04 SD). The data do not separate whether the tilt reflects option-level differential functioning or model misfit where guessing is common.

Declarations Appendix A • Fitting the models in mirt

The problem. In mirt 1.47, the default parametrization of the nominal model is P k ( θ )∝ exp( a1 ⋅ ak k ⋅ θ + d k ), with the slope multiplier of the reference category fixed at 0 and that of the last category at m -1. In 60 of 60 folds of 12 warehouse tables, checked with an EM of our own (and, in one fold of 8 of them, also with TAM and with mirt itself with a1 fixed at 1), the EM stopped below the maximum while reporting convergence, a median of 24 log-likelihood units below it (at most 87). The cause is the product a1 • ak: the EM enters a valley with a1 very small (0.001 to 0.07) and ak very large, and moves more slowly than the convergence tolerance. This is the parametrization of Thissen et al. (2010), with an overall slope and category scoring values anchored at 0 and m -1. The documentation of mirt warns of the symptom, though not of a stop below the maximum: the nominal model can become numerically unstable when poorly chosen high and low anchor values push ak above 10 in absolute value, and it recommends anchors that keep the estimates between 0 and m -1, chosen on theoretical grounds or by reestimating after convergence (Chalmers, 2026). Because the argument that set the anchors (nominal.highlow) was removed in mirt 1.16, we applied this rule by hand, in two folds of the table of each family where the default fit had stopped furthest below the maximum (68 to 277 log-likelihood units, among the largest gaps in the 5,800 folds described below). The re-anchored default parametrization did not reach the maximum in any of the 6 folds: after up to three reestimations it ended 0.14 to 56 units below it while reporting convergence, and anchors read off the maximum itself did not reach it either. S10.2 • Standard errors for the correlations.

We recomputed the correlations of Section 4.5 with a standard error per table, from a person bootstrap within folds and from the variation between samples, using the larger of the two. The SE of a difference between two correlations was about 0.005 to 0.006, three quarters of it due to which items fall in each half.

S14 • Item position in the ENEM booklets

ENEM gives the same items in several booklets in different orders and hands the booklets out by seating position so that neighbors differ, which Kanopka and Domingue (2025) treat as random. For each of the 52 ENEM tables, we linked every test-taker in the 10 samples to their booklet in INEP's public microdata (INEP, n.d.-b) and each item to its position in that booklet (1 to 45 within the area, with the foreign-language items of languages in positions 1 to 5); every held-out cell was matched, and in the natural sciences and mathematics cells of the first sample of each year the stored correctness agreed with the microdata in all cells. With the fits of the main analysis and no refitting, we regressed each held-out gain on the item's position after removing each item's mean (one pooled within-item slope per sample) and averaged the slopes over the 10 samples (SE across samples, too small for the reason given in Appendix B of the main text). The 2PL overpredicted correctness more the later an item appeared, by 0.9 percentage points per 10 positions on average (by more than two SE in 51 of 52 tables, also with the SE multiplied by the factor of Appendix B), a position effect in the sense of Kanopka and Domingue (2025).

It is an average over items: item by item, effects can go both ways, as in their 2014 mathematics test. The 3PL's gain over the 2PL on correctness grew with position in all 52 tables (by more than two SE in 47, and in 44 with the factor; mean 0.62 millibits per 10 positions) and was positive in the first 15 positions in all 52, including the 26 tables of areas that open a test day (median ratio of the last 15 to the first 15, 1.4). The nominal model's standing against the 3PL on correctness fell with position in 51 of 52 tables (by more than two SE in 45, and in 43 with the factor). In languages, which always opens its test day, the nominal model was not behind the 3PL in the first 15 positions in any of the 13 years; in mathematics, which always follows 45 items of another area on the same day, it was behind from its first 15 positions in all choice response records more than correctness.
