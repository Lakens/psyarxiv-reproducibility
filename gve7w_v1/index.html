How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

The words people use provide important insights into their emotional experiences.

Several measures for tracking emotions through language have been developed, including sentiment (i.e., the positivity and negativity of language) and I-usage (first-person singular pronoun use, thought to reflect negative self-focus). However, emerging research using large language model embedding techniques has quantified not just if people use "I" but how they use it, yielding a measure of self-representation (i.e., how strongly people see themselves as "good" and "able" rather than "bad" and "unable"). This measure tracks mental health outcomes in psychotherapy. Given that emotions and emotion regulation are intimately connected to how we view ourselves, linguistic self-representations may also offer a means for assessing emotional experiences. Here, we examined the associations between participants' linguistic selfrepresentations and daily emotional experiences in a 14-day expressive writing study (N = 53).

Within-person associations showed that on days when participants' self-representation embedding scores were closer to "good" or "able," they reported more positive and less negative emotions. At the between-person level, people with self-representations overall closer to "good" experienced lower negative emotional reactivity to expressive writing. These findings suggest that linguistic markers of self-representation hold promise as a tool for assessing emotional experiences, extending prior work linking it to mental health. Translations of these findings could contribute to earlier identification of changes in emotional functioning and provide new ways of assessing metrics of mental health through everyday language.

[div-01]

The words people use offer a window into their internal experiences (Boyd & Schwartz, 2021; Jackson et al., 2022). In particular, our words reflect what and how we are thinking, both of which are intimately connected to our emotions (Gross, 1998; Gross, 2015; Wadlinger & Isaacowitz, 2011; Yeo & Ong, 2024). Identifying linguistic markers of emotions offers a noninvasive and scalable method for observing affective processes as they unfold in real time. As we review below, such measures include sentiment (i.e., the positivity and negativity of language) and I-usage (i.e., how frequently people refer to themselves). Here, we leverage large language models to test whether a semantic assessment of self-representation may track within-person fluctuations in emotions and emotional reactivity.

There are many reasons why the valence of a person's language (called sentiment) can signal their affective state. Theories of emotion posit that language plays an active role in shaping emotional experiences (Barrett et al., 2007; Gross & Feldman Barrett, 2011; Lindquist et al., 2015; Nook et al., 2017a). In line with these ideas, sentiment has been shown to correspond with self-reported emotions (Kleinberg et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Zhang & Fesenmaier, 2018). Furthermore, the sentiment of social media interactions, psychotherapy exchanges, and journaling has also been linked to depressive, anxiety, and psychotic symptoms (Biró et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2014; Hur et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2025; Stamatis et al., 2022; Vartiainen et al., 2025). Thus, sentiment, emotions, and mental health are robustly linked (Aldao et al., 2010; Scott et al., 2020).

Another leading linguistic measure of emotion is first-person singular pronoun usage (e.g., "I," "me," "my"), called "I-usage." Self-referencing and construal-level theories posit that greater self-focused attention may reflect rumination, neuroticism, and reduced psychological "distance" from the self (Berry-Blunt et al., 2021; Holtzman & Delgado, 2023; Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008; Trope & Liberman, 2010), which are thought to foment elevated negative affect and vulnerability to distress (Abraham et al., 2023; Ingram et al., 1992). Indeed, greater I-usage in written text is linked with more negative emotions and greater use of negative emotion words (Holtzman & Delgado, 2023; Pennebaker & Chung, 2006; Tackman et al., 2019). By contrast, "distancing" one's language by reducing first-person pronoun usage or shifting to non-firstperson pronouns decreases negative emotions (Kross et al., 2014; Nook et al., 2017b; Orvell et al., 2019) and is associated with more successful emotion regulation (Nook et al., 2017b; Shahane et al., 2023; Whiting et al., 2026). I-usage has also been robustly associated with psychopathology (Edwards & Holtzman, 2017; Eichstaedt et al., 2018; Li et al., 2026; Rude et al., 2004; Stade et al., 2023; Tackman et al., 2019; Weisenburger et al., 2025). For instance, Tackman et al. (2019) found that more frequent I-usage related to worse depression symptoms, whereas distancing pronouns away from the first-person tracks symptom improvements in psychotherapy (Nook et al., 2022).

However, these studies primarily focus on the frequency of first-person singular pronouns without attending to the surrounding content contextualizing how a person refers to themselves. For instance, the sentences "I'm learning from my mistakes" and "I can't do anything right" both use "I" once, but their authors represent themselves very differently. Indeed, one study integrated sentiment and I-usage analyses to show that the valence of words around the use of first-person singular pronouns predicts depression symptoms (Anderson et al., 2008; Collins et al., 2023; Dozois et al., 2014). Emerging natural language processing and deep learning techniques allow researchers to use word embeddings to quantify where in "semantic space" people use specific words (Charlesworth et al., 2021; Devlin et al., 2019; Kjell et al., 2024). Language models have been trained on immense quantities of text and have learned which words and phrases tend to co-occur frequently, yielding a semantic space or "map" of words and phrases organized by similarity. One can then quantify word and phrase similarity within this space by comparing their embeddings (i.e., their location in semantic space).

Recently, Mesquiti et al. (2026) used such a technique to show that changes in where psychotherapy clients' representation of "I" fell in semantic space, along axes of how "good" and "able" they were, reflected changes in their depression and anxiety symptom severity over time.

Here we ask whether linguistic assessments of self-representation could also reflect emotional experiences in addition to psychopathology, given the tight connections between emotion and mental health. We test this question in an expressive writing paradigm, where participants wrote about a negative experience each day for 14 days (Pennebaker & Beall, 1986). Expressive writing offers a rich context for testing associations between language and emotion, as participants write about an emotionally evocative experience. Higher positive emotion word usage during expressive writing predicts greater improvements in health outcomes (Pennebaker et al., 1997; Pulverman et al., 2015). However, associations with firstperson pronoun usage have been mixed, with some studies finding that less I-usage predicts health benefits (North et al., 2011; Park et al., 2016) and others finding mixed or even opposing associations (Dunnack & Park, 2009; Robertson et al., 2021). It is possible that this new selfrepresentation measure, which attends not only to whether but also how people use "I," can clarify these discrepant results.

Expressive writing is also a relevant context for assessing emotion reactivity, as participants tend to report increased negative emotions immediately after writing (e.g., Esterling et al., 1999; Graf et al., 2008; Maslej et al., 2020; Pascual-Leone et al., 2016; Smyth, 1998).

This stands to reason, as participants are instructed to engage with negative experiences, akin to common laboratory tasks for inducing negative affect (e.g., Gillihan et al., 2007; Ozawa, 2021). However, stronger negative emotional reactivity to affective provocations is closely associated with emotion dysregulation and may reflect psychopathology (Bylsma et al., 2011; Heller et al., 2019). Interestingly, prior work links more positive self-representations with reduced emotional reactivity (Goldin et al., 2009; Jain et al., 2026). Here we test if people with more positive linguistic self-representations might also be less emotionally reactive to expressive writing.

In the present study, we apply a natural language processing technique to capture the semantic context of how individuals talk about themselves. We then test whether these linguistic self-representations relate to daily emotions and emotional reactivity. Establishing these links could help clarify how everyday language can be leveraged to track emotional fluctuations and improve our understanding of the complex relationships between emotions, self-schemas, and mental health.

Procedure Overview

As noted above, participants interested in the study first completed a brief pre-screening questionnaire. Pre-screening was conducted over the phone, during which an experimenter collected information about participants' demographics, English fluency level, mental health diagnoses, and psychological treatment history. Participants who passed the initial pre-screen were also instructed to complete the GAD-7 and PHQ-8. If a participant met inclusion criteria noted above, they then scheduled a "Day 1" initial lab visit.

On Day 1 of the study, participants attended an in-person lab session facilitated by an experimenter. Participants provided informed consent, and the experimenter read out instructions for completing the expressive writing exercise. Participants were given a Qualtrics link to the Day 1 survey, which contained a battery of questionnaires, an assessment of their emotions before writing, the expressive writing exercise, and a post-writing emotions assessment (details of these measures are provided below). During the in-person visit, participants first completed the Day 1 writing and emotion ratings, followed by three other tasks (not discussed in this paper) and the questionnaire battery, which included symptoms and demographics. During the session, the experimenter left the room so the participant could complete the survey privately, but the experimenter remained available to answer any questions.

The expressive writing paradigm used in this study was based on Pennebaker & Beall (1986) and its subsequent adaptations (e.g., Smith et al., 2015). Participants received the same prompt daily and could choose to write about the same topic or a different topic each day: "Please spend 15 minutes writing about your deepest thoughts and feelings about an experience that bothers you.

Please direct your writing to yourself, as if you are talking to yourself. For example, this could be an experience that makes you feel upset or is difficult for you to think/talk about. Really let go and explore your deepest emotions and thoughts. You might tie your topic to your relationships with others, including parents, lovers, friends, or relatives. You might link your writing to your past and who you were, your present and who you are now, or your future and who you would like to be. Remember, you may write about the same issues or experience on all days of writing or about different topics each day. Once you begin writing, please continue writing for the entire 15 minutes. You can use the timer below to make sure that you reach the 15 minute mark."

Following the initial lab visit on Day 1, participants received an email each day at 5:00pm for the following 13 days, which provided a link to the Qualtrics survey containing the same 15minute expressive writing exercise and questionnaires. If a participant did not complete a daily survey, an experimenter would send a reminder email prior to delivering the next survey. Each day, an experimenter checked on the previous day's writing data and emailed any participants who (i) wrote for a shorter time than instructed or (ii) submitted noticeably sparse content (e.g., a single sentence) to remind them of the instruction to write for 15 minutes. On Day 15, participants attended a final in-person session with an experimenter, during which they completed the final questionnaires. At the end of the study, participants were debriefed and compensated for their time.

All participants earned a base payment of $80 for completing the study and $10 for completing the follow-up at the end of the study. To encourage daily writing, a bonus payment system was implemented: participants could earn an additional $5 for completing at least 8 days of writing, $15 for completing at least 10 days of writing, $20 for completing 12 days of writing, or $30 for completing 14 days of writing, with a maximum possible payment of $120.

Compliance was high, with participants completing an average of 12.87 out of 14 daily writing sessions (91.1% completed; minimum = 10 of 14 [71.4%], SD = 1.18). On average, participants wrote for 20.49 (SD = 2.78) minutes and produced 485.86 (SD = 47.20) words per daily session.

Measures

Emotion Ratings

Participants reported how intensely they were feeling a series of emotions before and after the writing exercise, using sliding scales (0 = Not at all,100 = Extremely) adapted from prior daily diary and experience sampling studies (e.g., Erbas et al., 2022; North et al., 2011; Vedernikova et al., 2021). Daily pre-and post-writing positive emotion scores were computed by averaging across "Happy," "Excited," "Calm," "Content," and "Proud." Daily pre-and post-writing negative emotion scores were computed by averaging across "Sad," "Angry," "Disgusted," "Upset," "Scared," "Anxious", "Frustrated," and "Embarrassed." These emotions were selected to span positive and negative valences, high and low arousal, and both 'social' and 'basic' emotions.

Following prior work (e.g., Cohen et al., 2008; Maslej et al., 2020; Pascual-Leone et al., 2016), a negative emotional reactivity variable was created by taking the difference between participants' post-writing negative emotion score and pre-writing negative emotion score each day.

Good-Bad Embedding Score

To quantify participants' self-representation relative to the concepts of 'good' and 'bad' within their writing, we used RoBERTa-Large to compute an embedding score for each participant on each day of writing. RoBERTa-Large is a transformer-based language model with 24 layers and 1,024-dimensional hidden states (Liu et al., 2019). We used contextual word embeddings to estimate how strongly people saw themselves as good, bad, able, or unable by computing how "close" their use of first-person singular pronouns is to each of these anchor words. These methods were originally inspired by Charlesworth et al. (2024) and Havaldar et al.

(2023), and we have recently shown that they can track symptom levels in psychotherapy transcripts (Mesquiti et al., 2026). Here, we test their relations with self-reported emotions within an expressive writing study.

First, we constructed a "good-bad" axis in semantic space. We embedded two phrases at each pole of this axis ("I feel good"/"I am good" vs. "I feel bad"/"I am bad") and extracted the first-token embedding (the s token in RoBERTa's vocabulary) from the model's final hidden layer ⟨ ⟩ (layer 24) for each statement. These embeddings comprise a summary vector that represents the position of each phrase in semantic space (Liu et al., 2019). We then computed the centroids of the two positive-pole phrases and two negative-pole phrases by averaging their embeddings, yielding a midpoint between them that could serve as the two poles of our good-bad semantic axis. We then subtracted the negative-pole centroid from the positive-pole centroid to create a direction vector, which points from negative to positive valence in the model's embedding space. We then normalized the vector to a standard unit length of 1 to produce a vector representing the "good-bad" axis in semantic space.

After this axis was defined, we calculated "scores" that represented where each participants' self-referential language fell along this axis for each day of writing. We tokenized the text of each writing sample and extracted word-level embeddings from the final hidden layer of RoBERTa. Then, using regex pattern matching, we identified all occurrences of first-person singular pronouns ("I," "me," "my," "mine," "myself") and extracted their corresponding token embeddings for each day. Multiple occurrences of any one pronoun in a single writing sample were handled by averaging all of the pronoun's embedding scores to produce a single vector for that pronoun.

This resulted in a set of vectors that represented the embedding location of each firstperson singular pronoun in semantic space for each participant on each day of writing. We then subtracted the "good-bad" axis midpoint from each embedding to translate them onto the "good-bad" axis. Taking the dot product, we projected the resulting vectors onto the axis, yielding a "projection score" that reflected where each pronoun fell along the "good-bad" axis.

Lastly, we averaged all first-person singular pronoun projection scores within each writing sample to produce a single pronoun-level embedding score per participant for each day of writing, totaling 14 "good-bad" embedding scores for their 14 days of writing. Scores with a more positive value indicate closer alignment with the "good" pole, while scores with a more negative value indicate closer alignment with the "bad" pole. On days when participants did not complete the writing exercise or did not use any first-person singular pronouns, the corresponding embedding score was coded as missing.

Able-Unable Embedding Score

We used the same approach described above to compute scores of where participants' first-person singular pronouns fell on an "able-unable" axis. The only modification for this method was using the statements "I feel able"/"I am able" vs. "I feel unable"/"I am unable" to construct the axis poles. This resulted in daily "able-unable" embedding scores for each participant, where more positive scores indicate closer alignment with the "able" pole, and more negative scores indicate closer alignment to the "unable" pole. To confirm that "good-bad"

representations and "able-unable" representations should be examined separately, we conducted a repeated measures correlation analysis and found a small within-person association between "good-bad" representations and "able-unable" representations (rrm = .27, 95% CI [.20, .34], p < .001). Thus, although these measures are positively associated, the strength of the association is too small to warrant combining them.

Control Variables

Linguistic control variables were computed for each daily writing sample to account for potential linguistic confounds. Total word count, first-person singular pronoun usage (i.e., the proportion of first-person singular pronouns relative to total words), and sentiment were indexed using the LIWC-22 "WC," "i," and "Tone" dictionaries, respectively (Pennebaker et al., 2022).

Analyses and Hypotheses

Question 1) Does Self-Representation Relate to Pre-Writing Emotions?

Our first research question concerned how participants' "good" and "able" selfrepresentations each related to their pre-writing positive and negative emotions. In other words, these analyses test whether the ways in which people refer to themselves on any given day of an expressive writing exercise might reflect their emotional state before starting that writing session. Testing this question requires decomposing our linguistic predictors into within-person and between-person components to specifically isolate within-person relationships between language and emotions.

Following prior work (Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013; Enders & Tofighi, 2007; Mirea et al., 2026; Nook et al., 2022; Rodman et al., 2020), we created variables representing within-person and between-person variance in participants' first-person "good-bad" and "able-unable" embedding scores. The within-person component was computed by subtracting each participant's mean embedding score from each of their daily scores (i.e., this variable represents how strongly each day's score deviated from that participant's mean). The between-person component was computed by subtracting the overall group mean embedding score from each participant's average score (i.e., this is a constant variable for each participant that represents their average deviation from the group's overall average).

Examining "good-bad" and "able-unable" representations separately, we used four linear mixed effects models with random intercepts for participants to test the hypotheses that (i) self-representations closer to "good" would be associated with higher pre-writing positive emotions; (ii) self-representations closer to "good" would be associated with lower pre-writing negative emotions; (iii) self-representations closer to "able" would be associated with higher prewriting positive emotions; and (iv) self-representations closer to "able" would be associated with lower pre-writing negative emotions. In each model, the within-and between-person components of the embedding score being tested were entered simultaneously as predictors, and time (i.e., days in study) was included as a covariate.

Question 2) Does Self-Representation Relate to Negative Emotional Reactivity During

Expressive Writing?

We used the same analytical approach as Question 2 to examine the relationship between participants' "good" and "able" self-representations and negative emotional reactivity (i.e., how strongly participants increased their endorsement of negative emotions after engaging with expressive writing). We first verified that expressive writing tasks tended to induce negative emotions, as in prior work. Collapsing across days, post-writing negative emotions (M = 26.83, SD = 16.39) were higher than pre-writing negative emotions (M = 20.36, SD = 13.93; t = -6.08, 95% CI [-8.61, -4.34], p < .001). However, there was substantial variance in negative emotional reactivity across participants and days (M = 6.46, SD = 14.37, range = 133.25), allowing us to test whether this variance was explained by either within-person or between-person variance in self-representation.

Using two linear mixed effects models, we tested the hypotheses that reduced negative emotional reactivity would be associated with (i) self-representations closer to "good," and (ii) self-representations closer to "able," again using separate models for 'good-bad' and "ableunable" embedding scores. These models also controlled for participants' pre-writing negative emotion scores (again decomposed into their within-person and between-person components).

Question 3) Do These Relationships Withstand Controls?

Finally, we repeated the analyses above, adding a decomposed set of additional linguistic variables as controls. These controls included total word count, first-person singular pronoun usage, and sentiment, providing a test of whether the relationships established in Questions 1 and 2 persist even after controlling for these variables.

Due to their sensitive nature, written text data are not publicly accessible (an assurance we gave participants in the consent form). Written text data may be available upon request by researchers who have completed appropriate ethics training, agree to confidentiality restrictions, and whose research goals are in line with the purpose of this study.

We tested associations between decomposed embedding scores and emotions using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for participants (lme4, version 1.1.38: Bates et al., 2015). We ran repeated measures correlations using rmcorr, version 0.7.0 (Bakdash & Marusich, 2017). Linear mixed-effects models, repeated measures correlations, and t-test analyses were conducted using RStudio Version 4.3.1 (Posit team, 2024). Question 1) Within-Person Variance in Self-Representations Relate to Higher Positive and Lower Negative Pre-Writing Emotions

See Table 2 for results from all mixed-effects models testing relations between language and emotions for both research questions 1 and 2. To summarize, we found that selfrepresentation embedding scores on both "good-bad" and "able-unable" axes were consistently associated with higher positive and lower negative pre-writing emotions at the within-subjects but not between-subjects levels of analysis.

"Good-Bad" Representations and Pre-Writing Positive Emotions

As hypothesized, we observed a significant association between within-person variance in participants' "good-bad" self-representations and their pre-writing positive emotions: participants reported higher pre-writing positive emotions on days when their selfrepresentations were closer to the "good" pole. No between-person association emerged, suggesting that participants who overall felt more positive emotions did not overall have selfrepresentations closer to the "good" pole.

"Good-Bad" Representations and Pre-Writing Negative Emotions

"Good-bad" representations were also significantly related to within-subjects variance in pre-writing negative emotions: on days when participants' representations were closer to the "good" pole, they also reported lower pre-writing negative emotions. This relationship was not significant at the between-person level.

"Able-Unable" Representations and Pre-Writing Positive Emotions

As for the "able-unable" results, we observed a significant association between withinperson variance in participants' "able-unable" representations and pre-writing positive emotions: on days when participants reported greater pre-writing positive emotions, their first-person singular pronoun representations were closer to the "able" pole. This relationship was not significant at the between-subjects level.

"Able-Unable" Representations and Pre-Writing Negative Emotions

Finally, "able-unable" representations were also associated with pre-writing negative emotions at the within-persons level: on days when participants' representations were closer to the "able" pole, they reported lower pre-writing negative emotions. Between-person variance in "able-unable" embeddings were again not significantly associated with pre-writing negative emotions.

Question 3) These Relationships Survive Controls

To test whether our results remained robust after controlling for potential confounding variables, we repeated the analyses above while adding total word count, frequency of firstperson singular pronoun usage, and sentiment (i.e., the overall valence of the participant's writing) as controls in our mixed-effects models. We found that all results survived these controls, except for two: the within-person association between lower pre-writing negative emotions and "able-unable" self-representation, and the between-person association between negative emotional reactivity and "good-bad" self-representations were no longer significant after adding these controls (for details, see Supplementary Material).

Discussion

We examined how individuals' self-representations related to their daily emotional experiences by employing a natural language processing technique (following Mesquiti et al., 2026) on an expressive writing dataset. On days when participants' self-representation embedding scores were closer to "good" or "able," they also reported higher positive and lower negative pre-writing emotions. Additionally, reduced negative emotional reactivity to journaling was associated with "good" self-representations at both within-and between-person levels.

Together, these results (i) further evidence the connection between self-representation, emotions, and emotion regulation, and (ii) demonstrate the promise of leveraging natural language processing to innocuously assess these relationships through everyday language.

There are many reasons why positive self-representation would covary with daily emotions. Chronic and ruminative self-focus is associated with greater emotional distress and negative affect (Ingram et al., 1992; Mor & Winquist, 2002), whereas focus on positive selfaspects is associated with lower negative affect (Mor & Winquist, 2002). Thus, how one is feeling is tightly coupled with how one feels about oneself. Expressive writing may be a particularly sensitive context for detecting this covariation, as it prompts people to reflect on salient negative emotional experiences in their own lives. Crucially, we found that one's emotions before writing predicted self-representation during writing. This temporal separation suggests that ambient mood before writing can be captured using this linguistic measure. Our results for reactivity echo prior work showing that more positive self-evaluations are associated with lesser negative emotional reactivity (Jain et al., 2026), whereas negative self-beliefs are linked to greater reactivity (Goldin et al., 2009). Theoretically, this link likely reflects enhanced self-efficacy, emotion regulation, or resilience (Benfer et al., 2018; Notebaert et al., 2026), allowing participants with more positive self-views to feel less distressed when engaging with negative experiences. However, as we note below, it is important for future work to directly test these potential mediating mechanisms.

Our results fill several important gaps in the literature. First, we used natural language processing techniques to produce a linguistic metric that captures variance in emotion above and beyond two key leading alternative metrics, sentiment and I-usage (Edwards & Holtzman, 2017; Nook et al., 2022; Rude et al., 2004; Stade et al., 2023; Tackman et al., 2019). These results extend this growing body of research and speak to the utility of large language models for estimating complex semantic features in language (Abdou et al., 2025; Mesquiti et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025). However, two results did not survive controls. It is not clear why the withinperson relationship between lower pre-writing negative emotions and "able-unable" selfrepresentations was weaker than the other within-person language-emotion relationships we uncovered (which all survived controls). Additionally, as we discuss in our limitations, the between-person association between negative reactivity and "good-bad" self-representations may not have survived controls due to our smaller sample size reducing power for detecting between-persons effects. These effects merit further replication.

Second, our findings may offer explanations for prior results. Prior expressive writing studies showed inconsistent relationships between I-usage and health benefits (Dunnack & Park, 2009; North et al., 2011; Park et al., 2016; Robertson et al., 2021). It is possible that prior measures of I-usage neglect important contextual information that is important for strong measurement. Additionally, the only other paper using this linguistic marker of selfrepresentation shows that it is associated with internalizing symptoms in psychotherapy (Mesquiti et al., 2026). Demonstrating that this metric also tracks daily emotional experiences suggests this association could emerge because depression and anxiety typically involve intense negative emotions. Of course, whether emotions fully explain this association remains to be shown.

Our findings also carry important clinical implications. Language is produced in abundance both in everyday contexts and during psychotherapy. Given that emotional distress and negative self-representation are each a reliable signal of psychopathology (Anderson et al., 2008; Bathina et al., 2021; Collins & Winer, 2024; Dozois et al., 2014; Lalk et al., 2025), being able to simultaneously measure both through natural language provides a promising, unobtrusive means of monitoring important mental health mechanisms (Nook, 2023). Such tools could help rapidly identify who is experiencing symptoms (Large & Marcussen, 2000; Sperduti et al., 2013), trigger just-in-time interventions (Haag et al., 2025; Nahum-Shani & Murphy, 2026), or signal when therapeutic progress has stalled (Huston et al., 2019).

Although our study provides important advances, there are several limitations that should be addressed in future work. First, though embedding distance between "I" and "good/able" may serve as a good estimate for self-beliefs, the relationships we observed could nonetheless reflect other processes (e.g., stressors or affect itself). Future work could strengthen these findings by administering self-report measures of self-representation to corroborate these natural language assessments and explicate mediating mechanisms.

Additionally, although the longitudinal design allowed us to capture a large number of total observations, our sample size was relatively small, which constrains our ability to discover between-person effects and may account for smaller effect sizes. The correlational nature of this study also limits causal conclusions regarding the influence of emotions on selfrepresentation or vice versa. Finally, though beliefs about one's own worthiness and capability are central to emotion regulation and psychopathology (Beck et al., 2024; Peterson & Park, 1998), self-schema and appraisal literature suggests there are many self-construals that may be relevant to emotions and well-being (e.g., whether one is in control, trustworthy, etcetera; Clore & Ortony, 2008; Martin & Young, 2010; Monsonet et al., 2026). Future work could expand the scope to include other potentially relevant semantic axes. To conclude, our findings extend the research on linguistic markers of emotions by demonstrating that emotional experiences can be tracked through self-referential language.

These findings enrich researchers' toolkit for passively measuring emotions through natural language use and spur new directions for both computational affective research and clinical translations.
