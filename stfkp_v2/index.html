Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Ambiguous, multistable stimuli can give rise to multiple perceptual interpretations.

Bistable visual stimuli, in particular, have long been used to study neural and cognitive processes. However, many experimental paradigms rely on self-report to assess subjective perceptual experiences, introducing potential issues such as low reliability and interferences of reporting into the processes under investigation. No-report paradigms serve as a method to work around these problems by decoding perceptual content based on physiological or neural data. Here we discuss the strengths and pitfalls of no-report paradigms in the context of research using multistable perception. Focusing on experimental power related to decoding accuracy, we illustrate current progress of decoding perceptual content using three major data modalities (eye tracking, M/EEG and fMRI). We find that the predictability of the percept varies significantly depending on the paradigm and stimulus type. Finally, we introduce a method and open-source toolkit to calculate experimental power to plan future no-report studies, accounting for limited classification accuracy when decoding perceptual states.

Introduction

Visual multistability, as revealed by ambiguous stimuli (Figure 1), offers a unique window into the processes governing our perception (Kanizsa & Luccio, 1995; Long & Toppino, 2004). Experimental participants who view these ambiguous stimuli often experience a bistable (multistable) percept, with the stimuli alternating between two (or more) perceptual states over time. Visual bistability has been central to a wide range of research questions spanning both cognitive and neural domains. Aside from being studied extensively itself (e.g., Blake & Logothetis, 2002; Kruse & Stadler, 1995; Leopold & Logothetis, 1999; Levelt, 1965), neural activity related to alternations between the two perceptual states has yielded significant insights into perceptual organization (e.g., Hohwy et al., 2008; Schmack et al., 2016) as well as feature binding and information integration (e.g., Canales-Johnson et al., 2023; Haynes & Rees, 2005; Rose & Büchel, 2005; Tsuchiya et al., 2015). In this paper, we review the findings that contribute to the development of the two stages of no-report paradigms in bistable perception, namely the development of a classification pipeline (primary analysis) and the adaptation of this pipeline to investigate processes isolated from report (secondary analysis). We then provide recommendations to authors wanting to conduct studies involving visual multistability for topics in psychology and cognitive neuroscience. Finally, we propose a method to calculate experimental power to plan future no-report studies, specifically accounting for power attenuation due to imperfect classification in the decoding stage of no-report paradigms.

Issues with active report in experimental paradigms using multistability

Because perceptual reversals occur at apparently random times, (Brascamp et al., 2006; Leopold & Logothetis, 1999; Pastukhov & Braun, 2007), they are often tracked using self-report paradigms. When the temporal dynamics of perceptual reversals unfolding are of interest (Rubin & Hupé, 2004), researchers often rely on active report, probing the participants percept during a stimulus presentation.

Active report offers direct access to a participant's subjective perception, but it is not without caveats. Generally, introspection itself is likely an unreliable way of accessing cognitive processes (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977). But even if one regards perceptual content as synonymous with what participants are able to report, several issues remain. We identify three key issues with the reliance on active report in studies of multistable perception.

1. Interference with measurement

As active report engages various cognitive and physiological processes, a key challenge in studies aiming to isolate the neural correlates of perception is their separation from activity associated with active report. Specifically, active report involves moderators such as attention, post-perceptual processes such as working memory and decision making, and may engage downstream processes such as metacognition (Block, 2019; Cohen et al., 2020; Duman et al., 2022). Further, motor or verbal preparation is initiated when reporting momentary percepts, and since these processes overlap with perception related activity, the isolation of the latter becomes difficult (Aru et al., 2012; Block, 2019; Koch et al., 2016; Tsuchiya et al., 2015). In line with that, research indicates that parts of the cortical activation patterns during binocular rivalry (simultaneously displaying different images to each eye to induce multistability) seem to be driven by active report and introspection rather than the (transitions between) perceptual content itself (Block, 2019; Frässle et al., 2014; Goldberg et al., 2006). This issue is not restricted to measurements of brain activity, which will be elaborated on later.

2. Interference with perception

A second concern with using active report in multistable perception paradigms is its potential to influence perception, or perceptual transitions itself. It is well established that active report speeds up perceptual reversals (Fahle et al., 2011; Frässle et al., 2014; Naber et al., 2011). Further, the dynamics of perceptual reversals are markedly affected by voluntary control, attention, perception-related action and expectations (Alais et al., 2010; Beets et al., 2010; Dieter et al., 2016; Maruya et al., 2007; Paffen et al., 2006; Schmack et al., 2016; van Ee et al., 2005; Wohlschläger, 2000). Since active report requires attention, and potentially also engages higher-order processes (e.g., decision making), it cannot be excluded that active report affects perception via any of the pathways above.

3. Active report as a delayed measure

Active report generally serves as ground truth to determine the occurrence of perceptual alternations. However, it is a delayed measure (the report is always later than the transition itself), and it is not straightforward to estimate the temporal precision of the response directly. Certain physiological measures are known to be more tightly linked to stimulus features and therefore provide an indirect estimate of the temporal precision of active report. For example, Canales-Johnson et al. (2023) found average response delays ranging from 400 ms up to 1200 ms between perceptual reversals indicated by eye-movement and the successive button press for participants viewing a bistable stimulus.

Similar delays were determined by Kornmeier and Bach (2012), who found a delay of roughly 350 ms between EEG signatures of perceptual reversals and self report, and by Aleshin et al. (2019), who found that manual report is delayed by roughly 250 ms compared to an ocular response in unambiguous displays of motion reversals. Further, these delays seem to vary strongly within as well as between individuals (Kornmeier & Bach, 2003), which poses several problems for both research investigating multistability itself and research using multistability as a research tool for more general processes. For the former, inaccurate time stamps may obscure key information about the temporal dynamics of perception. For the latter, it may even be more problematic given that many perceptual correlates rely on averaging over many events (e.g., perceptual reversals). If these events do not precisely match neural signatures for perceptual reversals, experimental power is lost, with the possibility of masking short-duration signals altogether.

No-report experimental paradigms

No-report paradigms are experimental designs in consciousness and perception science that aim to infer a person's subjective experience without requiring explicit behavioral report (or more general decision-making), such as button presses or verbal responses. By excluding interfering and confounding processes related to active report, some authors have argued that these experimental setups have the potential for more valid and reliable insights into the underlying neural mechanisms of conscious experience (Block, 2019; Cohen et al., 2020; Pitts, Metzler, & Hillyard, 2014; Tsuchiya et al., 2015) and no-report task versions have been used in a range of experimental setups, including visual masking, auditory detection, dichoptic fusion and multistable perception paradigms (e.g., Alilović et al., 2023; Cohen et al., 2020 Cohen et al., , 2024;; Fahrenfort et al., 2012; Frässle et al., 2014; Hatamimajoumerd et al., 2022; Hesse & Tsao, 2020; Pitts, Metzler, & Hillyard, 2014).

Compared to classic report-based paradigms, no-report studies typically find that late, "global" signatures associated with conscious access, such as the P3 ERP component or frontal brain activity, reduce or disappear, while earlier, posterior sensory signals continue to track stimulus perception (Cohen et al., 2024; Hatamimajoumerd et al., 2022; Pitts, Metzler, & Hillyard, 2014; Pitts, Padwal, et al., 2014; Sergent et al., 2021; Tsuchiya et al., 2015). However, prefrontal activity as well as global ERP signatures have also been observed in no-report tasks, primarily in studies using (binocular) rivalry (e.g., Hesse & Tsao, 2020), though the results are mixed (Brascamp et al., 2015). These discrepancies may be explained by differences between tasks and/or experimental set-ups. Generally, no-report approaches aim to push theories of consciousness (e.g., local recurrency theory and global workspace theory; Dehaene et al., 2011; Lamme, 2004) to distinguish more sharply between neural correlates of experience, independent of post-perceptual processes and correlates of access/report (see Noorman et al., 2024 Noorman et al., , 2025, for alternative strategies to do so).

In simple masking or detection-type paradigms, one can directly compare report and no-report conditions by presenting the same stimuli in different blocks, one in which the participant is asked for a response and one in which it is not, and then comparing the observed neural signals. In multistable perception tasks (e.g., binocular rivalry), however, one does not know when the percept changes during no-report trials, because conditions (e.g., seeing a house or a face) are inferred post hoc based on reports of the participant.

Therefore, no-report studies of multistable perception mostly follow a similar two-step structure, starting with the establishment of an indirect marker of perceptual switches during a report session (e.g., eye movements, optokinetic nystagmus, pupil size, neural measures) to classify perceptual alternations (primary analysis; see left panel of Figure 2).

Often, a classifier, such as a support-vector machine or a convolutional neural network is trained and cross-validated on data labeled through self-report or a mixture of disambiguated replay, i.e., replaying a series of now unambiguous stimuli that mimic the reported percepts, and self-report (e.g., Canales-Johnson et al., 2023; Frässle et al., 2014; Hramov et al., 2017 Hramov et al., , 2019)). Then, data that was obtained without self-report can be labeled by the classifier to infer switch timing without overt responses from the participant (secondary analysis; see right panel of Figure 2). With this labeled data, hypotheses can be tested on features not used by the classifier.

Importantly, data that was used to classify perceptual states cannot be reused for hypothesis testing, as this would constitute a form of "double-dipping," i.e., using the same features to divide the data and to later test a model risks inflated false positives by capitalizing on noise (e.g., Kriegeskorte et al., 2009; Vul et al., 2009). Therefore, studies that aim to test condition differences often use two separated datasets, one to classify perceptual alternations and one to test predictions (e.g., Canales-Johnson et al., 2023; Frässle et al., 2014).

When developing a classification pipeline to label no-report data with a classification algorithm (primary analysis), using data labeled by active report involves a domain shift (Farahani et al., 2021). Specifically, since the conditions under which features are recorded differ between report and no-report conditions, the set of features that is used to set up a classifier is different from the set of features which the classifier is applied to.

This means that it is not straightforward to assess the classifier's accuracy, making it difficult to validate the classification of no-report trials.

Given that a valid and reliable classification of perceptual content is central to the success of no-report studies using multistable perception, in the following, we review the current progress and methods that allow or could allow for accurate classification of perceptual content in multistable perception paradigms. We emphasize the importance of establishing validity when establishing a classification pipeline and discuss several possibilities to achieve and evaluate validity. We divide classification possibilities by the type of physiological measurement taken, starting with eye tracking followed by M/EEG and fMRI. Since many studies do not employ classifiers with easy-to-calculate classification accuracies, we highlight the role of studies finding condition differences between perceptual phases and to what extent these insights could serve as a foundation for classification in future no-report studies. Finally, we present a method to assess the expected power in no-report studies using bistable perception given an expected classification accuracy.

Overall, the discussed aspects together with the methodology to calculate power should provide a useful guide to plan successful no-report studies.

Primary analysis: Classifying Perceptual Content

Successful classification of perceptual content depends on the effect size of a condition difference between two percepts. When it comes to the classification of momentary percepts in bistable perception paradigms, previous literature can be divided into studies that perform prediction, allowing for a more direct estimate of a classification accuracy, and studies that recover condition differences relating to specific percepts. While the first delivers direct insights for future no-report studies, the second can still give an indication of an implied classification accuracy assuming an effect size can be derived from published statistics. Specifically, an estimate of the effect size (e.g., Cohen's d) can often be calculated from test statistics or through reported means and variance (Rosnow & Rosenthal, 2003), which translates to an effect size in a possible classification scenario (Rice & Harris, 2005). Optokinetic Nystagmus

Optokinetic nystagmus (OKN) is a reflexive eye movement that occurs when viewing a moving visual scene, consisting of a slow tracking (pursuit) phase in the direction of motion followed by a quick saccadic reset in the opposite direction. It helps stabilize the visual field on the retina and is commonly used to assess motion perception and visual function (Hale et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2023; Reinecke, 1961). In the case of binocular rivalry, two gratings continuously moving in opposite directions can be presented to each eye while tracking eye movements. Because of parallel eye movements, the smooth pursuit phase of the OKN typically aligns with the motion direction of the dominant percept (i.e., one of the two gratings). OKN has been found to be a robust correlate of reported percepts, and is successfully used as basis for the classification of percepts in binocular rivalry setups (Aleshin et al., 2019; Enoksson, 1963; Fox et al., 1975; Wei & Sun, 1998).

While OKN-based classification is well-established for binocular rivalry, its applicability to other ambiguous stimuli is more limited. Very strong links between reported percepts and eye movements have also been shown for superimposed moving random patterns (Watanabe, 1999) and for moving plaids, which can be perceived as coherent or transparent motion, and these have been used in no-report setups (e.g., Canales-Johnson et al., 2023; Frässle et al., 2014; Fujiwara et al., 2017; Leopold et al., 1995; Naber et al., 2011; Wilbertz et al., 2018).

Pupil Dilation

The use of optokinetic nystagmus as an objective indicator of perceptual states can be enhanced by incorporating luminance-dependent pupil dilation as a measure for the percept (Acquafredda et al., 2022; Frässle et al., 2014; Naber et al., 2011). Due to a modulatory role of cognitive processes (e.g., Beatty & Lucero-Wagoner, 2000; Binda & Murray, 2015; Einhäuser, 2010; Granholm & Steinhauer, 2004; Privitera et al., 2010), pupil dilation tends to adapt to the brightness of an attended stimulus. This is especially useful for binocular rivalry paradigms, enabling the presentation of differently luminous stimuli to each eye. Since the pupillary response is typically symmetrical, both pupils tend to respond corresponding to the luminance of the currently attended stimulus. In practice, with one brighter and one darker stimulus present, the viewer's pupil tends to constrict when the brighter stimulus dominates perception and vice versa (Binda et al., 2013; Mathot et al., 2014; Mathôt et al., 2013; Naber et al., 2011).

While pupil dilation based on relative luminance has been shown to be a good predictor of spatial, covert attention (Binda et al., 2013; Mathôt et al., 2013), its usefulness in ambiguous stimuli is not as clear. For monocular rivalry, where two images are superimposed as one and simultaneously presented to both eyes, relative difference in luminance might distinguish between percepts, and could potentially be used to classify perceptual reversals, as shown by Naber et al. (2011) with stationary, overlaying red and green gratings. However, monocular rivalry likely has a different underlying mechanism than binocular rivalry or ambiguous figures (Georgeson & Phillips, 1980).

In addition to the pupillary light reflex, pupil dilation is influenced by central neural circuits involving neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine, acetylcholine, dopamine, and serotonin, reflecting the effects of arousal, attention, and cognitive effort on autonomic function (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005; Beerendonk et al., 2024 Beerendonk et al., , 2025;; Koss, 1986; Loewenfeld, 1999; Noudoost & Moore, 2011; Yoshitomi et al., 1985). Building on theoretical frameworks that emphasize the role of arousal (Nakano et al., 2021) and cognitive load (Sato et al., 2020) in the perception of ambiguous stimuli, transient pupil size changes have been investigated as physiological proxies for these cognitive processes. Some evidence for a link between pupil dilation as a proxy for perceptual reversals was presented by Einhäuser et al. (2008), who found that not only pupil diameter increases before the report of perceptual reversals in various visual (and one auditory) bistable stimuli (Necker cube, rotating cylinder, moving plaids, auditory rivalry), but also that the magnitude of this increase is related to the stability of the subsequent percept. Further involving arousal, Nakano et al. (2021) also found a decrease in average pupil diameter between one and five seconds before reported shifts while viewing the motion quartet over a prolonged time.

Importantly, this was only the case for endogenously induced (spontaneous) shifts and not for stimulus-driven shifts, leading the authors to conclude that transient drops in arousal, as indicated by the pupil constriction, are involved in inducing endogenously driven perceptual shifts. To rule out possible influences of active report on pupil dilation (see: Hupé et al., 2008 Hupé et al., , 2009;; Klostermann et al., 2013), Sato et al. (2020) had participants report their experiences after individual trials. They found that participants who were instructed to sustain an initially primed perspective of a Necker cube and experienced no perceptual shift after stimulus onset, had, on-average, larger changes in pupil diameter when seeing the cube from the above-perspective compared to seeing the cube from a below-perspective. However, they found no such effect for a passive viewing condition or in a separate experiment where the cubes were rotated to be perceived sideways. The authors presume that differences in pupil dilation within the experiments reflect perceptual content (i.e., seeing the cube from above vs from below) rather than perceptual reversals.

Other (Gaze Position, Microsaccades, Oscillations and Blinks)

As part of an overarching debate regarding the cognitive and retinal responsibility for perceptual selection and perceptual reversals (van Dam & van Ee, 2005), eye movements and blinks have been identified as possible drivers for perceptual reversals (Glen, 1940; Leopold & Logothetis, 1999; Necker, 1832; Pheiffer et al., 1956). In the context of determining the causal role of eye position on perception, a relationship between eye movement and perceptual reversals has for example been pointed out by Glen (1940) and Pheiffer et al. (1956). Later, Ellis and Stark (1978) found a difference between fixation duration around the time of perceptual reversals and durations at other time points.

Additionally, Einhäuser et al. (2004) found that participants viewing a Necker cube that could be perceived pointing left or right tended to fixate at one extreme at the horizontal plane around the time of the perceptual shift. Based on the polarity of the extreme, it was possible to find correlates of the current percept. Also based on eye-position, but using binocular rivalry, Kingir et al. (2025a) applied a paradigm previously established in monkeys (Hesse & Tsao, 2020) that allowed them to decode the momentary percept based on eye-position. By varying the position of a fixation cross between the two images presented separately to each eye, they could infer the momentary percept with very high accuracies up to 98%. Using a structure-from-motion stimulus (see Figure 1e), Ding et al.

(2021) found differences in eye-position as well as micro-saccade directionality between clockwise vs counterclockwise perception of the stimulus rotation.

Microsaccades are small shifts in the gaze during prolonged fixation and are thought to, among other roles, prevent visual fading of images (Martinez-Conde et al., 2006). There are several findings regarding microsaccade rate and directionality in multistable perception. Sabrin and Kertesz (1980) found that the rate of microsaccades was approximately 50% higher during the bistable condition compared to a free-viewing control condition. The directionality of these microsaccades also seems to vary and possibly contains information about the viewer's percept. Laubrock et al. (2008) found that shifts in ambiguous motion percepts are correlated with a preceding dip in microsaccade rate.

Extending the correlational findings, Romulus (2021) attempted to classify horizontal and vertical percepts in the short presentations of the motion quartet. They achieved accuracies ranging from barely above chance level up to 70% in a trial-based setup.

Also on slower timescales, there seems to be relevant information about the percept in eye-tracking data. Choi et al. (2020) found that for the Necker cube, a spinning ring of dots (racetrack) and a rotating cylinder, participants gaze positions follow a principal axis unique to the stimulus, and that changes in directional components of eye movement can reliably predict the upcoming percept using gaze position data of the 5 seconds preceding a reported reversal. While the classification accuracy is impressive (around 80-90%), it is not entirely clear how useful this approach is for the prediction of momentary percepts, as it first requires the classification of the time point of a perceptual reversal to then extract the relevant 5 seconds based on the basis of which the percept is predicted.

Further, some evidence suggests that blink rate is modulated around perceptual reversals (Brych et al., 2021; van Dam & van Ee, 2005). But given the low frequency of blink rates, prediction for individual time points in continuous viewing paradigms might be difficult.

Conclusions

The success of classifying perceptual states and perceptual reversals based on eye-tracking data depends strongly on the experimental paradigm and the concrete measure that is taken. Binocular rivalry allows for very accurate prediction using OKN and luminance-based pupil dilation, given any setup that provokes differential behavior between the two stimuli (Frässle et al., 2014; Fujiwara et al., 2017; Leopold et al., 1995; Naber et al., 2011). While OKN is also an effective measure for the moving plaid stimulus (Canales-Johnson et al., 2023), perception of other ambiguous stimuli is more difficult to classify. Many approaches show promising correlates between perception and eye-behavior such as arousal-related pupil-dilation, microsaccades, eye-movement or blinks, but the signal strength of these effects (and thus likely individual effect sizes) has been found to be small. Still, while these signals might not be strong enough to serve as accurate predictors for perception on their own for real-time prediction, they could possibly be combined for a more elaborate prediction model. Since all of these measures can be easily extracted from a single eye-tracking session, combining features based on pupil dilation, microsaccades and other eye-movement data could result in higher classification accuracies.

EEG and MEG (M/EEG)

Electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) are non-invasive methods that can follow neural dynamics at the sub-second timescale. EEG records voltage changes produced by synchronous post-synaptic activity and routinely resolves events with millisecond precision, albeit at the cost of reduced spatial detail. MEG captures the accompanying magnetic fields, which are less distorted by tissue conductivity (though they can be distorted by source-to-sensor distance; Srinivasan et al., 2007) while retaining comparable temporal acuity. The two main approaches to decode perceptual alternations using EEG and MEG can be summarized as methods based on event-related potentials (ERPs) and methods based on steady-state visually evoked potentials (SSVEPs). ERPs consist of positive or negative voltage deflections that occur in the EEG trace following discrete events such as the onset of a visual stimulus, a motor response, or a spontaneous perceptual reversal in an ambiguous figure. SSVEPs, on the other hand, are oscillatory responses that are phase-locked to periodic visual stimulation: when a display flickers at a constant frequency, that frequency (and its harmonics) is reflected in the visual cortex, yielding a steady-state signal that can be used to "tag" and continuously track the dominance of competing percepts without requiring overt reports.

ERPs

As signatures of cognitive processes, ERPs have potential as predictors for certain mental states or task-related intentions. Especially in the context of brain-computer interfaces, they can be leveraged to decode user intent in real time (Abiri et al., 2019; Wolpaw, 2013). Typically, reliable decoding leverages specific pre-extracted ERP features such as mean amplitudes within time-windows or spatial topographies, fed into machine learning models (e.g., support-vector machines, SVMs) trained for each individual and task. Although in many no-report studies, M/EEG signals are treated as the dependent variable, there are some indications that classification of percepts based on them might be feasible. In the case of ERPs, detection of percept related signatures usually revolves around events such as perceptual reversals or stimulus onsets. Due to the weak neural signatures of perceptual reversals themselves, it often takes trial-averaging to detect them in the first place. Further, as a measure with relatively low spatial resolution, it is especially difficult to disentangle signals resulting from report-related processes and percept related processes when training a classifier. Given this, the possible application of M/EEG to provide features for no-report classification might be limited. Still, some relatively recent studies successfully developed classification pipelines to decode perceptual content based on M/EEG.

In the case of binocular rivalry, stimulus content can be leveraged to decode perception. Especially the perception of faces is associated with distinct ERPs such as the N170 and the P2 (Rossion & Caharel, 2011). In an MEG study, Sandberg et al. (2013) leveraged the distinct signatures related to face perception by setting up a trial-by-trial binocular rivalry paradigm with faces presented to one eye and gratings presented to the other eye. A support-vector machine was trained to classify each time-point within a trial for classification of face vs grating perception (both within-subject and between-subject).

For time points that matched the timing of face-related ERPs, across trial-averaged prediction accuracy was as high as 90%. The highest prediction accuracy was achieved by combining readings from all sensors, with occipital readings likely contributing the most.

These results indicate that given the right features, a classification algorithm for entire trials could possibly result in high-accuracy prediction of binocular rivalry percepts using EEG or MEG. For the bistable motion quartet, Kaneoke et al. (2009) found comparatively greater overall MEG amplitude for horizontal percepts compared to vertical ones 160 ms after each frame onset. With each frame onset treated as a trial for the analysis, they were able to perform their analysis on a continuous presentation of the motion quartet, also providing the possibility of EEG classification in continuous-viewing no-report paradigms.

Frequency-based signatures

Multiple research groups have found frequency based M/EEG signatures that distinguish between bistable percepts. In the case of the bistable motion quartet, Başar-Eroglu et al. (1996) found an overall increased gamma-band power during perceptual reversals, indicating a possible predictive power in the detection of perceptual reversals.

Further, several studies found increased inter-hemispheric connectivity in the gamma band related to the direction of perceived motion (Alipour et al., 2016; Rose & Büchel, 2005; Strüber et al., 2013; Wälti et al., 2020). Importantly, as a measure that can be continuously assessed throughout stimulus presentation, it could be a suitable candidate for continuous viewing no-report paradigms.

In two separate experiments, Hramov et al. (2017) used EEG data and artificial neural networks to determine perceived Necker cube orientations, once using active report data and once using trial-based, delayed report data. For both setups they achieved around 85% classification accuracy within participants. Hramov et al. (2019) Further evidence for differential activity exists in the case of the motion quartet, with overall decreased M/EEG frequencies but specifically decreased alpha band activity before perceptual reversals (e.g., T. J. Müller et al., 1999; Strüber & Herrmann, 2002).

For other stimuli, such as the Necker cube or moving plaids, significant signatures for perceptual reversals or perceptual stability have been found (e.g., Britz et al., 2008; Canales-Johnson et al., 2023; Elbert et al., 1985; Johnston & Chesney, 1974; Piantoni et al., 2017). In one example, Hipp et al. (2011) were able to make above chance trial-per-trial predictions using EEG data for a bistable stimulus that could be perceived either as a ball bouncing off a surface or passing through it. The authors computed large-scale beta-band synchronization in terms of interactions across space, time and frequencies and found it increased between frontal, parietal and extrastriate areas whenever subjects perceived the stimulus bouncing compared to passing.

SSVEPs

Steady-state visually evoked potentials (SSVEPs), or so-called frequency tagging methods, are an oft-used tool in M/EEG research. By exciting the retina through visual stimulation with a certain frequency (e.g., a flickering target stimulus during an experiment), corresponding cortical activities observed in M/EEG reflect this induced frequency and/or multiples of it (Norcia et al., 2015; Regan, 1977). Importantly, when multiple stimuli flicker at distinct frequencies, one frequency often becomes predominant in M/EEG recordings, allowing researchers to determine which stimulus is being attended purely from the neural signal (Andersen et al., 2011; Morgan et al., 1996; M. M. Müller et al., 1998).

Making use of this effect, Parkkonen et al. (2008) frequency-tagged the vase and face regions of the vase-face stimulus to separate neural activity related to face perception from neural activity related to vase perception. Without checking for predictive accuracy, they found clearly separable MEG data streams based on the power at the tagging frequencies, demonstrating the possibility of using this effect to classify bistable stimuli.

Similarly, Chholak et al. (2020) modulated the brightness of the two ambiguous front/back faces of the Necker cube at different frequencies (6.67Hz and 5.57Hz) and noted that the average second harmonic of the modulation frequencies were different for deliberate interpretations of the Necker cube. They then used this data to classify interpretations of the Necker cube during free-viewing.

Also in case of SSVEPs, the prediction of transient percepts seems to benefit from the separation of the stimulation across eyes as achieved in binocular rivalry. Several studies found SSVEP frequency powers to be correlated with self-report (Alpers et al., 2005; Brown & Norcia, 1997; Jamison et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2006). Laukkonen et al.

(2024) found a near-perfect correlation between self-reported percept changes and estimates based on frequency tagging of the different stimuli. They however found no significant correlation between average estimated reversal-rates from a no-report condition and the average reversal-rates in the self report condition. While the authors explain this with attentional differences between the two conditions, the missing correlation does not support the validity of frequency tagging as a method to infer perceptual states in no-report paradigms. Research by Davidson et al. (2020) supports the notion of Laukkonen et al. (2024) that SSVEPs might track attention rather than conscious percepts, further questioning the feasibility of frequency tagging as a tool for no-report paradigms for multistable perception.

Conclusions

As of now, the usability of M/EEG to reliably classify transient conscious percepts in multistable perception paradigms is somewhat limited. If viewing paradigms are split into single trials, ERPs can be decoded to classify percepts relatively reliably. SSVEPs on the other hand seem to be more suitable to classify perceptual stability and perceptual reversals in prolonged viewing paradigms. Both approaches are highly stimulus dependent, with binocular rivalry and stimuli involving the viewing of human faces being the easiest to decode (Parkkonen et al., 2008). Especially the research on trial-based EEG measures related to perceptual reversals and perceptual stability is progressing, with signatures being recovered for many setups. All measures, however, suffer from the noise pervasive in M/EEG recordings (Nunez et al., 2016; Whitham et al., 2007). With a combination of other methodologies such as eye tracking, promising classification setups are imaginable.

Decoding perceptual states in continuous viewing paradigms seems difficult, although some techniques such as the temporal generalization method could turn out to be useful in this regard (King & Dehaene, 2014). Another way of making use of continuous viewing paradigms could be via the incorporation of other markers for perceptual reversals (e.g., pupil dilation) along with M/EEG. and V4v for red and green gratings (Haynes & Rees, 2006; Lee et al., 2004; Polonsky et al., 2000; Tong & Engel, 2001). Further, activity related to perceptual alternations can be found in frontoparietal regions (Frässle et al., 2014; Lumer et al., 1998), although as mentioned earlier, these frontal activations seem mostly related to active report of rivalry.

Omitting report seems to reduce frontal involvement (Frässle et al., 2014). In another binocular rivalry experiment, Wilbertz et al. (2017) decoded perceptual states by using red and blue, orthogonally oriented gratings using fMRI. By using multi-voxel-pattern analysis, they achieved continuous decoding accuracy of ≈ 60%. The relatively low classification accuracy might however be constrained by their choice of stimulus. Bertolino et al. (2014) achieved much higher classification accuracy (up to 78%) by using faces and houses as stimuli.

Also for ambiguous stimuli, some neural correlates of bistable percepts have been reported. In the case of the Necker cube and the vase-face illusion, (Wang et al., 2013) showed differential activity in posterior visual areas as well as in higher order frontal regions for continuous and intermittent bistable perception. For the motion quartet, Sterzer and Kleinschmidt (2007) found similar activations for exogenously as well as endogenously driven reversals in the apparent motion quartet. In the case of binocular rivalry, Haynes and Rees (2005) set up a study using orthogonal red and blue gratings. By employing Linear Discriminant Analysis, they were able to achieve accuracies up to 85% in The voxels that contributed most strongly to the decoder clustered in occipital cortex, again highlighting early visual areas as important ROIs for classifying perceptual reversals.

Conclusions

With its high spatial resolution, fMRI has the capability of highlighting differentiating activity between small regions of the brain. Many regions have been identified as showing differential activity when observing bistable stimuli (Devia et al., 2022). Regions identified in studies based on condition differences match the predictive areas from classification studies closely. Importantly, compared to EEG measured paradigms, successful fMRI classification studies were able to reconstruct perceptual states from continuous viewing setups, without having to resort to individual trials.

Every fMRI study that has successfully decoded bistable percepts has relied on multivariate approaches-most often support-vector machines or neural-network architectures. Since differential activity might be encoded in the covariance structure that spreads across early visual cortex, higher extrastriate areas, and perhaps frontal and parietal nodes, classification algorithms should be capable of capturing the inter-voxel interactions that underlie the perceptual reversal (Brouwer & van Ee, 2007).

Validity of the classification

Given the domain shift between the training data of the model and the data to be classified in the secondary analysis, special care has to be taken during that process.

Importantly, prediction models should not pick up on correlates tied to report-related processes (e.g., response preparation). These processes are specific to the task demands of active report, and are uninformative or absent during passive viewing. An example of such a feature may be arousal-related pupil dilation. Previous studies have shown that arousal-related pupil dilation might be largely tied to motor readiness or response execution (Hupé et al., 2008 (Hupé et al., , 2009) ) and is therefore unlikely to be a useful feature for tracking perceptual transitions in no-report sessions. Another example are features related to attention. Although some authors have argued against a fundamental distinction between attention and awareness (e.g., Cohen & Dennett, 2011), the majority view in the field is that they are separate processes (e.g., by Koch & Tsuchiya, 2007; Lamme, 2003; van Boxtel et al., 2010). To illustrate, in global workspace theory (e.g., Dehaene et al., 1998), attention is a prerequisite for conscious access but does not guarantee it. Other studies have shown that attention can be triggered by unconscious stimuli. Thus certain physiological markers might indicate necessary preconditions for perceptual awareness (or reversals; see Brascamp et al., 2015), whereas others are more directly linked to the act of report (such as arousal-related pupil dilation, see Hupé et al., 2009).

1: Use measures that are directly linked to stimulus parameters

SSVEPs, OKN, and luminance-dependent pupil dilation are directly linked to specific stimulus parameters (frequency, movement direction, and luminance) and are likely consequences of conscious perception (although they risk being indicators for attention; Davidson et al., 2020; Mathôt et al., 2013). Crucially, the directness of the stimulus-response relationship can be assessed. For example, OKN and SSVEP responses track stimulus content. In the case of OKN, the gaze trajectory is a direct reflection of the direction of stimuli motion (e.g., gratings) and the recovered frequencies of SSVEPs are tied to the inducing frequency (or its harmonics) at which a stimulus is presented.

Another example of content-specific signals can be found in the experimental design of Hesse and Tsao (2020) who trained monkeys to fixate on a fixation cross that varied in position depending on the perceived image in a binocular rivalry setup. Based on the point at which the monkeys fixated, the authors were able to infer the currently dominant percept. Given that this is a fairly automatic phenomenon, their setup provides an interesting middle ground that could reduce explicit report related processes. Similar setups could possibly be devised for other stimuli.

2: Supervised and unsupervised machine learning

A pillar of classical validation efforts is discriminant validity (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). To ensure a valid classification on no-report data, the utilized feature sets of report and no-report data should not differ. This can be tested and quantified to some degree as proposed in the machine learning community (Ben-David et al., 2009). In practice, developing a discriminator to classify data from these feature sets as belonging to "report" and "no-report" categories should result in low accuracy (as the feature is present in both conditions). A high classification accuracy here can indicate a significant portion of report-related signals in the feature set of the report data (as the feature is only present in the report condition). If this is the case, there is a higher chance of a no-report classification model relying on these purely report related signals. However, as these signals are missing in the feature set of no-report data, the no-report classification for a primary analysis is less likely to succeed. Building further on this idea, a domain adaptation could possibly achieved by extracting domain-invariant features (features that are present in both the active report training data and the no-report training data) in an architecture as described by Ganin et al. (2016). A related approach trains a content decoder on data from an independent no-report task and tests it on report data. Gnodde et al. (2026) trained a classifier to decode stimulus location from EEG recorded while participants passively viewed unmasked, clearly visible gratings, and then applied it to physically identical masked trials sorted by subjective visibility ratings. Because the no-report stimuli were unmasked, stimulus labels could serve as a proxy for percept labels, sidestepping uncertainty about what was perceived during training. A more ambitious extension would train the decoder in an unsupervised manner on no-report data in which the percept itself is uncertain (e.g., ambiguous or masked stimuli) and then validate it against report labels. This is likely a harder problem, since uncertainty would concern not only the timing but also the identity of the percept, which decreases the chances of finding a valid decision boundary. Still, if successfully validated on report data, this method could provide one of the most valid approaches to classify perceptual content in no-report paradigms.

3: Convergent validity

As with other validation efforts regarding inaccessible constructs, a type of convergent validity can strengthen a no-report measure. Observing similar classifications using two separate measures can considerably improve validity in the primary analysis (e.g., Frässle et al., 2014; Naber et al., 2011). When two mechanistically distinct proxies, as for instance pupil size and optokinetic nystagmus, independently track the same perceptual alternations, their agreement is difficult to attribute to any single measurement channel and is more parsimoniously explained by a common perceptual cause (e.g., Frässle et al., 2014; Naber et al., 2011). The strength of this inference scales with the independence of the two measures, for example, both measures may share the same confounds. If both are modulated by fluctuations in arousal (or attention), their agreement could reflect a shared driver rather than the percept itself (Overgaard & Fazekas, 2016).

4: Other validation checks

As indicated earlier, classifying disambiguated replay of the stimuli can help to validate a classifier, for example replaying the same image to both eyes in binocular rivalry studies to assure the signatures of unambiguous percepts match the ones of ambiguous ones (e.g., Frässle et al., 2014). This can also be done in the form of so-called catch trials which can be displayed intermittently in between ambiguous trials (e.g., Kingir et al., 2025b). Validating a classification through disambiguated replay has the advantage of excluding report-related processes as a predictive feature for the classification, however this approach works better for some stimuli than for others. While the disambiguated display for binocular rivalry can be done easily by displaying the same image for both eyes (e.g., Frässle et al., 2014), for other stimuli (such as the motion quartet and structure-from-motion stimuli) a disambiguated version might be too different from the ambiguous version, questioning the comparability between the two. Some evidence for this issue comes from studies showing that pairs of one disambiguated and one ambiguous structure-from-motion stimuli differ in terms of their grouping/ coupling dynamics compared to two ambiguous ones (e.g., Grossmann & Dobbins, 2003; Pastukhov et al., 2018).

One common method to check for an invalid primary analysis is to compare dominance durations between report and classified no-report time series (e.g., Canales-Johnson et al., 2023; Frässle et al., 2014; Naber et al., 2011). The distribution of dominance durations should be similar to the typical gamma-distribution of percept durations (Blake et al., 1971; Borsellino et al., 1972; Fox & Herrmann, 1967; Levelt, 1965).

However, this approach should be treated more as a safeguard rather than a validation of a classification. Dominance durations of report and no-report conditions can differ. For example, short dominance durations might be limited during active report (van Ee, 2009) and the act of reporting itself likely changes dominance durations (Fahle et al., 2011; Frässle et al., 2014; Naber et al., 2011).

Secondary analysis Expected power given classification accuracy

Given a validated classifier of perceptual content based on a primary dataset, time series of a secondary independent dataset can be divided into classes. These can then be used for a secondary analysis with a new dependent measure such as EEG or fMRI.

Although not comprehensive, the studies listed above and in Tables 1 and 2 give some indication of obtainable classification accuracies depending on the stimulus and physiological measure taken by reporting the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). This value roughly corresponds to a classification accuracy, assuming a classification based on two normal distributions with equal variance and a classification threshold set to the optimal point. The AUC value should therefore be treated as an optimistic value. Due to the risk of information leakage and the difficulties of validating the classification on no-report data, an important consideration of secondary analyses is power, the ability to find true positives (and its complement β or Type II error).

With a number of participants, trials and an expected classification accuracy, an indication of the probability of detecting a true effect can be made. An example of a violation of assumption 3 could be a classifier that is most unreliable during periods of perceptual reversals and an secondary effect that gets less strong for these moments. While this actually increases power, the reverse is also true: any classifier that is most accurate around perceptual reversals (e.g., because it classifies transition-related signals), will lead to a setup with lower power if the size of the secondary effect is strongest in moments of perceptual stability.

Extension for continuous cases

In continuous-viewing paradigms trials are not independent (assumption 4), and therefore, an estimate of the effective sample size can serve as a substitute for the trial count of trial based parameters. Two distinct sources of dependence act on the trial-level terms of λ, and they need not have the same effective count. First, the perceptual sequence is piecewise constant: samples within a single dominance period share the same percept, so a decoder that assigns one label per period essentially makes one classification decision per period. The classification-attenuation term is therefore governed by the number of dominance periods, m class ≈ number of periods, which an analysis of dominance-period dynamics in active report trials can estimate (with a slight optimistic bias, since active report speeds up reversals; Fahle et al., 2011; Frässle et al., 2014; Naber et al., 2011).

Second, the measurement noise has its own autocorrelation time t noise , independent of the perceptual dynamics. For AR(1)-like noise, m noise ≈ T /(2t noise ), whereas m class ≈ T / tdom.

Fast-decorrelating measures (eye or M/EEG features) thus retain many effective samples per dominance period. For slowly varying signals (e.g., BOLD), m noise shrinks, but it approaches the period count only when t noise ≈ tdom /2, and falls below it when the noise is slower still.

Planning a study

To plan a study using the tools described above, estimates for the expected standardized per-trial effect size, between subject variability, and classification accuracy are needed. For the extension to the continuous case, an estimate for the average dominance duration and the autocorrelation of the measurement noise are necessary. Values for the classification accuracy a, average dominance duration tdom , residual-noise correlation time t noise (residuals within dominance periods), and within subject variability σ can be estimated from an active report pilot study. Due to the domain shift, classification accuracy derived from an active report pilot should be seen as an optimistic upper bound. The trial standardized effect size d of the secondary measure and the between subject variability τ are more difficult to estimate. Since τ is a feature of the effect size distribution, and any estimate on a low n pilot would be very imprecise, it is best to use a conservative estimate.

General discussion

In this paper, we identified two-stages in the study of perceptual rivalry in no-report paradigms: a primary analysis to classify distinct perceptual phases and a secondary analysis to establish an effect independent of report-related processes. We reviewed the current research on (possible) physiological predictors of perceptual (rivalrous) phases and discussed the usefulness and validity of certain measures and features. Further, we provided a method to determine statistical power of the secondary analysis assuming an established classification accuracy in the primary analysis and listed possible pitfalls to avoid in future no-report studies involving bistable perception.

Generally, we find that the effectiveness of physiological measures as predictors for bistable percepts varies markedly: some -most notably those harnessing optokinetic nystagmus (OKN) during binocular rivalry -achieve near-perfect correspondence with self-report and may even outperform report when lapses in responses are considered.

Others however, barely exceed chance level and are therefore less suitable, especially when statistical power cannot be improved by increasing the number of participant or trials.

Binocular rivalry seems to provide the richest diagnostic signals, because the conflicting monocular inputs elicit robust reflexive responses. Regarding this, the most successful analysis pipelines embed stimulus features that drive involuntary oculomotor or autonomic reactions, such as motion-induced OKN or luminance-dependent pupil dilation. Static ambiguous figures, by contrast, offer few stimulus-locked physiological features and thus yield the poorest accuracy, whereas dynamic ambiguous stimuli, in particular the moving plaid, currently afford reliable eye-tracking-based classification, especially taking into account that accuracy has shown to improve further when neural measures (e.g., multivoxel fMRI patterns) are added to the feature set (Wilbertz et al., 2018).

Classifier performance is shaped by an interaction between stimulus class and measurement modality. For example, for EEG/ERP-based setups, the classification of perceptual reversals is difficult. This is mostly due to the low signal to noise ratio and the short duration of event-related components related to reversals. Nonetheless, in trial-based setups, EEG reveals clear correlates to perceptual states (Kornmeier & Bach, 2012), which possibly could be leveraged for more elaborate classifiers in continuous viewing paradigms in the future when analysis methods advance. EEG setups capturing SSVEPs have shown to be promising to track perceptual alternations during binocular rivalry (Alpers et al., 2005; Brown & Norcia, 1997; Jamison et al., 2015; Laukkonen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2006), and possibly for ambiguous stimuli as well (Chholak et al., 2020; Parkkonen et al., 2008). At present, it remains somewhat unclear however, if SSVEPs track perceptual content, attention or their interaction, in some situations (Davidson et al., 2020). fMRI based classification has also been relatively successful for some stimuli, particularly in binocular rivalry (e.g., face and house stimuli) and for dynamic ambiguous stimuli such as the moving plaid. The successful use of no-report paradigms with several stimuli (e.g., Canales-Johnson et al., 2023; Frässle et al., 2014), and the fact that classification is becoming progressively accurate, showcase the potential for future applications in this field.

Overall, matching specific measurement approaches to stimulus characteristics, and combining several measurement approaches, offers great opportunity for tracking perceptual states without having to rely on overt reports.

Based on the power analysis tool we proposed, we can note a few main aspects.

Firstly, misclassification shrinks the usable effect by a factor of (2a -1), so a drop in accuracy a from 0.9 to 0.7 roughly halves it. Secondly, between-subject variability limits the power that can be achieved through more trials or better classifiers. Finally, for continuous viewing, the number of expected dominance durations can be used as a accurate shorthand for the effective trial counts (for slower measures like BOLD, it is somewhat conservative). Generally, we recommend conservative estimates as inputs for the tool to rule out underpowered designs.

We hope that the practical recommendations presented here help make multistable perception paradigms even more powerful tools for studying the neural correlates and mechanisms of conscious perception, and ultimately for testing competing theories of consciousness.

Assumptions

The expressions above rely on the following assumptions.

1. Balanced classes. Each class occurs with (approximately) equal probability, P (y i = 0) ≈ P (y i = 1) = 0.5; class imbalance is neglected.

2. Trial-level responses.

Measurements are approximately normal with equal variance across classes, x | y = k ∼ N (µ k , σ 2 ).

3. Constant per-trial variance.

Both classes share the trial-level variance σ 2 , independent of ∆.

4. Random classification errors.

Inferred labels agree with the truth with probability a and are flipped with probability 1 -a, independently of the measurement x.

5. Trial dependence. Trials are treated as independent in ( 6)-( 7); serial dependence is accommodated in (8) through the effective counts m class and m noise.

6. Between-subject effects. Subject-specific effects are ∆ j ∼ N (∆, τ² ); the common-effect case (τ = 0) is the special case giving the optimistic upper bound (6).

7. Group-level analysis. For each subject, Dj is computed from (approximately) the same number of trials m, and the group t-statistic is referred to a noncentral t-distribution with df = n -1.
