REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Recognition memory is often studied as an individual process, yet many recognition decisions are made collaboratively. Collaborative recognition requires individuals to communicate and combine separate, imperfect memory evidence to reach a joint decision. Here, we develop a computational model of collaborative recognition memory (CO-REM) grounded in the well-established Retrieving Effectively from Memory (REM) model of individual recognition. We compare collaborative decision rules based on categorical old/new decisions, confidence judgments, and the direct integration of memory evidence. The simulations show that groups can outperform even their best individual member, but the benefits depend on differences in individual recognition ability and how memory evidence is communicated and combined.

Confidence sharing can produce collective gains or losses depending on how individuals map their memory evidence onto confidence judgments. The model also predicts greater collaborative benefits for recognizing previously studied items (targets) than for correctly rejecting new items (lures), consistent with some previous empirical findings. Although the predicted performance differences are small, CO-REM provides a framework for identifying the conditions under which collaboration improves recognition and for guiding future empirical investigations.

A REM Model of Collaborative Recognition Memory

Memory is often treated as an individual cognitive process, but many important memory judgments are made collaboratively. Witnesses discuss what they saw. Medical teams decide whether a current case resembles prior cases. Students answer questions together. Analysts judge whether a new event matches a known pattern. In these cases, and others, it is important to know when and whether collaboration improves memory or distorts it. Conventional wisdom tells us that "two heads are better than one." Empirically, this wisdom sometimes holds. For instance, Mellers et al. (2014) found that placing expert forecasters in groups to share information and discuss rationales improved the accuracy of geopolitical forecasts. Yet collaboration can also impair judgment, as in groupthink, when cohesive groups prioritize consensus, harmony, or loyalty over critical evaluation of alternatives (Janis, 1982).

These different outcomes suggest that group performance depends on the social and cognitive mechanisms by which individuals collaborate to form a collective judgment (Kameda et al., 2022; Kerr & Tindale, 2004; Kozlowski & Ilgen, 2006). Prior work has emphasized several such mechanisms, including how groups sample shared versus uniquely held information (Lu et al., 2012; Stasser & Abele, 2020; Stasser & Titus, 1985), how cohesion, goals, and norms shape members' motivation and attention to task-relevant information (Evans & Dion, 1991; Mullen & Copper, 1994; Podsakoff et al., 1997), and how aggregation rules combine individual judgments into a collective response (Hastie & Kameda, 2005; Sorkin et al., 1998). Altogether, these results reveal that collaboration produces gains or losses in performance not simply because multiple people are involved, but because interaction changes what information is expressed, how it is evaluated, and how it is integrated into a collective judgment.

Collaborative memory-when people form and retrieve memories in the company of others (Rajaram, 2024; Rajaram & Pereira-Pasarin, 2010)-adds an important complication to this general picture. During collaborative memory, particularly at retrieval, individuals often bring to the group not an explicit fact or stable judgment, but an internal memory signal or information derived from it. This information may be the recalled item itself, partial recollection, familiarity, uncertainty, or confidence that an event occurred (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968; Nelson & Shiffrin, 2013; Kahana & Wagner, 2024). During collaboration, these internal states must be translated into something shareable. Group performance, thus, depends not only on how individual responses are aggregated, but also on which aspects of memory evidence are expressed, how diagnostic those expressions are, and how they are integrated with the evidence supplied by others (Rajaram, 2024; Rajaram & Pereira-Pasarin, 2010). This effect of how memory evidence is expressed and integrated has been primarily studied in collaborative free recall. Here, individuals interact in groups of two or more to recall as many previously studied items as possible. Although conventional wisdom would suggest that that groups remember more, surprisingly, groups recall fewer nonredundant items than nominal groups formed by pooling the separate recall of the same number of individuals (Basden et al., 1997; Gates et al., 2022; Harris et al., 2008; Marion & Thorley, 2016; Rajaram & Pereira-Pasarin, 2010; Weldon & Bellinger, 1997). This collaborative inhibition effect is commonly attributed to retrieval disruption: during free recall, individuals rely on their own organization of the studied material, but partners' responses interrupt or redirect that search (Basden et al., 1997; Weldon & Bellinger, 1997). This idea parallels what is referred to as the part-list cuing effect in free recall (Slamecka, 1968; Raaijmakers & Shiffrin, 1980 , 1981; Andersson et al., 2006; Basden et al., 1997 Basden et al., , 2000)). Although retrieval cues often improve memory (Tulving, 1974), part-list cues can have the opposite effect: presenting a random subset of studied items as cues can reduce recall of the remaining non-cued items (Slamecka, 1968; Nickerson, 1984). By analogy, in collaborative recall, one group member's output may act as externally imposed cues that disrupt another member's preferred retrieval strategy, reducing access to items that might have been recalled when working alone. Recent computational models have formalized this idea of collaborative inhibition through different specific implementations, but they share a common explanation that collaboration can change the trajectory of individual memory search (Angne et al., 2024; Mannering et al., 2025).

Collaborative recognition, the topic of this paper, raises a related but distinct problem. In a typical collaborative recognition task, individuals study a common set of items and are later presented with test probes that must be jointly classified as "old" or "new" (Clark et al., 2000 (Clark et al., , 2006;; Hinsz, 1990; Rajaram & Pereira-Pasarin, 2007; Vollrath et al., 1989). Unlike free recall, the candidate item is supplied by the test itself. Collaborators therefore evaluate the same external retrieval cue, reducing the chance that one person's response will disrupt another person's memory search. Collaborative recognition has not been studied very often, but the results suggest that collaboration facilitates rather than inhibits performance (Clark et al., 2000; Hinsz, 1990; Rajaram & Pereira-Pasarin, 2007; Vollrath et al., 1989). Importantly, this advantage does not appear to be reducible to simple vote counting over independent old/new decisions. Clark et al. (2000) found that, for three-person groups, collaborative recognition exceeded what could be achieved by majority processes alone, suggesting that discussion contributed information beyond the separate votes of individual members. Rajaram and Pereira-Pasarin (2007) also found that discussion improved later individual recognition even when participants did not produce a single consensus response, indicating that collaborative benefits can arise from the exchange of memory-relevant information rather than from the final response rule alone. At the same time, the effects of collaboration may not be uniform across components of recognition. Clark et al. (2000) found stronger facilitation for targets than for distractors, suggesting that collaboration may improve hit rates more reliably than it reduces false alarms.

If collaborative recognition is not simply the aggregation of categorical old/new decisions, then collaborators may be exchanging or using richer information. Work on joint perceptual decision making has addressed an analogous problem by comparing computational models that differ in the information observers share when forming a joint decision about a common perceptual stimulus (e.g., identifying a higher-contrast Gabor patch; Bahrami et al., 2010) (see also Koriat, 2012). Bahrami et al. showed that when collaborators have similar perceptual discriminability, dyads can outperform their better members and approach the performance predicted by models in which richer information is shared. Critically, both their model and empirical results showed that this advantage declines as collaborators' abilities diverge: when one observer was substantially less sensitive than the other, dyads no longer reliably outperformed the better individual, and in many cases performed worse. Their account emphasized confidence as the information used to combine individual judgments. When collaborators have similar abilities, confidence provides a reasonably comparable signal of the strength of their evidence. As their abilities diverge, however, equally strong confidence can give the poorer performer too much influence because confidence is expressed relative to each observer's own internal evidence and uncertainty (see also Koriat, 2012). We draw on this work asking if and how similar dynamics might shape collaborative recognition memory.

To investigate collaborative recognition, we use the Retrieving Effectively from Memory (REM) model (Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997). REM is a global-matching model of recognition memory. It represents recognition evidence as a graded-likelihood signal: odds a test item is old vs new. Although recognition decisions can involve both familiarity and recall (e.g. Yonelinas, 2002), the decisions based on these are often highly correlated making a familiarity-based model sufficient for the present purposes (e.g. Dunn, 2008). We can then specify different collaborative rules in which individuals share only categorical old/new decisions, confidence-like transformations of their evidence, or the underlying memory evidence itself.

Our research focused on three linked questions. First, under what conditions does collaborative recognition exceed or fall below the best individual? Second, motivated by the findings from Clark et al. (2000), under what conditions can collaboration benefit targets more than lures and why? Finally, do the same boundary conditions hold in larger groups? We address this by defining the evidence available to each collaborative rule and comparing its predictions as group members' encoding quality varies. A simulation-first approach helps because collaborative recognition experiments can make the effects of different information-sharing rules hard to isolate. For example, when collaborators agree on a recognition decision before discussing or sharing information, the group is unlikely to change it. Moreover, different instructions and communication procedures may induce different forms of information sharing.

Rather than examining each combination empirically, the simulations allow us to identify the conditions under which collaborative gains or losses should occur and thereby guide the design of subsequent experiments.

Collaborative Retrieving Effectively from Memory (CO-REM)

Our modeling framework has two layers. The first layer starts with the well-established global-matching model of recognition memory, REM (Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997). REM is useful for the present purposes because it provides a mechanistic account of recognition decisions in which memory probes are compared against noisy, incomplete item traces and converted into likelihood-ratio evidence for an old-versus-new response (the odds). Each individual, alone or in a group, is assumed to start by obtaining an odds value and a decision based on it. The second layer consists of collaboration rules that let group members reach a joint decision based on what they can communicate to others and how they combine that information. We develop each in turn next. We refer to the full model as Collaborative REM, or CO-REM. We first describe the individual REM process and then turn to the collaboration rules.

Individual memory evidence via REM

REM is a global match model of recognition memory (Gillund & Shiffrin, 1984; Clark & Gronlund, 1996; Raaijmakers & Shiffrin, 2002). Like other global-matching models, REM posits recognition to depend on the match between a test probe and the contents of memory. It was designed to produce optimal decisions derived from its few assumptions. On this basis, it provides a likelihood ratio, called odds, of an item being old vs. new. If targets and lures occur equally often, an "Old" decision is made when the odds are greater than 1.0. Note that the decision variable in REM is not raw familiarity or summed similarity as in some global match models (e.g., Hintzman, 1988; Raaijmakers & Shiffrin, 2002), but diagnostic evidence for an old response relative to a new response.

REM as a foundation for CO-REM

REM provides a useful foundation for the present work because it has been used to explain several benchmark findings in recognition memory. These include a variety of mirror effects by which the better recognized class of items has both higher hit rates (probability of an old response to a target) and lower false-alarm rates (probability of an old response to a foil), compared with the class of items less well recognized. The prime example is word-frequency:

Low-frequency words often produce both higher hit rates and lower false-alarm rates than highfrequency words (Glanzer et al., 1993; Glanzer & Adams, 1985; Malmberg et al., 2002; Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997). Other findings REM predicts are strength-based mirror effects and the relative absence of list-strength effects in item recognition, which are explained through differentiation of memory traces (Ensor et al., 2021; Kılıç et al., 2017; Ratcliff et al., 1990; Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997); similarity-based false alarms, which follow from the feature-overlap structure of the model (Criss & McClelland, 2006; Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997), and implicit memory effects in perceptual identification (Schooler et al., 2001). Three assumptions are especially important for REM's success: Recognition is based on diagnostic feature matching, stronger encoding differentiates memory traces rather than merely increasing undifferentiated memory strength, and decisions are based on relative evidence for old vs. new.

In CO-REM, we use L (Equation 7) as the agent's latent internal memory evidence. The collaborative models developed below ask what happens when communication among the group members gives access to different transformations of this variable-such as categorical responses, confidence reports, or latent evidence itself-and allow the members to combine them in different ways to make a collective recognition decision.

Collaborative decision rules

To develop the collaborative decision rules, we consider a dyadic recognition task (Figure 1). We start with two agents and later extend the same logic to three agents. The agents study the same list separately and then receive the same recognition probes. Each agent produces private evidence, L a (t ), and makes an individual old/new decision, D a (t ). If the two decisions agree, that is assumed to be the joint choice. One can imagine situations where extended discussion might lead the agents to reverse an initially agreed decision, but we assume those cases would be quite rare. Thus, the collaboration rules apply only to trials on which the agents' initial decisions do not agree.

Simulation Methods

We provide a brief description of the methods we used to simulate the different CO-REM models in the dyadic recognition task. The full code for the simulations is provided here https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23652729.

We generated recognition evidence with the REM model for 200 studied items. Agent A had encoding quality c A = .70. Agent B varied from .10 to .90, inclusive; this same range was used in both Figure 2 and Figure 3. Each simulated condition used 2,400 balanced test trials across 24 dyad replications, enough to reduce prediction error to be less than the size of the condition symbols in the figures showing the results. We used g = .40, u = .04, and five storage attempts per feature. For the triad analysis, we added Agent C and simulated each c B × c C condition in 16 independent Monte Carlo replications. Each replication contained 2,400 balanced test probes (1,200 OLD and 1,200 NEW), yielding 38,400 test probes per condition.

The primary measure of interest is overall accuracy, reported as the average of hit rate and correct-rejection rate. We refer to this statistic as balanced accuracy. Also reported are the separate predictions for hits and correct rejections. These can be compared to the average performance of the agents making separate decisions (which in the simulations are the initial decisions before collaboration). This comparison would be the same as comparing the other models to the guessing model.

For any of these measures of accuracy, we also compare the group performance to that of the agent who performs best overall (on all trials, measured by the overall average of hit and correct rejection rates). This is reported by the collective benefit ratio, or CBR: dyad or triad accuracy divided by the accuracy of the best individual in that condition. A ratio above 1 indicates a collective benefit; a ratio below 1 indicates performance below the best individual.

Simulation Results

We used CO-REM to investigate collaborative recognition performance and in general how the different types of information people might share about their recognition during collaboration can impact performance. For the purpose of this paper, we focus on five questions.

First, when can a dyad outperform its best member? Second, when does confidence-based collaboration fail? Third, why might collaborative benefits be stronger for targets than for distractors? Fourth, does the same logic extend from dyads to triads? And finally given the limited number of empirical studies on collaborative recognition and the challenges of designing and running studies of collaborative memory, what are the magnitudes and characteristics of the predicted effects? We answer the first four questions in turn and address the fifth question about the characteristics of the effects as we answer the other four questions.

In our REM simulations, the agent's memory ability is manipulated by varying the correct-copy probability c, which determines the probability that the correct feature value is stored at study. By varying c we effectively manipulate encoding quality and ultimately the recognition ability of the agent. We focused our simulations on the copy probability c in the range [.1 .9] because more extreme abilities are unlikely in an empirical study, and because we wish to compare performance of the collaboration models to a model by which the choice on each trial is that of the best agent overall (thus requiring that the best agent be less than perfect and above chance).

Number of confidence ratings

To further understand the performance of the relative-evidence and absolute-evidence mappings and the difference between them, it is useful to examine the consequences of one other assumption: the number of confidence categories each model used. In our simulations, we set this number to three. As we noted earlier, this number was a bit arbitrary though we do know that people, in general, when communicating confidence use a limited vocabulary with an upper limit of no more than eleven terms or categories (Budescu, Weinberg, & Wallsten, 1988; Wallsten, Budescu, & Zwick, 1993). The confidence-resolution analysis plots how performance changes as we increase the number of possible confidence categories from three to ten. This is a deliberate coarse communication constraint, consistent with Wallsten and Budescu (1995).

The relative-evidence and absolute-evidence mappings use the same general confidenceselection rule but differ in how evidence is converted into confidence ratings. In the relativeevidence mapping (MCR), ratings are defined relative to each agent's own evidence distribution and are therefore used approximately equally often, and the absolute-evidence mapping (MCA), ratings correspond more directly to the evidential value and are calibrated to recognition accuracy. We next consider whether the patterns observed for dyads extend to triads. To do so, we extended the CO-REM collaboration rules to triads. Each agent first makes an individual old/new decision. When all three agree, the group retains that decision. Otherwise, the majority-voting model (MV) adapts the response of the two agreeing members. In the confidence sharing models (MCA and MCR), the group instead follows the decision of the member reporting the highest confidence. When the highest confidence rating is shared by members making opposing decisions, the majority decision takes precedence. Thus, a lone dissenter can override the majority only when reporting strictly higher confidence than both other members. As in the dyadic models, confidence ratings are treated as ordered categories rather than quantities to be integrated. In contrast, the OS model integrates the continuous log-likelihood evidence from all three agents. These rules allow us to compare majority voting, categorical confidence sharing, and the integration of continuous memory evidence in triads.

Several patterns emerge. First, the OS model consistently exceeds the best individual, providing an upper benchmark for collective performance. Second, majority voting does not necessarily outperform the best individual, particularly when members differ substantially in recognition ability. Third, both MCA and MCR can outperform majority voting and the best individual, with MCA generally performing better than MCR. The benefits depend not simply on how similar the members are in ability, but also on the distribution of abilities within the group. For example, even when one member has relatively poor memory, a triad containing two stronger members can benefit from sharing confidence.

To better understand the differences between the confidence-sharing models (MCA and MCR), we examined the trials on which a dissenting member overturned the majority decision (see Figure S4). Across the simulated conditions, approximately 68% of the trials produced a 2-1 split. Of these trials, MCR overturned the majority 17.5% of the time compared with 15.0% for MCA. However, only 59% of MCR's overturns were correct, compared with 73% for MCA.

Consequently, MCR improved balance accuracy by about 2 percentage points whereas MCA improved it by nearly 5 percentage points. The difference between the models is attributable to the degree confidence reflects individual recognition ability. Under MCR, each member uses the confidence categories equally often, regardless of their memory ability, giving weaker members similar opportunities to override the majority. Under MCA, confidence better reflects the diagnostic value of each member's evidence, so overturns are more likely to come from the stronger member and to be correct. Thus, as in dyads, the benefits of sharing confidence depend on how well confidence communicates the underlying memory evidence.

Discussion

Whether we are trying to identify a familiar face, determine if an event occurred, or judge whether we have heard something before, we often rely on others to help make those recognition decisions. Underlying such collaboration is the belief that "two heads are better than one." Yet, collaborating during recognition decisions introduces its own challenges. Each person brings separate, imperfect memory evidence to the decision and must find a way to communicate that evidence to others. The group must then find a way to use the shared information to reach a common decision. In this paper, we developed CO-REM to examine how different forms of information sharing shape collaborative recognition performance.

CO-REM builds on the well-established REM model of individual recognition memory, which represents memory evidence as a likelihood ratio favoring an old or new response (Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997). We used this model to investigate several ways individuals may make collaborative recognition decisions from sharing categorical old/new recognition decisions to confidence judgments to the direct integration of memory evidence. The simulations show that indeed that two heads can be better than one, with collaborating agents outperforming the best individual member, a so-called "assembly bonus effect" (Kerr & Tindale, 2004). However, this benefit depends on the recognition abilities of the individuals and how they communicate and use their memory evidence. In general, the collective benefit is greatest when individuals have similar recognition abilities and decreases as their abilities diverge, even when their memory evidence is optimally integrated. With more limited forms of information sharing, group performance can even fall below that of the best individual.

Our primary hypothesis is that during collaborative recognition, people communicate the strength of their memory evidence via confidence. This hypothesis aligns with our understanding of metacognition (Nelson & Narens, 1994), communication of uncertainty (Wallsten & Budescu, 1995), and collaborative perceptual decision making (Bahrami et al., 2010; Bang et al., 2017).

Our simulations suggest that how individuals map their memory evidence onto confidence judgment can substantially affect collaborative performance. When confidence is expressed relative to each person's own evidence distribution, weaker members can exert too much influence over the collective decision. In contrast, when confidence more directly reflects the diagnostic value of memory evidence, collaborative performance generally improves.

It is interesting to contrast our results with the better-studied case of collaborative recall.

In free recall, as we established, collaborating groups demonstrate collaborative inhibition where they typically recall fewer items than nominal groups formed by pooling the responses of individuals working separately (Basedn et al., 1997; Weldon & Bellinger, 1997). This inhibition is thought to occur because hearing others' responses disrupts an individual's own retrieval strategy. Collaborative recognition differs because the test item is provided, reducing the likelihood of any retrieval disruption. Instead, as CO-REM illustrates, collaborators must decide how to use the memory evidence each person generates in response to the same probe. Our simulations suggest that this creates collaboration to improve recognition, but also the possibility of poorer performance when individuals less diagnostic evidence have too much influence. Thus, instead of disruptions of retrieval, the benefits and costs of collaborative recognition may depend more heavily on how individuals communicate and combine their memory evidence.

As we noted in the introduction, in this paper we adopted a simulation approach to better understand the basic aspects of collaborative recognition and to identify clear predictions. To this end, CO-REM makes several predictions that may guide future research. One is that collaboration can produce greater benefits for recognizing previously studied items (targets) than for correctly rejecting new items (lures). In CO-REM this asymmetry arises from the skewed distributions of the evidence where memory evidence sometimes is particularly strong for target items, which in collaborative recognition creates opportunities for one member's evidence to correct another member's error. Consistent with this prediction, Cark et al. (2000) reported such an asymmetry with stronger collaborative facilitation for targets than for distractors. Another prediction is that gains and losses should vary systematically with differences in individual recognition ability and the composition of the group.

These predictions suggest several ways to investigate collaborative recognition empirically. One approach would be to first assess individual recognition ability and then form dyads whose members have similar or different abilities. During a subsequent collaborative recognition task, each participant could first make an independent old/new decision and confidence judgment before the dyad reaches a joint decision. The different dyad compositions would test the prediction of when there is a gain or loss from collective recognition.

Furthermore, manipulating whether participants communicate only their categorical decisions or also their confidence, can help test how information sharing and group composition jointly affect recognition performance. We could also manipulate feedback to learn if and how the calibration of confidence ratings might change could help distinguish relative from absolute confidence sharing. Finally, CO-REM simulations suggest the predicted differences are generally small, which implies the experiments will need a high number of trials to test these predictions.
