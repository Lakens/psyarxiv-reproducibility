Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Relational Calibration (RC) is a theoretical framework for organizing enacted instructor responses during ongoing instruction. It specifies three response dimensions: relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation. Interpersonal attunement coordinates these dimensions by linking emerging instructional evidence and its interpretation to enacted responses. The framework's functional target is sustaining engagement with demanding intellectual work while preserving intellectual demand. Grounded in research on professional noticing, adaptive and responsive teaching, contingent scaffolding, interpersonal teacher behavior, relational competence, and instructional communication, RC brings relational, instructional, and expressive aspects of response into a common event-level representation. It distinguishes observable evidence, interpretation, enacted response, instructional architecture, the functional target, proposed mechanisms, subsequent outcomes, and measurement. These distinctions provide a basis for examining how instructors coordinate responses within particular instructional events and how those responses relate to engagement and preserved demand. The manuscript develops the framework's theoretical foundations, dimensions, and coordinating process, then specifies a measurement and research agenda addressing event boundaries, response coding, independent criterion measurement, comparison with established frameworks, and enactment across instructional contexts.

Relational Calibration: A Framework for Dynamic Instructional Regulation in Complex

Learning Environments

When learning becomes difficult, teachers face a central instructional problem: how to sustain students' engagement while preserving the intellectual demands of the work. Responding involves decisions about the support to provide, the responsibility learners retain, and how expectations and encouragement are communicated. An instructor may clarify a next step, invite an explanation, maintain an expectation for reasoning, or change pace while the task remains the same. Relational Calibration (RC) addresses the organization of these enacted responses during unfolding instruction. The framework focuses on how instructors respond to emerging evidence in relation to the joint target of continued engagement and preserved intellectual demand.

RC draws on established research on how teachers attend to classroom information, interpret its significance, adjust instruction, provide contingent support, and respond within educational relationships. Professional noticing, adaptive and responsive teaching, scaffolding, interpersonal teacher behavior, and relational competence provide the attentional, instructional, and relational foundations for this account (Corno, 2008; Jacobs et al., 2010; Jönsson et al., 2024; Pennings et al., 2018; van de Pol et al., 2010). Instructional-communication scholarship connects instructional and relational goals with verbal and nonverbal enactment (McCroskey et al., 2004; Mottet, Frymier, & Beebe, 2006; Mottet, Richmond, & McCroskey, 2006). Withitness contributes an account of awareness, timing, and coordination amid overlapping classroom events (Emmer & Stough, 2001; Kounin, 1970). These traditions inform the organization developed here while retaining their own constructs and theoretical aims.

Descriptions of interpersonal stance, instructional support, and expressive behavior each illuminate part of an instructor's response. RC brings these aspects into the same event-level account so that their organization in relation to available evidence and the intellectual task can be examined directly. The focus is how an instructor coordinates a response as the interaction develops, including what is maintained, what is adjusted, and how those decisions relate to the work learners are being asked to do.

RC specifies three response dimensions: relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation. Interpersonal attunement coordinates them by linking interpretation of emerging evidence to the selection or maintenance of an enacted response. The framework's joint functional target is sustaining engagement with demanding intellectual work while preserving intellectual demand. Instructional architecture remains analytically outside the focal response representation. The contribution is an explicit organization of these response dimensions, their coordination, and their relationship to a defined instructional target.

RC examines the enacted response through the organization of these dimensions. A change in pacing can be considered alongside the instructor's interpersonal stance and the responsibility assigned to learners. Maintaining an expectation can be examined alongside changes in explanation or facilitation. This organization provides a way to investigate how instructors respond to difficulty while keeping the intellectual work in view. It also distinguishes the response from the evidence available to the instructor and the interpretation on which the response was based.

The framework separates the organization of a response from its functional target and subsequent outcomes. This permits researchers to describe what the instructor did and independently evaluate engagement, preserved demand, and later learning. The resulting research agenda connects the study of enacted response with the instructional problem that gives the framework its purpose.

The next section develops the relevant theoretical foundations. Subsequent sections define RC and its analytic distinctions, describe the three response dimensions, and explain interpersonal attunement and event-level regulation. The manuscript then examines RC in relation to existing frameworks and specifies a measurement program. The discussion addresses empirical status, boundary conditions, and feasibility, followed by future research and implications.

Relevant Theoretical Perspectives

RC draws together research on selective attention, instructional adjustment, contingent support, relational interaction, and communicative enactment. These traditions examine teaching at different timescales and with different constructs, providing complementary resources for specifying the framework. Professional noticing informs the relationship between evidence and interpretation; adaptive teaching and scaffolding inform responsive instructional action; and interpersonal and relational-competence research inform how teachers position themselves and adjust within interaction (Corno, 2008; Jacobs et al., 2010; Jönsson et al., 2024; Pennings et al., 2018; van de Pol et al., 2010). The following review develops these foundations and their relevance to the RC organization.

Professional Noticing and Professional Vision

Professional noticing and professional vision explain how practitioners organize what is salient in complex activity. Goodwin (1994) described professional vision as socially organized ways of seeing and making consequential features of activity intelligible. In teaching, professional noticing has commonly been represented through attention to significant events, interpretation of what those events mean, and decisions about how to respond (Jacobs et al., 2010). This tradition connects perception and interpretation to pedagogical judgment.

Van Es and Sherin (2021) expanded noticing beyond the identification of classroom events to include selective disregarding and the ways teachers act on what they notice. Their subsequent work on shaping describes teachers as creating conditions for additional information by eliciting details, using participation structures, and drawing on available resources to make student thinking more accessible (van Es & Sherin, 2026). Contemporary measurement work likewise treats noticing as selective and interpretive (König et al., 2022). Attention, interpretation, response selection, and recursive information seeking provide a foundation for RC's account of how emerging evidence enters an instructional response.

Adaptive and Responsive Teaching

Adaptive and responsive teaching addresses goal-directed adjustment during instruction. Corno's (2008) account of microadaptation describes teaching as responsive to learner and contextual variation at a fine grain, while broader reviews characterize adaptive teaching as involving diagnosis and modification before, during, and after instruction (Parsons et al., 2018).

The literature encompasses preplanned differentiation, post hoc correction, and changes in explanation, questioning, pacing, support, participation, grouping, and representations in response to evidence that emerges during teaching.

Responsive-teaching studies address disciplinary reasoning, participation, social and relational information, affect, motivation, and the maintenance of productive intellectual challenge. Work in this tradition has examined how teachers pursue explanatory rigor while responding to student thinking (Thompson et al., 2016), and adaptive-teaching research has used bounded episodes to distinguish diagnosis, support, and subsequent student understanding (Hardy et al., 2022). These studies connect instructional adjustment with the disciplinary, relational, and motivational conditions of ongoing work. They inform RC's treatment of instructional posture and its orientation toward engagement with preserved intellectual demand.

Contingent Scaffolding

The scaffolding literature supplies a particularly explicit account of contingency across linked teacher-learner exchanges. From early formulations of scaffolding (Wood et al., 1976) through later conceptual and empirical work, scaffolding has been concerned with adjusting support in relation to learner performance and transferring responsibility as competence develops. Reviews distinguish multiple forms of support (including cognitive, metacognitive, affective, and performance-oriented support) and emphasize contingency, fading, and transfer of responsibility as central features (van de Pol et al., 2010). Empirical studies have operationalized contingency through sequences in which learner evidence is followed by support calibrated to that evidence and then by a subsequent learner response or adjustment in support (van de Pol et al., 2011; van de Pol & Elbers, 2013).

Scaffolding encompasses support and the preservation of productive challenge. Reiser (2004) distinguished structuring from problematizing, emphasizing that support can organize complex work while pressing learners to grapple with disciplinary problems. Subsequent research has extended scaffolding analysis to recursive sequences, multiple support domains, and temporally organized teacher-student interaction (Hermkes et al., 2018; Jiang & Wang, 2025; van de Pol et al., 2019). This work connects diagnosis and contingent adjustment with pacing, framing, responsibility transfer, and subsequent learner evidence. It provides a foundation for examining how instructional posture changes within an event and how support relates to the continued demands of the work.

Interpersonal Teacher Behavior

A separate body of work examines teaching as an interpersonal process organized through recurring patterns of agency and communion. Research in the interpersonal tradition has long treated teacher behavior in terms of relational stance and its consequences for teacher-student interaction (Wubbels & Brekelmans, 2005). Alongside work on classroom climate and aggregate teacher profiles, dynamic extensions examine reciprocal teacher-student behavior as it unfolds over time, including lags between teacher and student behavior, actor-linked streams, and state-space representations of interaction (Pennings et al., 2018; Pennings & Hollenstein, 2020).

Dynamic interpersonal approaches examine relational stance, reciprocity, timing, and temporal patterning at a fine grain. They represent interpersonal behavior as a changing system and extend temporally resolved analysis into instructional settings (van Vemde et al., 2022). For RC, this work informs the examination of relational positioning within unfolding interaction. Its established constructs and analytic methods also provide resources for comparing event-level responses with longer-term patterns of teacher-student interaction.

Relational Competence

Relational-competence scholarship examines teachers' capacity to establish, maintain, and adjust educational relationships. This work includes attunement to students, communicative competence, and the relational consequences of how teachers respond within interaction (Aspelin & Jönsson, 2019). Recent studies have examined relational competence through fine-grained or structured interactional tasks, including teachers' verbal and nonverbal adjustment, responsiveness, and task guidance (Jönsson et al., 2024; Plantin Ewe & Aspelin, 2025).

The connection between relational responsiveness and task guidance is central to RC.

Relational-competence research informs how instructors attend to interpersonal information and organize their responses while an educational task is underway. Its treatment of attunement and communicative adjustment supports the coordinating role assigned to interpersonal attunement and the analysis of relational and expressive aspects of enacted response.

Withitness and Classroom Management

Withitness concerns awareness and action amid simultaneous classroom events. Kounin's (1970) work on classroom management addressed teachers' awareness of multiple and overlapping events, along with timing, smoothness, momentum, and group focus. Later syntheses retained these functions as part of effective classroom management (Emmer & Stough, 2001), while expertise research examined how experienced teachers distribute attention across classroom information (Sabers et al., 1991). This tradition places the timing and coordination of instructional action within the demands of group activity. It informs RC's treatment of the evidence environment in which instructors notice, interpret, and respond. The relationship between simultaneous classroom activity and the instructor's access to particular events is especially relevant to studying RC in whole-class instruction.

Instructional Communication and Teacher Immediacy

Instructional-communication scholarship treats teaching as an ongoing communicative process that includes instructional and relational concerns (Mottet, Richmond, & McCroskey, 2006). The General Model of Instructional Communication links teacher characteristics and verbal and nonverbal communication behaviors to student perceptions and instructional outcomes (McCroskey et al., 2004). Rhetorical/Relational Goals Theory treats teachers as pursuing instructional and relational goals through communication while students bring corresponding academic and relational needs (Mottet, Frymier, & Beebe, 2006). These accounts inform the relationship between the content of an instructional response and its interpersonal and expressive enactment.

Teacher-immediacy research examines relational meaning in verbal and nonverbal conduct. Immediacy has been defined in terms of communication that reduces psychological or physical distance and conveys approachability or closeness (Andersen, 1979; Gorham, 1988).

Nonverbal forms include facial expression, eye contact, gesture, posture, movement, proximity, and vocal behavior. Verbal measures include inclusive language, names, humor, praise, feedback, and invitations to interact. These constructs contribute to understanding the relational and expressive features of instructor response represented within RC.

A major meta-analysis found stronger associations between teacher nonverbal immediacy and affective or perceived learning than with performance-based cognitive learning (Witt et al., 2004). A later systematic review found predominantly positive associations between verbal or nonverbal immediacy and student motivation in a literature dominated by correlational and student-report designs (Liu, 2021). Experimental work cautions against assuming a simple "more is better" relation (Comstock et al., 1995), and cross-cultural validation studies show that immediacy measures and factor interpretations require contextual qualification (Zhang et al., 2007). Observation research has also directly coded nonverbal immediacy in classroom video using lesson-or teacher-level representations (Toivanen et al., 2026). These findings support attention to the form, context, and measured consequences of communicative behavior.

Instructional communication and teacher immediacy connect expressive enactment and relational meaning with student perceptions, affect, motivation, and instructional outcomes.

Within RC, this work informs relational positioning and real-time modulation while preserving the source constructs and the evidentiary scope of their findings. It helps specify why tone, pacing, emphasis, and affective expression matter to the analysis of an enacted response.

Defining Relational Calibration

Relational Calibration (RC) is defined as an ongoing instructional regulatory system in which an instructor uses interpersonal attunement to interpret emerging evidence from the instructional interaction and coordinate adjustments across three response dimensions (relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation), with the functional target of sustaining learners' engagement with demanding intellectual work while preserving the intellectual demands of the task. This definition specifies the analytic organization used in the present framework.

Analytic Roles Within Relational Calibration

The definition assigns distinct theoretical roles to elements that can easily be collapsed in ordinary descriptions of teaching. The three response dimensions constitute the RC response taxonomy. Interpersonal attunement coordinates those dimensions by linking interpretation of emerging evidence to the organization of a response, leaving the taxonomy explicitly three-dimensional. Emerging evidence serves as an input to interpretation. The framework therefore distinguishes what is observed in an instructional interaction, what the instructor infers from that evidence, and the response that follows.

Observable interactional evidence provides partial access to learner states such as comprehension, motivation, affect, or intention. Interpretation is therefore fallible. Within RC, response organization and interpretive accuracy are separate questions: an instructor may respond to emerging evidence while misinterpreting what a learner is thinking or feeling.

Distinguishing observed evidence, interpretation, and enacted response permits each to be represented and examined on its own terms.

Instructional Architecture, Functional Target, and Outcomes

Instructional architecture sits outside the RC construct itself. Formal features of a lesson or task, including materials, grouping arrangements, activity sequence, task structure, and representations, shape the setting within which a response occurs. RC focuses on how that response is enacted. The boundary permits instructional design and response processes to be represented separately while preserving their relationship. Adaptive-teaching and scaffolding traditions inform this treatment of design and in-the-moment response as related features of instruction (Corno, 2008; Parsons et al., 2018; van de Pol et al., 2010).

An implication of this boundary is that RC-relevant response can, in principle, be examined while instructional architecture is held fixed. An instructor could change relational stance, instructional posture, or expressive enactment while retaining the task, materials, grouping, and sequence. The distinction thus specifies a comparison between the organization of enacted response and the formal design within which it occurs.

The functional target occupies a separate analytic role from the occurrence of RC. The framework stipulates a dual criterion: sustaining learners' engagement with demanding intellectual work while preserving the intellectual demands of that work. Participation, compliance, comfort, persistence, task completion, learning, and performance may provide useful indicators or outcomes in particular studies, but each captures something different from the stipulated target. Visible difficulty or struggle likewise provides incomplete evidence about preserved intellectual demand. The target therefore serves as a criterion against which responses can be evaluated while remaining outside the response dimensions that define RC.

Separating the target from the construct allows process, target attainment, and downstream outcomes to be evaluated independently. A response can be coded as consistent with the RC organization and still vary in whether engagement is sustained, intellectual demand is preserved, learning improves, or other favorable outcomes follow. Favorable outcomes can also arise through other instructional processes. The joint target draws on work in responsive teaching and scaffolding concerned with maintaining productive challenge while adjusting support (Reiser, 2004; Thompson et al., 2016; van de Pol et al., 2010). Its role in RC is to provide a criterion for evaluating the response separately from describing its occurrence.

Proposed mechanisms, hypothesized outcomes, and measurement procedures have distinct roles outside the definition of RC. Future studies may test explanations for how particular responses support engagement or preserved demand, examine associations with later outcomes, and develop ways to represent RC-relevant events. Keeping the construct, proposed mechanisms, functional target, outcomes, and methods separately identifiable makes those relationships testable without folding explanation or measurement back into the definition itself.

Theoretical Status and Scope

RC is specified as a theoretical framework with an event-level response taxonomy and a coordinating process. These elements identify what a study would represent as enacted response and how that response relates to interpretation and the functional target. This specification provides the basis for the measurement and empirical program developed later in the manuscript.

The scope centers on the enacted response. Instructional architecture, observable evidence, inferred learner states, the functional target, proposed mechanisms, outcomes, and measurement retain their separate analytic roles. Keeping those roles explicit permits researchers to investigate response processes within their instructional setting and to examine their relationships with independently measured criteria and outcomes.

The three response dimensions specify the relational, instructional, and expressive aspects of that enacted response.

Dimensions of Relational Calibration

Relational Calibration organizes enacted instructor response into three theoretical dimensions: relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation. Together, they form the framework's response taxonomy. Each describes an aspect of how an instructor responds within an instructional event, allowing the interpersonal stance, distribution of responsibility, and expressive form of a response to be represented together.

Relational Positioning

Relational positioning classifies the instructor's interpersonal stance within an instructional interaction, including authority, accessibility, expectations, accountability, relational distance, and the momentary consequences attached to participation or error. Its meaning depends on how the stance functions in the instructional situation. Interpersonal teacher-behavior research represents relational stance through constructs such as agency and communion; relational-competence scholarship addresses attunement and communicative responsiveness; and instructional-communication research examines approachability, relational meaning, and verbal and nonverbal immediacy (Jönsson et al., 2024; McCroskey et al., 2004; Plantin Ewe & Aspelin, 2025; Wubbels & Brekelmans, 2005). Within RC, relational positioning identifies the interpersonal stance enacted as the intellectual work unfolds.

Instructional Posture

Instructional posture classifies the instructor's stance toward the intellectual work and the distribution of responsibility for that work. It includes explanatory, facilitative, collaborative or co-investigative, and evaluative modes. Calibration depends on how a posture functions in relation to available evidence, the task, and the instructional situation. Adaptive and responsive teaching informs changes in diagnosis and instructional support, while scaffolding research addresses contingent guidance, control, fading, and transfer of responsibility (Corno, 2008; Hardy et al., 2022; Robertson et al., 2016; Thompson et al., 2016; van de Pol et al., 2010).

Instructional posture locates the instructor's participation in the work and the responsibility assigned to learners within an event.

Real-Time Modulation

Real-time modulation classifies the moment-to-moment expressive and temporal form of an instructor's response: tone, pacing, emphasis, and affective expression. These features can change while the substantive content and intellectual rigor of the response are maintained.

Research on nonverbal and multimodal conduct, including gaze, gesture, posture, movement, and proximity, provides relevant observational context. These behaviors are not specified as separate RC dimensions. Dynamic interpersonal and relational-competence research informs fine-grained expressive behavior, and instructional-communication research informs verbal and nonverbal enactment (Jönsson et al., 2024; Pennings et al., 2018; Pennings & Hollenstein, 2020; Toivanen et al., 2026). Real-time modulation captures how the response is delivered as the interaction develops.

Calibration Across Response Profiles

Across the three dimensions, calibration is defined relationally and contextually, with no fixed response profile. The same behavior, stance, response frequency, latency, or level of expressive intensity may function differently across instructional situations. A stable stance can be appropriate, and so can a visible adjustment; warmth, immediacy, speed, and frequency therefore carry no universally preferred value within RC. Research on dynamic interpersonal behavior underscores the contextual and temporal character of interaction, and experimental work on teacher immediacy similarly cautions against assumptions that greater expressive intensity is always better (Comstock et al., 1995; Pennings et al., 2018). The adequacy of a response is interpreted in relation to the available evidence, timing, task, and instructional situation.

Theoretical Status of the Three-Dimension Taxonomy

The taxonomy distinguishes the aspects of response represented by the framework.

Relational positioning concerns interpersonal stance, instructional posture concerns participation in and responsibility for the intellectual work, and real-time modulation concerns expressive and temporal enactment. Existing classroom and interpersonal studies use bounded episodes, multiple codes, temporal units, and multidomain observations to represent unfolding interaction (Hardy et al., 2022; Larison et al., 2025; Pennings et al., 2018; Thompson et al., 2016). These methods inform the measurement program for examining the three dimensions within a common instructional event. Interpersonal attunement specifies how the dimensions are coordinated.

Interpersonal Attunement and Event-Level Regulation

Within Relational Calibration, interpersonal attunement names the coordinating process through which an instructor interprets emerging evidence from an instructional interaction and organizes adjustments across relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation. It links what is noticed and interpreted to the selection or maintenance of an enacted response.

Interpersonal Attunement as Coordination

This account of coordination draws on several connected traditions. Professional-noticing research addresses selective attention, interpretation, response, and active shaping of the information that becomes available (Jacobs et al., 2010; van Es & Sherin, 2021 , 2026). Adaptive and responsive teaching addresses goal-directed adjustment in relation to learner and contextual information (Corno, 2008; Hardy et al., 2022). Contingent-scaffolding research examines diagnosis, support, learner response, and subsequent adjustment across linked exchanges (van de Pol et al., 2011; van de Pol & Elbers, 2013). Interpersonal and relational-competence research examines dynamic changes in relational stance, attunement, expressive behavior, and reciprocal interaction (Jönsson et al., 2024; Pennings et al., 2018; Pennings & Hollenstein, 2020). RC uses interpersonal attunement to organize the connection between these interpretive activities and enacted responses across the three dimensions.

Within RC, interpersonal attunement coordinates the three specified response dimensions while preserving their distinct analytic roles. An instructor may maintain one aspect of a response while adjusting another, or maintain the current response altogether when the available evidence supports doing so. Coordination therefore includes both stability and change. Response frequency, latency, stance, and visible degree of adjustment are interpreted in relation to the evidence available, the timing of the response, the task, and the instructional context.

Emerging Evidence and Fallible Interpretation

The input to this coordinating process is emerging observable evidence from the instructional interaction. Such evidence can include what learners say or do, observable actions in relation to the focal work, and other interactional information available to the instructor. These observations provide evidence about the interaction without offering transparent access to comprehension, motivation, affect, or other internal learner states. Observed evidence, the instructor's interpretation of that evidence, and the ensuing response therefore remain analytically distinct. Interpretation is fallible, and the same observable event may support more than one plausible reading.

Professional-noticing and expertise research situates this process within the selective distribution of teacher attention across classroom information (König et al., 2022; Sabers et al., 1991). Withitness contributes an account of awareness, timing, and coordination amid overlapping events (Kounin, 1970; Emmer & Stough, 2001). The evidence available to an instructor at a particular moment therefore provides the context for interpreting how a response was organized.

Event-Level Regulatory Logic

For analytic purposes, the RC process can be represented at a bounded event level as a relation among emerging observable evidence, interpretation, an enacted response across one or more of the three dimensions, and subsequent observable evidence. The event provides an analytic window onto an ongoing process. Comparable literatures use bounded episodes, linked teacher-learner exchanges, actor-specific temporal streams, and later-response information to study contingent and dynamic interaction (Hardy et al., 2022; Pennings et al., 2018; van de Pol et al., 2011; van de Pol & Elbers, 2013). These approaches inform how an RC study could sample and link the elements of an event.

One proposed pathway concerns instructional posture. A change in posture may redistribute responsibility for the work or clarify access to the intellectual task by shifting the instructor's role in explanation, facilitation, collaboration, or evaluation. Research on scaffolding and adaptive teaching informs this pathway through its treatment of responsibility transfer, contingent support, and changes in instructional guidance. Within RC, the proposed relationship connects a specified aspect of enacted response with the conditions for continued intellectual engagement.

Calibration at the event level can involve either adjustment or maintenance. A theoretically relevant event may preserve an existing relational stance, instructional posture, or expressive form when that response remains appropriate to the evidence and task, just as another event may involve a visible change in tone, pacing, stance, or responsibility. The analytic RELATIONAL CALIBRATION 20 question is how the response is organized in relation to the evidence, task, timing, and stated functional target; the magnitude of visible behavioral change is secondary.

The functional target orients event-level analysis toward sustaining engagement with demanding intellectual work while preserving the intellectual demands of the task. This joint criterion draws on adaptive-teaching and scaffolding accounts of supporting participation in demanding work while maintaining productive challenge (Reiser, 2004; Thompson et al., 2016; van de Pol et al., 2010). Relational Calibration in Relation to Existing Frameworks

RC's relationship to existing frameworks can be understood through the organization it assigns to enacted response. Professional noticing, adaptive and responsive teaching, scaffolding, interpersonal teacher behavior, and relational competence inform the evidence, interpretation, and action represented within the framework (Corno, 2008; Jacobs et al., 2010; Jönsson et al., 2024; Pennings et al., 2018; van de Pol et al., 2010). Comparing these traditions with RC clarifies how the three response dimensions, interpersonal attunement, functional target, and analytic boundaries work together.

Shared Theoretical Foundations

Professional noticing connects selective attention and interpretation with pedagogical judgment, including information seeking and action on what is noticed (Jacobs et al., 2010; van Es & Sherin, 2021 , 2026). Adaptive and responsive teaching connects instructional adjustment with cognitive, social, motivational, and affective information (Corno, 2008; Parsons et al., 2018; Thompson et al., 2016). Scaffolding represents learner evidence, contingent support, and subsequent learner response as linked exchanges, including changes in responsibility over time (van de Pol et al., 2010 (van de Pol et al., , 2011;; van de Pol & Elbers, 2013). These accounts inform RC's linkage of evidence and interpretation with enacted instructional response.

Interpersonal teacher-behavior research examines relational stance and reciprocal dynamics at aggregate and fine-grained temporal levels (Pennings et al., 2018; Pennings & Hollenstein, 2020; Wubbels & Brekelmans, 2005). Relational-competence research connects attunement and communication with responsive task guidance (Aspelin & Jönsson, 2019; Jönsson et al., 2024). Instructional communication and immediacy address relational meaning, verbal and nonverbal enactment, approachability, expressive behavior, and their associations with student perceptions and outcomes (McCroskey et al., 2004; Mottet, Frymier, & Beebe, 2006; Mottet, Richmond, & McCroskey, 2006). These traditions inform the relational and expressive aspects of response and the coordinating role assigned to attunement.

Withitness addresses awareness of simultaneous events, timing, smoothness, and momentum in classroom activity (Kounin, 1970; Emmer & Stough, 2001). Alongside dynamic interpersonal and contingent-scaffolding research, it situates response within the temporal and group conditions of instruction. This context matters when examining how a response relates to the evidence available at a particular moment.

Theoretical Contribution of RC

RC's theoretical contribution is to make coordination across relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation a focus of event-level analysis. Examining the dimensions together describes how interpersonal stance, responsibility for intellectual work, and expressive enactment are organized in relation to the same evidence and task. A description of any one dimension captures an aspect of the response. Their common representation makes the organization of the response available for analysis, including how an instructor maintains some aspects while adjusting others through interpersonal attunement.

Consider an instructor who maintains the expectation that a learner explain their reasoning, shifts from explanation to facilitation, and slows the pace in response to an incomplete account. Relational positioning identifies the maintained expectation and accountability.

Instructional posture identifies the change in the instructor's participation and the learner's responsibility for the work. Real-time modulation identifies the change in pacing. Interpersonal attunement concerns how the instructor interprets the emerging evidence and coordinates these features of the response. Considered together, the dimensions describe how maintaining an expectation and adjusting support are organized around the same intellectual task.

The joint functional criterion gives this analysis an instructional purpose. It asks whether learners remain engaged with demanding intellectual work and whether the demands of that work are preserved during the interaction. Responsive-teaching research on disciplinary rigor and scaffolding accounts of structuring and problematizing demanding activity inform this orientation (Reiser, 2004; Thompson et al., 2016; van de Pol et al., 2010). Within RC, the criterion directs attention to engagement and the intellectual work together. Keeping it separate from the response dimensions allows researchers to investigate how a response was organized and whether the intended target was attained as distinct questions.

The separation of enacted response from instructional architecture clarifies how this organization can be studied within a lesson or task. Materials, grouping, sequence, and representations shape what learners are asked to do and the responses available to the instructor.

With those features held fixed, researchers could examine changes in interpersonal stance, instructional responsibility, or expressive enactment during the work. When architecture also changes, it can be recorded separately. The boundary thus supports inquiry into how an instructor enacts a response within a particular design and how that response relates to the continuing demands of the task.

Analytic Implications

The framework is grounded in research on the attentional, instructional, relational, and communicative processes it organizes. That research informs proposed pathways involving cognitive demand, affective conditions, instructional support, responsibility, and interactional fit.

RC's theoretical specification places those pathways in relation to defined response dimensions and a joint functional target, providing an organization for empirical investigation.

The analytic distinctions also define the evidence needed for that investigation. Coding an enacted response, measuring engagement and preserved demand, and examining subsequent learning or performance answer different questions. Representing these layers separately permits researchers to investigate relationships among response organization, target attainment, and downstream outcomes.

Event windows, multi-label coding, sequence representations, and fine-grained observation provide methodological approaches for examining the framework. Direct representation of constructs from established traditions supplies a basis for comparative studies of analytic and predictive value. The measurement program that follows specifies how an RC record could support those comparisons.

RC thus advances a conceptual organization with explicit construct boundaries and an accompanying empirical program (MacInnis, 2011; Suddaby, 2010). It brings three response dimensions together through interpersonal attunement while distinguishing the response from its inputs, instructional architecture, proposed mechanisms, functional target, outcomes, and measurement. These distinctions identify what researchers would observe, code, and evaluate in a study of dynamic instructional regulation.

Measurement and Empirical Evaluation

A measurement program for Relational Calibration should preserve the framework's analytic distinctions throughout sampling, coding, and evaluation. The proposed program specifies how instructional events could be sampled reproducibly, represented with reliable variables, and evaluated using separate records of observed evidence, instructor interpretation, enacted response, instructional architecture, the functional target, and subsequent outcomes.

Methodological resources from scaffolding, adaptive teaching, dynamic interpersonal research, professional noticing, and engagement and demand measurement inform these operations.

A Proposed Research Record with Separate Fields

A proposed research record would keep analytically distinct information in separate fields. It could include observable evidence from the interaction; any instructor interpretation independently recoverable from the record; zero or more labels for relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation; response timing and enactment; subsequent observable evidence; task and instructional-architecture information; contextual conditions; and separate variables for engagement and preserved intellectual demand. This structure identifies the information needed to develop and test RC measurement procedures.

The separation between observable evidence and interpretation is especially important. A recorded learner utterance, action, product, or interactional event is evidence; an inference about comprehension, motivation, affect, effort, strategy, or intention is a different analytic layer.

Where an instructor's interpretation is not independently recoverable from the research record, it should not be reconstructed as though it were directly observed. A future coding scheme therefore needs to distinguish what the record contains from what an analyst infers from it. This preserves the same fallibility constraint that governs the construct definition.

Event Boundaries and Unitizing Reliability

The phrase calibration sequence refers here to a bounded analytic sampling window within an ongoing process. Classroom and interpersonal studies use bounded episodes, actor-linked exchanges, fixed temporal samples, and other time-ordered records, including nested or overlapping structures (Hardy et al., 2022; Pennings et al., 2018; van de Pol & Elbers, 2013; van de Pol et al., 2019). These approaches inform the development of prospectively specified rules for sampling RC events.

For RC, event boundaries would therefore require explicit, prospectively stated rules tied to the research question and data source. Investigators would need to specify what initiates a candidate window, what terminates it, how actors are linked, whether windows may overlap or nest, and how ambiguous cases are handled. Segmentation reliability must be estimated directly; agreement on substantive labels cannot substitute for it. This distinction matters because substantive-code reliability is more commonly reported than reproducibility of event boundaries or temporal annotation. A reliable response label cannot compensate for an unreliable decision about where the event begins and ends.

Response Labels, Coverage, and Structural Status

Once event windows can be unitized reproducibly, the three response dimensions can be examined as separate theoretical labels. Classroom and interpersonal research provides examples of multi-attribute and co-occurring coding within the same episode or time sample (Larison et al., 2025; Pennings et al., 2018; Thompson et al., 2016). A multi-label RC representation would allow researchers to estimate coverage, separability, dependence, and structural relations among relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation.

Early validation work should therefore examine each label on its own terms. Coverage and negative cases matter as much as agreement: researchers need evidence about whether relevant enacted responses can be represented without forcing ambiguous activity into one of the three categories, whether coders can distinguish the categories consistently, and whether the same observable action can be classified differently when its evidence, function, timing, or sequence differs. This last possibility follows from the contextual character of the taxonomy: behaviors that appear the same may receive different classifications across events because their meaning depends on the surrounding evidence, function, timing, and sequence.

Response frequency and timing should be defined for a specific operation and interpreted in relation to the instructional event. Classroom studies provide examples of operationalizing particular waiting or response intervals (Häusler et al., 2024). Within RC, stability can be appropriate, and frequent or rapid adjustment does not by itself indicate better calibration.

Temporal variables should therefore be interpreted descriptively unless independent theory and evidence justify a specific relationship with the functional target or later outcomes.

Separating Architecture From Enacted Response

Measurement should preserve the boundary between instructional architecture and enacted response. A study could code response while task materials, grouping, sequence, and representations remain fixed, or record architecture separately when these features vary. In both cases, coders would need to distinguish lesson or task design from the enacted relational, instructional, and expressive response. Reliability estimates would then address the application of that distinction in authentic events.

Measuring the Functional Target

Evaluating the functional target requires measures of engagement with the focal intellectual work that can be distinguished from participation, compliance, comfort, persistence, task completion, accuracy, learning, or performance. Short-timescale engagement research provides relevant approaches, including task-anchored experience sampling, same-task multimethod profiles, and activity or discourse coding (Chi et al., 2018; Shernoff et al., 2003; Symonds et al., 2021). These methods capture different aspects of engagement and provide indirect access to internal engagement states, making the choice and interpretation of indicators part of the measurement design.

Preserved intellectual demand requires an independent record. Mathematics and science research has operationalized designed or setup demand separately from demand enacted during interaction by examining the reasoning, explanation, representation, connections, and disciplinary practices required during implementation (Boston & Wolf, 2006; Henningsen & Stein, 1997; Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2015). Discourse research likewise shows that similar tasks can be enacted with different levels or kinds of conceptual and discursive richness (Prediger et al., 2024). These approaches inform discipline-grounded measurement of the intellectual demands maintained during an RC-relevant event.

An RC study could align two independently measured criterion variables to the same task segment or instructional episode: evidence of engagement with the focal intellectual work and evidence that its intellectual demand was preserved. Alignment would permit evaluation of the joint target at a common timescale while keeping the two criteria distinct. Visible difficulty, struggle, participation, completion, accuracy, and perceived challenge provide different information and cannot substitute for a judgment of preserved intellectual demand.

A Staged Validation Program

The measurement program should proceed in stages because later inferences depend on earlier ones. First, researchers need defensible sampling and unitization, including reproducible event boundaries and explicit treatment of overlap, nesting, actor linkage, and missing or ambiguous data. Second, substantive response labels require variable-specific reliability, evidence about coverage and negative cases, and tests of whether the three theoretical categories can be distinguished without assuming a natural factor structure. Third, temporal order and links between instructional responses and later evidence can be added where the data support them, while keeping adjacency, lag, and sequence separate from causal inference. Fourth, the engagement and preserved-demand criteria need their own validation and temporal alignment, independently of RC occurrence and independently of downstream outcomes. Only after those foundations are established would stronger construct-comparison, predictive, criterion, or mechanism questions become interpretable.

Composite or global scoring would require evidence for the event boundaries, response labels, dimensional relations, reliability, criterion meaning, and weighting used to construct a score. A research codebook should therefore make its sampling rules, coding operations, and intended interpretations explicit. Developing these components separately would allow researchers to evaluate the basis for any later aggregate representation.

Together, these stages connect the theoretical organization to a defined empirical record.

Reproducible event boundaries, interpretable response labels, independent criteria, and explicit temporal relations provide the foundation for construct comparisons and studies of proposed mechanisms. The discussion considers how this program relates to RC's empirical status and the conditions of authentic instruction.

Discussion of Boundary Conditions and Feasibility

RC organizes enacted instructor response around three dimensions coordinated through interpersonal attunement and evaluated against a joint engagement-and-preserved-demand target.

Its analytic distinctions connect the theoretical account to a research record in which evidence, interpretation, response, architecture, criteria, mechanisms, and outcomes can be examined separately. The framework's scope is therefore defined by both the response organization and the instructional conditions within which that organization is studied. RC has not yet been empirically validated. RC-specific evidence is needed for event boundaries, temporal organization, coding reliability, dimensional structure, coverage, discriminant validity, criterion validity, predictive validity, and causal effects. The three dimensions are a theoretical taxonomy; their empirical separability, dependence, joint necessity or sufficiency, and any basis for weighting require investigation. Methods developed in adjacent literatures inform this work but do not establish these properties for RC. The framework's added analytic and predictive value likewise requires direct comparison with established accounts. Its current premises specify an organization, boundaries, and a functional target without yielding a prediction that clearly departs from the most relevant established framework. Proposed pathways involving cognitive demand, affect, support, responsibility, and interactional fit draw on processes studied in those traditions. Studies must distinguish evidence about the RC organization from evidence about shared pathways, and causal claims require designs that establish more than temporal sequence or correspondence.

These empirical questions must be considered within the conditions of classroom instruction. Multiple learners, simultaneous events, incomplete information, competing goals, and task demands shape what instructors can notice, interpret, and enact. They also shape what researchers can observe and reconstruct. The following boundary conditions identify how these constraints bear on the interpretation of RC evidence.

Selective Attention and Ambiguous Evidence

Teacher attention is selective. Professional-noticing research treats attention as the selection and interpretation of particular features of complex activity, and teacher-attention studies show uneven or strategically organized allocation across learners and events (König et al., 2022; Sabers et al., 1991). Expertise may change what is attended to and how cues are organized, but comprehensive awareness of every learner and signal remains unrealistic. RC studies must interpret an event within the instructor's access to evidence at the time. Ambiguous evidence also constrains interpretation. The same observable event may support several plausible readings, and an instructor may act on incomplete or misleading information. A research record should distinguish what was observably available from what was inferred and retain the possibility that the instructor's interpretation was partial or mistaken. This distinction is especially important when researchers have access to information unavailable during the interaction.

Simultaneity and Whole-Class Feasibility

Whole-class instruction involves simultaneous and overlapping activity. Withitness addresses awareness, timing, smoothness, momentum, and responses to overlapping events (Kounin, 1970; Emmer & Stough, 2001) Participation structures and instructional architecture also shape access to evidence.

Questioning formats, grouping, response systems, and task arrangements can make particular learner responses more or less available. Studies should record these features as design variables, sampling conditions, or characteristics of the evidence environment. The representativeness of that access, accuracy of interpretation, and reliability of response coordination remain separate empirical questions.

Context and Instructional Architecture

RC-relevant observations are context dependent. Discipline, class size, modality, culture, learner characteristics, prior knowledge, motivation, grouping, and task structure may affect available evidence, interpretation, and the suitability of a measurement procedure. These factors should initially be represented as contextual conditions, covariates, sampling constraints, and generalization limits. Whether a condition strengthens, weakens, enables, or constrains an RC relationship requires direct tests of the direction and magnitude of moderation.

Instructional architecture shapes the work learners are asked to do and the responses available to the instructor. Its analytic separation from RC permits studies to characterize those conditions alongside enacted response. That boundary does not establish the relative explanatory importance of design and response; their relationship must be examined within the relevant theory and research design.

Judging Preserved Intellectual Demand

Evaluating the functional target requires an independent judgment that intellectual demand has been preserved. Mathematics and science research provides methods for comparing designed or setup demand with enacted reasoning, explanations, representations, connections, and disciplinary practices (Boston & Wolf, 2006; Henningsen & Stein, 1997; Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2015). These methods can identify maintenance or decline in demand even when the nominal task remains the same.

The strongest existing procedures are discipline-and task-specific, and their rules may not transport across subjects, learner populations, or timescales. Visible struggle, participation, completion, accuracy, and perceived challenge are incomplete indicators: a learner may struggle with a low-level task, complete demanding work successfully, or participate without demonstrating the reasoning required. RC research needs discipline-grounded demand criteria and tests of whether judgments can be made reliably and independently of RC occurrence and engagement.

A rule that distinguishes maintained from reduced demand in one mathematics task or science practice cannot be assumed to function equivalently in another discipline, task, format, or population. The development of a domain-general preserved-demand criterion remains an open measurement problem. The current literature also does not establish a validated measure of both engagement and preserved demand over the same interval. The joint target can be specified theoretically while its integrated evaluation requires further measurement development.

Trainability and Authentic Transfer

Whether coordinated RC can be learned and transferred to authentic teaching requires separate study. Existing traditions examine component skills such as noticing, evidence elicitation, contingent scaffolding, relational communication, and particular interactional responses. Evidence concerning these skills does not establish trainability of the coordinated system. Component performance, structured practice, simulation performance, authentic enactment, and transfer should be evaluated as distinct stages.

Measurement development and professional-development effectiveness also require different evidence. A research codebook could support identification of events, while reliable instructor enactment and transfer to whole-class teaching require their own tests. Performance in a video task, simulation, dyadic interaction, or selected episode cannot establish transfer under competing classroom demands. Applications as a teacher rubric, effectiveness standard, training recommendation, certification system, or accountability measure would additionally require reliability, validity, fairness, subgroup invariance, and evidence about the consequences of use.

Boundary Conditions as an Empirical Constraint

These boundary conditions specify how RC evidence should be interpreted. Studies need to characterize the instructor's access to information, distinguish research reconstruction from live enactment, ground demand judgments in the relevant discipline and task, and test transport and transfer directly. Theoretical specification, measurement development, and practical application involve related questions with different evidentiary requirements.

The empirical agenda follows from these distinctions: establish reproducible event records, compare response representations, investigate proposed pathways, and examine enactment across instructional contexts.

Testing the Candidate Organization

Initial studies should estimate whether event boundaries and response labels can be applied reproducibly. This includes coverage of relevant responses, treatment of negative or ambiguous cases, and the empirical relations among relational positioning, instructional posture, and real-time modulation. Multi-label and multidomain methods provide starting points for examining whether the three dimensions can be distinguished consistently and how they co-occur within instructional events.

Once a record is sufficiently reliable, comparative studies can estimate what information the RC organization contributes beyond indicators derived directly from established frameworks.

One approach is to compare a representation based on those frameworks with an additive representation containing the three RC response domains. A later configuration model would require prospective theoretical specification of particular combinations and their predicted consequences. Interactions, mismatch, complementarity, synergy, compensation, thresholds, weighting, and optimal combinations should be treated as questions for further theoretical development rather than predictions of the current specification.

Analytic and predictive value should be evaluated through appropriate representation of the competing accounts, replication, and validity evidence. Comparisons should estimate the contribution of the RC representation after established indicators have been modeled directly.

Findings supporting RC, null findings, and findings favoring established frameworks would each inform the interpretation of the proposed organization.

Practical Implications

For researchers, RC directs observation toward instructional events in which relational stance, responsibility for the work, and expressive enactment can be recorded together. A study could examine what prompted a response, which aspects were maintained or adjusted, and how the response related to the same focal task. This approach would allow researchers to investigate coordination within an event while measuring engagement and preserved intellectual demand independently. It could also support comparisons of enacted responses within the same instructional architecture, making the instructor's ongoing response a separately identifiable object of study.

For educators, RC could guide reflection on how they respond when learners encounter difficulty. An instructor could examine what evidence was available, how it was interpreted, what responsibility learners retained, and how expectations and support were communicated.

Considering those questions together would keep the response connected to the intellectual work underway. The joint functional target would also focus reflection on both continued engagement and the demands maintained during the interaction. RC could therefore help educators articulate how their relational, instructional, and expressive decisions were organized within a particular episode.

A research codebook could translate these questions into explicit rules for sampling events, recording evidence, coding responses, and measuring the functional target. Such a record would make the decisions underlying an RC analysis open to examination and comparison.

Studies of professional learning could then investigate how instructors develop coordination across the dimensions and how that coordination transfers into authentic teaching. These applications connect the framework's theoretical organization to the study and interpretation of instructional practice.

Research Priorities and Open Questions

The research priorities form a sequence. Reproducible event records and interpretable response labels provide the foundation for independent measurement of engagement and preserved demand. Comparative studies can then examine analytic and predictive value, and pathway studies can investigate relationships among response organization, the functional target, and subsequent outcomes. Whole-class feasibility, coordinated learning, and transfer connect that work to authentic instruction. This program connects the theoretical organization with the observations and comparisons needed to investigate it. Each stage addresses a defined relationship within the framework while preserving the distinctions among representation, explanation, evaluation, and application.

Conclusion

Relational Calibration addresses the instructor's work of sustaining engagement when intellectual difficulty emerges. It makes the organization of an enacted response central to understanding instructional responsiveness: how an instructor interprets emerging evidence, participates in the intellectual work, and communicates a response as the interaction unfolds.

Examining that coordination connects the immediate response to the continuing intellectual purpose of the task.

RC contributes an event-level account that keeps learners' engagement and the demands of their work jointly in view. It gives researchers and educators a way to examine how instructors adjust their responses while maintaining the intellectual work learners are being asked to do. Its contribution to understanding effective instruction lies in making this relationship explicit where teaching unfolds: the instructor's ongoing response to learners as they engage with demanding intellectual work.
