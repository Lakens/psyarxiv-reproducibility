Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How reproducibility_check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Introduction

Development is a lasting change in the way a person perceives and deals with their environment. (Bronfenbrenner, 1979) Immersion in graphical systems of communication has profound consequences for perception and behavior (Dehaene, 2009; Dehaene et al., 2010). Literacy acquisition -the process of learning to map meanings onto graphic forms -enhances meta-linguistic awareness (Olson, 2016), sharpens fine-grained visual discrimination (Duñabeitia et al., 2015), alters orientation processing (Fernandes et al., 2016), and shapes visual search strategies to align with reading direction (Woods et al., 2013). But prior to reading words, children in industrialized societies are almost constantly surrounded by symbolic and graphical artifacts -such as arrowsthat they come to understand and use long before school age (Amsel & Byrnes, 2002). The consequences of this constant but incidental type of enculturation on how children perceive symbolic artefacts are rarely studied -leaving open how children come to assign meaning to such symbols, which are among the first forms of graphic symbols they come to master. Here we present three studies tracing the comprehension of arrows and markers throughout the preschool years.

Symbolic reference often relies on iconicity or convention, such as a pedestrian crossing sign depicting a human silhouette. However, reference can also stem from spatio-temporal contiguity, as when a colored dot on an object signals ownership (Kanngiesser et al., 2019).

Symbols grounded in physical contact, proximity, or directionality are known as deictic (Burks, 1949; Peirce, 1998). In face-to-face interaction, physical actions like touching or holding objects establish early joint attention (Tomasello, 2010) and underpin the development of pointing (O'Madagain et al., 2019; O'Madagain, 2027) -arguably human communication's most foundational form of reference. In the graphic domain, markers and arrows serve as the visual analogues of touch and pointing. Markers represent the more intuitive cue, relying purely on spatial proximity to a referent. Arrows, by contrast, depend on convention: decoding them requires extrapolating an abstract vector from the arrowhead to a target -a interpretative process that is neither trivial (Jakobsen et al., 2013) nor inherent (Wittgenstein, 1953).

Studies with children routinely rely on their ability to follow pointing gestures and physical markers, so the timeline of children's grasp of these cues is well understood. In standard object-choice tasks, an experimenter hides a toy and indicates its location via pointing or placing a marker. Successful choice requires children to process both the cue and their partner's helpful intent (Tomasello, 2010). Children succeed with pointing by 12 months (Behne et al., 2012; Sodian & Thoermer, 2004; Tomasello et al., 2007) and with physical markers by 18 months (Tomasello et al., 1997; Zlatev et al., 2013). By contrast, developmental data on arrow-symbol comprehension remains scarce. Studies report group-level success between ages two and three when arrows are held above targets (Leekam et al., 2010), between 30 and 36 months for printed arrows placed between choices (Jakobsen et al., 2013), and by age four for on-screen displays (Lee et al., 1998).

However, these studies fail to pinpoint success across the third year and suffer from a key ambiguity: placing the arrowhead closer to its target than a distractor cues the location via proximity as well as direction. Consequently, it remains unclear whether young children understand arrows as vector symbols or merely markers. In the case of finger-pointing, O'Madagain and colleagues (2019) pitted finger proximity to one location against directional alignment with another to disentangle these two possible interpretations. Children aged 18 and 36 months favored targets cued by proximity, six-year-olds were indecisive, and only nine-year-olds and adults consistently followed the vector (O' Madagain et al., 2019). This indicates that strict vector extrapolation is a mature strategy, and raises the possibility that understanding arrows as vector symbols is similarly late-developing.

Attentional cueing studies often contrast gaze and arrows as prototypical natural and conventional cues (Ciardo et al., 2019; Ristic et al., 2002). A common aim is to test whether arrows guide attention due to a conceptual decoding of their direction (endogenous cueing), or rather a perceptual response to visual asymmetry (exogenous cueing) as arrowheads arguably carry more perceptual weight than their shafts (Colombo, 2001; Jakobsen et al., 2013). Isolating directional meaning from physical weight using variations of weighted as well as balanced double-headed arrows, Jakobsen and colleagues (2013) found that infants and 3-to 4-year-olds are driven by visual weight, whereas 5-to 6-year-olds and adults follow vector directions. They concluded that children properly understand arrows as vector symbols no sooner than five years of age (see Supplementary Figure 1 for an overview of stimulus arrangements used in the studies reported above).

From six years onward, perceptual cueing via arrows triggers reflexive orienting comparable to gaze -even when cues are uninformative, presented briefly, or despite explicit instructions to the contrary (Ciardo et al., 2019; Hommel et al., 2001; Senju et al., 2004; Wainwright & Bryson, 2005). By school age, arrow-following is so deeply ingrained that it is considered overlearned. That is, the processing of this conventional and acquired cue has become fully automatic and reflexive -reminiscent of developmental processes in literacy acquisition (Dehaene, 2009; Dehaene et al., 2010). Nevertheless, existing developmental accounts leave open whether proximity or vector direction drives arrow interpretation in younger children, and precisely when a mature directional interpretation takes precedence. Since only orientation can be understood as a genuine abstract interpretation of the arrow, while proximity is a 'natural' feature of the graphic element, the transition between proximity and orientation gives us a way to check at what age children's ability to apply abstract meanings to graphic elements truly begins.

To address this, the three studies presented below smoothly trace the development of graphical deixis across the preschool years employing novel stimulus configurations. Study 1 tests spontaneous arrow comprehension free of spatial proximity or perceptual-weight confounds, while contrasting performance directly with graphic markers. Study 2 disentangles the competing roles of spatial proximity and vector direction in children's interpretation of arrows by presenting ambiguous displays, and Study 3 pioneers a set of novel vector symbols to demonstrate when preschoolers productively generalize the vector principle to unfamiliar instances. The objectchoice paradigm employed in all three studies goes beyond attentional cueing and requires children to actively interpret and respond to these cues under conditions of minimal instruction and without feedback.

Study 1 -Markers and Arrows

To trace the onset of children's comprehension of arrows and markers, Study 1 tested children between 2 and 5 years of age using a picture-book-style object-choice task in which a cartoon agent was providing a marker or simplified arrow to guide children's choices. Based on the literature (Leekam et al., 2010; Tomasello et al., 1997; Zlatev et al., 2013), we derived the hypotheses that children would succeed earlier in the Marker condition than in the Arrow condition (H1), but would perform above chance in both conditions by age four (H2).

All participants came from a predominantly white population of middle to high income families and were recruited in [MASKED FOR REVIEW], a major European city. Stimuli and Procedure

Tasks were presented on a screen in the style of an interactive picture-book (cf. Figure 1 A). To introduce the game, the experimenter (E) presented a cartoon monkey (Coco) who placed a banana under one of two cups in plain sight. Children were asked, "Where is the banana?" and indicated their choice by pointing. E provided immediate feedback ("Well done!" or "Hmm, let's try again!") while revealing the banana in 4 to 8 total familiarization trials depending on whether the children chose correctly or needed more training. After familiarization, E announced that the monkey wanted to play a new game where they could not know the banana's location. An occluder covered the lower half of the screen, obscuring the hiding process. E narrated, "Coco is going to help you find the banana,". As soon as the monkey placed a marker or arrow to guide children's choice, they were prompted "Where is the banana?". E provided no feedback during test trials and merely acknowledged responses with a neutral "Thank you!". Children were neither instructed to follow cues nor did E describe the cues as markers or arrows. Trial presentations lasted 20-30 seconds. Children completed up to 16 test trials -8 per conditionadding up to a 5-10 minute session per participant. The order of conditions was blocked and counterbalanced across participants as was the side of the target. To be included in the analysis, children were required to succeed in at least 66% of familiarization trials, and to complete a minimum of eight valid test trials.

In the Marker Condition, the monkey placed a circle directly on either hiding place, akin to a sticker covering the target partially (see Figure 1 B). In the Arrow Condition, the monkey placed an equilateral triangle in the bottom center of the screen, with one vertex directed at either the left or right target (Figure 1 C). Crucially, this cue was lacking an arrow shaft reducing its visual weight and the potential for exogenous cueing. Furthermore, the triangle was placed on a mid-line such that the bulk of its mass and, hence, greater visual weight was leaning towards the distractor. Finally, the triangle as such was ambiguous as each of the vertices may be seen as pointing out (Figure 1 D). Hence, children were required to appreciate the cue information provided by a helpful interlocutor, view and organize the entire display, and extrapolate a vector from one of the triangle's vertices to either referent. was sitting behind the children in order to not distract them while supervising data collection. Coding and Analyses

All familiarization and test trials (𝑁 = 1728) were coded for being valid (0/1). A total of 42 trials (2.43 %) were excluded due to children quitting early, not responding, for responses being unclear, or due to experimenter error. For all valid trials, children's choices were coded as correct or incorrect (1/0). A third of test and familiarization trials were judged by a second coder.

Coder agreement on trial validity (𝜅 = 1, 𝑝 < .000) and performance (𝜅 = 1, 𝑝 < .000) was unanimous. A final sample of 1152 valid test trials from 72 participants was submitted to analyses.

Using Bayesian logistic generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs; Bernoulli family with a logit link), we modeled participants binary choices (0 = 𝑖𝑛𝑐𝑜𝑟𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑡,1 = 𝑐𝑜𝑟𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑡) as a function of condition (Marker/Arrow) and age to estimate the probability of children interpreting cues correctly. Our Model (𝑐𝑜𝑟𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑡 ∼ 𝑐𝑜𝑛𝑑𝑖𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛 * 𝑐. 𝑎𝑔𝑒 + 𝑐. 𝑡𝑟𝑖𝑎𝑙 + 𝑠𝑒𝑥 + (𝑐. 𝑡𝑟𝑖𝑎𝑙 | 𝑠𝑢𝑏𝑖𝑑)) contained fixed effects for condition and mean-centered age as well as their interaction while controlling for mean-centered trial number (c.trial) and sex. The Marker Condition was set as reference level. To account for individual variability, we included random intercepts by participant (subid) as well as random slopes for trial number. To evaluate our model formula, we compared the preregistered model with a reduced model containing the same predictors but lacking the interaction of condition and age via Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC) scores and weights (McElreath, 2018) as well as the difference in Expected Log Predictive Density (ELPD) via the function loo_compare. All models used default priors and were run in Stan (Stan Development Team, n.d.) via the function brm of the package brms (Bürkner, 2017).

Diagnostics and model comparisons are reported in the Supplementary Materials for brevity.

In order to define when children as a group perform above chance in the two conditions, we plotted model estimates as smoothed curves surrounded by their Credibility Intervals (CrI) (House, 2017; Kachel et al., 2026). Drawing values from the posterior predicted distribution via the function fitted, we created smooth developmental trajectories with 95% CrIs for children's performance. The criterion for settling when children performed above chance was essentially graphical: we approximated the point at which the lower 95% CrI for a each condition did no longer overlap with a mid-line demarcating the 50% chance level. All analyses were preregistered prior to data collection. We deviate from the preregistration when comparing models via ELPD differences (Sivula et al., 2020). While our primary conclusions draw on Bayesian inference, supplementary materials also provide conventional analyses binning participants according to their age in years (cf. Supplementary Table 1) to facilitate comparisons with prior literature. Above-chance performance was tested via two-tailed one-sample t-tests with a chance level set to .5 and presented along with Cohen's d.

Study 2 -Distance and Direction

Previous work did not disentangle the role of pointing direction and spatial proximity in the comprehension of arrows. In study 2, we tested children between 2 and 6 years of age in the same object-choice task but placed the arrows either closer to the target they are pointing at (Congruent), or closer to one target while pointing to the other (Incongruent, cf. Discussion

In three complementary studies we tested young children in picture-book-style symbolic object-choice-tasks to investigate their comprehension of markers, arrows and novel vector symbols. Study 1 established that children are capable of reading markers and arrows in the context of graphic displays at about 35 months of age. These findings contradict the presumption that only five-year-olds begin to grasp the conceptual meaning of arrows (Jakobsen et al., 2013).

As the study employed a reduced arrow cue lacking visual weight, results cannot be explained by exogenous cueing and low-level visual features. Rather, we find children to be able to follow directional cues in the absence of visual weight and proximity confounds just before their third birthday.

In Study 2, children completed tasks with either redundant or competing displays of arrow cues, where spatial proximity and vector direction aligned or conflicted. Here our goal was to understand at what age children prioritize the orientation of the symbol -the abstract content, that is -when proximity would predict a different target. In congruent cases, effects of proximity and direction generally added up, but children succeeded basically at the same ages in the Congruent condition as in the Marker and Arrow conditions of Study 1, which may be indicative of a developmental threshold: children just master dual representation at the end of the third year (DeLoache, 2011) and may not be able to succeed earlier when tested with deictic symbols in the graphic domain. The Incongruent condition yielded a remarkable developmental pattern: up to three and a half years children favored a proximity-based interpretation of the stimulus and systematically ignored pointing direction. Performance improved strongly between the fourth and fifth birthday and -by five years of age -extrapolating a vector from an arrow appears to be so internalized and habitual that children systematically ignored conflicting proximity cues leading to stable above-chance performance from 59 months onward. These results are in line with analogical results in the comprehension of the pointing gesture: children were found to overcome a proximity bias, and interpret pointing gestures purely in terms of their orientation, only after six years of age (O'Madagain et al., 2019). The results from the Incongruent condition indicate a strong change in the way young children spontaneously structure an ambiguous graphic array, switching from favoring a proximity-based interpretation driven by salience and perceptual grouping to a vector-based interpretation resting on an abstract cultural norm. Accordingly, we interpret these results to document the emergence of a specific form of visual expertise in the graphic domain that mirrors effects of literacy acquisition (Dehaene, 2009; Dehaene et al., 2010), but emerges significantly earlier. Results are also in line with documented shifts from exogenous to endogenous primacy in attentional cueing (Colombo, 2001; Hommel et al., 2001; Jakobsen et al., 2013; Wainwright & Bryson, 2005) as well as with the finding that arrow comprehension appears mature by six years of age (Landry et al., 2019).

In Study 3, targets were cued with various novel and unfamiliar vector symbols. Children decoded these novel cues spontaneously by about 41 months of age, and just at the onset of the interpretational shift documented in Study 2. To the extent that the cues had little (cf. Figure 5 D & E) to no visual weight (Figure 5 B, C, F) towards their pointing direction and appeared to be functionally equivalent, the results cannot be explained by exogenous cueing or low-level salience. Our preferred interpretation is that children around three and a half years of age have sufficiently internalized the principle of vector symbols that they systematically read such novel displays as arrows. With the exception of the use of a texture gradient in an attentional cueing paradigm (Brignani et al., 2009), this is the first time an experimental manipulation investigated children's proficiency with deictic symbols on cues that are not resembling conventional arrows, pointing gestures or gaze. Study 3 may thus provide a compelling demonstration of symbolic sensitivity (DeLoache, 2011), showing that growing familiarity with graphic conventions enhances children's ability to navigate novel symbolic forms. Finally, testing diverse types of unconventional vector symbols may offer fresh perspectives on the development of endogenous cueing, answering to a call for research that illustrates how attentional control is shaped by behavioral relevance in practical contexts (Ristic & Kingstone, 2012).

Together, our findings show a clear developmental trajectory in the interpretation of graphic deixis. Children read both arrows and markers by the end of their third year -coinciding with the onset of representational insight (DeLoache, 2011). However, both proximity and orientation can play a role in fixing the reference of these symbols. When these two referential principles conflict, children favor marker-based proximity up to 3.5 years; however, throughout the fourth year, this preference shifts toward directionality. Already at this stage, vectorprojection is not restricted to conventional arrows, but serves as a productive semiotic principle that children spontaneously apply to novel symbols in relevant contexts. By the fifth year, the vector-based interpretation of directional cues becomes so deeply ingrained that it dominates even against competing spatial evidence. We propose that growing up immersed in graphic environments fosters perceptual habits and symbolic expertise that lead children to treat specific graphic cues as inherently directional. Future research should directly track individual exposure to graphic symbols and provide cross-cultural comparisons in less graphically saturated contexts.

Methodologically, key next steps include testing whether these proximity-vector trade-offs extend to 3D physical spaces (O'Madagain et al., 2019) and using eye-tracking to capture realtime attentional allocation under cue conflict. Ultimately, the three studies presented here establish a clear empirical foundation for tracking graphic deixis across early childhood. They provide key examples for how enculturation and exposure to graphic conventions continuously shape young children's visual expertise and behavior from the dawn of symbolic competence to card as used by Lee et al. Effective sample sizes for all fixed effects in the full model (Bulk ESS, 𝑀 = 6741, range 2433 -16200) and the reduced model (Bulk ESS, 𝑀 =5573, range 1975-10819) were >1000, indicating reliable posterior estimations. For both models, PPCs indicated excellent fit, and Rhat valueswere equal to one. The gain in fit resulting from including an interaction of age and condition justifies the additional complexity. This is corroborated by comparing weights based on the WAIC: the full model gains 97.56% of the model weight, whereas only 2.44% of the weight is allocated to the reduced model. Finally, a comparison of the models' WAIC via ELPD shows that the difference (ELPD diff = -54.24) is much larger than its standard error (𝑆𝐸 = 9.85), showing that the full model would predict out of sample data much more accurately. In line with these results, we report estimates from the full model.
