Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Abstraction is central to human cognition, yet the term covers at least three senses that are routinely conflated: abstractness (how far a concept is removed from perceptible experience), specificity (how general or inclusive a category is), and the process by which conceptual change. We propose GEST-A (Grounded, Embodied, Situated Trajectories of Abstraction), a hierarchical and dynamic framework that separates these senses. Being abstract is a concept's position in a dimensional space of grounding (physical-world constraints), embodiment (body-specific sensorimotor and interoceptive experience), and situatedness (dependence on context of use), with specificity as orthogonal descriptor. Becoming abstract, in this view, is a trajectory through this space, driven by generalization and re-weighting of grounded, embodied, and situated content, and linked to a shift in feature structure from complete overlap to family resemblance.

Using number concepts to illustrate these dynamics, we position GEST-A against earlier hybrid accounts of grounding, clarify that linguistic labels are cues and binding devices rather than constituting concepts, and argue that abstract concepts constrain rather than refute embodied cognition. We also revisit concreteness ratings and the symbol grounding problem, and derive testable predictions. Abstract concepts reveal language as a powerful scaffold for building conceptual knowledge that exceeds individual sensorimotor experience.

1. Introduction

The capacity to form abstract concepts, that is, to extract regularities across experiences and represent them independently of particular instances, is fundamental to human intellectual achievement (Barsalou, 2005; Dove, 2016). From mathematics to morality, from justice to jealousy, abstract concepts permeate thought and communication. Yet, as with William James's remark about attention, everyone knows what abstraction is until asked to define it. James himself pressed the difficulty from the other side: we ought to speak of "a feeling of and, a feeling of if, a feeling of but," as readily as of a feeling of blue or of cold (James, 1890, vol. 1, pp. 245-246). Even the most abstract logical relations, on this view, have an experiential and situated character (see also Johnson, 2017, Chapter 2). This insight anticipated the contemporary debate about whether, and how, abstract concepts are grounded in experience (Friedrich et al., 2025; Kiefer & Trumpp, 2026).

That debate has been slowed by a terminological problem. The word "abstract" is used in (at least) three different senses: It is used for abstractness, the degree to which a concept is removed from perceptible, sensorimotor experience (JUSTICE versus CANDY); for generality, the degree to which a category is inclusive rather than specific (FOOD versus CANDY; JUSTICE versus RESTORATIVE JUSTICE); and for abstraction, the process by which concepts are formed, generalized, and transformed. These senses are related but not equivalent. BANANA and FRUIT are both concrete yet differ strongly in specificity; FREEDOM and FREEDOM OF SPEECH are both abstract yet differ in specificity too (Bolognesi et al., 2020). Conflating these senses has consequences: findings about "abstract concepts" may reflect either of these senses, and claims about conceptual grounding hold for one but not the other (Bolognesi, 2025).

This paper asks what a theory of abstract concepts must explain once these senses are separated, and develops a hierarchical and dynamic framework, GEST-A (Grounded, Embodied, Situated Trajectories of Abstraction), in response. GEST-A builds on the TEST theory of cognition (Myachykov et al., 2014), on similar hierarchical views of numerical cognition (Fischer, 2012 (Fischer, , 2018 (Fischer, , 2024)), and on our earlier commentary in this journal, which proposed that abstractness has grounded, embodied, and situated aspects (Myachykov & Fischer, 2019) in response to the account of abstract concepts as words used as social tools (Borghi et al., 2019).

Several ingredients of our argument are not new. Proposals that abstract concepts form a continuum rather than a dichotomy, that they are heterogeneous, that concreteness ratings are an imperfect measure, and that hybrid accounts combining grounded, linguistic, and intermediate representations are viable, all have a long history (Section 3). The novel contribution we claim is more specific, and it has five parts: 1.

A separation of abstractness, specificity, and abstraction, with working definitions that can be mapped onto a recent consensus glossary (Reilly et al., 2025) (Section 2).

2. A coordinate system for abstractness in which three ordered constraints (grounding, embodiment, situatedness) jointly position each concept, with proposals for how each coordinate can be measured (Sections 4 and 5).

3. A dynamic account in which becoming abstract is a trajectory through that space, tied to changes in feature structure (Pulvermüller, 2013 (Pulvermüller, , 2018a (Pulvermüller, , 2023) ) (Section 6). 4. A deliberately limited claim about language: linguistic labels cue, bind, and coordinate concepts, but do not constitute them (Sections 2.3 and 6.3).

5. A set of testable predictions, identifying outcomes that would count against GEST-A (Section 9). Section 2 sets out terminology. Section 3 positions GEST-A against earlier hybrid accounts. Sections 4 and 5 describe being abstract and its relation to brain-based and simulation theories. Section 6 describes becoming abstract and works through number concepts as a case study. Section 7 revisits the symbol grounding problem, Section 8 draws methodological consequences, Section 9 states predictions, and Sections 10 and 11 consider implications for development and education.

2. Terminology: Abstractness, abstraction, specificity, and the concept-word distinction 2.1 Abstractness, concreteness, and specificity A recent multidisciplinary consensus project defined "abstraction" as the process of forming general ideas or concepts by extracting similarities across instances, and separated it explicitly from "abstractness", a property of concepts or words (Reilly et al., 2025). The glossary also records principled views on how abstractness should be defined, which shows that the field has not converged. We adopt this process-property distinction and use the terms as summarized in Box 1. By abstractness we mean the degree to which a concept's content is detached from direct, perceptible, bodily experience. It is graded, and we argue that it is expressed by three dimensions (Section 4). Concreteness, as indexed by rating studies, is its conventional opposite and serves as an empirical proxy, not a definition. Specificity (or its inverse, generality) refers to where a category sits in an inclusion hierarchy, that is, its semantic granularity.

The empirical decoupling of concreteness and specificity is now well documented. Bolognesi et al. (2020) operationalized and tested the relation between conceptual concreteness and categorical specificity and argued that they should be treated as separate. Muraki and Pexman (2026) have since published specificity norms for 8,500 English words, and Bolognesi (2025) argues that specificity is a missing piece in theories of embodied language comprehension. From this, we draw two conclusions: First, ratings of concreteness and ratings of specificity measure different things, and a theory of abstract concepts must say which of them it addresses. Second, embodiment is unlikely to be a unitary phenomenon, operating identically across conceptual categories: How a concept is grounded depends not only on perceptual accessibility but also on semantic granularity, and linguistic and other contextual constraints. GEST-A is primarily a theory of abstractness and of the abstraction process that alters it. It treats specificity as an orthogonal descriptor (Section 4.5).

2.2 Abstraction as a process

We distinguish two sub-processes that the single word "abstraction" covers.

Generalization is the extraction of similarity across instances, accompanied by loss of specificity; this is the sense captured in Reilly et al.'s (2025) consensus glossary.

Detachment is the progressive re-weighting of grounded, embodied, and situated content through which a concept's abstractness increases. The two can dissociate: FRUIT is more general than BANANA but not necessarily more abstract. Conversely, a child's THREE, initially anchored in fingers and subitized arrays, can become a node in a relational number system (Section 6.5) without becoming more inclusive. "Becoming abstract" in the present article refers to detachment, with generalization being one among several routes.

2.3 Concepts, words, and labels

A second source of confusion concerns the relation between concepts and words. We take a concept to be a mental representation of a category, kind, or relation that supports categorization, inference, and action, and that can in principle exist and operate without a word. Three observations motivate this separation.

First, concepts exist outside language. Preverbal infants and nonhuman animals categorize objects and discriminate quantities, and adults hold concepts for which they have no ready word. Second, one lexical form can map onto several concepts. MARS the planet, MARS the Roman god, and MARS the confectionery are distinct concepts that happen to share a form; they are not three instances of one concept. Such polysemy and homonymy matter for how word-level ratings should be interpreted, but they are a lexical phenomenon and not evidence of contextual flexibility within a single concept. Third, dissociations between number concepts and number words (for example, preserved exact calculation despite severe agrammatism; Varley et al., 2005) suggest that even the most language-dependent concepts are not identical to the words that name them.

What, then, is the role of language? We distinguish three relations between a label and a concept. As a cue, the label activates or constrains a concept in context (Lupyan & Lewis, 2019). As a binding device or placeholder, the label links instances that share no common perceptible feature during learning and maintenance, so that a coherent category can form and persist (Carey, 2009; Dobler et al., 2024 Dobler et al., , 2026;; Pulvermüller, 2018a). As a coordination device, the label allows members of a community to align and negotiate meanings (Borghi et al., 2019). We do not claim the stronger thesis that the label is constitutive of the concept, so that removing the label would remove the concept. We claim language-dependence, not language-identity: For concepts whose instances lack shared perceptible features, the formation, coherence, and stability of the category depend causally on a label or another stable symbol. This dependence is graded, varies between concepts, and is empirically testable. A "label" in this sense need not be a word of a natural language; numerals, notations, and gestures can serve.

Finally, concepts "inside" and "outside" language are not two kinds of thing. A concept formed largely through linguistic input, such as DEMOCRACY, can be used in nonverbal reasoning, and a concept formed through direct experience, such as HAMMER, can be named, described, and revised through language. GEST-A describes how much of a given concept's content and organization relies on each source at a given time, not a dichotomy between language-based and experience-based concepts.

3.1 Hybrid accounts of grounding

The idea that abstract concepts combine components that are grounded in experience with components that are linguistic, and perhaps components in between, is not new. Harnad's (1990) solution to the symbol grounding problem (how do concepts become meaningful?) is a hybrid of three representations: iconic (analog projections of sensory input), categorical (learned invariance detectors), and symbolic (names and propositions composed from grounded names). Paivio's (1986) dual-coding theory posits both verbal and imagistic systems whose relative contributions differ for abstract and concrete words.

Similarly, Prinz (2002), Chatterjee (2010), and Dove (2014 Dove ( , 2016 Dove ( , 2020) each, in different ways, treat concepts as composites of grounded, linguistic, and intermediate components. Barsalou and colleagues' language and situated simulation account holds that linguistic and simulation systems interact during conceptual processing (Barsalou et al., 2008; Barsalou & Wiemer-Hastings, 2005). Vigliocco et al. (2009) and Andrews et al. (2009) argued that experiential and distributional information are complementary sources of meaning. Borghi et al. (2017 Borghi et al. ( , 2019) ) also emphasize multiple sources of grounding, including social and introspective experience, and the role of words as social tools. Zwaan (2014) is an important precursor with respect to situatedness and displaced reference, and Louwerse (2011) showed that language itself encodes perceptual and embodied relations. Brain-constrained modelling (Pulvermüller, 2013 (Pulvermüller, , 2018a (Pulvermüller, , 2023) ) supplies a mechanistic account of how labels interact with feature structure. 3.2 What GEST-A retains and what it changes

We retain the core of the hybrid view: no single code accounts for all concepts, and the contribution of language grows as the contribution of perceptible instances shrinks.

However, GEST-A differs in four respects. First, we propose dimensions rather than representational types. In Harnad's scheme the representational types together make up a symbol-grounding system. In ours, grounding, embodiment, and situatedness are coordinates that characterize any individual concept at a given time. Every concept has a position on all three, and the same concept can occupy different positions at different times or in different contexts. Second, we suggest a decomposed sensory base. The grounded dimension concerns invariants of the physical world, such as gravity and stable object locations, that constrain what can be learned (Friedrich et al., 2024). The embodied dimension concerns body-specific and experience-dependent states, including interoception and culturally shaped motor habits, such as finger counting. Our use of "embodiment" is therefore narrower than Barsalou's "grounded cognition", which covers all modal re-enactments. To avoid ambiguity, we use "grounded cognition" for the broader research programme and "embodiment" only for learning-related constraints.

Third, we identify context-dependence or situatedness, rather than linguistic labels, as the most influential cognitive constraint. Task, social, and formal-system contexts matter, while a label is only one mechanism among several. Fourth, GEST-A treats being abstract and becoming abstract as two aspects of one dynamic description: It poses both a coordinate system and trajectories through it at the time scales of moment-to-moment context, individual development, and cultural history.

3.3 Do abstract concepts challenge embodied cognition?

It is sometimes said that abstract concepts challenge embodied cognition because they possess no sensorimotor features (e.g., Mahon & Caramazza, 2008).

That statement needs qualification. If embodied cognition means that every concept is directly and exclusively grounded in modal re-enactment, so that cognition is embodied from top to bottom, then abstract concepts that lack a sensorimotor core do challenge it. The resolution we propose, that some representations are directly grounded whereas others depend on language and on other grounded concepts, is incompatible with that strong view. What abstract concepts do not challenge is a hierarchical, systemic version of grounded cognition of the kind that Harnad's (Vincent-Lamarre et al., 2016) hybrid position already allows. Our claim is therefore that abstract concepts constrain what embodied cognition can mean. They rule out monolithic accounts and require grounded cognition to be hierarchical and language-inclusive.

Importantly, pursuing a dimensional approach to meaning has a tradition. Osgood et al. (1957) proposed that connotative meaning is organized along evaluation, potency, and activity. Dodds et al. (2026) recently challenged that framework and its successors with large-scale corpus analyses, arguing that meaning is better described by power, danger, and structure. These frameworks concern the affective-evaluative meaning of words, whereas ours concerns the sources of conceptual content, so these views are compatible.

They also illustrate a more general point, namely that no single axis, including the concrete-abstract continuum, spans conceptual space.

4. Being abstract: A hierarchical, three-dimensional framework

GEST-A rests on a hierarchy of constraints on cognition (Fischer, 2012 (Fischer, , 2018 (Fischer, , 2024;; Myachykov et al., 2014; Myachykov & Fischer, 2019; see also Körner et al., 2023; Pezzulo et al., 2011 Pezzulo et al., , 2013)). Grounding constrains embodiment, and embodiment constrains situatedness. We first describe each dimension and then how they combine.

4.1 Grounding: Constraints of the physical world

Grounding concerns the extent to which a concept maintains a connection to observable, physical referents and to the invariant structure of the world (Shepard, 1984; Friedrich et al., 2024). Gravity, stable object locations, and object permanence constrain what organisms can perceive. These constraints are evolutionarily mediated and hierarchically foundational (the "tropic" component of Myachykov et al., 2014). On the ontological reading of abstractness, DEMOCRACY, LOVE, and ANGER are abstract because they lack bounded physical referents, whereas STONES, BOOKS, and DOGS are concrete.

This corresponds to the philosophical contrast between natural kinds, which exist independently of human observers, and social kinds, which depend on human agreement and institutions (Dunn, 2015). A stone would exist without humans to perceive it; democracy would not. The connection between a concept and the world it is about (Pezzulo & Castelfranchi, 2007) acts as a hard constraint and a precondition for embodiment.

Two caveats apply. First, equating ungrounded concepts with social kinds is not fully appropriate. BLUE is grounded in sensorimotor experience, yet where people draw its boundaries is shaped by social and linguistic factors (Lupyan et al., 2020). Grounding is therefore one dimension of abstractness and not its whole. Second, concepts without physical referents plausibly emerged from the need to conceptualize relations that cannot be pointed to in physical space but are crucial for social coordination, such as roles, norms, and moral principles (Borghi et al., 2019). They are abstract not because they are less real or less important, but because their content cannot be anchored in a bounded physical referent.

4.2 Embodiment: Body-specific experience

Embodiment concerns the body-specific, experience-dependent states associated with a concept: its sensory and motor capacities (Lynott et al., 2020), interoception and emotion (Tallon-Baudry, 2023), and learned cultural practices (Göbel et al., 2011). It is contingent on grounded structure, since the body evolved within, and is constrained by, the physical world.

Embodiment can vary independently of grounding. The planet MARS is ontologically concrete, yet experientially remote, since most people have no direct sensorimotor contact with it. LOVE, by contrast, is ontologically abstract but experientially rich: most people have embodied experience of loving and being loved, which is reflected in the abundance of similar conceptual metaphors across languages (Kövecses, 2003; Myachykov & Fischer, 2019). A concept can therefore be physically real yet experientially remote, or physically abstract yet experientially rich. The embodiment dimension captures the density and variety of sensorimotor and interoceptive traces associated with a concept, whether or not those traces connect to a bounded physical referent. Because the traces are modality-specific, embodiment is not a single quantity but a profile across the senses, interoception, and action.

4.3 Situatedness: Context of use

Situatedness concerns how far a concept's content is determined by the context in which it is formed and used, rather than by invariant world constraints or by the body (Robbins & Aydede, 2009; Casper & Artese, 2023). Relevant contexts include the task and goal, the social situation, the surrounding discourse, and, for formal concepts, the symbolic system in which they are defined. Situated constraints are transient, but they shape ongoing processing most strongly, because they serve goal accomplishment (Fischer, 2012 (Fischer, , 2024)). Abstractness is therefore not a fixed property but varies with use (Wittgenstein, 1953).

Two properties of situatedness should be kept apart. The weight of the situated component is how much of a concept's content is fixed by context of use rather than by world and body. Its variability is how much that content changes across contexts. To illustrate, JUSTICE has a high situated weight and high variability, because what counts as just shifts with legal, moral, and interpersonal context. PRIME NUMBER also has a high situated weight, because its content is fixed entirely by definitions within a formal system, but very low variability, because that system is explicit and stable. Separating weight from variability removes an apparent paradox, in which precise formal concepts seem at once highly situated and context-free. Variability can be estimated from corpora with measures of contextual or semantic diversity (Hoffman et al., 2013).

Context-dependence within a single concept is well documented. The features that come to mind for PIANO differ depending on whether one is imagining moving it or hearing it played, and context-dependent properties are retrieved only in situations that make them relevant (Barsalou, 1982; Yee & Thompson-Schill, 2016). In our terms, the same concept shifts along the embodiment dimension as context changes: it is more embodied in a moving context (weight, grasp, effort) and less so in a context of music theory. This is different from polysemy, where distinct concepts share a lexical form.

Linguistic context is the most important situating context for abstract concepts.

Distributional learning, that is, learning from the co-occurrence of words with other words, is one mechanism by which linguistic context shapes conceptual content. We therefore place it within situatedness: it is a route by which the linguistic context of use becomes part of a concept (Andrews et al., 2009; Louwerse, 2011; Lenci, 2018).

4.5 Where specificity fits

Specificity is not a fourth coordinate of the same kind, because it describes a category's place in an inclusion hierarchy and not the source of its content. We treat it as an orthogonal descriptor that interacts with the three dimensions. BANANA and FRUIT share high grounding and embodiment but differ in specificity. The more general FRUIT subsumes instances with different perceptual features and should therefore tend to evoke more variable simulations. At the abstract end, FREEDOM OF SPEECH is more specific than FREEDOM and is also more constrained by context. This generates predictions that cannot be derived from concreteness alone. The two sub-processes move concepts along different axes: generalization moves a concept up the inclusion hierarchy, whereas detachment moves it through the three-dimensional space.

4.6 Emotion is not a further dimension

Some accounts hold that abstract concepts differ from concrete ones in being more emotional (Kousta et al., 2011; Vigliocco et al., 2014). However, when full data distributions are plotted instead of fitted curves, the U-shaped relation between emotionality and concreteness appears to be driven by clusters of items (Winter, 2022).

Emotional valence explains little variance in concreteness ratings and appears orthogonal to abstractness, not constitutive of it. The dispute concerns how general the special role of emotion is, not whether emotion matters for some abstract concepts. We therefore do not list emotion as a separate dimension. Affective and interoceptive content enters through embodiment, and it varies systematically within abstract subtypes, being strongest for social-emotional concepts and weakest for philosophical-logical ones (Villani et al., 2019), rather than tracking the concrete-to-abstract transition itself.

4.7 Abstract concepts are not a unitary class

Cluster analyses with multiple rating dimensions consistently reveal subgroups of abstract concepts, such as social-emotional (LOVE, ANGER), philosophical-logical (TRUTH, NECESSITY), and numerical-mathematical (INFINITY, PROBABILITY), that differ systematically in cognitive profile (Villani et al., 2019; Muraki et al., 2022). In GEST-A the subtypes of abstract concepts occupy different regions within the same space and do not require separate theories. Their heterogeneity nevertheless has a theoretical consequence: any account of "abstract concepts" must say which subtypes it addresses, since a theory developed around social-emotional concepts may fail for mathematical ones and vice versa. Feature structure, introduced in Section 6.2, cross-cuts the subtypes. FEAR, with consistent bodily signatures across instances, differs from JEALOUSY, whose instances vary across social scenarios, although both are socialemotional. ADDITION with small numbers differs likewise from INFINITY within mathematics.

5. Relation to brain-based and simulation accounts

>Relation to brain-based and simulation accounts

5.1 Brain-constrained models

GEST-A is compatible with neurobiological constraints for each of its three dimensions.

For grounding, physical constraints have shaped the sensory capacities, motor constraints, and connectivity of human brains over phylogeny and development (Neubauer et al., 2018). For embodiment, repeated sensorimotor activity shapes representations through synaptic adjustment (Qin et al., 2023). For situatedness, contextual demands transiently adjust neural processing through attentional mechanisms (Fiebelkorn & Kastner, 2019). These are plausibility arguments, not tests of GEST-A.

Brain-constrained computational modelling gives a more specific mechanistic account (Pulvermüller, 2013 (Pulvermüller, , 2018a (Pulvermüller, , 2023)). In Pulvermüller's account most concepts lie on a continuum and are held together by features that are shared to different degrees. Concrete concepts typically show perceptual overlap, in that all instances share defining features.

Many abstract concepts instead show a family-resemblance structure, in which features are shared pairwise but no single feature is common to all instances. In network simulations this difference leads to distinct learning patterns, and it explains why abstract concepts weight situated components more heavily. Their representational variability requires linguistic labels as compensatory binding mechanisms for conceptual coherence (Dobler et al., 2024 (Dobler et al., , 2026)). The results come from simulations constrained by neuroanatomy and neurophysiology, and direct evidence from human learners is more limited; Section 9 states what such evidence would look like.

5.2 Simulation theories

GEST-A is also compatible with simulation theories (reviewed in Friedrich et al., 2025), most prominently Barsalou's situated simulation theory (Barsalou, 1999 (Barsalou, , 2008)). On this view, concepts are not static amodal symbols but dynamic, multimodal representations in the brain's systems for perception, action, and introspection. Cognitive processing reenacts relevant states in a selective, context-sensitive way. Simulations for abstract concepts may be more schematic, more reliant on introspective and social content, and more tightly coupled with linguistic forms (Barsalou & Wiemer-Hastings, 2005; Borghi et al., 2022).

This theoretical commensurability has several implications: First, simulation is a plausible mechanism for the embodied dimension: for HAMMER it involves motor simulation of grasping and swinging, whereas for DEMOCRACY it involves social scenarios, evaluations, and linguistic contexts. Second, the context-sensitive tailoring of simulations to current goals is a plausible mechanism for the situated dimension, and it explains why a concept's position shifts with context. Third, simulation theories agree that highly abstract concepts are multimodal but may draw more on interoceptive and social experience than on object-oriented sensorimotor experience. JUSTICE may be grounded in simulations of emotional responses to fairness, in memories of social interactions, and in experience of legal discourse, which complements the multiplegrounding view (Borghi et al., 2017; Villani et al., 2019). Finally, simulation theories also acknowledge a role for language: linguistic forms can trigger simulations, constrain them, and provide schematic frames for organizing multimodal experience (Barsalou et al., 2008; Casto et al., 2025). Abstract concepts may therefore rely more heavily on linguistic cues to coordinate complex and heterogeneous simulations.

6. Becoming abstract: Abstraction as a dynamic process

In Section 4, we described concepts at a point in time. However, abstraction is the process by which concepts move through the hypothesized 3D space, and it also operates on multiple time scales: Moment to moment, as context shifts a concept's position; over individual development; and over cultural and evolutionary history. In the sense of detachment, a central feature of becoming abstract is a progressive reduction in the weight of embodied content relative to linguistically mediated and situated content. We see this as re-weighting and not as loss.

6.1 Generalization, simplification, and displacement

One route to becoming abstract is the gradual removal of properties of individual objects, which yields more general, automated, and simplified concepts. The process includes generalizing across instances to extract common features, simplifying by discarding irrelevant detail, automating access through familiarity, and categorizing by grouping entities with shared properties. Generalization lowers specificity.

Displacement is a second route, and it is more directly linked to detachment. Early communication was plausibly anchored in the here and now, but over evolutionary and developmental time language acquired the capacity to refer to absent entities, past and future times, hypothetical scenarios, and imagined situations (Zwaan, 2014). This progression frees reference from perceptual anchoring. Reference to present, perceivable entities can draw on rich sensorimotor simulation, whereas reference to displaced or imagined entities relies more heavily on linguistic representation with attenuated embodied content.

6.2 Feature structure and the shift from complete overlap to family resemblance

Changes in feature structure provide the mechanistic link between being and becoming abstract. Concrete concepts, and some abstract ones, show complete feature overlap: all instances share features that can be directly experienced. HAMMER involves grasping, swinging, and striking across all exemplars, so a conceptual system can extract the common sensorimotor pattern through repeated experience. Many other concepts show family resemblance (Wittgenstein, 1953): DEMOCRACY is instantiated by voting with raised hands, by ballot boxes, and by parliamentary procedure, and no single perceptible or action feature characterizes all of them, yet instances share features pairwise.

Brain-constrained network simulations indicate that concepts with family-resemblance structure are formed and maintained differently from those with complete overlap. Verbal symbols support the formation of concrete concepts but are enabling for abstract ones (Pulvermüller, 2018a (Pulvermüller, , 2023;; Dobler et al., 2024 Dobler et al., , 2026)). The label "democracy" can bind instances that share only partial features into a coherent representation despite the absence of complete overlap.

On this account, becoming abstract often involves a shift from complete overlap towards family resemblance as a concept is applied to more varied instances and contexts. The conceptual shift connects static and dynamic descriptions. A concept's position in the 3D space (for example, low embodiment and high situated weight) is the outcome of a history of re-weighting, and feature structure explains why re-weighting requires linguistic binding. It also implies that feature structure, and not abstractness as such, should predict how much linguistic scaffolding a concept needs. FEAR, with consistent bodily signatures across instances, requires little; JEALOUSY and JUSTICE, whose instances vary across social scenarios, require more. A concrete-seeming concept with family-resemblance structure, such as GAME, should show a labelling benefit that its concreteness alone would not predict.

6.3 What labels do and do not do

The role of language in this process of conceptual shift needs a precise statement because it is the claim most open to misreading. The claim defended here is that, for concepts with family-resemblance structure, a label or other stable symbol plays a binding role in learning and maintaining the category, and that its contribution grows as shared perceptible features shrink. This does not make the label the concept.

There is evidence that labels do bind. In infants, hearing a novel word applied to a set of objects promotes categorization of those objects (Waxman & Markow, 1995), and in brain-constrained simulations labels improve category formation and persistence (Pulvermüller, 2018a; Dobler et al., 2024 Dobler et al., , 2026)). Abstract concepts are acquired late, and their acquisition benefits from explicit labelling and from the situations in which labels are used. Whether the labelling benefit is disproportionate for family-resemblance concepts, relative to concepts with complete overlap and matched on concreteness, is one of the predictions we make.

There is also evidence that labels are not identical to concepts. Severely agrammatic patients can retain the ability to reason about exact numbers and to calculate (Varley et al., 2005), which suggests that the grammatical machinery of natural language is not what constitutes number concepts. The symbols that support exact number are not restricted to spoken words: numerals, finger-counting postures and routines, and written notation can all serve (Wasner et al., 2014; Sixtus et al., 2023). Our term "label" therefore covers any stable symbol that can be attached to a set of instances.

The two accounts of labels as constitutive vs. binding make different predictions. If labels were constitutive, a concept should be lost when its label is lost, and a label presented without instances should be sufficient to form the concept. If labels are binding devices, removing the label should degrade category coherence in a graded way while instancebased structure remains partly intact, and a label without instances should not yield a usable concept. Section 9 states these as separate predictions.

Finally, language is known to influence thought in abstract domains such as time (Boroditsky, 2001) and exact number (Frank et al., 2008). Whether such linguistic effects are larger for abstract concepts than for concrete ones has not been tested with matched materials, and we state it as a prediction and not as an established fact.

6.4 Trajectories need not point towards abstractness

The account described so far might suggest a single path from rich sensorimotor grounding to linguistic abstraction. Developmental evidence shows that this is too simple.

Abstract concepts such as JUSTICE are learned late, and one explanation is that they are learned largely from linguistic and distributional regularities, whereas concrete concepts are learned through ostensive links to referents (Sloutsky & Deng, 2019). Distributional models show that considerable semantic structure can be recovered from word cooccurrence (Lenci, 2018), and early in acquisition words co-occur mainly with their referents, whereas later they increasingly co-occur with other words, a shift in salience from word-to-world to word-to-word associations (Bolognesi, 2020; Louwerse, 2008 Louwerse, , 2011)). For adults, distributional profiles may be stronger than direct word-to-world connections for many concepts, with embodied content remaining accessible but no longer dominant.

Three consequences follow from this proposal: First, for a concept such as JUSTICE the starting point is already situated: it is acquired through language and social interaction and begins with a high situated weight. Second, such a concept can later move away from abstractness through experience. Novel concepts learned from language alone can be rapidly mapped onto the body schema (Günther et al., 2020), abstract or socially complex concepts can be represented on a body map that organizes affective and introspective states alongside sensorimotor information (Gatti et al., 2024), and even statistical concepts such as regression to the mean can be grounded through suitable educational experience (Fischer et al., 2022). Number concepts span the whole range from perceptible quantity to formal abstraction, and their development and cultural history are well studied (Fischer, 2018; Sixtus et al., 2023). We use them to show how a concept moves through the proposed 3D space and how the role of labels changes along the way. Figure 2 depicts four stages.

In Stage 1, perceptual grounding, small quantities within the subitizing range (about one to four items) are immediately apprehended and can be embodied in finger counting.

These representations show complete feature overlap: all representations of "three" involve the same quantity and, in a given culture, the same number of fingers. The particular fingers used reflect culturally shaped (embodied) and context-specific (situated) constraints (Pika et al., 2009; Lindemann et al., 2011; Wasner et al., 2014).

Even here, number representations remain imprecise until children acquire languagebased knowledge such as number words and arithmetic facts (Mix et al., 2002).

In Stage 2, linguistic binding, quantities beyond the subitizing range shift from perceptually grounded quantities to linguistic-symbolic constructions (see the contrast between knowers of particular numbers and learners who have grasped the cardinality principle; Sarnecka, 2021). SEVEN can be instantiated as seven fingers, seven objects in an array, the numeral 7, the word "seven," or a position in a counting sequence. These instances share features pairwise but lack a single perceptual core, and a stable label binds them into one category (Sixtus et al., 2023). This resembles the placeholder role that Carey (2009) proposed for the counting list, which children first learn as a sequence whose meaning is filled in later through bootstrapping. The placeholder is not the number concept; it is the structure through which the concept is built.

In Stage 3, relational structuring, the meaning of concepts, such as fractions and negative numbers, depends on their position in a formal system of rules and relationships.

Properties such as divisibility and decomposability are inherently relational and propositional (Shapiro, 1997), and manipulating numbers relies heavily on languagespecific processes (Göbel et al., 2011).

In Stage 4, axiomatic instantiation, concepts such as imaginary numbers and different orders of infinity have no possible sensory analogue, and their content is fixed by explicit definitions and inferential relations within an axiomatic system. 6.6 Re-weighting, not loss

The empirical signature of detachment is a changed distribution of processing and a changed vulnerability to interference. Meta-analyses of neuroimaging show that concrete and abstract words engage partly different networks: concrete words engage more perceptual and association regions, and abstract words engage more left inferior frontal and anterior temporal, language-related regions (Binder et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2010).

The contrast in sensorimotor cortex itself is not uniform across studies, and we regard the imaging evidence as suggestive only. Behaviorally, concrete concept processing is disrupted by concurrent sensorimotor tasks (Scorolli & Borghi, 2007), whereas abstract concept processing is more vulnerable to tasks that compete for linguistic or workingmemory resources (Borghi & Zarcone, 2016; Fini et al., 2022). This pattern suggests that abstract concepts depend more on linguistically mediated binding, which is itself a limited resource.

Reduced embodiment does not imply reduced richness or precision. PRIME NUMBER is extraordinarily precise despite minimal sensorimotor content (Table 2): low in grounding and embodiment, high in situated weight because it is defined within a formal system, and low in variability because that system is stable. Its precision derives from definitions and relational position, not from embodied experience, and this combination of minimal sensorimotor footprint and maximal definitional precision is itself a signature of advanced abstraction. Embodied traces are backgrounded by efficient symbolic routines but not erased. Finger-based representations influence symbolic number processing in adults across cultures (Dehaene, 2011, pp. 181-183; Domahs et al., 2010; but see Lepoittevin et al., 2025), and latent embodied content can be expected to return under load or in unfamiliar situations.

7. The symbol grounding problem revisited 7.1 What grounding requires

A widespread assumption holds that every concept must be directly grounded in sensorimotor experience in order to be meaningful, so that abstract concepts, which lack a bounded and perceivable referent, pose a challenge. This reading goes beyond Harnad (1990). His claim is that the conceptual system as a whole requires grounding, to avoid the infinite regress of symbols defined only by other symbols, not that every individual symbol must be grounded directly. Analyses of dictionary structure suggest that only a minority of words needs direct grounding, with the remainder learnable from definitions built on grounded words (Vincent-Lamarre et al., 2016). The universal grounding assumption nevertheless motivates a research programme that seeks grounding for every abstract concept, typically by correlating abstractness with interoception, emotional valence, or social interaction. Borghi et al. (2017), for example, read the symbol grounding problem as requiring that symbols ultimately be grounded in their world referents, and the challenge of abstract concepts follows from the absence of such referents.

Two levels of analysis are often conflated here: the structural organization of concepts (complete feature overlap versus family resemblance) and the mechanism of semantic reference (direct grounding versus linguistic mediation). This conflation produces divergent solutions: The systemic solution holds that abstract concepts are coherent because they are linguistically defined in terms of other, ultimately grounded concepts, so that the apparent challenge dissolves (Goldinger et al., 2016). The strong embodiment solution holds that the challenge is real and is met by a richer, distributed grounding in social, emotional, introspective, and linguistic experience, that is, multiple grounding (Borghi et al., 2017).

GEST-A does not force a choice between them. With respect to organization, it tracks the transition of a concept's dominant mode from sensorimotor to linguistic-relational, which fits the systemic account. With respect to content, it tracks the transformation of grounding, which fits the multiple-grounding account. A concept's grounding changes and is re-weighted instead of disappearing, and linguistic binding repurposes embodied content instead of replacing it. This is a hybrid position, and it does not claim that debates about the best reading of Harnad (1990) are settled.

7.2 When perception is absent

Research on congenitally blind individuals is a critical test case. Blind individuals develop rich and accurate knowledge of visual attributes that they have never perceived directly, including colors, visual verbs such as "glitter," and animal appearances (Bedny et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2019 Kim et al., , 2021)), and this knowledge is predicted by distributional semantics, that is, the statistical structure of word co-occurrence (Lewis et al., 2019). The finding does not show that such concepts are purely linguistic, since they are certainly integrated with, and scaffolded by, other grounded modalities such as touch, audition, and social explanation. It shows that linguistic distributional information can provide a dominant organizing scaffold for conceptual coherence when a primary perceptual modality is missing. For abstract concepts in sighted individuals the parallel is that, where stable perceptual commonality is absent, labels and their distributional relations can become a primary organizing principle.

7.3 Direct grounding where feature overlap is complete

The family-resemblance structure of many abstract concepts requires linguistic support for their acquisition, but it does not follow that all abstract concepts lack direct grounding. Some highly abstract concepts retain complete feature overlap across instances. CAUSATION, arguably among the most abstract concepts humans have, can be grounded in repeated action-perception sequences such as pushing, pulling, and pressing, which share a common causal structure across all instances (Pulvermüller, 2018b; Wolff, 2007). Fear, anger, and joy have characteristic bodily signatures (arousal, facial expression, action tendencies), and emotion concepts can be grounded in interoceptive and motor systems (Seth, 2013; Winkielman et al., 2018 Winkielman et al., , 2023)). This contrasts with DEMOCRACY or JUSTICE, where instances share features only pairwise and linguistic labels become necessary for coherence (Barsalou & Wiemer-Hastings, 2005; Borghi & Zarcone, 2016). The relevant distinction is therefore not between abstract and concrete concepts; it is between concepts with complete overlap, which permit direct grounding, and concepts with family-resemblance structure, which require binding. A continuum runs from concepts that require substantial linguistic construction (JUSTICE, DEMOCRACY, INFINITY) to concepts that can be grounded directly in repeated action-perception patterns (CAUSATION, FEAR, small numbers), irrespective of their ontological abstractness.

7.4 Language encodes embodied information

The claim that some abstract concepts are largely linguistic does not deny embodiment.

Language encodes information about perceptual and spatial properties of referents. The co-occurrence of city names in text mirrors their geographical distances (Louwerse & Zwaan, 2009), linguistic context predicts the perceptual modality of words (Louwerse & Connell, 2011), and word order and co-occurrence carry embodied relations (Louwerse, 2008 (Louwerse, , 2011)). On the linguistic shortcut view, language provides efficient access to embodied knowledge without full perceptual simulation (Louwerse, 2018). Abstract concepts, built largely from language, thereby inherit indirect connections to embodied experience, even when they lack direct sensorimotor grounding. This view fits the tradition of treating meaning as use (Wittgenstein, 1953) and the finding that large language models, although trained on text alone, capture substantial regularities in human conceptual structure (Piantadosi & Hill, 2022). Such findings need not be read as a threat to grounded cognition, since they are what one would expect if linguistic structure encodes embodied information (Winter, 2019). Our claim here is limited: Text carries embodied regularities. We do not claim that models trained on text understand language in the way that grounded human learners do (cf. Chemero, 2023).

8. Methodological implications

To investigate the proposed re-weighting of conceptual dimensions empirically, research has long relied on concreteness ratings. Large-scale norms such as those of Brysbaert et al. (2014) treat concreteness and abstractness as endpoints of a single bipolar scale and are an invaluable foundation. Methodological critiques nevertheless show that this unidimensional approach has limits, and GEST-A helps to specify them.

The first limit concerns the scale instruction. The norms of Brysbaert et al. (2014, p. 905) asked participants to rate words from "very abstract (language-based)" to "very concrete (experience-based)." This conflates a concept's dominant acquisition modality with its abstractness and risks circularity: if abstract concepts are defined by their dependence on linguistic explanation, finding that they rely on language is a tautology (Lupyan & Winter, 2018). Ratings of this kind may capture the very shift from sensorimotor to linguistic dominance that is central to the early stages of detachment (Figure 2). The recent specificity norms of Muraki and Pexman (2026) address a different conflation, between abstractness and specificity, and, together with the work of Bolognesi et al. (2020), allow concreteness and specificity to be modelled jointly.

The second limit concerns the concrete pole. Global concreteness ratings are biased towards visual experience and can overshadow grounding in other modalities. Modalityspecific strength ratings (for touch, sound, smell) often predict lexical processing better than a single concreteness score (Connell & Lynott, 2012). Concepts tied strongly to nonvisual senses (ECHO, PUNGENT) may therefore receive middling concreteness ratings not because they are abstract but because the scale fails to capture their specific sensorimotor features. Embodiment is multimodal, and its reduction during detachment may proceed differently across perceptual systems.

The third limit concerns polysemy and context. Treating abstractness as a stable property of a word form is challenged by the fact that decontextualized ratings force participants to average across senses, for example grasping a cup versus grasping an idea (Löhr, 2022).

The word MARS offers a clearer illustration: a rating for the form averages over distinct concepts (planet, God, confectionery), and the same is true of any homonym (Section 2.3). Abstractness is better regarded as a property of a specific meaning in a specific context, corresponding to a position in the space of Figure 1, than as a lexical tag (Petrova et al., 2018; see also Pollock, 2018; Strik Lievers et al., 2021).

These observations do not negate the concrete-abstract distinction. They decompose it.

The classic uni-dimensional scale confounds at least four things: (1) the balance between sensorimotor and linguistic representation, (2) the distribution across perceptual modalities, (3) dependence on context for meaning specification, and (4) specificity. The empirical signature of detachment, such as reduced sensorimotor engagement or a shift from motor to linguistic interference, is best interpreted as evidence of re-weighting within a multidimensional space. We recommend that researchers acknowledge the limitations of concreteness ratings, use several operationalizations of abstractness, use sense-level or context-specific materials, include modality-specific and specificity measures, and avoid treating concreteness as the sole or primary dimension of conceptual structure.

10. Implications for development and education

If abstraction involves progressive re-weighting from sensorimotor grounding towards linguistic construction, and a transition from complete overlap to family resemblance, then education should ensure strong initial grounding while recognizing that advanced thinking necessarily becomes more symbolic. The process is a re-weighting and not an erasure, and it generalizes across domains.

In mathematics, effective instruction should progress from situated, experiential representations towards symbolic ones, treating situatedness and embodiment as continuous design dimensions (Chatain et al., 2025). Manipulatives provide crucial grounding (Carbonneau et al., 2013), but students must eventually move to symbolic manipulation for fluency. The work on inner speech and working memory in mental arithmetic (DeStefano & LeFevre, 2004; Fini et al., 2022) shows that symbolic routines are language-dependent. Forcing abstraction before adequate grounding may impair learning, and insisting on manipulatives indefinitely may limit efficient abstract reasoning. Consistent labels throughout the transition ("multiplication," "product,"

"times") are likely to facilitate integration, which is a prediction of the binding account.

For concepts lacking complete overlap, including many mathematical, scientific, and philosophical abstractions, explicit verbal labels during learning may be essential and not optional (Pulvermüller, 2018a; Dobler et al., 2024 Dobler et al., , 2026)). Evidence that hands-on learning precedes symbolic instruction in Montessori education is consistent with this sequence (Lillard et al., 2025).

Moral and legal reasoning offers a related lesson. Legal judgment retains an interoceptive component even in expert practitioners (Angioletti et al., 2022), which suggests that grounding is a persistent substrate on which instruction should build and not only a scaffold to be discarded. The pedagogical challenge is to support learners through reweighting while keeping connections to grounded foundations and providing linguistic scaffolding for family-resemblance concepts. Individual differences in abstract reasoning may partly reflect the efficiency of re-weighting and the capacity to use labels to integrate concepts whose instances overlap only partially.

11. Conclusions

Abstraction is currently used both as a property of concepts and a process of conceptual development, and a theory of either must first separate abstractness, specificity, and abstraction. As a property, abstractness is multidimensional: concepts are positioned by grounding, embodiment, and situatedness, with specificity as an orthogonal descriptor, and they vary continuously and not categorically. As a process, abstraction includes generalization and detachment. Detachment is marked by a re-weighting of grounded, embodied, and situated content, with a reduction in embodied weight that transforms and does not erase sensorimotor foundations.
