Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. The aim of this study was to explore the interrelationship between cognitive polyphasia and cognitive dissonance in the management of contradictions. We argue that both concepts maintain a circular relationship. To support this position, we establish a theoretical path structured around four core axes: 1) the recognition that Festinger and his followers already considered dissonance management mechanisms that, much like cognitive polyphasia, maintain inconsistency; 2) from a diachronic perspective, dissonance can stem from cognitive polyphasia; 3) from a synchronic perspective, the varieties of cognitive polyphasia can be systematized within the functional approach to cognitive dissonance; 4) this systematization raises questions regarding the self and identity, bringing Social Representations Theory closer to contemporary models of the self. COGNITIVE DISSONANCE THEORY: WAYS OF COPING WITH CONTRADICTION

In Festinger's original work (1957), cognitive dissonance is conceptualized as "the existence of nonfitting relations among cognitions" which, "being psychologically uncomfortable, will motivate the person to try to reduce the dissonance and achieve consonance" (p. 3). Consonance can be achieved in three ways: 1) altering the behavioral cognitive element, which consists of "changing the action or feeling which the behavioral element represents" (p. 19); 2) altering the environmental cognitive element, whereby the situation to which the cognition refers is modified; and 3) adding new, compatible cognitive elements capable of eliminating or minimizing dissonance.

Although these processes are considered the most common, Festinger (1957) recognized the existence of other possible means of coping with dissonance. In addition to selective forgetting, denial, and reducing the importance of the cognitive content area in which the dissonance manifests, it would still be possible to avoid it by "compartmentalizing different cognitive clusters so that they, in effect, have nothing to do with one another" (Festinger, 1957, p. 271). Although the author admitted to not knowing how to address compartmentalization and other processes of this nature, subsequent empirical studies examined this mechanism (Anderton et al., 2011; Amiot et al., 2017a). Even so, such strategies were only systematically developed in later reviews.

In the late 1950s, these reviews began to question the classic postulates of CDT, giving rise to a series of reformulations via mini-theories (Aronson, 1992; Harmon-Jones, 1999).

These proposals emphasized different dimensions in managing inconsistency, such as the selfconcept (Aronson, 1992), the aversive consequences of counterattitudinal actions (Cooper & Fazio, 1984), and the maintenance of self-esteem (Steele, 1988; Sherman & Cohen, 2006).

Despite their contributions, these approaches relegated the functional implications of inconsistency management to the background, an issue addressed more directly by Kelman and Baron (1968a).

As a way of overcoming the limitations of models that reduced dissonance to a mere motivational state, Kelman and Baron (1968b) advanced a functional analysis of inconsistency.

For these authors, inconsistency is initially perceived as a signal that activates a search process aimed at evaluating its implications. When this evaluation reveals no threat to goals, values, or coping resources, no further response is elicited. When, however, relevant functional implications are identified, individuals may reduce the resulting tension without necessarily eliminating the inconsistency itself. On this basis, the model systematizes responses to contradiction according to two combinable dimensions: the nature of the process mobilized (avoidance or confrontation) and the nature of the outcome achieved (reduction or maintenance).

Based on this framework, Kelman and Baron (1968a) identified fourteen distinct mechanisms: eight oriented toward inconsistency reduction and six toward its maintenance.

These mechanisms are distributed across avoidance and confrontation processes, yielding four reduction-avoidance mechanisms and four reduction-confrontation mechanisms, as well as three maintenance-avoidance modes and three maintenance-confrontation modes. Their model therefore constitutes a conceptually robust formulation capable of accounting for both the elimination and the preservation of contradiction over time.

For the purposes of the present study, particular attention is given to mechanisms of inconsistency maintenance. Reduction mechanisms, regardless of the process involved, culminate in the outcomes' characteristic of CDT in its classical formulationnamely, the elimination of contradiction and the restoration of consonance. Maintenance mechanisms, by contrast, make it possible to understand how the tension associated with inconsistency may be regulated without eliminating the contradiction itself, thereby opening space for the coexistence of incompatible rationalities. Particularly noteworthy within this set are Maintenanceavoidance strategies such as compartmentalization, institutionalized insulation, and compensatory ritualism, as well as maintenance-confrontation strategies including bolstering, differentiation, and transcendence (Kelman & Baron, 1968a). Among these mechanisms, compartmentalization displays a particular affinity with forms of cognitive polyphasia, a point to which we return below. Two caveats are necessary at this stage. First, despite their divergence from Festinger's classical formulation, contemporary reviews of CDT largely maintain that earlier controversies have been superseded by broader forms of theoretical integration capable of reflecting the complexity of human cognition (Harmon-Jones & Mills, 2019; Cancino-Montecinos et al., 2020). DIACHRONIC APPROACHES TO COGNITIVE POLYPHASIA AND COGNITIVE DISSONANCE: CONVERGENCES

Etymologically, the term polyphasia derives from the combination of the Greek root poly (many) and the suffix phasia, originating from phásis and the verb phanai, both associated with the act of speaking (Bluteau, 1720). Although Moscovici offered little explicit discussion of the direct inspirations underlying the formulation of the concept, in his dialogue with Ivana Marková concerning the knowing subject, he evoked the characters Bouvard and Pécuchet from Flaubert's unfinished novel (1881/2015) "[…] as prototypes of the subject of common sense" (Moscovici, 2003, p. 322).

In the novel, the protagonists exemplify the simultaneous and amateur appropriation of heterogeneous forms of knowledge in an attempt to comprehend the world in its totality (Flaubert, 1881 (Flaubert, /2015)). The coexistence of contradictory forms of knowledge appears explicitly in a passage in which Bouvard praises Balzac and Pécuchet replies: "-I consider him chimerical [...] He believes in the occult sciences, in monarchy, in rank; is dazzled by rascals; turns up millions for you like centimes; and Middle-class people are not with him middle-class people at all, but giants" (p. 129). A similar idea also emerges in George Orwell's classic 1984 widely popularized in the postwar period. The dystopian novel introduces the notion of doublethink, characterized by the ability "[…] to hold simultaneously two opinions which cancelled out, knowing them to be contradictory and believing in both of them, to use logic against logic, [...] to forget whatever [...], then to draw it back into memory again at the moment when it was needed" (Orwell, 2009, pp. 40-41).

Beyond Orwellian literary fiction, the need to sustain contradictory cognitive states constitutes a recurring feature of contemporary democratic-plural societies. This was observed by Provencher (2007) in her examination of cognitive polyphasia within the controversy surrounding the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine and its alleged association with autism in the United Kingdom.

Although this perspective does not focus on ongoing cognitive conflicts, it encompasses the societal and cultural conditions that make them possible. Since cognitive dissonance emerges following decisions made in contexts of conflict, diachronic cognitive polyphasia provides the conditions for its possibility. Selective prevalence, the most recurrent form, may be compared to a system of separate drawers, in which different systems of knowledge are alternately activated depending on context and functional demands. Displacement, in turn, involves the predominance of one system of knowledge, resulting in the displacement or exclusion of another. Empirical evidence compatible with this typology was identified by Falade and Bauer (2018) in a study conducted in Nigeria, which observed the coexistence of faith in science and faith in God. Such an articulation requires not only the recognition of convergences, but also an examination of zones of overlap and potential divergences between forms of inconsistency maintenance and varieties of cognitive polyphasia -a task that guides the analytical developments that follow.

COMPARTMENTALIZATION AND MULTIPLE GROUP MEMBERSHIPS: THE PLACE OF THE SELF AND IDENTITIES

While selective prevalence is restricted to managing knowledge systems, compartmentalization involves storing core components of the self within relatively separate compartments. At least three assumptions underlie this mechanism: a) the self possesses a multifaceted structure (Showers et al., 2004), though still oriented toward consistency and unity (Roccas & Brewer, 2002); b) simultaneous belonging to multiple groups implies the coexistence of multiple identities (Tajfel, 1984); c) these identities can conflict. In line with these assumptions, compartmentalization has been empirically investigated in studies on identity processes and self-organization (Amiot et al., 2019).

Although some of its assumptions can be traced back to the foundations of SRT and CDT, the self did not occupy a central position in the original formulations of these frameworks.

In SRT, Moscovici (2003) acknowledged that the self could be constituted of representations, but the theory privileged the triadic ontological Ego-Alter-Object model. In CDT, self-related processes were emphasized primarily in models examining the role of the self-concept in intensifying cognitive dissonance (Aronson, 1992). Today, however, both traditions have converged more explicitly toward self-related processes.

Within the scope of SRT, it has been proposed that cognitive polyphasia reflects the polyphony of the self and its capacity to sustain multiple identities and positions (Martinez, 2018). This proposal was supported by a study articulating cognitive polyphasia with Bakhtinian notions of polyphony and positioning, as well as Dialogical Self Theory (DST) (Renedo, 2010). Within this framework, polyphasia and the polyphony of the self are coconstructed through representational processes wherein individuals adopt and negotiate "Ipositions" across various interactive contexts.

DST, in turn, sought to reconcile William James's emphasis on the unity and continuity of the self with the Bakhtinian conception of multiplicity and discontinuity (Hermans et al., 1992; Hermans, 2001). From this synthesis emerges the notion of the I-position, whereby the self is conceived as "dynamic multiplicity of I-positions in the society of mind" (Hermans, 2012, p. 8). As a "mini-society", the self organizes itself through positions situated in space and time. Spatially, the "I" can shift from one position to another in a process of positioning and counterpositioning (Hermans, 2001). Temporally, it engages in positioning and repositioning.

Considered spatio-temporally, the "I" can fluctuate between different and potentially contradictory positions, both within the self and in relation to perceived, remembered, or imagined others (Hermans, 2001). Although these positions can become strained, they tend to be either rejected or integrated as parts of a relatively coherent structure (Hermans, 2012).

While promising, many of DST's assumptions regarding contradiction are not entirely unprecedented in social psychology. Festinger (1957) already highlighted that changes in social position can generate cognitive dissonance, especially when the individual "accepts the opinions and the values of the position into which he has moved" (Festinger, 1957, p. 272). In the example provided by the author, someone who held disparaging views of their teachers while in the student position feels compelled to adjust their opinion upon becoming a teacher themselves. The idea of repositioning, therefore, was already implicit in the dissonance tradition. Additionally, contemporary literature on the self and identity shows no resistance to recognizing the features that DST claims to reconcilefeatures that, we argue, reinforce the appropriateness of subsuming selective prevalence under compartmentalization.

In the expanded cognitive dissonance literature, compartmentalization has been investigated through three primary lenses. First, as a coping mechanism for normative conflicts, whether between ingroups to which the individual belongs (Becker et al., 2023) or between personal and group values (Glasford et al., 2008). Under these circumstances, the group can becomeas Festinger (1957) originally suggestedsimultaneously a source of consonance

This occurs, for instance, when the ingroup violates its members' personal values (Glasford et al.,2008), when there is overt disagreement among members (Matz & Wood, 2005), or when individuals witness their group engaging in inconsistent behavior (Norton et al., 2003). In the latter case, this is referred to as vicarious dissonance, as the discomfort stems from imagining oneself in the position of the actor performing the inconsistent action.

Second, compartmentalization has been treated as a strategy to handle cultural conflicts in the construction of bicultural identities (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005). When differing cultural affiliations are perceived as incompatible (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005) or generate cognitive dissonance (Maertz et al., 2009), cultural identities may be kept in separate compartments of the self.

Finally, compartmentalization appears as a mode of subjective representation of the interrelationships between multiple social identities within the self (Roccas & Brewer, 2002) or as a stage in the process of integrating new identities into the self-concept (Amiot et al., 2007).

In this latter approach, Roccas and Brewer (2002) proposed the Social Identity Complexity Model (SICM), whose core postulate is that multiple social identities are internally organized through mental representations distributed along a continuum from lower to higher complexity, depending on the degree of overlap between the groups to which the individual belongs. Four representations are described: a) intersection, when identity results from a hyphenated and simultaneous combination of group memberships; b) dominance, when competing identities are subordinated to a primary group identification; c) compartmentalization, when important identities are activated in isolated contexts; d) fusion, when social identity corresponds to the integrated sum of different belongings. x.14 inhibit (perceived threat, status and power asymmetries) or facilitate (coping/adaptation and social support) this integration process within the self.

Both models emerge as a response to two classic limitations in group studies: the focus on a single salient social identity (Becker et al., 2023) or a single ingroup-outgroup categorization (Roccas & Brewer, 2002), and the scant attention paid to intraindividual processes (Amiot et al., 2007; Becker et al., 2023). The model we adapt for the study of contradiction management aims not only to overcome these limitations but also to systematically integrate the advances of this literature on the self and identity into the framework of inconsistency mechanisms.

SCOPE AND CHALLENGES OF THE INTEGRATIVE MODEL

In order to delineate the scope of the contributions of an integrative model of contradiction management, we explicate its theoretical implications, its analytical boundaries, and the questions that remain open.

First, this approach appears particularly suited to specific types of social groups and contradictions, especially so-called crosscutting groups. According to Roccas and Brewer (2002), these groups emerge when different dimensions of classification and membership do not fully coincide, such that individuals defined as an ingroup on one dimension may be an outgroup on another. As previously argued, the frantic encounter between different forms of knowledge, cultural pluralization, and the coexistence of multiple symbolic authorities (Jovchelovitch, 2008a; Giddens, 2012) make membership in crosscutting groups increasingly common. In a study on conflicts experienced by obstetrician-gynecologists, for instance, both abortion providers and conscientious objectors reported tension between professional and religious identities when faced with requests for legal abortion (Mikael-Silva et al., 2025).

However, unlike the objectors, the providers tended to compartmentalize the medical and the religious/spiritual viewpoints, activating them depending on the context. Another example is Mikael-Silva's (2024) study on Flat Earth YouTube channels, which included a PhD in geophysics and a Master's graduate in Intelligence and Security. These YouTubers belonged to the institutional scientific community while simultaneously advocating for a Flat Earth, managing the contradictions between academic affiliation and conspiracy adherence through x.15 mechanisms of inconsistency maintenance, most notably compartmentalization. Regarding contradictions, we suggest paying closer attention to inconsistencies between: a) norms and social or personal identity; b) one or more identities and identifications; c) perspectives and positions adopted by the same individual; d) antagonistic social practices within the same subject or group; e) competing knowledge systems or regimes of truth. Each of these forms of contradiction stems from potential interactions among the normative, identity, epistemic, positional, and practical levels. This enumeration, however, does not intend to exhaust the phenomenon but rather to indicate initial analytical axes that can be refined and structured in future investigations.

To systematize the analytical axes involved in the emergence and management of contradictions within plural contexts, we propose a multilevel model that articulates societal, social, cross-cutting, situational, operational, and functional dimensions (Table 2). These levels are structured from the broader historical, sociocultural, and institutional conditions down to the situated mechanisms of inconsistency management and their regulatory effects. By explicating the processual contribution of each level, the table organizes the phenomenon of inconsistency not as an isolated cognitive failure, but as an institutional and dynamic product of contemporary social life. The societal level implies that culturally plural and hybrid societies, marked by multiple authorities, tend to exhibit greater conflict and conditions that favor diachronic cognitive polyphasia. These same conditions foster the coexistence of cross-cutting groups at the social level. Since groups are sources of norms, social identities, epistemologies, positions, and practices, the cross-cutting nature of these memberships and axes generates potential inter-level tensions and contradictions (cross-cutting level). At the situational level, the need to manage these contradictions may depend on the salience of one or more identities within a given context, situational demands, individual and group goals, and the communication systems that organize how contradictions are framed, potentially stimulating synchronic cognitive polyphasia. Once activated, the operational level predicts that individuals and groups will mobilize mechanisms of contradiction reduction or maintenance, the effects of which are simultaneously functional and systemic: functional, because they regulate knowledge and social representations, conflicts between group memberships, and inconsistencies that threaten the self, self-concept, and identity; and systemic, because the outcomes of inconsistency management reverberate through higher levels, feeding back into new social configurations and contradictions (García Canclini, 2005).

CONCLUSION

In this study, we have argued for a circular relationship between cognitive polyphasia and cognitive dissonance. Even before Moscovici formulated the notion of cognitive polyphasia, Festinger (1957) already recognized different ways of coping with dissonance, which were subsequently systematized by Kelman and Baron (1968a). By encompassing both reduction and maintenance mechanisms of inconsistency, their model parsimoniously accommodates the synchronic manifestations of cognitive polyphasia. Only selective prevalence appears conceptually redundant and can be subsumed under compartmentalization, the ramifications of which bring SRT and CDT closer to more robust formulations of the self and identity.

Building upon this trajectory, we have also proposed an integrative systematization for the study of inconsistency management within the scope of SRT, outlining analytical axes and research challenges. Our proposal offers a complementary contribution by bridging intra-and interpersonal levels while simultaneously expanding the study of cognitive dissonance to intraand intergroup levels. In addition to strengthening the investigation of intrapersonal conflicts associated with multiple group memberships and their effects on the self, this model allows for a more precise examination of the different modes of managing inconsistency.

Despite these contributions, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, we focused our analysis on the articulation between cognitive polyphasia and cognitive dissonance; a broader dialogue between these traditions exceeds the scope of this article, having been explored in greater depth in another work (Mikael-Silva, 2024). Second, we restricted ourselves to three inconsistency maintenance mechanisms, relegating other processes proposed by Kelman and Baron (1968a) to the background -a more detailed study of which could expand and refine the model presented here. Third, it remains necessary to broaden the dialogue with other approaches within SRT, different formulations of CDT, and other social psychology theories that can strengthen, strain, or revise the proposed framework. Finally, our proposal adopts an integrative orientation that does not always find consensus within social psychology, particularly regarding the intersection of SRT and intraindividual cognitive processes. This debate, however, is already present within the SRT tradition itself, especially in its structural approach. Here, we share the stance of Rateau et al. (2012), who remind us that although social representations are collective constructions situated between the social and the individual, they are still partially elaborated by individuals.
