Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Voluntary perioral movements, including speech articulation, deliberate lip posturing, and postural stabilization during oral closure, require motor precision despite the absence of muscle spindles and proprioceptive receptors in perioral musculature. The facial nerve is purely efferent, providing no direct sensory return. How the motor system achieves stable control under these constraints has remained unresolved. We propose the Orofacial Region as Integrated System (ORIS) model as a mechanistic candidate tailored to voluntary and automatic segmental perioral behaviors. The model identifies cutaneous mechanoreception via the trigeminal nerve as the primary afferent channel for perioral motor stabilization and proposes that the controlled variable is the spatial equilibrium of dermal surface tension across the perioral sheet, monitored by slowly and fastadapting mechanoreceptors concentrated in the perioral region. The orbicularis oris sphincter provides circumferential tensile stability against which directional muscles act, with the modiolus anguli oris serving as the primary force-transmission junction. Three testable predictions follow from this architecture. Clinical implications for facial motor rehabilitation are discussed.

1. The perioral motor control problem

The present framework concerns the perioral musculature (mimic muscles), defined here as the set of muscles surrounding and mechanically controlling the oral aperture, including primarily the orbicularis oris and its functional interactions with neighboring lower-face muscles involved in lip shaping, closure, and aperture regulation. The account is deliberately limited to the lower face (with the possible addition of the frontalis): not all mimic muscles are alike, and the orbicularis oculi -governed largely by protective reflexes and under limited voluntary control -falls outside its scope. These muscles participate in several partially overlapping functional domains requiring continuous sensorimotor stabilization: (1) voluntary fine-grained movements requiring online sensorimotor integration (e.g., lip protrusion, deliberate aperture modulation, speech articulation, voluntary facial expressions), (2) coordinative interactions with automatic orofacial behaviors mediated by partially distinct brainstem systems (e.g., swallowing, respiration, and mastication-related oral coordination), and (3) postural stabilization (maintenance of oral closure against mechanical, gravitational load or facial expression inhibition).

Despite their functional heterogeneity, these behaviors share a common computational requirement: real-time monitoring of perioral mechanics and online correction when actual tissue deformation deviates from the desired motor outcome. The present formulation focuses primarily on the sensorimotor stabilization mechanisms supporting voluntary control of the lower face, while also considering their contribution to automatic and postural aspects of perioral behavior. The model also encompasses involuntary and affective expressions at the level of their stabilization: once such an expression has been initiated, maintaining and modulating the resulting lower-face configuration is predicted to rely on the same mechanoreceptive feedback. The model is not concerned with how these expressions are generated.

Among the motor behaviors the human face produces, voluntary perioral movements stand out for a genuinely puzzling reason. People generate fine-grained modulations of mouth configuration during voluntary facial expression, deliberate posturing, and postural maintenance, i.e. movements requiring continuous adjustment of onset, trajectory, amplitude, and offset 1, 2 . That precision arguably implies continuous closed-loop motor control, not unlike what underlies fine manual dexterity.

The control shows itself in two complementary ways. First, under perturbation: when an unexpected load pulls the lower lip during the bilabial closure for /b/ or /p/, the lips compensate within tens of milliseconds to preserve the closure, and they do so without any view of the mouth 3 . Second, when the relevant feedback is removed: with a lower lip left fully mobile but numbed by dental anaesthesia, the lip seal leaks, saliva and food escape, and the lip or cheek is bitten inadvertently, markers of failures of online correction rather than of muscle force.

The anatomical substrate does not obviously support it. In limb and trunk musculature, closedloop stabilization depends on muscle spindles, which encode length and velocity via group Ia and II afferents, and Golgi tendon organs reporting force via Ib fibers 4 . These, together with joint and cutaneous afferents, give the central nervous system the state information it needs to update motor commands in real time 5 . Eliminate that feedback, as in large-fiber sensory neuropathy, and interjoint coordination degrades substantially 6.

Notably, deafferented patients can partially compensate for proprioceptive loss through continuous visual monitoring of the moving limb ("visual proprioception") 7 . An equivalent compensatory strategy is unavailable for perioral control: unlike a moving limb, one's own face lies outside the visual field during natural behavior, so ongoing perioral movements are not accessible to visual monitoring.

Beyond this lack of a visual fallback, the facial system differs from the limb case in three further respects that make extending the deafferentation framework problematic. First, the primary mimic muscles appear largely devoid of classical proprioceptive receptors. Muscle spindles are sparse or absent in the zygomaticus major, orbicularis oris, and orbicularis oculi [8] [9] [10] , and a recent cadaveric study confirms overall densities far below those of limb muscles, with the buccinator highest and the orbicularis oculi lowest 11 . Golgi tendon organs are similarly absent or negligible.

Second, although trigeminal afferents clearly provide facial sensory information, the absence of a dedicated proprioceptive component within CN VII complicates the definition of a canonical facial motor feedback architecture 12, 13.

Third, facial muscles do not cross synovial joints. They insert into the dermis or the superficial musculoaponeurotic system (SMAS), not onto bony processes across a joint cavity 14, 15 . Their mechanical effect is a deformation of a continuous compliant sheet, not a torque about a joint axis. Joint-angle control frameworks are geometrically inapplicable here.

These three constraints together define what we call the "perioral voluntary motor control problem": the perioral motor system achieves motor precision that ordinarily requires proprioceptive or visual feedback, using an arrangement in which the standard afferent channels are either absent or unavailable. Partial answers exist. Cutaneous mechanoreceptors in facial skin respond to facial muscle contraction 16, 17 , and the facial and trigeminal nerves share multiple communicating branches that could route tactile signals toward motor centers 13 . These observations raise the possibility of cutaneous substitution for spindle proprioception, but no mechanistic architecture for such substitution has been specified. That is what the ORIS model attempts, with the explicit understanding that what follows is a theoretically motivated candidate architecture, not an established account.

2. Sensory Substrate

The cutaneous surface of the face is innervated by three trigeminal branches: V1 supplying the forehead and periocular region, V2 the cheek and upper lip, and V3 the lower lip, chin, and jaw. This trigeminal innervation constitutes the principal candidate afferent channel for facial motor feedback, anatomically distinct from the efferent CN VII, which carries no sensory fibers.

Of the four classes of low-threshold mechanoreceptive afferents in human skin 18 , two are particularly relevant to motor stabilization. SA2 units (Ruffini endings) have diffuse receptive fields and respond preferentially to skin stretch and directional shear -they are the most likely candidates for encoding directional deformation of the perioral sheet during muscle contraction 16 . FA1 units (Meissner corpuscles) encode the velocity and timing of dynamic skin displacement, suited to detecting expression onset and offset. SA1 units (Merkel cells) provide spatial detail about static surface configuration, while FA2 (Pacinian) afferents are largely absent from the perioral and periocular regions, a structural gap that permits selective delivery of low-frequency vibrotactile stimulation to facial tissue without engaging the high-frequency channel 19, 20.

Innervation density varies considerably across the face. Whole-body density estimates indicate that the full face and lip region, spanning all three trigeminal divisions and covering roughly 675 cm² of skin, is innervated by approximately 19% of all cutaneous tactile afferent fibers, comparable in total fiber count to both hands combined 21 . Within the face, innervation density increases from the forehead and periocular region (V1: ~48 units/cm²) through the mid-face (V2: ~67 units/cm²) to the lower lip, chin, and jaw (V3: ~84 units/cm²), with the immediate perioral skin likely exhibiting locally higher densities still 21 (See heat map Figure 2). At the finest spatial scale, vibrotactile detection thresholds within the lip vermilion are significantly lower at midsagittal sites than at lateral sites 22, 23 , consistent with greater receptor density and receptive field overlap at the midline. Three independent lines of evidence suggest these receptors respond to facial muscle contraction, not only external contact. Nordin and Hagbarth 16 recorded from single afferents in the human infraorbital nerve and found that most slowly adapting units (interpreted as probable Ruffini corpuscles) responded to skin stretching and facial muscle contraction in the absence of any external stimulus, leading them to conclude these receptors may serve as proprioceptors for facial kinaesthesia. Trulsson and Johansson 17 confirmed that facial skin afferents respond to deformations accompanying lip and jaw movement during speech and chewing. Cobo et al. 8 identified capsular sensory structures in the zygomaticus major and buccal muscles immunoreactive for ASIC2 and TRPV4, consistent with detecting contraction-induced tissue deformation. While these findings do not establish sufficiency, they provide strong evidence that the relevant sensory substrate is present and is activated by appropriate mechanical inputs.

The anatomical pathway from trigeminal afference to facial motor output runs through the communicating branches between CN V and CN VII documented by Cobo et al. 13 . These provide a multisynaptic substrate for trigeminal signals to reach the facial nucleus and cortical motor areas. However, the pathway linking trigeminal input to the facial nerve may also rely on other central circuits. Cortical facial motor control spans at least five areas -primary motor cortex, ventral lateral premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and anterior and posterior cingulate -all receiving somatosensory trigeminal input via thalamo-cortical relays 24 . Two recent lines of neurophysiological evidence make this pathway more than anatomically plausible. Pilurzi et al. 25 demonstrated, using afferent inhibition protocols, that cutaneous inputs from facial skin contribute to sensorimotor integration and plasticity in facial primary motor cortex; Ginatempo et al. 26 extended this work by showing short-and long-latency afferent inhibition in cranial muscles, indicating that trigeminal sensory inputs can modulate facial motor cortical output at latencies compatible with sensorimotor integration. That said, whether these pathways carry functionally significant correction signals specifically during expression production -as opposed to speech or chewing, which were the test conditions in those studies -remains to be established 27 (see figure 1). The controlled variable is the spatial distribution of surface tension across the perioral sheet, not joint position or muscle length.

Taken together, these findings establish that the facial sensory substrate is both anatomically present and functionally responsive to self-generated deformation. Cutaneous afferents encode skin stretch and motion induced by muscle contraction. Indeed, the trigemino-facial pathways operate at latencies compatible with online sensorimotor integration. What remains unresolved is how these signals are organized and integrated. In contrast to limb motor control, where well-defined models specify how proprioceptive and cutaneous feedback are integrated to estimate joint state and guide correction, there is no equivalent framework for the face, given that the insertion of most of its facial muscles is not on the bone substrate. The system lacks canonical state variables (joint angles, muscle lengths), and yet it is capable of achieving stable, precise, and rapidly modulated configurations of a mechanically continuous tissue. The critical question, therefore, is not whether facial sensory feedback exists, but what variable it informs and how it is integrated to control expression. Current evidence demonstrates activation of the substrate, but does not specify the controlled quantity, the structure of the feedback loop, or the level at which correction is computed. In other words, the field lacks a mechanistic model linking trigeminal sensory input to the control of facial motor output. Here we propose the ORIS model, which addresses this gap by redefining the controlled variable and the architecture of facial motor control.

3. The ORIS Model

Before specifying the model, we delimit its scope. ORIS concerns the sensorimotor stabilization of ongoing perioral movements, not the processes by which movements are selected or initiated; a posed expression, for instance, involves a volitional decision that the model does not attempt to explain, and enters the account only once initiated, at the point where the resulting configuration must be held and corrected.

3.1 Controlled variable

The absence of an explicit control model for facial motor behavior reflects a fundamental mismatch between classical motor control frameworks and the physical structure of the face. Existing models are built for articulated systems with discrete degrees of freedom, such as the limbs, where state variables such as joint angles or muscle lengths can be defined and precisely assessed. The face, by contrast, is a deformable, mechanically continuous sheet, lacking joints and fixed reference points. It is important to note that the mandible and its masticatory muscles (e.g., masseter) are a partial exception: they act across the temporomandibular joint and receive proprioceptive input via the trigeminal mesencephalic nucleus. But masticatory muscles are not muscles of facial expression, are not innervated by CN VII, and fall outside the motor problem defined here. This implies that facial motor control cannot be understood as a reduced version of limb control, but requires a different principle, one operating on distributed mechanical fields rather than discrete kinematic variables. Critically, this field is not uniform. Facial muscle insertions converge onto specific anatomical nodes, most prominently the modiolus, where multiple force vectors interact and are redistributed across the perioral sheet (see Figure 2). These sites constitute localized hubs of mechanical tension, where small changes in muscle activation can produce large, spatially extended effects. Here we propose a new model, ORIS, where the controlled variable is the spatial distribution of surface tension across the perioral sheet, structured around these convergence points, in particular the modiolus. In this framework, trigeminal afferents signal local deformation, and motor commands act to stabilize or reshape a global tension field. The ORIS model formalizes this account, providing a functional neuroanatomical architecture dynamically linking sensory feedback to the control of facial motor output.

3.2 Hub architecture

Not all facial muscles contribute equally to surface tension control. The orbicularis oris is sphincteric: it encircles the oral orifice with circumferential fiber geometry. Sphincteric contraction reduces orifice perimeter and generates centripetal tension in all radial directions simultaneously, making this muscle a natural tensile stabilizer -a baseline against which directional muscles act. That mechanical role follows directly from sphincteric fiber geometry. The further inference -that the orbicularis oris serves as the primary reference hub for surface tension control in the perioral region -is a model claim requiring experimental confirmation.

Anatomical focus and limitations.

The ORIS model is formulated primarily for the perioral motor system, including the orbicularis oris, modiolus, and associated directional muscles (e.g., zygomaticus major, risorius, depressor anguli oris, levator labii superioris, and buccinator), treated as a functionally integrated lower-face network. This focus reflects the relative anatomical and neurophysiological tractability of the perioral region, where trigeminal-facial sensorimotor interactions, dynamic aperture control, and mechanically coupled muscle actions can be studied within a coherent behavioral framework encompassing voluntary facial expression, speech articulation, feeding-related coordination, and oral postural stabilization. The present model does not exclude the possibility that similar force-balance and sensorimotor stabilization principles may apply to other circular facial systems, particularly the periocular region, where the orbicularis oculi also exhibits distributed insertions and coordinated aperture regulation. However, the orbicularis oculi is not incorporated into the current formulation because its functional organization is substantially more heterogeneous. In particular, the tarsal component is strongly constrained by automatic reflexive control (e.g., corneal and protective blink circuits), whereas the orbital component shows mixed voluntary and involuntary properties. Moreover, the mechanical and sensorimotor interactions linking periocular structures to the perioral stabilization network remain insufficiently characterized. Extension of the ORIS framework to periocular control, therefore, requires separate anatomical and functional analysis and is deferred to future work. The predictions developed here apply specifically to the perioral system.

The directional muscles, i.e. zygomaticus major, risorius, depressor anguli oris, levator labii superioris, and their synergists, pull from bony origins toward dermal insertions near the modiolus and labial commissure (see figure 3). Activating any of them shifts the perioral tension equilibrium toward a new configuration. Because they insert into a mechanically continuous sheet, this also changes tension at neighboring insertion sites and at the sphincteric hub itself, generating the distributed mechanical coupling that the sensory system must monitor. The modiolus -the fibromuscular condensation at the lateral labial commissure -is where at least seven perioral muscles converge 28 . Force vectors from the zygomaticus major, risorius, depressor anguli oris, depressor labii inferioris, levator labii superioris, and buccinator all arrive at this node and are transmitted onward to the orbicularis oris. That convergence architecture is established anatomically 28, 29 . The ORIS model infers from it that monitoring the entire perioral tension field may be achievable from a small number of sites near the modiolus and labial midline, consistent with the observation that mechanoreceptor density peaks precisely at these locations 16, 22 . Direct confirmation is still needed. Functional MRI data from Rams et al. 30 offer some support: the zygomaticus major undergoes isotonic contraction exclusively during joy while being passively elongated during other expressions, demonstrating expression-specific loading. D'Andrea and Barbaix 29 also constrain the dimensionality of the control problem. Their dissection and MRI study identifies two functionally inseparable perioral units -a deep unit built around the buccinator and inner orbicularis oris ring, and a superficial unit encompassing the depressor anguli oris, zygomaticus, risorius, and outer ring -with mechanical interdependence tight enough that independent actuation of constituent muscles appears unrealistic. The motor system may control the balance between hub stabilization and directional deformation at the two-unit level rather than independently regulating eight to twelve muscles (see figure 4). The specific weighting principles implementing this balance remain to be characterized. The oral hub (upper panel) shows the orbicularis oris acting as a sphincteric tensile stabilizer, with directional muscles (e.g., risorius) producing antagonistic force vectors converging at the modiolus -a serial arrangement operating on a deformable surface without proprioceptive or visual feedback. The arm (lower panel) illustrates the classical limb architecture: parallel muscles acting on a joint-based system with discrete degrees of freedom, supported by spindle-based proprioception and eventually visual monitoring. Limb control stabilizes joint kinematics through spindle-based proprioception and an active gamma motor system that continuously adjusts spindle sensitivity during voluntary movement, preventing the limb from reverting toward a fixed mechanical equilibrium. By contrast, the facial motor system lacks an equivalent gamma mechanism. Consequently, voluntary perioral configurations are transient deviations from a preferred resting configuration rather than stable alternative equilibrium states. The ORIS model therefore proposes that facial motor control stabilizes a distributed surface tension field around this default mechanical configuration.

3.3 Feedback loop

A critical feature of the perioral system is its asymmetric resting-state architecture. The default configuration (mouth closed, lips approximated) represents the preferred mechanical state of the perioral apparatus rather than a neutral equilibrium. This configuration emerges primarily from the elastic and viscoelastic properties of the lips and surrounding soft tissues, together with brainstem reflex organization that favours lip approximation when the system is perturbed, without requiring continuous tonic activity of the orbicularis oris at rest, which appears to be infrequent under normal resting conditions 31 . Oral closure at rest is maintained principally by the anterior temporalis -a jaw-closing (masticatory) muscle -rather than by the perioral musculature: its low-level tonic activity holds the mandible against gravity at the rest position, whereas the masseter contributes mainly during active biting rather than postural maintenance 32 . The perioral muscles themselves remain essentially quiescent at rest, consistent with the near-absent tonic orbicularis oris activity noted above. Because these jaw-closing muscles lie outside the perioral system defined here (Section 3.1), the default tension field refers specifically to this low-activity perioral configuration. Mouth-opening muscles, by contrast, are recruited mainly during voluntary or task-dependent behaviours rather than contributing to the default resting configuration. Most voluntary perioral movements therefore represent departures from this preferred mechanical state. Directional muscles reshape the perioral tension field away from its default configuration, while the circumferential geometry and passive mechanical properties of the orbicularis oris provide a stable tensile reference against which these deformations occur. The ORIS model therefore treats the resting oral closure configuration as the default tension field from which voluntary deviations are monitored and corrected. Unlike the limb motor system, which relies on spindle-based proprioception and γ-motoneuron regulation to support multiple stable postural configurations, the facial motor system lacks an equivalent γ-motor mechanism. Consequently, voluntary perioral configurations are transient deviations from a preferred resting configuration rather than alternative equilibrium states.

Motor commands from the facial nucleus and cortical motor areas activate mimic muscles, deforming the perioral sheet. Those deformations generate local changes in skin tension and displacement at mechanoreceptor locations throughout the perioral region (See figure 4). SA2 Ruffini afferents detect sustained stretch direction and magnitude; FA1 Meissner afferents encode displacement velocity and onset timing. This information travels via trigeminal branches to the brainstem, where rapid connections through trigemino-facial communicating branches could modify the ongoing motor command 13 . Slower thalamo-cortical pathways allow integration with predictive signals from premotor and supplementary motor areas 24 . Deviations from the target tension distribution would generate error signals driving corrective commands. Despite the absence of a dedicated proprioceptive nerve, this constitutes a plausible closed-loop architecture: efferent channel CN VII, candidate afferent channel CN V. Whether it operates with sufficient latency and precision for voluntary perioral movement is the central empirical question the predictions below address.

3.4 Scaling with uncertainty

The model predicts that control precision and level scale with afferent uncertainty. Under favorable conditions -intact mechanoreception, well-practiced voluntary movement, minimal perturbation -brainstem-level feedback via short-latency trigeminal-facial arcs may be sufficient 5 . Two things can degrade this regime. Afferent precision can drop through receptor adaptation or desensitization, weakening the error signal itself. Alternatively, the target tension configuration may be underspecified when it must be constructed online, as in a deliberately posed expression, rather than being supplied by a well-practised motor program. Whether an affective or limbic contribution also helps specify this target in spontaneous expression is an open question we return to below. In either case the model predicts escalation to cortical control, with premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and parietal somatosensory areas recruited to supplement degraded peripheral signals and to construct or hold the target through internal forward models 33.

3.5 Energetic implications

Sphincteric geometry distributes centripetal tension in all radial directions simultaneously, so a modest orbicularis oris activation may provide baseline tensile support for the entire perioral surface, with directional muscles shifting the equilibrium through smaller additional contractions. Critically, their combined effect is integrated at the modiolus -the convergence node where the vector sum of all active insertions determines the resulting tension field. Because the motor system monitors this resultant rather than individual muscle states, the computational load collapses: independent regulation of eight to twelve perioral muscles reduces to management of a single distributed quantity at a small number of high-density receptor sites (See Figure 4). One energetic consequence follows. Posed expressions, decoupled from spontaneous emotional drive, may be less able to rely on a pre-specified target of the kind a well-practised, emotionally driven expression can provide, and may require greater explicit directional recruitment per unit of surface displacement. The asymmetry between Duchenne and non-Duchenne smiles is consistent with this: posed smiles have faster onsets 34 , tend toward left-sided asymmetry where spontaneous smiles are more nearly symmetric 35 , and recruit the corticobulbar route rather than the subcortical limbic route that drives emotional expression 36, 37 . This does not conflict with the model's feedback-dependence: the routes differ in what they supply at initiation, not in whether the movement is stabilized by afferent correction. On this account, a well-practised, emotionally driven expression may carry a pre-specified target that allows stabilization largely at brainstem level, whereas a posed or volitional expression, lacking such a target, would construct it online and lean on the slower cortical arc (the escalation under uncertainty predicted in Section 3.4). We present this as a hypothesis rather than an established mechanism: whether, and how, an affective state contributes to specifying the target tension configuration remains an open empirical question, and the Duchenne/non-Duchenne asymmetry noted above is consistent with it but does not establish it. Direct energetic measurement has not been performed.

4. Three Testable Predictions

The predictions below are designed to be discriminable from both null models and from existing sensorimotor accounts. Each prediction specifies the expected signature under the ORIS model and includes an explicit criterion for falsification.

Prediction 1: Perioral desensitization will disturb kinematic stability without reducing amplitude

The first prediction concerns the functional role of the perioral region. More specifically, the orbicularis oris and adjacent perioral skin serve as the primary hub-level for feedback stabilization monitoring. If this region provides critical sensory input for online correction, then selectively reducing mechanoreceptive sensitivity (for example through topical anesthesia or vibrotactile adaptation) should impair feedback without disrupting motor command generation. Thus, under such conditions, we expect a preserved perioral displacement amplitude but an increase in trial-totrial kinematic variability, accompanied by a shift in EMG dynamics toward lower temporal frequencies, reflecting degraded high-frequency correction. Conversely, a reduction in amplitude would point toward a more general motor impairment, while the absence of any measurable effect would argue against a functional role of cutaneous feedback during real-time control.

Prediction 1a spatial specificity

Critically, this prediction is spatially specific. ORIS predicts that desensitization effects should be maximal at hub locations (particularly the orbicularis oris and the modiolus, where multiple muscle forces converge and are redistributed across the face) and weaker at non-hub sites such as the belly of the zygomaticus major. Reducing sensitivity at a hub should therefore produce a larger increase in knematic variability than an equivalent manipulation elsewhere. Absent such a dissociation, a more distributed feedback architecture would be favored over the hub-based organization that ORIS proposes.

Prediction 1b distributed correction under tension perturbation

External tension perturbation evokes distributed corrective responses. A complementary prediction follows from the claim that the controlled variable is surface tension rather than position or muscle activation per se. If facial motor control operates on a tension field, a controlled mechanical perturbation of the facial surface (for instance applying calibrated skin stretch near the modiolus during an ongoing expression) should elicit rapid corrective responses that oppose the perturbation. Because the perioral sheet is mechanically coupled, these corrections should be distributed across multiple muscles rather than confined to the directly perturbed region. Failure to observe corrective responses, or responses restricted to the perturbed site, would argue against tension-field regulation.

Prediction 2: A temporally structured EMG response will follow perioral surface perturbation

If a closed trigeminal-facial sensorimotor loop contributes to online facial motor control, brief mechanical displacement of upper lip skin during an ongoing voluntary perioral movement should elicit a temporally structured EMG response in orbicularis oris and associated perioral muscles. An early short-latency component (approximately 20-40 ms) would be consistent with rapid trigemino-facial sensorimotor circuitry mediated at the brainstem level, whereas a later longlatency component (>60 ms) may reflect cortical sensorimotor re-engagement and corrective integration. Importantly, the early response is not predicted to be uniformly excitatory, but may instead consist of mixed inhibitory and facilitatory modulation across different motor-unit populations, consistent with documented heterogeneity in human perioral short-latency reflex physiology 38.

Rapid compensatory responses to perioral perturbation have been described in speech motor control, including coordinated upper and lower lip muscle adjustments following mechanical loading during speech production 3 . These compensatory responses occur despite the relative absence of classical muscle spindle architecture in mimetic musculature and are thought to depend predominantly on cutaneous and mucosal mechanoreceptive afferents. More broadly, rapid sensorimotor corrective responses are well characterized in limb motor control systems 4 , although the facial system likely relies on distinct trigeminofacial mechanisms adapted to deformable softtissue control rather than canonical spindle-mediated stretch reflexes.

Comparative evidence further supports a distributed perioral coordination architecture. In macaques, communicative facial behaviors show strong and selective coordination among perioral mimetic muscles, with orbicularis oris acting as a central coordinating hub during facial communication but not during ingestive behaviors 39 . This organization is consistent with the force-balance architecture proposed by the present model.

A further prediction is that selective infraorbital nerve block should attenuate or abolish the early short-latency component while leaving later long-latency responses relatively preserved, thereby pharmacologically dissociating rapid trigemino-facial circuitry from later cortical sensorimotor processes. If the early component remains unaffected by infraorbital blockade, the specific afferent pathway proposed by the model would require revision. Conversely, if perioral perturbation elicits only a monophasic long-latency response, or if infraorbital blockade suppresses both temporal components equally, the proposed dual-loop brainstem-plus-cortical architecture would not be supported.

Prediction 3: Selective perioral desensitization will dissociate voluntary from affective motor control

If ORIS correctly identifies voluntary perioral control as feedback-dependent and affective expression as pre-programmed, then selective disruption of perioral mechanoreception (e.g., via topical anesthesia of the infraorbital territory) should impair voluntary perioral movements (lip rounding on command, sustained protrusion, deliberate aperture modulation) while leaving the motor production of spontaneous affective expressions (smiling, grimacing) intact. This dissociation is the most direct test of the model's foundational scope claim. Disconfirmation: if perioral desensitization equally impairs voluntary and affective movements, the distinction between feedback-dependent and pre-programmed motor control is not supported, and the model's scope restriction requires revision. Conversely, if voluntary movements are selectively impaired, ORIS predicts that the deficit should be maximal at hub locations (orbicularis oris, modiolus) and minimal at non-hub sites, replicating the spatial specificity pattern of Prediction 1 within a clinical paradigm.

5. Implications

>Implications

5.1 Clinical implications for perioral motor disorders and rehabilitation

Peripheral facial palsy, facial motor apraxia, and developmental dyspraxia of speech are typically interpreted primarily as disorders of motor output or motor planning. The ORIS model proposes a complementary deficit that may contribute substantially to impaired function across these conditions: disruption of the trigeminal-cutaneous feedback architecture supporting perioral motor stabilization.

In peripheral facial palsy, dysfunction of the orbicularis oris does not merely reduce movement amplitude. It may also disrupt the deformation-dependent afferent signals that normally contribute to stabilization of the perioral tension field. Recovery of voluntary movement amplitude may therefore fail to restore the dynamic correction mechanisms required for stable perioral control.

A related dissociation is frequently observed in bucco-facial apraxia and impaired voluntary facial mimicry: patients may retain relatively preserved spontaneous affective expressions while showing difficulty with commanded, imitative, or voluntarily configured perioral movements such as deliberate lip rounding, sustained protrusion, or precise lower-face shaping. Classical interpretations attribute this dissociation primarily to separate limbic and cortical motor pathways. The ORIS model instead suggests that different behavioral regimes rely to different degrees on online sensorimotor stabilization. Highly flexible voluntary and imitative perioral behaviors may place greater demands on trigeminal-cutaneous feedback than more stereotyped affective expressions.

Within this framework, disruption of perioral sensorimotor integration -through trigeminal neuropathy, central lesion, developmental failure of sensorimotor coupling, or incomplete recovery after facial palsy -should degrade movement stability even when gross motor output remains structurally intact. The model therefore predicts that affected patients may show: (1) elevated perioral vibrotactile thresholds, reflecting impaired afferent signaling; (2) abnormal reflex responses to perioral perturbation, consistent with disruption of rapid trigeminofacial stabilization pathways; and (3) preserved mean movement amplitude accompanied by increased kinematic variability during voluntary perioral movements, as predicted in Prediction 1. This reinterpretation has potential therapeutic implications. Rehabilitation strategies designed to restore or augment perioral mechanoreceptive feedback -including vibrotactile biofeedback, mirror-assisted sensorimotor training, or neuromuscular electrical stimulation protocols aimed at re-engaging afferent pathways rather than simply driving contraction 40 -may improve movement stability and speech intelligibility more effectively than motor-only interventions. The ORIS model therefore provides a mechanistic rationale for sensory-motor integration approaches across a range of conditions involving impaired lower-face control, including developmental dyspraxia of speech, unilateral facial palsy with incomplete voluntary recovery, oral-motor dyspraxia following stroke or traumatic brain injury, and facial reanimation after Moebius syndrome smile surgery.

5.2 Implications for Affective neuroscience

The ORIS model also bears on accounts of emotion recognition and sensorimotor simulation. Classical facial-feedback accounts hold that facial motor activity contributes to emotion processing by feeding back into central affective systems 41 , but the afferent pathway carrying that signal has remained underspecified. ORIS supplies a candidate: the critical afferent signal is not musclebased proprioception but cutaneous mechanoreception conveyed by trigeminal pathways. On this view emotion-related facial simulation depends on the integrity of a perioral surface-tension monitoring system, in which skin deformation rather than muscle contraction per se carries the relevant feedback.

A methodological implication follows directly. Much of the facial-feedback literature has manipulated mimicry by constraining movement -holding a pen or chopstick between the teeth or lips, or paralyzing muscles pharmacologically -on the assumption that blocking the movement blocks the feedback. ORIS reframes the operative variable: what the system reads is not movement as such but the pattern of cutaneous deformation, skin stretch and shear, that contraction imposes on the perioral sheet and that low-threshold mechanoreceptors (SA2 Ruffini, FA1 Meissner) transduce. Constraining movement is therefore only an indirect and noisy proxy for that afferent signal. It engages several muscles at once, alters effort and attention, and still leaves a residual uncontrolled skin deformation, since the constraint itself deforms the tissue. This may be one reason facial-feedback effects on emotion recognition have proven difficult to replicate. The model predicts instead that manipulations targeting skin deformation directly -directional skin stretch or taping, or low-frequency vibrotactile stimulation that injects noise into the mechanoreceptive signal, selectively deliverable given the paucity of Pacinian afferents in perioral skin -should isolate the afferent pathway more cleanly than movement constraint and yield more consistent effects on the accuracy with which observers recognize others' expressions. This advantage holds only insofar as the deformation manipulation is decoupled from incidental motor consequences: contraction deforms skin, and externally imposed deformation can itself evoke reflexive motor adjustment, so the two factors are separable in principle but must be carefully dissociated in practice.

Evidence from congenital facial paralysis is broadly consistent with this account. Möbius syndrome involves congenital hypoplasia or aplasia of the abducens (CN VI) and facial (CN VII) nuclei, with corresponding impairment of facial motor control. Affected individuals show, to varying degrees, deficits in emotion recognition despite intact visual processing, pointing to disrupted simulation-based mechanisms 42, 43 . From an ORIS perspective this deficit reflects not merely absent motor output but the failure to establish a functional afferent loop during development. Without early coupling between facial movement and cutaneous feedback, the system never acquires the calibrated mapping between surface deformation and affective state that would normally support simulation.

The congenital case has an acquired counterpart that the model distinguishes sharply. In acquired peripheral palsy, such as Bell's palsy or post-surgical paralysis, the deformation-to-affect mapping was calibrated normally in development and is disrupted only later. ORIS therefore predicts a milder and potentially recoverable deficit, one that should track the status of the afferent loop rather than motor output alone. This fits the mixed empirical picture: acquired palsy tends to slow emotion recognition and increase perceptual ambiguity while leaving accuracy largely preserved, consistent with compensation by an already-established mapping 44, 45 . The contrast yields a testable prediction. Congenital paralysis should produce a more fundamental simulation deficit than acquired paralysis of comparable motor severity, and in acquired cases recognition should recover with afferent rather than purely motor rehabilitation. This interpretation extends to surgical rehabilitation. Procedures such as gracilis free-muscle transfer can restore visible movement 46 but do not reinstate the original mechanoreceptive architecture (Belluardo et al., submitted). The transferred muscle operates without an established sensory facial reference, which may limit its contribution to affective simulation even when motor output recovers.

Taken together, ORIS reframes the mechanism of facial feedback, shifting attention from motor execution to sensorimotor coupling and from muscle-centered accounts to skin-based afferent signaling. The perspective generates testable predictions. Selective disruption of perioral mechanoreception should impair emotion recognition even when overt facial movement is preserved, and interfering with cutaneous feedback during the observation of expressions should impair simulation-based processing. This second prediction builds on evidence that facialexpression processing specifically engages the somatosensory channel: viewing facial expressions selectively modulates the somatosensory P300 47.

5.3 Boundary conditions

The ORIS model addresses feedback stabilization of voluntary perioral movements, not the motor programming of emotional displays. Some experimental manipulations conducted under the facial feedback framework (e.g., constraining facial movement to test emotion effects) may inadvertently disrupt voluntary motor control rather than affective expression per se. This distinction requires careful experimental dissociation in future work. Three results would require revision of the model or a component of it -but first, two things the model does not claim. It addresses motor stabilization of ongoing voluntary perioral movements; it says nothing about how movements are selected or initiated. It also does not account for perceptual recognition of expressions in observers, except insofar as a companion perceptual account proposes that production and perception may share a representational substrate. First, if the sparse spindles documented in the buccinator prove sufficient for the control precision observed, the primacy of CN V cutaneous mechanoreception would be downgraded to a modulatory role. Second, if force transmission across the SMAS is diffuse rather than nodal, the modiolus may be anatomically prominent but mechanically peripheral. Third, the most direct competing account is open-loop feedforward control: the motor system relies on highly practiced motor programs and corollary discharge, with no real-time afferent correction.

6. Conclusion

The perioral system executes voluntary movements with precision it anatomically should not be able to achieve. Spindles are sparse or absent. The facial nerve is purely efferent. There are no joints. And yet the motor system manages.

The ORIS model proposes that cutaneous mechanoreception via the trigeminal nerve is what makes this possible, organized around the orbicularis oris sphincter monitoring a surface tension equilibrium across the perioral sheet. The orbicularis oris provides the circumferential tensile anchors; the modiolus transmits forces among converging directional muscles; SA2 Ruffini afferents are the most plausible candidates for encoding the distributed deformation pattern that constitutes the proposed controlled variable. These proposals are grounded in established anatomy and physiology. Their functional assembly into a working closed-loop architecture has not been demonstrated. That is the gap. The model's contribution at this stage is to organize the available evidence into a falsifiable architecture, one that specifies, for each of its three predictions, what result would confirm a specific component and what result would require revision. A theoretical proposal that cannot say what would refute it is not yet science. This one can.

The ORIS model therefore proposes that facial motor control stabilizes a distributed surface tension field around this default mechanical configuration.
