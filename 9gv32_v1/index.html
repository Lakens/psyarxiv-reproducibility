Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
P = 0.082 Bonferroni correction retains the combined contrast but not the incident one (adjusted P = 0.082).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

BACKGROUND: Psychological treatment and SSRIs are both first-line options for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) and body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), but whether people in deprived areas receive the same kind of care is unknown. NICE has published tables from English primary care records (CPRD Aurum) for two possibly overlapping cohorts: 20,253 people diagnosed within three years and 7,271 newly diagnosed.

To establish whether deprivation is associated with which care is recorded, mental health referral or SSRI prescribing, in adults and children.

We verified NICE's 1,171 values, rebuilt counts from rounded percentages, repeated every test over all admissible counts with multiplicity, clustering and missing-data sensitivity analyses, and compared findings with national prescribing, NHS Talking Therapies data and 25 published studies.

RESULTS: Among newly diagnosed adults, 42% in the most deprived fifth had a mental health referral recorded, against 52% in the least, while SSRI prescribing barely differed (61% against 62%). In the three-year cohort, referral was 41% against 48% and SSRI prescribing higher in the most deprived fifth (54% against 49%). The two outcomes' slope indices differed by 17.0 percentage points (three-year cohort; Holm-adjusted P < 0.001) and 15.5 (newly diagnosed; Holm-adjusted P = 0.041; Bonferroni 0.082). NICE's comorbidity-adjusted estimates were similar. In newly diagnosed children, exploratory analyses showed referral of 46% against 54% (odds ratio 0.70 to 0.75), and lower SSRI prescribing in Asian than White children (0.60 to 0.65). In national NHS Talking Therapies data, OCD formed a smaller share of anxiety referrals in areas whose referrals came more from deprived neighbourhoods (Spearman's rho -0.48), unlike generalised anxiety disorder.

In what are, to our knowledge, England's only national primary care data on OCD and BDD, deprived areas had less recorded mental health referral but no less SSRI prescribing: equal prescribing should not be read as equal care. Child differences need confirmation; linked records could show whether the gap is in treatment received.

INTRODUCTION

OCD affects about 2% of adults in England (2.2%, 95% CI 1.7 to 2.8, in the 2023/4 Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey), and only one in ten adults with OCD (9.6%) reports ever having been diagnosed with it by a doctor [1, Tables 1.3 and 1 .12]. BDD affects about 1% of young people in England [2], and OCD is recorded in only 0.2% of primary care patients [3]. Psychological treatment and serotonergic medication are both recommended, and people in England have been offered a choice between them according to severity and preference since 2005 [4]. Whether that choice is available equally is a question of equity in the kind of care, not only in access: a health system can deliver similar amounts of care to different groups while delivering different kinds of care. Socioeconomic and ethnic differences in psychological therapy have been described for common mental disorders in UK primary care [5] [6] [7], but a targeted search found no such study for OCD or BDD. Because the pub-lished referral outcome, NICE's "any psychological referral", also counts referrals to community mental health teams and other mental health support, not only to psychological therapy, we call it mental health referral, and the data examined here assess differences in recorded prescribing and referral activity rather than equitable access to the recommended psychological treatment.

In 2026, as background to its guideline update [8], NICE analysed primary care records in the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum [9] for more than 20,000 people with a recorded diagnosis of OCD or BDD, and published the results as tables describing prescribing and referral by ethnicity, deprivation, age and sex [10]. To our knowledge, these are the only national data on how these disorders are managed in English general practice. The report tested differences in adults one outcome at a time; for children and young people it gave percentages only.

The individual records cannot be shared, but the tables give group sizes and percentages for every comparison, enough to rebuild the counts. We treated them as a national dataset in their own right and asked four questions that matter to commissioners and service planners: whether deprivation is associated with which care is recorded, referral or medication; whether the pattern extends to children and young people; how far the 30% with deprivation unrecorded could change the picture; and how the findings compare with national prescribing statistics and published studies. We also verified every published value and set the results against NICE's adjusted estimates. Our appraisal of the draft guideline is reported separately [11].

METHOD

Data source and cohorts

We used the PDF of NICE's health inequalities report A from the consultation page (56 pages; SHA-256 checksum in the supplementary data). People had a recorded diagnosis of OCD or BDD (SNOMED codes) and at least 12 months of GP registration before diagnosis [10, p. 7]. OCD and BDD were analysed together because too few people had a BDD diagnosis to analyse separately (p. 8); the report gives no separate counts. Two analyses were reported. The combined analysis took everyone with a diagnosis recorded in the three years before 1 February 2025, newly or previously diagnosed, and counted prescriptions and referrals in the three months before that date (n = 20,253; 17,614 adults and 2,639 children and young people; pp. [7] [8] 14). The incident analysis took people with a first recorded diagnosis and no earlier diagnosis in records going back to 2000, followed for 12 months, and was restricted to people "without prior comorbidities or related prescribing": 7,271 of 21,547 people with a first recorded diagnosis (3,949 adults and 3,322 children and young people; pp. [7] [8] 32). Periods affected by COVID-19 were excluded (p. 8). The restriction removed two thirds of people with a first recorded diagnosis (14,276 of 21,547). Conditioning on the absence of earlier comorbidity or prescribing can introduce selection bias: if people in more deprived areas, or in some ethnic groups, more often present late or with comorbidity already recorded, the incident cohort keeps a less typical subset of them, and its deprivation and ethnicity differences may not apply to everyone newly diagnosed. The report does not give the calendar years of diagnosis, say which periods were excluded, or say how deregistration, death or incomplete follow-up were handled. A first recorded diagnosis is not incident disease: records back to 2000 cannot exclude earlier or unrecorded illness, and the report notes that most patients are diagnosed in mental health outpatient settings (p. 54). Because the combined analysis includes newly diagnosed people (p. 1), the two cohorts may overlap; the report does not say. We therefore compare them as two descriptions, not as stages of one pathway, and agreement between them is not independent replication.

Exposures and outcomes

Exposures were area deprivation in fifths of the Index of Multiple Deprivation from linked data (IMD1 most deprived to IMD5 least deprived; p. 12), ethnicity in NICE's categories (White; Black or Black British; Asian or Asian British; Mixed; Chinese or Other), age band and sex. The report does not state the IMD edition, whether deprivation was assigned from the patient's or the practice's postcode, which practices were eligible for linkage, why deprivation was missing, or the source of ethnicity. NICE defined each outcome as the proportion of a group with at least one prescription, or at least one referral, of interest during the observation period (p. 9). We used SSRI and antipsychotic prescribing (the drugs in each class are not listed) and NICE's "any psychological referral". The report does not define the last. Its tables show it with three referral categories that overlap at the person level (their percentages sum to more than the total): referral to a community and generic mental health team; "other mental health support", which combines psychologist, counselling, psychotherapy, primary care mental health or gateway worker and guided self-help; and IAPT/Talking Therapies (pp. [10] [11]. It is therefore a referral to mental health services of any kind, not specifically to psychological therapy, and we call it mental health referral. Talking Therapies referral is the only category specific to psychological therapy. Neither prescriptions nor referrals were linked to OCD or BDD: the data do not record indication (p. 2), so a drug may have been prescribed, or a referral made, for another condition. We therefore describe recorded prescribing and referral among people with OCD or BDD, not treatment of OCD. A recorded referral is also not confirmed access: self-referrals to Talking Therapies need not appear in the GP record (p. 54), and the record does not show whether a referral was accepted, attended or completed.

Transcription and verification

We transcribed every value in the 28 results tables (16 descriptive, 12 regression; 1,171 values). A script checked every row against the text of the PDF, requiring its numbers to appear in the transcribed order on the cited page. All values passed. Because a script cannot confirm that a value sits in the right table, row and column, MP independently checked against the PDF, by table, row and column, every row behind the main findings and every finding in Supplementary Table S1: 61 rows, all confirmed, with no corrections needed. We then tested the tables' internal consistency: group sizes against the printed cohort sizes; NICE's unadjusted odds ratios against those rebuilt from the descriptive tables; the weighted mean of each outcome across the tables that describe the same cohort; and covariate estimates across models of the same cohort. We class each finding as a confirmed inconsistency (arithmetically incompatible values or text), a probable labelling error, or a question for NICE (Supplementary Table S1).

Reconstruction and rounding

Event counts were rebuilt as group size multiplied by the printed percentage. Because NICE printed whole-number percentages, each corresponds to a range of integer counts (those within ±0.5 percentage points of the printed value). We repeated every test over that range. Two-group comparisons were enumerated exhaustively over every admissible pair of counts. For the trend tests the exact extremes were found analytically: for each admissible total count the statistic is linear in the group counts, so its extremes follow from ordering the groups by score. For the slope index of inequality every admissible combination was enumerated (up to 9.0 million per test). Two blind re-implementations, each written by a separate instance of the same AI tool from a written specification and without access to our code or results, checked the analysis. The first reproduced the trend tests, odds ratios, absolute differences, slope indices, family false discovery rates and missing-deprivation derivation, and exposed one error, which we corrected. The second, at revision, covered what the first had not: the two co-primary contrasts with an exhaustive search of every admissible count (up to 8.6 million per outcome), their Holm and Bonferroni correction and design-effect thresholds, the extreme-fifth contrasts, all 96 secondary tests with their family and pooled false discovery rates, and the missing-deprivation ranges and tipping points. It reproduced every reported value and led to two corrections: a tighter bound for the people with sex unrecorded, and a tipping point that had been reported for a trend that was never significant, which the paper does not cite (statistical audit in the supplementary data). Such checks guard against coding errors but not against a misreading of the method shared by the specification and both implementations; all code is public with the supplementary data (https://osf.io/v4n8y/). We report the range of each estimate, the widest confidence interval and the largest (worst-case) P value.

Analyses

For deprivation we estimated the absolute difference between the most and least deprived fifths (Wald confidence intervals), the slope index of inequality and the relative index of inequality [12], and Cochran-Armitage tests for linear trend [13]. Each fifth was given its ridit score, the midpoint of its share of the cumulative population (IMD1 nearest 0, IMD5 nearest 1). The slope index of inequality (SII) is the slope of the outcome on this score by weighted least squares, with weights n/[p(1-p)], in percentage points; it is positive where the outcome is higher in less deprived areas. Its variance on the proportion scale is 1/Σw(x -x̄)², using plug-in binomial variances p(1 -p)/n without estimating an additional residual variance from the five fifths; the slope and its standard error were multiplied by 100 for reporting in percentage points, and inference used the normal distribution. The relative index of inequality (RII) is the fitted rate at the least deprived end (score 1) divided by the fitted rate at the most deprived end (score 0), so values above 1 also mean a higher rate in less deprived areas. We call a pattern a gradient only where it rises or falls steadily across all five fifths; otherwise we describe the deprivation difference (most against least deprived) or the overall trend. For children and young people, for whom NICE gave no inferential statistics, we computed crude odds ratios (Woolf intervals) against NICE's reference groups.

The central question was whether the deprivation difference in mental health referral differs from that in SSRI prescribing. The two co-primary tests are the differences between the SII for referral and the SII for SSRIs in adults, one in each cohort. Both outcomes are measured on the same people and their covariance is not published, so each contrast uses the most conservative bound on its standard error (the sum of the two standard errors, the largest possible for any correlation), at the admissible counts least favourable to a difference. We corrected the two co-primary tests by Holm's method, with Bonferroni correction as a sensitiv-ity analysis; this strategy was chosen at revision, after the results were known. The same contrasts of the extreme fifths, and all contrasts under independence, are sensitivity analyses.

The other tests were grouped into four families, defined during this secondary analysis after the central estimates had been seen and not preregistered: adult trend tests (16); child trend tests (6); child odds ratios (60); and absolute deprivation differences in both populations (14). Every test in each family is listed in the supplementary results (sections 9a to 9c). We report Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rates (q) within each family from the worst-case P values [14], every q where a result belongs to more than one family, and, as a sensitivity analysis, q with all 96 tests pooled as one family (supplementary results, section 9d). Where we report a widest 95% confidence interval, it is the envelope of the conventional intervals over all admissible counts (lowest lower limit to highest upper limit), not a single confidence interval. Practice-level clustering cannot be recovered from the tables. For each result we report the design effect at which its worst-case P would reach 0.05 (the nominal threshold): an assumed variance-inflation threshold, not an estimate of the actual design effect, and not a correction for confounding by practice. For the two coprimary contrasts we also report the threshold that keeps them significant after multiplicity correction: under Holm, the contrast with the smaller P must reach two-sided 0.025 and the other 0.05, with the first still significant; under Bonferroni, both must reach 0.025.

All our estimates are unadjusted. To show what adjustment changes, we set them against NICE's adjusted odds ratios for adults (Tables 6 and 20), which adjust each exposure separately for depression and anxiety (SSRIs and referral) or psychosis, schizophrenia and epilepsy (antipsychotics), but not for age, sex, ethnicity, region or practice (p. 12).

Missing deprivation

The sex tables cover almost everyone in a cohort (sex was unrecorded for 8 of 20,253), whereas the deprivation tables cover only those with deprivation recorded. The difference between the two gives the rate among people with deprivation missing, with a range for rounding in both tables. The subtraction assumes that everyone in the deprivation table is also in the sex table. Five adults in the combined analysis have sex unrecorded (three children; none in the incident analysis), and the report does not say whether the deprivation tables include them. If they do, 5,379 adults lack deprivation (17,614 minus the 12,235 in the deprivation table) and the subtraction misplaces up to five of them; if they do not, the subtraction gives exactly the 5,374 with sex recorded, and up to five more lack deprivation. The group therefore holds 5,374 to 5,379 adults. The derived ranges allow for any placement of these people and any outcome among them, which widens each range by at most 0.1 percentage points, and the tipping points below are unchanged when recomputed at either extreme (supplementary results, sections 8 and 12). This also requires the two tables to describe the same people, outcome definitions and observation period. NICE presents both as stratifications of one cohort (p. 11), and the weighted mean of each outcome agrees within one percentage point across the tables of a cohort, which is consistent with this but does not establish it. We then asked whether the derived rates could arise from a mixture of the recorded fifths (both prescribing outcomes at once, on a 1% grid, confirmed by exact linear programming); recomputed the deprivation difference under three specified allocations of the missing group (in proportion to the recorded fifths; 60:40 to IMD1 and IMD2; 40:60 to IMD4 and IMD5), each giving the group its derived rate; and, with proportional allocation, found how much the missing group's own rate would have to differ between the most and least deprived fifths for the combined difference to vanish or its trend to lose significance (a tipping point). In the tipping model the missing group's rate changes by equal steps from one fifth to the next; the steps are centred so that the group's overall derived total is preserved, and any combination giving a rate below 0% or above 100% in any fifth is excluded. The concentrated allocations are illustrative scenarios, not estimates. If missingness reflects which practices were eligible for linkage, it is tied to geography and service availability and clustered by practice, which none of these analyses model. All are sensitivity analyses under stated assumptions, not general bounds.

Context: national prescribing, NHS Talking Therapies and published studies

For context only, we compared the deprivation pattern with national prescribing statistics [15, : patients dispensed any antidepressant or antipsychotic, for any indication, by IMD 2025 quintile, 2024/25] and with published studies. These comparisons differ from NICE's data in population, measure and period: the national figures are annual counts of patients, by quintiles of equal numbers of small areas rather than of people, with practice postcodes substituted where a patient's is missing, and without population denominators, so their ratios are not prescribing-rate ratios. They are reported in Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Text S1. Published studies were identified by six PubMed topic searches on 12 October 2026 (socioeconomic position and registers; ethnicity and treatment; CPRD and other UK electronic records; pharmacoepidemiology; NHS Talking Therapies; BDD), which returned 122 unique records, followed by related-article and targeted searches whose strings and counts were not recorded. Twenty-five studies reporting OCD or BDD, or mental disorders or psychotropic prescribing in UK primary care, by socioeconomic position, ethnicity, age or sex were retained (Supplementary Table S2).

We also used NHS England's annual reports of NHS Talking Therapies for anxiety and depression, 2017-18 to 2024-25 ( [16]; 2018-19 is published only in an archive format we could not open). These public tables give OCD (the presenting complaint; the problem descriptor before 2020-21) and the patient's area deprivation decile only as separate breakdowns, never together. We report OCD nationally and, for 2022-23 to 2024-25, compare across the 42 integrated care boards (ICBs) OCD's share of referrals for anxiety and stress-related disorders with the share of each ICB's referrals that come from the most deprived fifth of small areas (Spearman's rho, two-sided permutation P), with the share for generalised anxiety disorder as a comparison condition. The comparison is ecological, and it describes each ICB's deprivation by the people referred rather than by its residents, because area statistics could not be reached from the analysis environment.

The analyses of NICE's tables and of the Talking Therapies files use the Python standard library, and the extraction of the NHSBSA workbooks uses openpyxl; the transcription, scripts and full results are in the supplementary data. Ethics approval was not required.

In sensitivity analyses, the comparison of extreme fifths in the incident analysis (referral difference -10, SSRI difference -1) did not reach significance under the conservative bound (z = 1.56 at the central counts, 1.26 at the least favourable).

All adult deprivation trends in referral and in antipsychotic and SSRI prescribing in the combined analysis, and in re-ferral in the incident analysis, had worst-case P below 0.01 and q below 0.03 whether the tests were grouped in families or pooled. They would lose nominal significance only at assumed design effects of 2.2 (SSRIs, combined), 4.7 (referral, incident) and 9.5 (referral, combined).

NHS Talking Therapies

In 2024-25, NHS Talking Therapies received 25,221 referrals with OCD as the presenting complaint (2.6% of referrals with a condition recorded; 46.7% of all referrals had none) and finished 18,249 courses of treatment for OCD ( [16]; Supplementary Table S4). Recovery after treatment was 44.6% for OCD against 50.5% for all conditions, and reliable recovery 38.7% against 47.5%, after more appointments (mean 11.3 against 8.4); recovery for OCD has been between 44.6% and 46.3% each year since 2020-21. Body dysmorphic disorder was the presenting complaint in 1,028 referrals (recovery 48.2%). Across all conditions, referrals from the most deprived tenth of small areas were 2.07 times those from the least deprived, but recovery was lower (42.8% against 56.2%; slope index of inequality +14.0 percentage points). Across the 42 ICBs, OCD made up a smaller share of anxiety and stress-related referrals where more of the ICB's referrals came from the most deprived fifth of areas: Spearman's rho -0.47 (P = 0.002) in 2022-23, -0.53 (P < 0.001) in 2023-24 and -0.48 (P = 0.002) in 2024-25 (share ranging from 2.9% to 9.0% across ICBs in 2024-25; Figure 4). The share for generalised anxiety disorder did not vary with deprivation (rho -0.04 to +0.08), and neither did recovery from OCD (rho -0.02 to +0.07). The presenting complaint was less often recorded in more deprived ICBs (rho +0.28 to +0.43), which the anxiety-referral denominator sets aside. S4 gives all three years.

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

Among adults with recorded OCD or BDD in England, those in the most deprived areas were less likely than those in the least deprived to have a mental health referral recorded, but no less likely to be prescribed an SSRI. Four findings stand out. First, the deprivation difference in GP-recorded mental health referral differs from that in SSRI prescribing: referral is lower in more deprived areas while SSRI prescribing is not consistently lower, a contrast that holds under a conservative bound on the correlation of the two outcomes and after Holm correction, robustly in the combined analysis and narrowly in the incident analysis, where Bonferroni correction or modest clustering would remove it. Second, in exploratory analyses of newly diagnosed children and young people, the referral difference is of similar size to that in adults, and Asian children were less often prescribed SSRIs in both analyses; only the finding in newly diagnosed Asian children survives every multiplicity grouping we examined. Third, nearly a third of adults lacked recorded deprivation; under the allocations we tested, the referral difference persisted, and under proportional allocation removing it required a substantial reversal of the referral pattern within that group, whereas the SSRI difference was fragile. Fourth, the published tables are largely internally consistent: one confirmed inconsistency, one conditional on the sample of a model, and five probable errors or questions, none affecting these analyses.

Comparison with other studies

We found no published journal study of OCD or BDD in UK primary care reporting prescribing or referral by deprivation or ethnicity; that establishes only that our targeted search found none, and NICE's report is itself the first source of the adult findings. The nearest comparator, a Lambeth study of adults with any recorded mental disorder, found Talking Therapies use, against no recorded treatment, higher in more deprived areas, and medication alone unrelated to deprivation ( [7]; Table 6). Its outcome (therapy received), population (mostly depression and anxiety), deprivation scale (relative to one borough) and comparison (against no treatment) all differ from ours, as may geography, period and model, so the two are not directly comparable. Lower SSRI prescribing for Asian than White children agrees in direction with national primary care data on first antidepressant prescriptions [17], and ethnic minority groups are under-represented among people treated for OCD in specialist services [18]. Supplementary Text S1 sets out the full comparison, including national prescribing statistics (Supplementary Table S3), the antipsychotic pattern, need, recording and data quality.

The 2023/4 Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey gives a population benchmark for the balance of treatments. Among adults meeting criteria for OCD, 53.0% were taking medication and 25.3% receiving psychological therapy for a mental or emotional problem, and 64.3% either (134 participants; [1, Table 2.2]). The survey measures current treatment from any source, whatever its indication, and the primary care record measures GP-recorded prescribing and referral, so the figures are not directly comparable; both, however, place medication well ahead of psychological treatment. The survey does not report treatment of OCD by deprivation.

National NHS Talking Therapies data point the same way at the level of areas. In each of three years, OCD made up a smaller share of anxiety referrals in ICBs whose referrals came more from deprived neighbourhoods, while the share for generalised anxiety disorder did not change; that specificity makes a general difference in the mix of anxiety referrals, or in how completely the presenting complaint is recorded, an unlikely sole explanation, although services in deprived areas could also record OCD less specifically. An association between areas cannot be transferred to individuals, but its direction matches the lower recorded referral of people with OCD in deprived areas in NICE's data, in a service people can refer themselves to.

Interpretation

The first finding concerns the balance of recorded activity, not the total amount of care any person received, which the tables do not describe: a person may have both a prescription and a referral, either, or neither. It is consistent with a pattern of care in which people in deprived areas do not show consistently lower recorded SSRI prescribing but are less likely to have a mental health referral recorded, but the published tables cannot show individual treatment combinations or substitution. They give the two outcomes separately, not for the same people together, so they cannot show whether medication replaced referral for anyone, and they record neither indication, severity, preference nor dose. Lower referral may reflect fewer referrals, more self-referral outside the GP record, different service availability or different preferences. What is recorded as a GP referral also depends on local pathways. In many areas GPs give patients the details of NHS Talking Therapies and patients refer themselves, so no referral is recorded, and practices are often not told whether the patient made contact. Where more severe or specialist presentations, OCD and BDD among them, go instead to a primary care mental health service, the coding can change when that service is reorganised, for example when it moves into secondary care: the same clinical decision may be recorded as a referral in one area and not in another, or before a reorganisation and not after it.

Where no specific code exists, the same referral may also be recorded under different codes, such as counselling, mental health worker or community mental health team, even within one practice, so whether it counts depends on the code lists used. NICE's referral outcome is not specific to OCD or BDD, and its code lists are not published, so this variation cannot be assessed from the tables. If service configuration differs between more and less deprived areas, it would appear as a deprivation difference in recorded referral. Choices made with patients, and the uptake of what is offered, may also differ with health beliefs and expectations, which can vary with deprivation, and for practical reasons. Psychological treatment means repeated appoint-ments, often online or some distance away, and digital exclusion, travel costs and inflexible or multiple jobs are likely to weigh more heavily in deprived areas, which could make medication the more feasible option for some people. The tables cannot test this, and in the incident analysis SSRI prescribing was not higher in the most deprived fifth (61% against 62%), so lower recorded referral there was not matched by more prescribing. Higher SSRI prescribing is not evidence of inappropriate prescribing. The contrast between the combined and incident analyses (an SSRI difference in one and not the other) reflects two differently selected cohorts and cannot be read as prescribing changing after diagnosis.

Confounding and selection limit what the associations mean. Age, region, practice, comorbidity, severity and the local availability of services could each be related both to deprivation and to recorded care. NICE's adjustment for depression and anxiety changed the referral estimates little (Table 5), but that does not show the absence of confounding: no estimate is adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, region or practice, and none for severity, which is not recorded, so residual confounding remains likely. Antipsychotic prescribing, for example, rose from 9% of adults aged 18-24 to 25% of those aged 65 and over in the combined analysis [10, Table 7], and the age structure of the deprivation fifths is not published; if older adults are unevenly spread across the fifths, age alone could produce some or all of the antipsychotic difference. The cohorts are also selected: they include only people whose OCD or BDD was diagnosed and recorded, the incident cohort only those without earlier comorbidity or prescribing (Methods), and recording is itself incomplete (Supplementary Text S1) and may vary by deprivation and ethnicity. Equal need in the population therefore does not imply equal need within these diagnosed cohorts, and the referral difference cannot be attributed to deprivation itself.

The child findings matter because NICE drew no conclusions for children. The incident child cohort is close in size to the incident adult cohort (3,322 against 3,949), and its deprivation fifths held 399 to 712 children, enough for exploratory unadjusted comparisons; the combined child cohort is much smaller than the adult one (2,639 against 17,614), and most ethnic minority groups are small. The referral difference in newly diagnosed children is of similar size to the adult one but is fragile: its false discovery rate depends on how the tests are grouped (q 0.047 to 0.149), it does not survive modest clustering, and it should be tested in patient-level data before it informs policy. The lower SSRI prescribing in Asian children is the more robust of the child findings. Help-seeking and expectations of treatment for OCD differ between ethnic groups in the UK [20], and Asian young people elsewhere are diagnosed with OCD far less often than their symptom levels predict [21]. Our data cannot distinguish under-prescription from differences in presentation, severity, family or cultural preferences about medication for young people, diagnostic and referral pathways, or the selection of children into the diagnosed cohort. The primary care record cannot show whether prescribing was clinically appropriate in either group, so lower prescribing is not by itself evidence of under-treatment, nor higher prescribing of over-treatment.

NICE found higher recorded referral among Black, Asian and Mixed adults, which runs against most evidence on ethnic inequality in psychological therapy, although that evidence concerns therapy received rather than GP-recorded mental health referral. In South London, Black African, Asian and Mixed groups were less likely than White British people to refer themselves to Talking Therapies [6]. If that holds nationally, GP-recorded referrals would overstate minority groups' access relative to White patients, who more often bypass the GP. The same mechanism could act on deprivation in either direction, which is why recorded referral should not be read as access.

NICE's comparisons exclude the 31% of adults with deprivation unrecorded. Their rates can be derived only imprecisely from the published tables, and are compatible both with the recorded population and with many deprivation profiles. NICE's report states that deprivation came from linked data and was missing for 30.3% of the combined cohort (pp. 12, 14), but not how it was assigned or why it was missing; other UK primary care sources lack it for 0.3% to 3.4% (Supplementary Text S1). If the missingness follows practice-level linkage eligibility, it is tied to geography and services, which our allocations do not model. The referral difference withstood the allocations and tipping points we tested; the SSRI difference did not. Patient-level data with the reasons for missingness would allow a full sensitivity analysis.

Strengths and limitations

The analysis uses the only national primary care data NICE published for this guideline, verifies every value, carries the rounding of the published percentages through every estimate and states its multiplicity strategy for the central contrasts. Its limitations are those of aggregate data and are set out above: estimates unadjusted beyond NICE's comorbidity models, clustering assessed only by assumed thresholds, recorded activity rather than treatment received, OCD and BDD combined, and a third of adults without recorded deprivation. In addition, the incident cohort excludes people with prior comorbidities and may overlap with the combined cohort; the report gives no code lists, IMD edition or calendar period; ethnicity recording in CPRD is incomplete [22] and NICE's categories are broad; the published tables give each characteristic separately, so how deprivation, ethnicity, sex and age combine cannot be examined, although barriers can compound, as Black and South Asian women describe in seeking perinatal mental health care [23]; values were transcribed and checked by script and, for the rows behind the main findings, by MP, and no check can detect an error in the source; the statistical checks were blind re-implementations by the same AI tool, not an audit by an independent statistician; the report is a consultation draft and may change; the Talking Therapies comparison is ecological, describes each ICB's deprivation by the people it refers rather than by its residents, and depends on a presenting complaint that was unrecorded for 47% of referrals in 2024-25; and the comparisons with national prescribing and published studies are context only, resting on a targeted search by one reviewer.

The tables do not identify pregnancy or the year after birth. Women made up most of the adults (10,479 of 17,609 with sex recorded in the combined analysis), most adults were of reproductive age (12,807 of 17,614 aged 18 to 44), and OCD is more common in pregnancy and after birth than in the general population [24]. For women in the perinatal period, a lower rate of SSRI prescribing may reflect concerns about taking psychotropic medication in pregnancy and breastfeeding, for which NICE gives separate guidance in CG192 [25], rather than less care provided. Referrals for OCD support in the perinatal period may also be signposted to specialist perinatal mental health services or to psychological services, which NICE's referral outcome may not capture; the report does not specify. Therefore, neither outcome can be generalised to perinatal women, and the deprivation gap may differ in this group.

Implications

Equal prescribing should not be read as equal care. Psychological treatment is a first-line option for OCD at every level of severity [4], and a referral, or a self-referral, is usually the route to it. In what are, to our knowledge, the only national primary care data on these disorders, people in the most deprived areas were prescribed SSRIs as often as others but had a mental health referral recorded less often: 42% against 52% among newly diagnosed adults.

Whether that gap means fewer referrals, self-referrals the GP record does not capture, local coding or different preferences cannot be settled from these data, and that uncertainty is itself a finding: the routine data on which services are planned cannot show whether people in deprived areas reach the treatment recommended for them.

Three changes would make it possible to tell. First, complete recording of area deprivation in research extracts of primary care data, which was missing for 31% of adults here against 0.03% in national prescribing statistics. Second, consistent coding of mental health referrals, including to primary care mental health services, so that they can be counted, and notification to practices when patients refer themselves. Third, linkage of GP, NHS Talking Therapies and secondary care records, including the Mental Health Services Data Set, analysed at patient level with adjustment for age, sex, comorbidity, region and practice, which could test whether these differences in recorded activity are differences in treatment received, separate OCD from BDD, and examine prescribing and referral together in the same people; CPRD's pregnancy registers [26] would also allow women in pregnancy and the year after birth to be examined separately. For commissioners, the referral gap is a reason to monitor uptake of psychological treatment for OCD and BDD by deprivation, not only prescribing. NHS Talking Therapies already records both the presenting complaint and the patient's area deprivation; publishing OCD by deprivation, which its annual tables do not, would show directly whether the gap continues into psychological therapy. For guideline developers, including NICE in its current update, the same tables support recording that deprivation is associated with less GP-recorded referral but not less SSRI prescribing in adults, that a similar referral difference appears, provisionally, in newly diagnosed children, and that a third of adults lack the deprivation data such comparisons depend on.

Supplementary Text S1. Comparison with national prescribing and published studies.

Our targeted PubMed search found no published journal study of OCD or BDD in UK primary care (CPRD, QResearch, THIN or SAIL) reporting prescribing or referral by deprivation or ethnicity; NICE's report is itself the first source of the adult findings. The search was targeted, by one reviewer, and its follow-up search strings were not recorded, so the comparison is contextual background, not a systematic review. The comparators are studies of other mental disorders in UK primary care with the same structure of exposure and outcome, OCD studies from specialist services, registers and surveys, and studies of data quality (Table 6; all 25 studies in Supplementary Table S2). All differ from NICE's data in population, measure or period, so agreement and disagreement are indicative only.

National prescribing.

In England in 2024/25, 2,228,126 people in the most deprived quintile and 1,546,416 in the least received an antidepressant (ratio 1.44), and 254,044 and 126,498 an antipsychotic (ratio 2.01), for any indication (Supplementary Table S3); the pattern was the same in 2025/26 (1.44 and 1.98). Among adults with OCD or BDD the SSRI comparison was weaker (ratio of percentages prescribed 1.10 in the combined analysis, 0.98 in the incident analysis), and the antipsychotic comparison reversed (0.65 and 0.64). Deprivation was unknown for 0.03% of patients in the national statistics, against 31% of adults in NICE's combined analysis. These national figures count patients dispensed a drug for any indication, by quintiles of equal numbers of small areas, without population denominators, so their ratios are not prescribingrate ratios and do not establish an OCD-specific pattern (Supplementary Table S3).

Deprivation and psychological therapy. The nearest comparator runs the other way, although it measures something different. In Lambeth, among 110,419 adults with any recorded mental disorder, the odds of receiving NHS Talking Therapies rather than no recorded treatment fell steadily from the most to the least deprived quintile (odds ratio 0.68, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.86, least against most deprived), as did secondary care psychological treatment (0.40) [7], and at area level Talking Therapies use is higher, and recovery lower, in poorer areas [5]. Several differences may explain the contrast. Catalao's outcome is therapy received, ours a referral recorded by the GP, which misses self-referral; its quintiles are relative to one deprived borough, where only 0.5% of people with a diagnosis lived in the least deprived quintile; its population has mostly depression and anxiety, whose care in Talking Therapies is better established than exposure and response prevention for OCD; its comparison is against no treatment, ours against everyone with the diagnosis; and geography, population composition, period and modelling also differ. Our referral outcome also includes community mental health team and other mental health referrals, not only therapy. The two findings cannot be reconciled from published data; linked data are needed.

Deprivation and medication.

Our finding that SSRI prescribing is not lower in deprived areas is not contradicted by the comparators. In Lambeth, there was no clear evidence of a difference in medication alone between the least and the most deprived quintile (0.96, 0.77 to 1.21) [7]; among children and young people in England, first antidepressant prescriptions rose with deprivation once region was taken into account (most against least deprived Townsend fifth, 1.29 to 1.48 in adolescents) [17]; and nationally 1.44 times as many people in the most as in the least deprived quintile received an antidepressant (Supplementary Table S3). Among children with OCD or BDD, by contrast, SSRI prescribing was 41% in both extreme deprivation fifths, so the population gradient in children's antidepressant use, which mixes need with treatment, is not apparent among those already diagnosed.

Antipsychotics.

In the general population, antipsychotics are prescribed far more often in deprived areas: 2.0 times as many patients in the most as in the least deprived quintile nationally (Supplementary Table S3), and a prevalence ratio of 2.80 to 3.87 in UK primary care [19]. Among adults with OCD or BDD the gradient reverses (13% against 20%; ratio 0.65; 0.64 in the newly diagnosed). In Swedish specialist care, about three quarters of people with OCD taking an SRI also received other drugs, antipsychotics among them (27%) [29]. Antipsychotics may be prescribed as augmentation for OCD, but the present data cannot distinguish augmentation from treatment of comorbid conditions. One hypothesis, which these data cannot test, is that augmentation, or the specialist advice that usually precedes it, is reached less often from deprived areas. Severity, comorbidity and age could also explain it, and the published tables cannot adjust for them; antipsychotic prescribing in NICE's combined analysis rose steeply with age, and age may differ across deprivation fifths.

Ethnicity. The lower SSRI prescribing for Asian children agrees in direction with national primary care data: first antidepressant prescriptions for Asian children and young people were 0.41 to 0.70 times those for White children, and for Black children 0.32 to 0.71 [17]. Those are population rates, which combine need, presentation, diagnosis and treatment; ours are conditional on a recorded diagnosis, so a difference persists after diagnosis. Ethnic minority groups are underrepresented among people treated for OCD by 57% in South London specialist services, against 29% for depression [18], and are diagnosed with OCD less often in New Zealand's secondary care [37] and Canada [21]. In UK primary care, minority adults more often received medication without psychological treatment (Asian 1.22, Black African 1.11), and Black African and Asian adults less often reached Talking Therapies (0.68 and 0.87) [7], consistent with lower self-referral [6]. NICE's finding of more GP-recorded referral among minority adults may therefore reflect the route to therapy more than access to it.

Need, recording and direction.

OCD is under-recorded in primary care: GPs recorded it in 0.2% of patients [3] against a population prevalence of 1.1% in adults in 2000 [32] and 2.2% in 2023/4 [1] and 0.25% in children [31], and BDD affects 1.0% of young people in England [2]. Recorded diagnosis is therefore itself selective, and help-seeking is lower when OCD occurs without comorbidity (14% against 56%) [32]. In the population, the burden of OCD is not lower in deprived groups [31, 34, 43]: in England in 2023/4, age-standardised past-week prevalence in adults was 2.0% in the most deprived fifth of areas and 1.7% in the least, without a gradient across the fifths ([1, Table 1.9]; no confidence intervals are published for these estimates). That does not establish equal need within the diagnosed cohorts, whose selection through diagnosis and recording may itself vary by deprivation. OCD also lowers educational attainment and employment [35, 36], so some of the association between deprivation and treatment may run from the disorder to the area of residence.

Missing data.

Deprivation was missing for 31% of adults in NICE's data (30.3% of the whole combined cohort), against 0.3% in QResearch [17], 3.4% in Lambeth DataNet [7] and 0.03% in national prescribing statistics. Missingness on this scale is unusual for area deprivation, which needs only a postcode; it may reflect a restriction in the linkage NICE used rather than missing addresses, but the report does not say. Recorded ethnicity agrees with the 2021 Census for at least 96% of White British people but at most 75% of Mixed and 71% of Other groups [42], so the estimates for NICE's Mixed and Chinese or Other categories are the least reliable.
