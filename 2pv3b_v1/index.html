Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Although stress is a key driver of chronic disease risk and all-cause mortality, it is rarely assessed or treated in most medical settings. Moreover, existing stress management interventions are not tailored to patients' most pressing needs. To address these issues, we developed a fully remote, 12-week precision stress management program called the Precision Intervention for Stress and Resilience (PRECISE), which identifies each person's primary stress-related functional problem (i.e., thinking style, social relationships, sleep, eating, physical activity) and assigns treatment accordingly. Diverse participants (N = 426, 18-86 years old) were randomized to a passive control arm (assessment only), active control arm (environmental toxins reduction education), or PRECISE. Average treatment effects (ATEs) revealed that participants receiving PRECISE exhibited greater reductions in the primary outcome, perceived stress, both immediately following treatment (reduction: 15.9%; passive control: ATE = -1.58, 95% CI [-2.83, -0.32], d = -0.42; active control: ATE = -0.71, 95% CI [-1.99, 0.57], d = -0.19) and 12 weeks later (reduction: 18.7%; passive control: ATE = -2.93, 95% CI [-4.20, -1.67], d = -0.78; active control: ATE = -1.49, 95% CI [-2.81, -0.18], d = 0.39). PRECISE is thus a highly efficacious and scalable intervention enabling precision stress management. ClinicalTrials.gov Registration: NCT06063174

Introduction

Experiencing psychological stress, such as feeling overwhelmed or not having enough resources to cope, negatively impacts cardiovascular, metabolic, immune, and neurobiological systems in a manner that can degrade mental and physical health, accelerate biological aging, and increase mortality risk across the life course 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 . Indeed, stress is implicated in 9 out of the 10 leading causes of death in the United States today, including heart disease, cancer, respiratory disorders, and suicide 6 . In the U.S. alone, these effects are estimated to cost taxpayers more than $300 billion annually in absenteeism, lost productivity, and healthcare expenditures, and they are also very common: in 2022, nearly three quarters of Americans reported that stress negatively impacted their health in the last month and more than 25% said that on most days, they were too stressed to function 7 . Despite these far-reaching effects on morbidity and mortality, stress is not routinely assessed or addressed as a treatment target in most medical settings 8, 9.

One reason health care providers cite for not assessing or addressing stress involves the present lack of readily available referral options-namely, evidence-based stress management programs to which providers can reliably refer patients to reduce their stress and risk of developing stress-related health problems 10 . In the absence of such options, providers often recommend psychotherapy, such as cognitive behavior therapy (CBT), but (a) these treatments are not specifically designed to treat stress and (b) they are often based on generalized protocols rather than personalized to address a patient's specific biopsychosocial risk profile, goals, or needs 11, 12 . Consequently, stress is rarely treated in primary or specialty care, and when it is, providers typically use a largely untailored, one-size-fits-all approach 13 . Given the great variability in how different people respond to stress (e.g., changes in eating, sleep, physical activity)-and how such differences may underlie risk for chronic health conditions such as P R E P R I N T depression, hypertension, and chronic pain-a more tailored, precision medicine-based approach to reducing stress and enhancing biopsychosocial resilience is sorely needed 14.

Present Study

To address this striking paradox of stress being one of the most common but least addressed medical issues of our time, we developed the first fully remote, highly scalable, multimodal precision stress management intervention program, called the Precision Intervention for Stress and Resilience (PRECISE). PRECISE has two key components that combine to enable the delivery of a highly tailored, low-cost precision medicine-based approach to addressing stress.

The first component is a novel screening tool called the Consequences of Stress Scale (CSS), which we developed as a brief 15-item questionnaire that assesses the extent to which stress negatively impacts a person's thinking style, social relationships, sleep, eating, and physical activity over the past two weeks (see doi: 10.17605/OSF.IO/3HA9N). Separate risk scores are derived for each domain, giving health care providers the ability to quickly determine the specific functioning domain requiring the greatest attention. Second, we created a 12-week, precision stress management treatment program that consists of five different evidence-based interventions designed to normalize patients' biopsychosocial functioning in the five domains described above (i.e., Think Well for thinking style, Be Well for social relationships, Sleep Well for sleep, Eat Well for eating, and Move Well for physical activity) 14 . These domains were selected because they are the most common stress-related complaints and, in addition, each promotes health-damaging biology when aberrant 8, 15, 16 . Each of the five intervention programs include (a) watching a prerecorded coaching video on the topic (∼15 minute), (b) completing digital modules on the content (20-30 min), (c) attending a 30 minute online coaching session, and (d) completing a brief five-minute comprehension check on the material, each once a week for twelve weeks.

P R E P R I N T

For our clinical trial testing the efficacy of PRECISE, which we called the California Stress, Trauma, and Resilience Study (CalSTARS) 14 , we created risk scores based on each participant's responses on the CSS. Next, we randomized participants to either a passive control or active control arm, or to their tailored PRECISE treatment arm, in a 1:1:3 ratio. This ratio was selected to provide additional power to conduct subgroup analyses both within, and comparing across, the five individual PRECISE arms. Participants in the PRECISE treatment arm received the specific intervention they were determined to need the most. For example, those primarily endorsing catastrophic thinking during times of stress received the 12-week CBT-based thinking styles intervention, whereas those primarily reporting challenges with eating (e.g., too much or too little) received the 12-week mindful eating-based intervention. In contrast, participants in the active control arm received an environmental toxins exposure reduction program that mirrored the exact structure of the PRECISE intervention, but which focused on reducing daily life exposure to environmental toxins (e.g., from cooking and cleaning supplies). Finally, participants in the passive control arm completed the exact same assessment protocol as those in both the PRECISE and active control arm, but they did not receive any educational content.

Here, we summarize, for the first time, the efficacy of this novel precision stress management intervention program for reducing participants' perceived stress levels-our primary trial outcomegiven the well-known link between stress, chronic diseases of aging, and all-cause mortality 6, 17, 18.

We assessed participants' perceived stress levels at baseline, following the 12-week intervention (or control) period, and again 12 weeks later (delayed follow-up). At these timepoints, we also assessed several secondary outcomes that mapped onto our five domain-specific intervention arms (i.e., thinking styles, social relationships, sleep, eating, and physical activity). Based on the extant literature, we hypothesized that participants randomized to receive PRECISE would exhibit greater reductions in perceived stress over time than those in both the passive and active control arms. Consequences of Stress Scale. Prior to randomizing participants to either the PRECISE or a control arm at eligibility, we used the CSS scale to stratify participants based on their primary domain of stress-related dysfunction-namely, thinking style, social relationships, sleep, eating, or physical activity (see Methods). As shown in Figure 4, we calculated the proportion of participants in every PRECISE arm who met each CSS domain's eligibility threshold at each timepoint or, in other words, who indicated experiencing stress-related dysfunction in each domain. In addition, we conducted χ2 tests of proportions to examine whether the proportion of participants in a PRECISE arm who reported dysfunction varied across CSS domains at each time point.

At baseline, as expected based on the matching procedure, a greater proportion of individuals reported dysfunction in the CSS domain matching their PRECISE arm (100%) compared to the other domains (21.6-66.7%). Exploratory Outcomes

In addition to the CSS, we collected data on six exploratory outcomes-namely, mindfulness, social conflict, loneliness, insomnia, stress eating, and physical activity (see Methods for scales). As shown in Table S3, baseline scores for these outcomes generally did not differ between the arms except for loneliness, which was elevated at baseline in the Be Well arm (p = 0.002). Furthermore, only changes in insomnia severity following the intervention consistently differed between those receiving PRECISE vs. the control arms. Specifically, participants in every PRECISE arm reported greater decreases in insomnia severity than those in the passive control arm (see Table S4, Figure S2), with especially large reductions found for the Sleep Well arm compared to both control arms (vs. passive control: ds = -1.40 to -1.24; vs. active control: ds = -0.90 to -0.84). Participants who received the Sleep Well intervention also reported significant increases in mindfulness (ds = 0.95-1.09) and decreases in loneliness (ds = -0.58 to -0.55), relative to both control arms at the delayed follow-up (week 24).

Together, these findings suggest that levels of mindfulness, social conflict, loneliness, insomnia, stress eating, and physical activity were similar across the PRECISE arms at baseline.

Furthermore, while individuals in all of the PRECISE arms reported improvements in insomnia following the intervention, these effects were greatest for those in the Sleep Well arm. Participants receiving Sleep Well also exhibited significant increases in mindfulness and decreases in loneliness, suggesting that normalizing sleep using PRECISE may improve other psychosocial processes.

Sensitivity Analyses

Importantly, sensitivity analyses yielded the same pattern and statistical significance as the primary results. Specifically, as shown in Figure 3 and Table 2, participants in PRECISE arms reported greater decreases in perceived stress than those in the passive control arm at both timepoints for the complete case and propensity score-weighted (see Figure S3) analyses (postintervention: ATEss = -1.73 to -1.66, p-adjusted < 0.017; delayed follow-up: ATEss = -3.03 to -3.02, p-adjusted < 0.001). Further, as with the primary analyses, there was a significantly greater reduction in perceived stress for participants in PRECISE vs. the active control arm at the delayed follow-up (ATEs = -1.66 to -1.57, p-adjusted < 0.045), but not immediately post-intervention (ATEs = -0.98 to -0.91, p-adjusted > 0.255). As a final sensitivity analysis, we tested whether the effects of PRECISE on change in perceived stress over time differed for those in study wave 1 (n = 206) vs. study wave 2 (n = 217). Results revealed that study wave did not moderate treatment effects at any time point (ps > 0.101), and the effect sizes for changes in perceived stress within each of the PRECISE arms were very similar across the two waves (wave 1: d = -1.11 to -0.95, wave 2: d = -1.00 to -0.81). Moreover, ATEs vs. the passive control arm for both waves remained statistically significant at the delayed follow-up (ps < 0.029) even when reducing the sample size by approximately half and examining each wave separately.

Post-hoc Analyses

To examine possible dose-response effects, we examined whether the number of coaching sessions attended, modules completed, videos watched, or weekly diaries completedas well as a mean composite of these variables (see Table S5 for descriptive statistics)-were related to treatment response. As shown in Table S6 and Figure S4, there were no significant P R E P R I N T main effects of any "dose" variable (ps > 0.26), nor were there significant interactions with treatment arm (ps > 0.10). Therefore, the effects of PRECISE do not appear to be strongly driven by treatment dose.

Discussion

Although stress is one of the greatest medical challenges of our time 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 , it is rarely assessed and almost never addressed as a primary treatment target in most medical settings 8, 9, 10, 22.

Compounding this issue is the fact that individuals differ widely in how they respond to stressful situations 17,23,24,25 , meaning there are various pathways from stress to chronic illness that require tailored interventions. To address these challenges, we developed and tested the first highly scalable precision stress management intervention program designed to reduce perceived stress and enhance resilience by targeting each person's most significant stress-related problem. Our Randomized Clinical Trial (RCT) was largely successful, with participants randomly assigned to receive PRECISE exhibiting greater reductions in perceived stress than those in both the passive control arm (at both follow-ups) and active control arm (at the delayed follow-up). Specifically, the estimated PSS-10 score decrease for those receiving PRECISE was 3.49 points (d = -0.92) from baseline to post-intervention and 4.12 points (d = -1.09) from baseline to the delayed followup (12 weeks after the intervention ended). These changes reflect reductions in perceived stress of 15.9% and 18.7%, respectively. The fact that participants' perceived stress levels continued to decline three months following treatment suggests that PRECISE leads to lasting improvements in psychosocial functioning. Moreover, participants generally had high rates of attendance and interaction with intervention content (see Table S5), and there did not appear to be dose-dependent effects of PRECISE, as attendance and intervention component completion rates did not moderate treatment responses (see Table S6).

P R E P R I N T

Although there is not an established minimal clinically important difference for the PSS-10, standardized effect sizes for changes in PSS-10 scores for participants receiving PRECISE were on par with, or larger than, those recently reported for other stress management programs 26, 27, 28 . ATEs in PRECISE were moderate-to-large compared to the passive control arm (ds: -0.78 to -0.42) and small-to-moderate compared to the active control arm (ds: -0.39 to -0.19). The relatively smaller difference in PSS-score change for PRECISE vs. the active (as compared to the passive) control arm is not unexpected given the many similarities between PRECISE and our active control arm, Live Well. In particular, participants in Live Well also watched prerecorded coaching videos, completed digital modules on the content, and attended online coaching sessions, with the only meaningful difference between PRECISE and Live Well being the specific educational content taught. That we found a moderately sized ATE of PRECISE at the delayed follow-up timepoint, despite these many similarities, highlights the strengths of PRECISE and benefits of precision stress management.

When we examined each PRECISE intervention arm separately, we found that the effect sizes were highly consistent with those detected for PRECISE as a whole, but that the ATEs were not consistently significant. Given the similarity in effect sizes, this pattern likely reflects the relatively lower statistical power available for detecting significant changes in perceived stress in these sub-group analyses compared to the main analyses, which included participants in all five PRECISE arms. In general, we saw that perceived stress declined in a similar manner across each PRECISE arm except for Think Well, which exhibited greater baseline-to-postintervention than baseline-to-delayed follow-up improvements in perceived stress. Although we want to avoid over-interpretation, one possible explanation for this finding is that, as the only intervention that was primarily cognitive (vs. behavioral), it is possible that the skills taught in Think Well may be more susceptible to degradation over time. Because Think Well participants P R E P R I N T had the lowest average perceived stress score of all PRECISE arms immediately postintervention, another possibility is that this group could have had greater "regression to the mean" following this intervention compared to the others.

In addition to reducing perceived stress, PRECISE led to substantial improvements in domain-specific functioning, which we assessed by examining the proportion of participants who scored five or more (out of seven) on at least one Consequences of Stress Scale (CSS) domain item at each timepoint. This analysis revealed that while, at baseline, participants were greatly affected by the CSS domain associated with their PRECISE arm-as would be expected, given that is how they were assigned to treatment-this was no longer the case following the intervention. Instead, the percentage of participants reporting severe functional impairment in their target domain dropped precipitously for all of the PRECISE arms, down from 100% (baseline) to 33.3% (delayed follow-up) for Think Well and Be Well, for example, and from 100% to 21.1% for Sleep Well (see Figure 4). Although these data do not directly compare our method of precision intervention assignment with other possible methods (e.g., vs. random assignment to an intervention or sequential assignment to multiple relevant interventions), the results do indicate that participants consistently improved more in the CSS domain related to their PRECISE arm than in other domains, thus providing evidence of the potential benefits of precision stress management.

In addition to the CSS, we collected data on six exploratory outcomes-namely, mindfulness, social conflict, loneliness, insomnia, stress eating, and physical activity. The most notable findings here involved sleep. In particular, participants receiving PRECISE exhibited improved sleep (as indexed by the Insomnia Severity Index) relative to those in the passive control arm, and that these effects were especially pronounced for those in the Sleep Well arm, who exhibited greater improvements in sleep as compared to those in both the active and passive P R E P R I N T control arms. When considered together with the fact that PRECISE also reduced perceived stress, this finding is consistent with the potential for PRECISE to disrupt the known positive feedback loop between stress and sleep, which may thus have other downstream beneficial effects on health 20 . Sleep Well also improved mindfulness, reduced loneliness, and (to a lesser extent) normalized eating. In contrast, we did not see consistent improvements in the other exploratory outcomes assessed, suggesting that these scales might not have been well-matched to the different PRECISE arms. Consistent with this possibility, we used metabolic equivalents to assess domain-specific improvements in Move Well, but the intervention itself focused on increasing movement more generally rather than on increasing intensity and duration, which contribute to metabolic equivalent.

Given the above-described benefits of PRECISE for both perceived stress and domainspecific functioning, we believe the intervention could help bend the population-level chronic disease curve downward if widely adopted. This is because although psychological stress has farreaching effects on cardiovascular, physiologic, metabolic, immune, and neurobiological systems 1,2,15,17,25 -with resulting downstream implications for social and cognitive functioning, mental and physical health, biological aging, and early mortality 3, 4, 5, 6, 29, 30, 31 -stress is rarely a treatment target in primary or specialty care, leaving the vast majority of stress-related disease burden unmitigated 8, 9, 10, 13 . Moreover, when stress is addressed in clinical settings, patients are almost always prescribed a one-size-fits-all treatment such as CBT that is not specifically focused on stress 21 . Aside from being permissively misapplied, psychotherapies like CBT are generally expensive and have significant barriers to access, highlighting the critical need for a highly scalable, evidence-based precision intervention for reducing stress and enhancing psychosocial resilience at scale 32, 33 . The present results address this pressing need and support using PRECISE This study has several strengths. First and foremost, unlike many RCTs, the trial was conducted entirely remotely, meaning that the intervention itself-as well as all study procedures (e.g., participant screening, consenting, onboarding, and participant running)-were done at home, without participants needing to come into a major academic medical center or lab. This critical study design feature enabled participants from a diverse variety of backgrounds to participate, including individuals living in rural areas, people with disabilities and limited time availability (e.g., because of caregiving responsibilities and/or holding multiple jobs), and those with limited or no transportation. Consequently, we had very good treatment adherence, but also excellent participant diversity and, therefore, much higher external validity, generalizability, and translational potential. For example, in contrast to the racial/ethnic substantial homogeneity found in the vast majority of clinical trials, the present sample was 18 to 86 years old and 56.58% non-White, making it similar to the United States as a whole and much more diverse than other RCTs 19,34,35.

P R E P R I N T

Second, PRECISE, as an intervention, was developed to maximize accessibility, scalability, and translational impact. The intervention can be reused with relatively low additional cost, and combining the online coaching sessions with the self-paced learning materials, prerecorded coaching videos, and weekly comprehension checks gives individuals with many different strengths, learning styles, and preferences the ability to get the most possible out of the program. Moreover, we believe that combining use of the CSS as a screening tool, alongside the precision stress management modules, establishes a novel health care framework that could transform chronic disease prevention and treatment efforts by targeting stress as a central pathophysiological process in a non-invasive, evidence-based, and highly scalable fashion.

Several limitations of this study should also be noted. First, we collected data only from participants living in California, which could limit the generalizability of the results. Second, because we had seven trial arms and primarily sought to test the efficacy of the five PRECISE arms combined, our statistical power for testing arm-specific effects was lower, and those results should be interpreted with more caution. More robust testing of each individual PRECISE intervention is a natural next step now that the efficacy of PRECISE as a whole has been established. Third, because we sought to develop a new model for precision stress management, participants in PRECISE were assigned to their specific intervention arm using the CSS, and we did not examine how this precision assignment might compare to randomly assigning participants to an intervention, or to multiple interventions, or a lower-priority intervention.

Fourth, our ability to evaluate participants' treatment response trajectories, and work toward identifying minimum and ideal treatment durations, would have been enhanced by including additional assessment timepoints during treatment. Finally, we did not assess participants' treatment expectancies or how blind they were to their arm assignment, thus limiting our ability to evaluate these effects. Importantly, given the fundamental nature of PRECISE and, in P R E P R I N T particular, the fact that participants are expected to apply domain-specific skills in order to improve, true blinding was neither expected nor possible.

Looking forward, in addition to the behavioral data reported here, we collected numerous other biological, physiological, psychological, and social data types that are not yet assayed or cleaned for analysis 14 . These data include dried blood spot samples collected at each timepoint, which will be used for multi-omic analyses including proteomics, lipidomics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, microbiome data derived from stool samples, genomics data derived from saliva samples, and physiological data collected using research-grade wearable watches, which will enable us to explore physiological and behavioral metrics such as heart rate, heart rate variability, physical activity, and sleep/circadian rhythmicity over time and across the interventions. As a part of several sub-studies, we also collected Ecological Momentary Assessment-style weekly diary data, continuous glucose monitor data, exposome data on environmental pollutant exposure, and urinary measures of toxin exposure on some participants. Finally, we collected rich psychosocial data related to health, stress, identities, social connection, and resilience, which will provide deeper insight into the many intersecting factors that influence associations between stress, resilience, and health. Together, these data will enable us to study the impacts of stress-and the efficacy of PRECISE-in a highly diverse, well-characterized sample to elucidate the systems biology of stress and trauma exposure, and better understand biopsychosocial mechanisms underlying PRECISE-related improvements in clinical functioning.

Looking beyond this RCT, we see great value in comparing our CSS-guided method of prescribing treatment to being randomly assigned to an intervention arm to examine the potential benefits of using a precision stress management approach. We also envision using factorial designs to test ideal combinations and orderings of PRECISE program modules and including more frequent assessments to help characterize the timing of treatment effects in ways that would help facilitate protocol refinement.

P R E P R I N T

Finally, we look forward to evaluating whether PRECISE can reduce individuals' biopsychosocial reactivity to stressors, rather than just their basal stress levels, by timing the deployment of PRECISE to occur before a major stressful life event (e.g., move, job change, exam), and by using lab-based paradigms that involve assessing psychological and biological processes before and after a stressor (e.g., Social Evaluation Task, Trier Social Stress Test, Anger Incentive Delay Task) 36, 37, 38, 39.

In conclusion, medicine presently has an enormous scalability problem: far too much disease burden and not nearly enough health care providers. Healthcare is already transitioning away from care provided by medical doctors, and in the future, most care will occur online and at home using home-based diagnostic kits and scalable interventions. These interventions can be aimed at any number of treatment targets, but we feel the field will benefit most if we make stress a centerpiece, given that stress is implicated in 9 out of every 10 deaths in the U.S. today 6.

By providing patients and health care providers with a precision stress management program like PRECISE, we hope to not only reduce stress-related mortality risk but also enhance wellbeing and extend healthy years lived in the United States and beyond.

Methods

CalSTARS was submitted to ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06063174) on September 6th, 2023, and the complete protocol details are described elsewhere (see ref 14 ). In brief, CalSTARS is a parallel randomized controlled trial in which participants were assigned to a specific stress management program arm based on their responses to the Consequences of Stress Scale (CSS).

Participants were then randomized to receive their target precision stress management intervention arm (i.e., based on the CSS), or to the passive control arm or active control arm. Although the study started on June 30th, 2023, and enrollment began in July 2023, the first participants enrolled completed only the separate cross-sectional study that was also registered on ClinicalTrials.gov and not the longitudinal RCT covered in this article. With respect to the present data, the first participant began treatment on September 26, 2023, and the trial ended after the last participant completed the final follow-up assessment on October 30, 2024. As described in greater detail below, participants were recruited in two waves-from July to August 2023 and from January to February 2024-and the results of the two waves were essentially identical when restricting the sample to wave 1 or wave 2 only, even though this subgrouping reduced the sample by approximately 50% (see Results).

To recruit a highly diverse sample and reduce barriers to participation as much as possible, all participant screening, consenting, onboarding, data collection, and intervention activities were conducted entirely remotely. Participants completed online surveys and provided biological samples

Participant Recruitment and Eligibility

Participants for this trial were recruited to participate in a study on stress and resilience.

Specifically, the recruitment email stated: "We aim to better understand how childhood experiences and current stress affect physical and mental health, and what can be done to increase resilience."

Participants were also told that the intervention, if received, would be an online 12-week stress management program, but they were not told what conditions were available in the study.

As noted above, we recruited participants in two waves, and nothing differed procedurally between the two waves except for when they occurred. The first wave was recruited from July to August 2023, and the second wave was recruited from January to February 2024. Participant recruitment lists were generated by the UCLA Clinical and Translational Science Institute, drawing from patient rosters of those who had received care anywhere in the UCLA health system in the past 6 months. This timeframe was selected to increase the likelihood of potential participants (a) still being in California and (b) having relatively recent electronic health record data that could be used in future analyses. To be included on these rosters, potential participants had to be at least 18 years old, reside in California, and proficient in English. Exclusion criteria included any history of human immunodeficiency virus, a myocardial infarction or stroke within the last year, a cancer diagnosis or treatment within the last 2 years, and use of certain anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory medications within the past month 14 . To increase the representativeness of the sample, we did not exclude individuals with common mental or physical health conditions except for those noted above.

To recruit a socially, economically, and demographically diverse sample of participants from those who appeared to meet initial eligibility criteria, we took potential participants' area deprivation P R E P R I N T index (ADI) score into account. More specifically, after receiving recruitment lists of potential eligible participants, we sent a greater number of study invitations to potential participants who were more disadvantaged (ADIs 6-10) vs. more advantaged (ADIs 1-5) in a 3:2 ratio, based on the assumption that those from more disadvantaged backgrounds could face greater barriers to participating, leading to lower enrollment, as compared to those from more advantaged backgrounds.

Study invitations were sent using email and invited potential participants to take an online eligibility screener to determine if they were eligible to participate in a study on stress and resilience.

We next reviewed the eligibility screener responses to ensure that potential participants met the inclusion criteria detailed above and were willing to comply with study procedures (i.e., willing to provide biological samples and wear a smartwatch throughout the duration of the study, willing and able to attend virtual coaching sessions during the intervention period). Additionally, participants had to score greater than 15 on the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)-10, which assessed perceived stress levels over the last month. For reference, approximately 80.5% of individuals in the general population would meet this criterion at any given point in time. Participants also had to score greater than five (out of seven) on at least one domain item on the CSS, which assesses stress-related impairment over the last two weeks. These criteria ensured that participants had at least moderate stress levels and some stress-related dysfunction in a specific domain targeted by PRECISE so that participants would have the potential to benefit from the intervention. There were no changes to any of these criteria following study commencement.

If eligible for PRECISE based on the above-described criteria, participants were next assigned to one of five PRECISE arms based on their responses to the CSS scale. When participants were eligible for more than one treatment arm, we took their availability for the coaching sessions into account, rating of the most-affected domain, and need for study balance, in that order. After PRECISE arm assignment, participants were then randomized in a 1:1:3 ratio to one of the three P R E P R I N T arms (i.e., passive control, active control, or PRECISE) by a study coordinator, who used an online random number generator to generate the random allocation sequence. This ratio was used to ensure adequate power for the main analyses comparing the five PRECISE arms combined with the two control arms (primary aim) while also permitting us to conduct subgroup analyses within, and comparing, the individual PRECISE arms to each other (secondary aim). After random assignment, a second independent study coordinator who was blind to the allocation sequence sent invitations to assigned participants inviting them to enroll in the study. Only the first study coordinator on our study team had access to the random allocation sequence to prevent this information from affecting any aspect of the trial or analysis. Participants were kept blind to the overall trial design to the extent possible. In particular, they were told their group name (e.g., Think Well, Be Well), but they did not know what the comparator arms were, or how many arms existed, or that the trial included a passive or active control group. The five individual PRECISE arms and the active control arm all followed the same timing and structure.

PRECISE and Control Arms

The intervention arm, PRECISE, consisted of five arms, each focusing on a different key stress-related functional domain: thinking style (Think Well), social relationships (Be Well), sleep the same samples, and wore a smartwatch. Although participants were told the name of their own arm after assignment (e.g., Sleep Well, Be Well), which did give some indication as to the type of intervention content a specific participant was receiving, participants were not made aware that there were five intervention arms, a passive control arm, and an active control arm.

PRECISE Arms

Think Well (Thinking Style). The Think Well intervention program was based on the Cognitive Behavior Therapy treatment model 40 and also incorporated mindfulness practices. The educational content focused on teaching participants how to identify negative thinking patterns and how to use cognitive restructuring strategies to reframe negative thoughts.

Be Well (Social Relationships).

Participants in the Be Well program were taught how to navigate interpersonal conflict using problem solving skills based on the Interpersonal Psychotherapy Therapy treatment model 41 . The program also included educational content on the importance of social relationships and belonging to their overall health and well-being.

Sleep Well (Sleep).

Based on the Transdiagnostic Intervention for Sleep and Circadian Dysfunction (TranS-C) treatment model 42, 43 , the Sleep Well program was designed to improve people's sleep hygiene. Participants in this arm tracked their sleep using sleep diaries and learned about the importance of getting good sleep and maintaining healthy sleep patterns, as well as practical approaches to improving their own sleep.

Eat Well (Eating).

The Eat Well program was based on the Mindful Eating treatment model 44 . The educational content focused on forming mindful eating habits and breaking emotional eating cycles, as opposed to counting calories or focusing on intake of specific macronutrients.

Move Well (Physical Activity).

In Move Well, participants were taught how to incorporate more movement into their everyday lives with mindful movement approaches. The educational P R E P R I N T content focused on the importance of adequate movement and exercise for health and wellbeing based on the self-efficacy arm of the Physical Activity Maintenance Model 45 . Participants were shown how to move a great number of body parts throughout their bodies and given practical approaches to managing everyday tasks for those with limited mobility.

Live Well (Active Control Arm)

The active control arm was designed to educate participants about environmental pollution and toxin exposures, the typical sources (e.g., cooking and cleaning supplies) and health impacts of these exposures, and practical ways to reduce their exposures. Although the effects of this program on stress levels have not yet been examined, the program was very active in nature but was neither designed nor expected to reduce participants' stress levels. During the intervention period, Live Well participants completed a lifestyle audit in which they documented the foods they consumed and the household and personal care products they used for one day, and they were also provided feedback about their personal exposures and strategies to reduce these exposures. The intervention and active control arms were administered as planned.

As shown in Table S5, treatment adherence was good for PRECISE and the active control arm, especially considering the intervention was administered entirely online. Moreover, there were no differences in completion rates between the intervention (i.e., PRECISE) and active control arm.

Outcome Measures

The primary main trial outcome was change in perceived stress levels over time, as assessed by the PSS-10. The main secondary outcomes were scores on each of the five CSS domain subscales, as well as changes in six exploratory outcomes-namely, mindfulness, assessed using the Five-Factor Mindfulness Scale short form (thinking style) 46 ; social conflict, assessed using the Conflict Scale (social relationships) 47 ; loneliness, assessed using the UCLA Loneliness Scale short form (social relationships) 48 ; insomnia, assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index (sleep) 49 ; stress P R E P R I N T eating, assessed using the Salzburg Stress Eating Scale (eating) 50 ; and physical activity, assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form (physical activity) 51 . There were no changes to the trial outcomes after study commencement.

Power Analysis

The sample size calculation assumed a PSS-10 standard deviation of 7.6 based on prior research 52 . We conducted two power calculations. The first calculation determined that using a twosided two-sample t-test, 85 participants per control arm could detect a difference in the PSS-10 of 3.25 or greater with 80% power. The second calculation using an ANOVA model demonstrated that a difference of 2.65 or greater in the PSS-10 could be detected between the combined PRECISE arms (n = 255) and a chosen control arm (n = 85) with 80% power. We also assumed 15% loss to follow-up and thus aimed to recruit 500 participants to obtain a final sample size of 425 total, or 255 participants in PRECISE (5 arms × 51/arm) and 85 in each of the two control arms.

Data Analysis Plan

Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.1, June 2025) from June to July 2025, and all statistical code is freely available on the Open Science Framework (doi: 10.17605/OSF.IO/3HA9N).

To estimate ATEs of the PRECISE intervention on PSS scores for the intention-to-treat population, we used extended ANCOVAs (i.e., cLDA) per our published protocol 14 . Specifically, we compared changes in PSS scores from baseline to the follow-up timepoints between PRECISE and each control arm, adjusting for baseline PSS levels. All models also controlled for sums of ACEs, state-level ADI, age, and sex. For these analyses, dummy codes were created for the first and second follow-up timepoints, such that change to both follow-ups could be assessed in the same model. The main effects of these session dummy variables represented PSS score change in the arm coded 0 (here, either the passive or active control arm), and interactions between session dummy variables and the treatment variable provided estimates of the difference in change observed for PRECISE arms P R E P R I N T relative to the control arms (i.e., the ATE). Importantly, the main effect of the treatment variable was excluded to adjust for baseline differences in PSS scores across arms, as is required for ANCOVA models in the multilevel framework. Model diagnostics on residuals were conducted using the DHARMa R package (version 0.4.7) 53, 54 . This analysis was repeated while controlling for multiple comparisons using Dunnett's method to adjust p-values when comparing EMM effects (emmeans, version 1.11.2) 54 . This package was also used to calculate standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d).

Next, to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings, we conducted three sensitivity analyses: (a) a complete case analysis of data only from participants with PSS scores observed at all three timepoints, (b) models using propensity score weighting to weight the collected sample in a manner that better approximates the characteristics of the entire eligible population, and (c) comparing participants assigned to a specific PRECISE intervention to only those enrolled in a control who were eligible for that specific arm (vs. the control arm as a whole) (for more information, see the Supplementary Materials). Additionally, we conducted follow-up analyses to test for possible "dose-dependency" effects of PRECISE by examining whether the magnitude of PSS score change in each experimental arm was related to the percent of (a) coaching sessions attended, (b) modules completed, (c) review videos completed, and (d) weekly diaries completed.

Finally, we examined treatment effects for the secondary, domain-specific outcomes using a similar strategy as the main analyses for PSS. Given their exploratory and ancillary role as outcomes, we did not correct for multiple comparisons for these analyses.

Considering patterns of missing data, we fit all models using linear mixed effect models (glmmTMB, version 1.1.11) rather than generalized estimating equations, as the latter require the restrictive assumption of data missing completely at random (MCAR) 14, 55, 56 . Mixed effects models estimated using maximum likelihood, on the other hand, only require that missing outcome data do not depend on values of the outcome after adjusting for predictors to avoid bias [i.e., missing at random (MAR)] 57 .

P R E P R I N T

Missingness was < 1% for all covariates, 12.94% for PSS scores post-intervention and 17.41% for the delayed follow-up. Supporting the assumption that outcome data were indeed MAR, logistic regression models predicting likelihood of missingness revealed that younger individuals were moderately more likely to have missing PSS data at both follow-ups (ps < 0.04), and baseline PSS levels were not related to missingness at either timepoint (ps > 0.79). Moreover, rates of missingness did not differ between PRECISE and either the passive (ps > 0.06) or active control arm (ps > 0.24).
