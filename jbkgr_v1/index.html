Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Full text

Abstract

Over the last half century, we have made little progress in the treatment of schizophrenia. A key contributor to this is an insufficient understanding of what is causing the disorder. There are currently two 5 broad families of hypotheses: those focused on a dopaminergic dysfunction, and those focused on a glutamatergic dysfunction. Based on recent findings that antipsychotic drugs exhibit cell-type specific and long-term effects on cortical circuit function, we 10 integrate the two hypotheses into what we call the control limit hypothesis of schizophrenia. It builds on the assumption that schizophrenia is the result of a developmental miswiring of cortical circuits that is based on glutamatergic dysfunction and causes 15 aberrant cortical activity dynamics. These cortical alterations likely underlie cognitive dysfunction, one of the core treatment-resistant symptoms of schizophrenia. The control limit hypothesis further postulates that the brain responds to and can 20 partially mitigate these aberrant cortical dynamics by increasing dopamine levels in the striatum. When this regulatory system is driven to a control limit and dopamine levels exceed a given threshold, psychosis emerges. Beyond explaining much of the existing 25 data, the hypothesis makes several predictions about cortical function testable in mice and humans.

INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia is a frequently disabling mental disorder that affects about 1 in 100 people. Despite over a century of research, the pathogenesis of the disorder is insufficiently understood. What represents a particular challenge to etiopathogenetic theories of schizophrenia is the multitude of etiological factors along with a high variability in clinical manifestations of the disorder. Evidence suggests a neurodevelopmental origin with (poly-)genetic and environmental influences acting early in brain development (Fatemi and Folsom, 2009; Lewis and Levitt, 2002). However, to date there is no satisfactory explanation for why schizophrenia typically manifests with psychosis only in adolescence or early adulthood, following a prodromal phase marked by cognitive dysfunction and affective symptoms. The course of the disorder is highly variable, but often follows a relapsing-remitting pattern, with life stress being an important trigger of psychotic episodes (Martland et al., 2020). It remains unclear how the multitude of known etiological factors results in a pathophysiology that gives rise to the variety of clinical manifestations. There are currently two prominent classes 50 of hypotheses for the pathophysiology of schizophrenia: the dopamine hypothesis and the glutamate hypothesis. While both hypotheses are supported by ample empirical evidence, their integration into a unified account of schizophrenia remains challenging. Here, we propose the 55 control limit hypothesis as a new way to integrate existing pathophysiological hypotheses in a coherent model that accounts for some of the hitherto unexplained manifestations of schizophrenia and offers novel, testable predictions.

60

Dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia. The dopamine hypothesis postulates that schizophrenia is caused by a dysregulation of the dopaminergic system. The central pillar of the hypothesis was the demonstration that dopamine receptor 2 (D2) affinity directly correlated with 65 clinical effectiveness of the antipsychotic drugs available at the time (Creese et al., 1976; Seeman et al., 1976). It has evolved substantially over the years and now postulates that multiple problems interact to drive a dopamine dysregulation that leads to psychosis (Heinz, 2002; Heinz 70 and Schlagenhauf, 2010; Howes and Kapur, 2009; Kapur, 2003).

Glutamate (or synaptic) hypothesis of schizophrenia. The second hypothesis focuses on glutamatergic circuit dysfunctions. The glutamate hypothesis was initially based 75 on findings that dissociative drugs like phencyclidine (PCP) and ketamine can induce psychosis-like states in humans (Luby et al., 1962). Because these drugs act on N-methyl-Daspartate (NMDA) receptors (Javitt and Zukin, 1991), the speculation arose that a glutamatergic dysregulation could 80 drive psychosis, possibly by triggering increases in dopamine levels. The glutamate hypothesis thus postulates that dysregulated cortical circuits are a core circuit problem in schizophrenia. This has since been supported by genetics pointing to risk genes in cortical and hippocampal neurons 85 (Singh et al., 2022; Trubetskoy et al., 2022). In its most recent forms, the glutamate hypothesis proposes that a hippocampal and prefrontal cortex dysregulation affecting glutamatergic synapses leads to striatal dopamine increases and psychosis (Heinz et al., 2019; Howes and the late stage of the illness (Insel, 2010; Murray and Lewis, 1987; Weinberger, 1986). At the clinical level, this notion is supported by the observation that cognitive difficulties, which are distinct from positive and negative symptoms (McCutcheon et al., 2023), are often apparent long before the onset of other symptoms (Brewer et al., 2005; Keefe et al., 2006; Reichenberg et al., 2006). Cognitive symptoms are also present in the absence of antipsychotic medication (Fatouros-Bergman et al., 2014). Subjective cognitive and perceptual symptoms, so-called basic symptoms, are characteristic of the prodromal phase of schizophrenia (Huber and Gross, 1989; Schultze-Lutter, 2009). Thus, cognitive difficulties are likely an integral aspect of the disorder. This idea is also supported by findings of correlated anatomical changes in at-risk individuals with 110 cognitive disturbances and patients with schizophrenia (Koutsouleris et al., 2025). Based on such considerations, it has long been speculated that schizophrenia is caused by a developmental miswiring of cortical circuits that initially manifests with cognitive symptoms and only later results in 115 psychosis (Schmitt et al., 2023).

Antipsychotic drugs.

Most antipsychotic drugs are thought to exert their effects primarily through antagonism at D₂ receptors (Creese et al., 1976; Seeman et al., 1976), particularly within mesolimbic pathways (Schmack et al., 120 2021), and, to some extent, through antagonism at 5-HT₂A serotonin receptors, which are prominently expressed on layer 5 (L5) cortical pyramidal neurons (Weber and Andrade, 2010). However, it is still unclear how this D2 and 5-HT2A antagonism changes cortical circuit function in a 125 way that reduces psychosis. Research in humans using noninvasive neuroimaging methods such as fMRI or EEG offers only limited access to cortical circuit mechanisms and cannot resolve cell-type specific effects of antipsychotics. Accordingly, human neuroimaging work has not yielded a 130 clear picture with only small and highly variable effects (Debruille et al., 2013; Dominicus et al., 2023; Yoshimura et al., 2007). However, using cell-type specific activity measurements in mouse cortex, a subset of clinically effective antipsychotic drugs was recently found to drive a selective decorrelation of the activity in L5 cortical neurons (Heindorf and Keller, 2024). This effect is independent of the receptor binding profile of the drug and largely absent in neurons of superficial layers of cortex. The decorrelation effect could be caused by drug-driven plasticity that increases long-range functional inhibition in cortex (Lupori et al., 2026). Based on these findings, we have speculated that an increase in the long-range correlations between L5 neurons could result in false inferences that manifest as psychosis, and that the L5 decorrelation may be the treatment-relevant effect of antipsychotic drugs (Keller and Sterzer, 2024). How an increase in L5 correlations may come about in the disorder, or how antipsychotic drugs lead to a decorrelation of deep cortical layers is still unclear.

HYPOTHESIS 150

Based on the considerations reviewed above, we here propose the control limit hypothesis, a novel hypothesis for the pathophysiology of schizophrenia. It differs from the most recent versions of the glutamate hypothesis (Howes and Onwordi, 2023; Lisman et al., 2008) primarily in 155 proposing a mechanistic link for how cortical circuit miswiring results in increased dopamine levels: The dopaminergic system acts as a feedback control system to maintain cortical activity patterns within an optimal physiological range. It builds on the idea that dopamine 160 levels need to be maintained within dynamically changing limits (Ashcroft et al., 1981).

Control limit hypothesis of schizophrenia.

The first step in the cascade that eventually results in what we describe as schizophrenia is a developmental miswiring of cortical 165 circuits. The primary disorder-relevant consequence of this neurodevelopmental miswiring is a specific functional shift within cortical L5 excitatory neurons. Beyond a generalized excitation-inhibition imbalance typically attributed to synaptic loss, we speculate that a reduction in long-range 170 inhibitory influence disrupts communication within L5 networks, impairing their ability to transition fluidly between distinct network states. Cognitive functioning relies on an optimal speed of these cortical dynamics, that is, the rate at which L5 ensembles transition between 175 stable attractor states corresponding to distinct representations. If these dynamics become too slow, the network struggles to make appropriate associations, retrieve memories efficiently, or disengage from persistent representations, manifesting clinically as cognitive 180 dysfunction. Conversely, a speed that is too high might result in thought racing, spurious associations, and false inferences, giving rise to psychotic experiences. The speed of these cortical dynamics is likely tightly controlled through neuromodulatory feedback loops. We postulate 185 that in the cortex, dopamine is one way by which the brain can control the speed of cortical dynamics, and that psychosis is the consequence of this feedback control reaching an upper limit.

According to the control limit hypothesis, the brain actively 190 counteracts abnormally slow cortical dynamics by increasing dopamine levels. Dopamine modulates cortical activity through a synergy of direct dopaminergic signaling and indirect regulation via the cortico-striatal-thalamic loop (Figure 1). Elevated dopamine levels accelerate state 195 transitions to keep cortical dynamics within a physiological range, thus potentially reflecting an allostatic adaptation. If this control system is then additionally taxed by life stressors that further increase dopamine levels, the dopaminergic control of cortical activity can reach a control 200 limit.

High dopamine levels thus act analogously to a fever: an endogenous allostatic response to an underlying circuit problem (slowed attractor dynamics), rather than merely a pathological excess of dopamine. When driven beyond a critical threshold, this response triggers severe side effects-in this case, an acceleration of cortical dynamics that underpins hallucinations and delusions. Psychosis, according to this view, is the behavioral-phenomenological consequence of a regulatory feedback controller pushed past its operational limit.

Evidence the control limit hypothesis is based on.

Developmental cortical miswiring and cognitive dysfunction. Schizophrenia likely involves a developmental miswiring of cortical circuits (Jaaro-Peled et al., 2009; Lewis and Levitt, 2002). The precise nature of this miswiring and its functional consequences at the neuronal level are still unclear. Behaviorally, however, miswiring could manifest as cognitive dysfunction. The time course of cognitive and basic symptoms mirrors that of anatomical indicators of miswiring (Cannon, 2015). The consequence of this could be altered inhibition in cortex (de Jonge et al., 2017). It has been speculated that a reduction in cortical inhibition might account for disease-relevant mouse phenotypes (Yizhar et al., 2011), which could be caused by a dysfunction in parvalbumin-positive interneurons (Lewis et al., 2012). More recent genetic evidence also points to a central role of a subtype of L5 somatostatin-expressing interneurons (Duncan et al., 2025). These interneuron dysfunctions could be the basis for the hypothesized loss of 230 long-range inhibition in L5 networks.

Bidirectional interactions between cortical dynamics and striatal dopamine.

An increase in striatal dopamine levels is one of the most robust findings in schizophrenia (McCutcheon et al., 2018). Most likely, this increase in striatal dopamine levels is what drives psychosis (Schmack et al., 2021). Importantly, dopamine levels are sensitive to changes in cortical activity patterns. Blocking NMDA receptors in prefrontal cortex with ketamine increases dopamine release in rats (Del Arco et al., 2008; Lorrain et 240 al., 2003; Usun et al., 2013), and modulation of frontal cortex activity influences mesolimbic dopamine signaling in a frequency-dependent manner (Olaitan et al., 2025). In humans, striatal dopamine synthesis capacity is inversely related to prefrontal glutamate concentrations (Gleich et al., 2015). Ketamine administration can increase the dopamine synthesis capacity via a D2-independent pathway (Kokkinou et al., 2021), consistent with a causal influence of cortical activity patterns on striatal dopamine levels.

Conversely, changes in dopamine levels can alter largescale cortical activity patterns, as demonstrated by direct effects on long-range coupling of cortical activity in both mice (Costa et al., 2006) and humans (Preuss et al., 2026). Dopamine level increases enhance functional connectivity between striatum and cortex in a region-specific manner (Kelly et al., 2009; Nagano-Saito et al., 2008). Dopamineenhancing manipulations often strengthen task-related deactivation of default-mode regions (Minzenberg et al., 2011; Tomasi et al., 2011), and striatal dopamine markers covary with the degree of default-mode reconfiguration during attention and working memory (Braskie et al., 2011; Nour et al., 2019; Tomasi et al., 2009). Moreover, higher striatal dopamine levels are associated with an increased involvement of prefrontal cortex and thus enhanced 265 cortical control of goal-directed behavior in healthy individuals (Deserno et al., 2015). In this way, similar to how we think about the role of dopamine in the control of timing and vigor of movements (Arber and Costa, 2022), dopamine could alter the ease with which cortex exits one 270 state and enters another. Together, this evidence is compatible with the notion that the brain actively regulates dopamine activity to correct for undesirable changes in cortical dynamics.

L5 correlation as link between dopamine and psychosis.

275 Crucial support for the idea that increased dopamine levels drive changes in cortical dynamics comes from the finding that antidopaminergic antipsychotic drugs act to decorrelate activity in L5 across cortex in the mouse (Heindorf and Keller, 2024) likely by increasing the 280 reliability of long-range inhibition in cortex (Lupori et al., 2026). Given that L5 neurons are the primary mediators of long-range cortico-cortical communication, changes to the L5 correlation structure likely directly translate to changes in functional connectivity. Conversely, in treatment-naïve, 285 first-episode schizophrenia patients there is a general increase in cortical functional connectivity (Anticevic et al., 2015; Du et al., 2021; Li et al., 2017). Based on this and other considerations, we have speculated that increased correlations in the L5 network are the neuronal basis of 290 psychosis (Keller and Sterzer, 2024). Changes in L5 correlation such as those observed under antipsychotics (Heindorf and Keller, 2024), we speculate, reflect changes in the speed of cortical dynamics, which need to be maintained in some optimal window. Importantly, 295 increased dopamine levels in the striatum are likely a driver of psychosis more generally and not specifically in schizophrenia. Dopamine synthesis capacity is also elevated in bipolar disorder with psychosis (Jauhar et al., 2017) and other psychotic disorders (Cheng et al., 2020), as 300 well as in individuals with a genetic disorder associated with psychosis (Rogdaki et al., 2023).

Clinical observations.

A central tenet of our hypothesis is that psychosis is the consequence of dopamine regulation exceeding a control limit, when dopamine levels increase 305 from an already elevated level in response to acute challenges. This notion is supported by broad evidence for psychosocial stress as a factor that can precipitate psychosis in vulnerable individuals (Georgiades et al., 2023; Henssler et al., 2020) and, more specifically, by findings showing that acute life stress increases dopamine levels (Egerton et al., 2017; Vaessen et al., 2015). Finally, support for the control limit hypothesis comes from the clinical observation that cognitive function does not improve and possibly even worsens with antipsychotic treatment, as function after administration of antipsychotics in healthy individuals (Osugo et al., 2025). Moreover, evidence has accumulated that antipsychotic drugs hasten cognitive dysfunction in individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia (Allott et al., 2024 (Allott et al., , 2023;; Husa et al., 2014), especially at high doses (Sakurai et al., 2013). It is thus unlikely that increased dopamine levels per se are the cause of cognitive dysfunction. Rather, the brain may try to compensate for a circuit miswiring that underlies cognitive dysfunction by increasing dopamine levels. According to the control limit hypothesis, antipsychotics downscale dopaminergic tone to decelerate cortical attractor dynamics. On standard measurement timescales, this deceleration manifests empirically as a reduction in L5 network correlation. While this slowing successfully reduces psychotic symptoms, it simultaneously exacerbates the underlying cognitive dysfunction.

Further findings that may be explained by the control limit hypothesis.

The control limit as a convergence point for multiple risk factors. A dopaminergic control system that the brain uses to correct cortical activity patterns could also explain why the disorder is so polygenic with a very low penetrance of individual genetic mutations and exhibits a strong influence 340 of life experience. Only when multiple problems coincide or amplify is the control system driven to its control limit, and the defining symptoms of the disorder manifest. Many people have some of the genetic predispositions and experience life events that are risk factors for schizophrenia. Yet, schizophrenia develops only in those in whom these issues coincide and the control system reaches its limit.

Explaining the clinical characteristics of antipsychotic action. The control limit hypothesis may also explain key clinical observations regarding the effects of antipsychotic drugs. If the brain uses dopamine levels to regulate the speed of cortical dynamics, this control is likely mediated, at least in part, by dopamine-dependent plasticity. Dopamine is a well-established modulator of plasticity in 355 both cortical circuits (Huang et al., 2004; Kolomiets et al., 2009; Speranza et al., 2021) and cortico-striatal loops (Reynolds and Wickens, 2002). Prolonged elevations in dopamine drive compensatory plasticity within these networks, structurally biasing them to faster dynamics.

Consequently, when antipsychotic drugs abruptly reduce the direct effects of dopamine, cortical function does not immediately normalize. Because the cortical circuit has been sensitized by this extended, dopamine-driven plasticity, it must gradually undergo reverse remodeling, essentially unlearning these adaptations, before returning to baseline dynamics and providing relief from psychosis. This functional remodeling would explain the relatively slow onset of action of antipsychotic drugs. Furthermore, it accounts for why antipsychotic treatments become less effective the longer patients remain untreated (Marshall et al., 2005). If chronic increases in dopamine levels trigger entrenched plasticity in how cortical circuits respond to changes in dopamine levels, this could explain why treatment is more effective when initiated early in the 375 course of the disorder.

Beyond dopamine.

Importantly, antipsychotic efficacy is not restricted to antidopaminergic mechanisms of action. A subset of more recently developed antipsychotic drugs and candidate drugs in development do not target D2 receptors 380 or even the dopaminergic system in general (e.g., evenamide, a selective voltage-gated sodium channel blocker, or xanomeline, a muscarinic acetylcholine receptor agonist). If dopamine functions as a regulator of cortical activity, and altered cortical activity is the neuronal 385 substrate of psychosis, there are likely other ways to alter cortical activity dynamics that do not involve dopamine. Assuming the control limit hypothesis is correct, dopamine might just be an easy entry point, as it is the brain's endogenous way to regulate cortical activity.

390

Conversely, some patients are unresponsive to D2-blocking antipsychotic drugs. The brain likely has other ways beyond dopamine to regulate cortical activity patterns. For instance, availability of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors in vivo is reduced in patients with schizophrenia (Raedler et 395 al., 2003). In mice, both acetylcholine (Yogesh and Keller, 2023) and noradrenaline (Dahl et al., 2022) can influence the cortical correlation structure. In dopamine treatment resistant patients, it may be a parallel feedback control system that has reached its control limit, and then 400 treatments capable of modulating cortical circuitry directly, e.g., via 5-HT2A serotonin receptors or by the effects of electrical stimulation, may be more effective.

Integrating cortical hypodopaminergia.

While schizophrenia is associated with a hyperdopaminergic state 405 in the striatum, concurrent hypodopaminergic states have been reported in the cortex (Okubo et al., 1997). A potential explanation for this discrepancy is a compensatory mechanism: If the hypodopaminergic state in cortex is of genetic origin, a systemic upregulation of 410 dopamine to correct this localized shortage could inadvertently drive striatal hyperdopaminergia. Dopamine influences cortical dynamics either directly via local cortical release, or indirectly by modulating cortico-striatal loops via striatal release. However, given that most antipsychotic 415 drugs act via D2 receptors, which are predominantly expressed in the striatum, striatal dopamine release and the resulting disruption of cortico-striatal loops are likely of primary relevance for psychosis.

Explaining the failure of mGlu2/3 receptor agonists. Finally, 420 the control limit hypothesis may explain why the metabotropic glutamate 2/3 (mGlu2/3) receptor has proven to be a challenging antipsychotic target. While accumulating evidence for the glutamate hypothesis of schizophrenia initially generated significant enthusiasm for 425 mGlu2/3 agonists (Dogra and Conn, 2022), efforts to develop glutamate receptor-based antipsychotics have thus far failed (DelaCuesta-Barrutia et al., 2024). If antipsychotic efficacy depends specifically on the regulation of cortical L5 activity (Heindorf and Keller, 2024), the failure of ubiquitously targeting glutamate receptors may not be surprising. Global modulation indiscriminately affects all excitatory neurons rather than selectively engaging the L5 network.

TESTABLE PREDICTIONS

The key novel assumption in the hypothesis we present here is that dopamine levels are used as a feedback control system to maintain cortical activity patterns within an optimal physiological range. This hypothesis has a few directly testable predictions mainly related to the interplay between the dopaminergic system and cortical function. Some of these can be tested using cell-type specific interventions in the mouse, others can be tested directly in humans.

Predictions testable in mice:

• Increased levels of dopamine in the brain increase the speed of cortical dynamics. We predict that experimentally increasing levels of dopamine should result in increased speed of cortical dynamics that can be measured as an increase in correlations in L5 of cortex.

• Long-term increases of dopamine levels drive aberrant plasticity of cortical circuits. Long-term increases in dopamine levels should drive plasticity that biases cortical circuits towards faster dynamics, 455 and as a consequence increases in measured levels of cortico-cortical correlations.

• Genetic susceptibility for schizophrenia should manifest as alterations in cortical activity patterns.

We should find that a common feature of different genetic mouse models of schizophrenia is that they exhibit alterations to cortical activity patterns that bias them to increased correlations.

• Aberrant cortical activity can trigger increased levels of dopamine activity. Dopamine release should be driven by a specific set of cortical activity patterns. We predict that an artificial reduction in the dorsal cortexwide speed of L5 activity or L5 correlations results in increased dopamine release. This can be tested experimentally by optogenetically inducing aberrant L5 activity patterns and measuring the resulting responses of subcortical dopaminergic neurons.

Predictions testable in humans:

• Decorrelation of striatal activity by antipsychotic drugs. As L5 cortical neurons are one of the dominant direct inputs to the striatum, and their projection preserves topography (Kemp and Powell, 1970; Lehéricy et al., 2004; Selemon and Goldman-Rakic, 1985), the decorrelation of L5 neurons observed after antipsychotic administration in mice should be 480 reflected in decorrelation of striatal activity in humans. This should be detectable in fMRI recordings.

• Dopaminergic perturbations should change cortical activity patterns measured non-invasively with neuroimaging methods. Recently, evidence of a 485 dopaminergic influence on EEG patterns has indeed emerged (Preuss et al., 2026). Thus, we expect to find dopamine-driven changes in functional connectivity between cortical areas.

• Non-dopaminergic antipsychotics will lead to similar 490 changes in cortical activity patterns as D2 antagonists.

The treatment-relevant changes to cortical activity patterns should not depend on the molecular mechanism of action of the treatment. Similar to the data in the mouse, we should find signatures of 495 antipsychotic efficacy in drug-driven changes to cortical activity patterns common to all treatments that are effective in improving psychosis.

• The influence of dopamine release on cortical activity patterns should be different in high-risk individuals.

500

In individuals at high clinical risk of psychosis, baseline dopamine levels are likely already increased. However, it is not clear whether the cortical response to dopamine de-or hypersensitizes with prolonged increases in dopamine. We would expect to find 505 characteristic differences in the cortical dopamine response of high-risk individuals.

FAQ

If the dopaminergic system has feedback control over cortical dynamics, why does it fail to keep them within an 510 optimal range? There are at least two possible reasons for this. First, this could be the result of a mismatch between the anatomical resolution of the feedback control system and the anatomical extent of the underlying circuit problem. For example, there could be a genetic problem in 515 part of the prefrontal circuit that results in activity dynamics that are too slow specifically in that part of prefrontal cortex. Or the circuit dysfunction could preferentially affect lateral communication within only one specific cell type. The dopaminergic feedback control does 520 not have the resolution to only increase the speed of activity dynamics in that part of prefrontal cortex or just that cell type, but can only influence all of prefrontal cortex (or all of cortex) at once. Alternatively, it is possible that there is an imbalance of direct and indirect cortico-striatal 525 loops and dopamine acts on both D1 and D2 receptors. As a result of such a resolution mismatch between the underlying circuit problem and the dopaminergic feedback controller, the system cannot fully compensate for the problem across the entire circuit at the same time. Second, 530 it is possible that through an extended increase of dopamine levels, the cortical circuit or cortico-striatalthalamic loops are reshaped by plasticity to naturally tend to faster dynamics, making the network hypersensitive to even physiological changes in dopamine input.

535

Conceptually, this can be thought of as a dynamic decrease of the upper limit of the permissible dopamine range (Ashcroft et al., 1981).

How could the dopamine system sense cortical activity patterns?

We propose that the midbrain dopaminergic system acts as a sensor for aberrant cortical activity, specifically detecting changes in the speed of cortical dynamics, possibly by using measures of the correlation structure of deep-layer networks. This sensing could occur through a combination of direct prefrontal projections and 545 indirect pathways mediated by the striatum. Through these circuits, the midbrain dopaminergic system can monitor either a global increase in long-range L5 correlations or a specific decoupling in the synchrony between L5 intratelencephalic (IT) and extratelencephalic (ET) 550 populations. When these patterns deviate from the physiological optimum, signaling a breakdown in efficient cortical dynamics, the midbrain dopaminergic system increases dopamine release in an attempt to restore circuit stability.

555

What is the role of cortical plasticity? A secondary problem may be compensatory plasticity. The efficacy of dopaminergic control is likely self-limiting. We speculate that through a process of compensatory plasticity, the cortical circuit adapts to sustained dopaminergic signaling.

560

This would create a feedback loop that gradually erodes the intended regulatory effect, leading to a state of diminished responsiveness or tolerance. Thus, to reach the same level of feedback control, the regulatory system is gradually driven toward its control limit.

565

Why is treatment onset so slow? D2 receptor occupancy in the striatum reaches therapeutic levels within hours of initiating antipsychotic treatment, yet the reduction of positive symptoms typically develops over the course of days and weeks. Why does clinical improvement lag behind 570 receptor blockade? This clinical delay reflects the distinction between acute pharmacological blockade and circuit-level reverse remodeling. While D2 antagonists acutely diminish the subcortical driving force on cortical dynamics, the cortical network has undergone prolonged, 575 dopamine-dependent synaptic plasticity (sensitization of L5 pyramidal ensembles and cortico-striatal synapses) (Huang et al., 2004; Reynolds and Wickens, 2002). Consequently, the cortical circuit remains structurally biased toward hyper-correlated states. Relief from psychosis requires an extended period of activitydependent "unlearning" to re-establish stable attractor landscapes. This also explains why longer durations of untreated psychosis predict poorer long-term treatment response (Marshall et al., 2005):Prolonged duration allows maladaptive, dopamine-driven synaptic remodeling to become progressively more entrenched.

What about patients resistant to dopamine-targeting treatments?

If dopamine elevation is considered a primary driver of psychosis, why does a substantial proportion of 590 patients fail to respond to D2-receptor antagonists and frequently exhibit normal striatal dopamine synthesis capacity? Under the control limit hypothesis, the fundamental circuit correlate of psychosis is cortical attractor dynamics that are pathologically accelerated.

595

While we speculate that dopamine represents a primary endogenous controller of cortical attractor speed, it is unlikely to be the only one. Parallel neuromodulatory loops, notably cholinergic and serotonergic feedback systems, similarly regulate L5 network dynamics. In non-600 responders, psychosis may be driven by failure within one of these parallel control systems, which could explain why clozapine (a weak D2 antagonist with a profile characterized predominantly by 5-HT2A antagonism and partial agonism at muscarinic M4 receptors) remains uniquely effective in 605 treatment-resistant schizophrenia. In addition, direct alterations to the L5 network itself can also accelerate attractor dynamics. For instance, the psychosis observed in certain neurodegenerative disorders may be triggered directly by structural degeneration of L5 ensembles.

610

Furthermore, in longstanding or severe schizophrenia, chronic overactivation of the control system may induce profound structural remodeling within L5 microcircuits. Once attractor barriers are structurally degraded, dynamic instability becomes entrenched and refractory to upstream 615 neuromodulatory tone; at that stage, simply dampening dopamine signaling can no longer restore attractor stability.

Overcoming treatment resistance will ultimately require novel biomarkers to stratify patients by the specific circuit mechanism underlying their symptoms.

What is the relationship between the cortical correlation structure and the speed of cortical dynamics? We have used the phrase 'speed of cortical dynamics' as an intuitive shorthand for the underlying stability and depth of cortical attractor states. When attractor wells become too shallow, 625 the energy barriers between distinct network representations are reduced. As a result, the transition between attractors becomes abnormally rapid, or the network may even become bistable, allowing for the aberrant parallel activation of unrelated attractor states 630 (Loh et al., 2007; Musa et al., 2022). These accelerated dynamics can drive an apparent increase in network correlation purely due to the temporal limits of our recording techniques. Modalities such as calcium imaging and fMRI are restricted to timescales ranging from tens of 635 milliseconds to seconds, allowing us to measure broad correlation structures but not the true, underlying speed of fast attractor state transitions. If dynamics accelerate, causing the cortex to rapidly cycle through, or simultaneously activate, distinct states within a single 640 measurement frame, sequentially activated neurons will appear to be active simultaneously. Consequently, these rapid temporal transitions blur together over our slow measurement windows, manifesting as increased spatial correlation. Measured hyper-correlation thus serves as a 645 time-averaged proxy for fast, shallow cortical dynamics.

Intriguingly, it is possible that the sensing of the speed of cortical dynamics by the dopaminergic system could also rely on slower biological integration mechanisms that are essentially sensing this resulting correlation structure.

What is the relationship between the proposal here and the role of dopamine in the control of movement?

Dopamine is thought to control the timing and vigor of cortically controlled movements through its influence on the striatum (Arber and Costa, 2022). The direct pathway provides excitatory drive to the intended movement, while the indirect pathway concurrently suppresses alternative movements; together, this combination produces a functional center-excitation, surround-inhibition dynamic (Bronfeld and Bar-Gad, 2011). Dopamine excites the direct 660 pathway via D1 receptors and inhibits the indirect pathway via D2 receptors (Gerfen, 2022; Lanciego et al., 2012). This concurrent modulation provides more drive to the target movement while reducing overall inhibition, facilitating transitions to subsequent movement states. From a 665 dynamical systems perspective, activating D2 receptors can be thought of as decreasing the depth of attractor wells in the neuronal movement state space, allowing for more rapid transitions between actions (Puig et al., 2014). When dopamine transmission is overactive, this state space becomes overly permissive, resulting in uncoordinated, involuntary movements (such as dyskinesias) because multiple motor programs are executed in parallel or in too quick succession. The control limit hypothesis proposes that dopamine exerts this exact same influence via cortico-675 striatal loops on cortical activity patterns in general, extending beyond purely motor functions. Excessive D2 activation results in cortical dynamics where attractor states are too shallow. We further speculate that this mechanism explains why D2-receptor antagonist antipsychotics are highly effective in treating Tourette's syndrome. If these overly shallow attractor states occur primarily in sensorimotor cortico-striatal loops, they manifest as involuntary motor tics; conversely, if they occur in associative loops, they result in the thought disorders characteristic of schizophrenia (McCutcheon et al., 2019). The clinical divergence between Tourette's and schizophrenia may simply reflect which part of the cortex is primarily affected by the developmental circuit miswiring.

Would this theory mean that treating schizophrenia with 690 antipsychotic drugs is similar to treating influenza with antipyretic medicine? If our theory is correct, antipsychotic drugs do indeed only treat the symptoms. But like in the case of fever, this can be lifesaving and is the best treatment we have at the moment.

CONCLUSION

The control limit hypothesis of schizophrenia as proposed here provides a unification of the dopamine and glutamate hypotheses as well as a concrete circuit mechanism and experimentally testable predictions. But what are the 700 implications for treatment and the development of novel therapies? Our best hope is that research inspired by the control limit hypothesis will help us to find a way to drive targeted plasticity. Effective biological treatment of schizophrenia will likely involve a combination of acutely 705 relieving the symptoms of psychosis with antipsychotic medication, followed by treatments that can drive targeted plasticity in the affected circuitry. We speculate that in the short term, clinically the most relevant approaches to induce targeted plasticity will likely be electric or magnetic 710 stimulation that can be applied focally -e.g., repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS). In the long term, we believe that it should be possible to develop small molecule-based drugs that take advantage of heterogeneous expression patterns of neuromodulatory 715 receptors, find a way to deliver such drugs in a targeted manner (e.g. via ultrasound uncaging (Ozdas et al., 2020)), or develop a gene therapy-based approach. In any case, we believe effective biological treatment of schizophrenia will rely on a combination therapy that acutely reduces 720 psychosis and drives targeted plasticity to return cortex to healthy attractor dynamics.

Box 1 | Comparing the Control Limit Hypothesis (CLH) to Existing Hypotheses

Progress in understanding schizophrenia has long been framed by two major pathophysiological accounts: Dopamine (DA) Hypothesis: Classically views striatal hyperdopaminergia as the primary driver of psychosis.

Relation to CLH: The CLH reframes hyperdopaminergia not as the primary pathology, but as an allostatic compensatory mechanism. The brain actively elevates dopamine to modulate the speed of cortical dynamics. Psychosis emerges when this feedback controller reaches an upper control limit.

Glutamate / Synaptic Dysregulation Hypothesis: Focuses on NMDA receptor hypofunction and E/I imbalance, proposing that prefrontal/hippocampal miswiring drives downstream subcortical dopamine increases.

Relation to CLH: The CLH incorporates glutamatergic/interneuron miswiring as the developmental "first hit." However, it extends this hypothesis by providing a concrete biological signal that triggers dopamine release (aberrant layer 5 correlation structures and slowed attractor dynamics) and explains why this cortical alteration initially manifests as cognitive impairment long before psychosis appears.

Box 2 | Relationship between the control limit hypothesis and the predictive processing models of psychosis

The computational framework of predictive processing posits that psychosis arises from an imbalance in the precision weighting assigned to prior beliefs relative to incoming sensory prediction errors (Adams et al., 2013; Corlett et al., 2019; Fletcher and Frith, 2009; Sterzer et al., 2018). The control limit hypothesis provides a physiological, circuit-level implementation for these computational phenomena by linking them to the stability and transition speeds of cortical attractor networks. When the dopaminergic feedback controller exceeds its control limit, the resulting over-acceleration and destabilization of layer 5 (L5) attractor dynamics map directly onto key computational failure modes in predictive processing:

Priors collapse before sensory evaluation: If attractor states are excessively shallow and dynamic transitions occur too rapidly, internally generated priors may decay or shift before they can be effectively evaluated against incoming bottom-up sensory streams. This yields an unstable internal generative model of the world that fails to constrain moment-to-moment perception, aligning with behavioral and computational findings of increased perceived environmental volatility in schizophrenia (Deserno et al., 2020; Fromm et al., 2023; Hauke et al., 2024).

Aberrant precision weighting of prediction errors:

In an attractor network undergoing rapid, erratic state fluctuations, baseline cortical activity is inherently non-stationary. Minor sensory mismatches that would normally be filtered out as noise are instead registered as significant, highly salient unexpected events, translating computationally into an aberrantly high precision weighting of prediction errors (Adams et al., 2013; Corlett et al., 2016; Sterzer et al., 2018).

Rapid hypothesis cycling and secondary hyper-rigid priors: Unstable attractor landscapes can cause high-level networks to cycle rapidly through candidate hypotheses, which may manifest phenomenologically as thought racing. Over time, to protect against this perpetual volatility, the system may adaptively develop hyper-rigid, unshakeable higher-order beliefs (delusions) as a compensatory mechanism to enforce stability on an otherwise chaotic lower-level sensory stream (Corlett et al., 2019; Griffin and Fletcher, 2017; Powers et al., 2025; Sterzer et al., 2018).

Autonomous activation of high-level states (hallucinations):

When attractor barriers become too low or cortical ensembles become hyper-correlated, the network can spontaneously settle into high-level attractor representations in the absence of corresponding bottom-up sensory evidence. In predictive processing terms, this corresponds to top-down perceptual expectations completely overriding sensory reality, manifesting directly as hallucinations (Cassidy et al., 2018; Corlett et al., 2019; Horga et al., 2014; Powers et al., 2025 Powers et al., , 2017)).
