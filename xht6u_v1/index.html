Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Mental imagery about suicide is a commonly experienced risk factor for suicide, but subtypes and their relationships to behaviour remain largely unexplored. This study aims to identify and describe common themes in suicide imagery content, perceived function, and subjective meaning in relation to suicide behaviour.

Participants (N=20) provided quantitative and qualitative data about content, perceived function, and subjective meaning across 30 unique instances of suicide imagery via online surveys and semi-structured interviews.

Analysis resulted in five key themes: (a) Determination imagery depicting a single action script detailing a chosen suicide method, (b) Solution imagery highlighting wanted impacts of suicide, (c) Mental Exercise or fantasy imagery, (d) Discouraging imagery of unwanted impacts of suicide, and (e) Consideration imagery weighing up different methods. Determination and Solution imagery were commonly associated with suicide behaviour. Imagery prior to attempt was often deliberately constructed, wanted, and prolonged (Median duration = 90 minutes).

Findings suggest that imagery themes may vary on dimensions reflecting facilitation and deterrence of behaviour, which may interact to produce ambivalence. In addition to volitional impacts via suicide action script rehearsal, suicide imagery may increase motivation towards suicide. Prior to attempt, suicide imagery may be acceptable, comforting, or even sought after. This study provides a nuanced understanding of suicide imagery subtypes in relation to suicide behaviour and provides a framework to assist clinicians in assessment and formulation.

• Imagery subtypes vary in facilitation and deterrence of suicide behaviour.

• Facilitating imagery can provide action scripts and/or boost motivation to suicide.

• Imagery before attempt may be deliberate, wanted and prolonged; not only intrusive.

INTRODUCTION

Suicide is a major health crisis claiming over 700,000 lives globally each year (Davis Weaver et al., 2025), but the efficacy of existing treatments has shown minimal improvement over the past five decades (Fox, Huang, et al., 2020). A greater understanding of modifiable cognitive markers and drivers of suicide risk is needed to inform novel intervention development. One such cognitive factor is suicide mental imagery (Holmes et al., 2007; Lawrence et al., 2023; O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Current clinical risk assessment and interventions for suicidal ideation focus almost exclusively on verbal thoughts (e.g., "I will [method] on Tuesday night when the house is empty"). Yet, growing evidence indicates that mental imagery of suicide (e.g., mentally imagining suicide behaviours in sensory detail) is a prevalent symptom (74% in clinical samples) (Lawrence et al., 2023) and potential driver of suicide risk, more so than verbal ideation (Wetherall et al., 2018). However, no existing studies have evaluated whether suicide imagery varies in relation to the presence or absence of attempt behaviour. This knowledge is crucial for the development of testable hypotheses regarding suicide imagery mechanisms and the development of targeted interventions.

Mental imagery refers to the internal perception of sensory information in the absence of sensory input (Kosslyn et al., 2001), including visual experiences, sound, and even kinaesthesia (Andrade et al., 2014). Imagery-based thoughts evoke stronger emotional and motivational responses (Holmes & Mathews, 2005; Ji et al., 2021; Wicken et al., 2021), increase performance of imagined motor behaviours (Rannaud Monany et al., 2022), and increase the likelihood of completing avoided behaviours with motivational barriers (Ji et al., 2021; Renner et al., 2019) compared to verbal thoughts. Imagery-based interventions are supported across contexts including psychological disorders (Blackwell, 2021; Kroener et al., 2023), physical rehabilitation (Rannaud Monany et al., 2022), behaviour change (May et al., 2015), and athlete performance enhancement (Cumming & Hall, 2002).

In suicide, imagery-based ideation has been associated with an increased likelihood of both having a plan and previously attempting suicide compared to verbal thoughts, and prospectively predicts severity of ideation (Lawrence et al., 2023; Lawrence et al., 2022; Ng et al., 2016; Wetherall et al., 2018). Disrupting suicide imagery via visuospatial competing tasks such as eye-movements has shown promise as an adjunct alongside treatment as usual for reducing suicide intention (van Bentum et al., 2017). This accumulating evidence has led several prominent researchers and clinicians to call for the development of novel interventions to target suicide imagery (De Rozario et al., 2021; Holmes et al., 2007; Paulik et al., 2023).

However, efforts to develop evidence-based interventions are hampered by the heterogeneity of suicide imagery and lack of an evidence-based framework for understanding the functional impact. Mental imagery about suicide can include varied content (Holmes et al., 2007), be associated with a range of subjective appraisals (Holmes et al., 2007), and lead to a range of emotional responses (Crane et al., 2012). Hales et al. (2011) described examples of suicide method imagery that were associated with inclination towards behaviour for some individuals, but discouraged others. There has been no in-depth study comparing suicide imagery when intention was acted upon versus when it was not acted upon.

One limiting methodological factor is the assessment of suicide imagery. The most frequently used tool is the Suicide Cognitions Interview (SCI; Holmes et al., 2007), which has enabled in-depth mapping of the range of suicide imagery experiences. However, all items are anchored to a time when the participant was "most despairing or suicidal" (Holmes et al., 2007, p. 426), limiting comparison of imagery characteristics in relation to the enaction of, or refrainment from, suicide behaviours.

A retrospective study of self-harm (McEvoy et al., 2017) compared cognitions on instances of self-harm to instances where the participant did not self-harm. Prior to engaging in nonsuicidal self-injury, imagery tended to be more comforting, to depict self-harm behaviours, and to be appraised as encouraging of self-harm. In contrast, imagery during episodes when an urge was not acted upon was more distressing and tended to depict negative impacts of self-harm. This approach represents a feasible method for mapping differences in imagery as a function of behaviour. To date, no equivalent evaluations have been conducted with suicide imagery.

This study compares the content and characteristics of suicide imagery preceding attempt to imagery not associated with immediate attempt behaviour. The primary aim is to identify common themes in the content, perceived function, and subjective meaning of suicide imagery in relation to suicide behaviours. Secondarily, the study aims to describe the broader characteristics of suicide imagery in relation to suicide behaviour.

METHODS

This study is part of the Qualitative SUicide IMagery (QSUIM) project. Findings relating to precipitating stressors and behavioural responses to suicide imagery will be reported elsewhere. This project was conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number HC230455) and all participants provided informed consent.

Design

Qualitative and quantitative data from a semi-structured interview was supplemented by quantitative demographic and suicidality data to characterise participants.

Lived Experience Engagement

Two Lived Experience Advisors were paid for their time in sharing decision-making regarding study design, materials, and safety protocols via a written material review and a two-hour workshop with the lead researcher. Although both advisors were offered the opportunity to contribute to data analysis and manuscript preparation, neither was engaging in advisory work at the time.

Participants

English-speaking adults (>18 years) residing in Australia who experienced suicide imagery were recruited via online advertisements in newsletters and social media posts followed by male-focused advertisements to increase male recruitment. Two subgroups included: (a) individuals describing imagery without suicide behaviour (non-attempt imagery), and (b) individuals describing both (i) non-attempt imagery and (ii) imagery followed by suicide behaviour (attempt imagery). Individuals were excluded if they had experienced active suicide intent, preparation, or behaviour within the past 4 weeks, and were provided with information about support services and helplines.

The study utilised purposive sampling to ensure diversity across gender and attempt behaviours (attempt versus non-attempt imagery). To determine sufficient sample size, the notion of saturation of novel ideas (Saunders et al., 2018) was applied. Recruitment was paused after 20 participants to assess saturation. As no new codes were identified after the sixteenth interview, it was determined that saturation had been reached and recruitment was ceased.

Materials

Online questionnaires

Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview-Revised (SITBI-R).

Select items from the online self-report version of the SITBI-R (Fox, Harris, et al., 2020) were used to evaluate suicidality history. The SITBI-R has good alternate-form reliability for the subscale items used in this study (Kappa range 0.63-0.87 (Fox, Harris, et al., 2020)) and good convergent validity with the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) (Posner et al., 2011), (Kappa >0.74) (Fox, Harris, et al., 2020).

Spontaneous Use of Imagery Scale (SUIS).

The SUIS (Reisberg et al., 2003) assesses trait tendency to utilise mental imagery in daily life across 12 everyday scenarios on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("never") to 5 ("always"). It has good internal reliability, test-retest reliability, and convergence with other mental imagery questionnaires (Nelis et al., 2014). Internal reliability within the present sample was excellent, Cronbach's α = 0.90.

Suicide Imagery Interview

An interview guide (S1) was developed through in-depth consultation with Lived Experience Advisors and clinical researchers (AM, FS, PM). Quantitative items (Lawrence et al., 2022) evaluated how often suicidal thoughts included images and words on 5-point Likert scales (1 = "almost never";5 = "almost always"), followed by forced choice endorsement. The interview then followed a semi-structured guide assessing overall flow of cognitions (e.g. linear train of thought versus circular or repetitive), then content, meaning, and perceived function of each image. Additional questions assessed sensory modalities, imagery source, temporal orientation, proportion of visual content, perspective, vividness, intrusiveness, and metacognitive beliefs.

Procedure

Participants completed electronic consent and pre-screening questionnaires, then telephone screening interviews. Questionnaires were completed via the online survey platform Qualtrics prior to one-on-one interviews with AM via Zoom between November 2023 and March 2024.

Interviews began with a psychological risk assessment and explanation of verbal versus imagery-based thought. Three participants elected to receive an optional check-in call the following day. Participants were reimbursed with electronic gift cards (AUD40 for 1-hour non-attempt interviews and AUD80 for 2-hour interviews exploring both attempt and nonattempt imagery). Interview audio recordings were deidentified before transcription by a secure third party.

Analysis

Imagery Event Categories

AM and CC reviewed transcripts to categorise imagery episodes as non-attempts (no immediate suicide behaviour), attempts (suicide behaviour was enacted), aborted attempts (the participant commenced a suicide attempt but decided to stop before taking action), and interrupted attempts (the participant commenced action but was interrupted by an external force prior to the attempt being fully enacted) in line with Fox, Harris, et al. (2020), consulting with PM and FS as required.

Quantitative Data

Quantitative ratings were summarised using descriptive statistics in a stratified manner per imagery event type, collapsing interrupted attempts within attempts due to conceptual overlap (interrupting factors were external and occurred after imagery) and low base rate (n = 3) that limited the reliability of group-level estimates. Mean scores were calculated for scale response data and frequency scores for categorical and count response data.

Qualitative Data

Qualitative data were analysed using the Framework Analysis approach (Gale et al., 2013) using NVivo 12 (see S2 for theoretical perspective, positionality, and reflexivity statements).

A preliminary codebook was based upon prior research and clinical experience. AM and CC familiarised via repeated exposure to transcripts and recordings. Independently, AM and CC coded five interviews from each subgroup line-by-line. Additional inductively-derived codes were added after discussion with DR. An analytical framework was developed separating data across the three domains of investigation. Codes and themes relating to content, meaning, and perceived function were refined through discussions between AM and CC, with feedback from remaining authors. The framework systematically compared themes across cases of suicide imagery categorised by behaviour. Interrupted attempts were retained as a distinct category. Rigour was maintained using an in-depth audit trail, repeated discussions during sense-making, and the application of reflexivity (S2).

RESULTS

Participants

Twenty participants with a mean age of 38.3 years (range: 19-67, SD = 14.0) provided a sample diverse in gender, sexuality, neurodivergence, and suicide histories (Tables 1 2). The average tendency to use imagery in daily life (SUIS; M = 39.35, SD = 11.8) aligned with existing non-clinical samples (M = 37.9-40.1) (Nelis et al., 2014). The mean age of ideation onset was 21.1 years (range: 8-59, SD = 15.73). Fourteen participants (70%) had experienced between one and 30 attempts. All participants reported that their ideation typically includes imagery (scores of 2 or above), but 25% reported that their ideation almost never includes verbal content (Table 3).

Imagery Event Categories

Of the 30 imagery instances assessed, 17 were non-attempts, three aborted attempts, three interrupted attempts, and seven suicide attempts.

Quantitative Ratings

Overall Event Characteristics

The median suicide crisis duration was two hours and 15 mins (IQR = 1-5 hours, raw range = 10 min -28 days) and the median imagery duration was half of the crisis (IQR = 34-77%), varying between less than a minute to several weeks of persistent imagery (Table 4). Most cognitions were imagery-based and most episodes flowed linearly (i.e., one cognition sequentially leading to the next) (Table 4).

Imagery Characteristics

Vividness. Attempt imagery was rated at very strongly vivid, while non-attempt and aborted attempt imagery were rated as strongly vivid (see Table 5).

Emotional impact.

On average, participants endorsed mild feelings of both distress and comfort, although aborted attempt imagery was rated as moderately distressing (Table 5). On average, participants reported moderate feelings of control arising from their imagery.

Metacognitive appraisals. On average, participants appraised their suicide imagery as moderately controllable, strongly distracting, and strongly compelling (Table 5).

Sensory modalities.

On average, participants' imagery included 3.57 sensory modalities (Table 5); the most common were visual (100%), tactile (56.7%), and auditory imagery (50%).

Temporal orientation.

Attempt imagery was always future-oriented, while non-attempt imagery was mostly but not exclusively future-oriented (Table 6).

Content source.

Almost a third of attempt imagery included content based on first-hand source material (e.g. memories of attempts) or explicitly recalled second-hand source material (e.g. online media such as videos or written reports). Non-attempt imagery was almost always reported to be exclusively imagined with no content based on identified source material (Table 6).

Intrusiveness. Across the sample, suicide imagery was mostly reported to onset spontaneously. Half of the attempt imagery instances included some degree of deliberate construction, while non-attempt imagery included deliberate construction less than a quarter of the time. Once in mind, attempt imagery was experienced as welcome and wanted in most instances, while non-attempt imagery was wanted about half of the time (Table 6).

Perceived impact on urge. Attempt imagery was retrospectively perceived to increase suicide urges in most instances, while non-attempt imagery was almost never reported to increase suicide urges (Table 6).

Change in imagery over time

Six participants spontaneously reported changes in their suicide imagery across time. Four participants reported that their suicide imagery onset between the ages of 10 and 20 years old, with three reporting persistence in this imagery across decades of their life. Three reported increases in their suicide imagery over time.

Qualitative Results: Imagery Content, Meaning, and Perceived Function in Relation to

Suicide Behaviour

Five themes of suicide imagery were identified and charted onto cases of suicide behaviour (Table 7). Participants could experience a combination of imagery themes within a single episode.

Theme 1: Imagery Reflecting the Individual's Determination or Resolution to suicide

Suicide imagery reflecting determination or resolution to suicide ('determination/resolution imagery') frequently co-occurred with attempt behaviour. This imagery primarily depicted a single method of suicide behaviour or a single suicide plan, which we termed a suicide action script. These suicide action scripts tended to be extremely detailed ("down to what I was wearing", "exactly how...and where"), highly realistic, and portrayed methods that were feasible, accessible, and lethal. They often included preparatory behaviours (e.g. preparing materials or how to leave home without alerting family) and were reported to feel mechanical and unemotional ("very matter of fact"), in contrast to the emotional methods in Mental Exercise Imagery. Two participants described suicide action scripts becoming more detailed and salient immediately preceding attempt ("right before I did it was the strongest"; "The longer I went into it, there was more detail"). In contrast, suicide action scripts not associated with attempt behaviour tended to be less detailed (e.g., fewer details regarding clothing, items, scene details) and omitted preparatory behaviours.

The reported meaning of determination/resolution imagery tended to be passive and conveyed resignation to suicide as inevitable, or as a single path with lack of choice ("I had no choice…This is just what is being done" and "I just wanted to die"). Two participants described the perceived function as pre-experiencing -a passive and automatic mental experiencing of their imminent suicide attempt which subjectively felt like a video "replay" as seen in sports games. This contrasts with the active planning in Solution Imagery.

Some suicide action scripts were cued by incidental exposure to method-related stimuli (e.g., encountering method implements/locations while running errands). Such stimulus-cued intrusions were often accompanied by imperative verbal phrases about lethality and accessibility that encouraged action ("I could just"; "I can just do it"; "I could do it").

Two participants noted being surprised on reflection that determination imagery did not contain imagery of "blockers" or reasons against suicide (e.g., impact on pets/loved ones, impact on self if attempt is non-lethal such as permanent disability).

Theme 2: Imagery Framing Suicide as a Solution to Struggles

Imagery depicting suicide as a solution to a problem or goal to be worked towards ('solution imagery') was more evenly distributed across attempts and non-attempts (Table 7). Solution imagery conveyed a sense of action and depicted wanted impacts that suicide was believed to offer as a solution for self or others. This typically included positive impacts on others, such as imagery of loved ones (spouse, children, parents, friends) being relieved from burden, happier, and financially secure (e.g., buying a house). For one participant this imagery also included wanted negative impacts on others (suffering and guilt to be experienced by emotionally abusive family). Solution imagery was appraised as offering freedom, hope, peace, escape from one's own struggles or negative emotions, or as a solution for others (e.g., unburdening others). Some participants interpreted their suicide imagery as a reflection or realisation of the extent to which they were struggling at that time ("It's my mind, my whole body telling me something is not right."), although this appraisal was typically not associated with an attempt.

The perceived function was often reported as planning or preparation to suicide, although differentially across cases. Imagery prior to attempt was described as preparing oneself to enact an already-decided plan ("to prepare myself for what I was going to do") or to motivate towards suicide to solve problems (e.g., "to make me do it, in a way.", "for fear" [of consequences if don't attempt]). Non-attempt imagery was more centred around forming the plan (e.g., "It was about planning. It was about problem solving. It was outcome driven. It was focused on practicalities"; "…I start thinking [about] how would I do this").

Solution imagery prior to attempt was sometimes reported to serve a soothing or relieving function that was centred around anticipation of imminently enacting suicide ("relief to me because I had a way out", "comforting, controlling thought of this will be the outcome"). This contrasts with Mental Exercise Imagery where soothing is derived from the act of imaginal rehearsal, rather than anticipation of imminent behaviour.

Theme 3: Suicide Imagery as a Mental Exercise or Fantasy

Imagery depicting suicide as a mental exercise or fantasy that provides feelings of control or relief ('mental exercise imagery') was almost exclusively reported on non-attempts. Mental exercise imagery content centred around mentally simulated feelings ("this fantasy of how I would feel") with a focus on the cessation of negative feelings or events for the self, and the term "relief" was used often. This imagery was perceived to represent control, agency, or choice ("this was my way of gaining control", "the sense of control that this is something I can choose to do or choose not to do").

Mental exercise imagery often described methods that were fantastical, romanticised death, not aligned with real-world details, vague, or less lethal. Examples included imagined methods ("…this weird contraption thing…"), imagined locations, or locations that no longer exist (e.g. childhood buildings that are now renovated or located internationally).

Romanticised imagery was sometimes described as a "fantasy", daydream, or "imaginary plane".

Participants described the perceived function of this imagery as an emotion regulation or coping strategy wherein the act of mentally simulating suicide provided soothing or relief ("that image did…make me have a better control of the emotions"; "a coping mechanism"; "the function was soothing"). This relief was derived from the act of imagining. One participant described the coping nature as a form of expression that "allowed me to express my need to die without having to take that out of my mind into the open world for everyone to see".

Theme 4: Imagery Discouraging of Suicide

Imagery discouraging suicide ('discouraging imagery') was primarily associated with nonattempt instances and to a lesser extent aborted attempts. This imagery commonly depicted the unwanted impact of suicide on other people, with participants conveying that even during their suicidal distress they had the capacity to experience deep care and compassion for others, including both loved ones and strangers (e.g., bystanders). Content included immediate impacts (e.g. first responders, witnesses, finding the body, loved ones being distressed when informed of the death), medium-term impacts (e.g. mourning and funeral imagery), and long-term impacts such as dependent loved ones (e.g., children, pets) struggling at key milestones or missing the deceased.

Discouraging imagery also depicted unwanted impacts for the self, including injuries and future consequences of non-lethal attempts, or the discomfort of dying. Some participants reported imagery of their body after lethal suicide behaviour. Non-attempt instances included distancing references to "the mess" and "the body". Aborted attempts included detailed and graphic impacts (details omitted due to method dependency), which participants described using more emotive ("carnage"; "confronting"; "pretty f**ked"; "pretty morbid") and less distancing ("what I would look like") language. Several participants reported that imagery of their body led to imagery about the impact on others who might find their body.

Discouraging imagery included appraisals that suicide would be incongruent with values, traits, reputation, or goals ("I thought that's really inconsistent with …how I see myself"; "It's a really selfish way to go... I am a very caring person"). For some participants this imagery reflected their decision that suicide is not their chosen option at this time, including imperative language such as "…you need to stay…" and "we're not going through that". One participant explicitly acknowledged that this imagery "represented ambivalence" and their desire to stay alive at that time.

The perceived function was reported as motivation to not suicide, including references to deciding against suicide ("put some things off the agenda") or finding another path ("me trying to agree with something else"). This included imagery of reasons for living ("my reasons why I definitely wouldn't") that focused on loved ones ("my friend"; "my cats").

Theme 5: Consideration without Commitment to Action

A subset of imagery that was only reported on non-attempt instances reflected the experience of considering multiple suicide methods without selecting or committing to enacting a specific suicide action ('consideration imagery'). This involved weighing up options for several suicide methods without choosing one ("weighing up options"; "the various methods") and sometimes included imagined barriers to lethality (e.g., literal barriers or responder intervention). Some participants appraised the imagery with detached observation ("just a stupid intrusive thought") and perceived the function as an automatic (and potentially conditioned) response to distress, without current functional utility. One participant reported that their consideration imagery on a non-attempt episode later changed to a single action script prior to attempt.

DISCUSSION

This study was the first to explore the characteristics of suicide imagery across cases of suicide behaviour. We extended existing explorations of suicide imagery (De Rozario et al., 2021) by clearly describing five key themes: (a) Determination imagery depicting single method action scripts, (b) Solution imagery highlighting wanted impacts, (c) Mental Exercise or fantasy imagery, (d) Discouraging imagery highlighting unwanted impacts, and (e) Consideration imagery exploring different methods. We then systematically explored these themes in relation to the presence or absence of suicide behaviour. Although others found that suicide imagery may at times be alternately encouraging or discouraging (Hales et al., 2011), we advanced understanding by clearly describing imagery that were most closely associated with attempt behaviour (Determination and Solution Imagery) versus those less associated with attempt behaviour (Mental Exercise, Discouraging, and Consideration Imagery).

We suggest that future investigations may conceptualise suicide imagery subtypes along two dimensions representing facilitation and deterrence of suicide behaviour, as well as the interaction between these dimensions (Figure 1). This conceptualisation is not intended to present a comprehensive theoretical model of suicide imagery or suicide behaviour risk, but rather a tool to encourage consideration of suicide imagery factors. The barrier icon acknowledges non-imagery factors (e.g., helper intervention) that may interrupt or prevent attempt behaviour despite strong Facilitating and weak Deterring imagery.

Imagery as a Facilitator of Behaviour

We suggest that Determination and Solution imagery warrant investigation as potential facilitators. Both subtypes explicitly focused upon working towards suicide as a goal and were associated with suicide behaviour.

Determination imagery aligns well with 'flash-forward' imagery (Holmes et al., 2007), which has been used to refer to suicide imagery more broadly (Hales et al., 2011; Ng et al., 2016; Schultebraucks et al., 2020). In our sample, Determination Imagery was characterised by a detailed single-action method script, a sense of pre-experiencing this method, and resignation or determination to imminently enact it. These characteristics align with the rich sensory details and perceived "nowness" that prompted Holmes and colleagues to draw parallels between suicide imagery and post-traumatic flashbacks. This theme is also consistent with the Integrated Motivational-Volitional model's(IMV) account of suicide imagery as a volitional factor that influences suicide behaviour through cognitive rehearsal (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). The single action scripts are also consistent with the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide's (IPT; Joiner, 2005) suggestion that rehearsal imagery may contribute to acquired capability.

However, we also identified Solution Imagery as a second facilitator theme precipitating attempt behaviour. This imagery was perceived as motivating to suicide, assisting method planning, and providing relief in the anticipation that problems would soon be solved via suicide. Solution Imagery may play a role in enhancing motivation by amplifying perceived desirability of suicide behaviour, consistent with Elaborated Intrusion Theory's (May et al., 2015) assertion that imagery-based elaborations of desired states (in this case relief through suicide) increase motivation for behaviour.

Solution imagery included an unexpected instance of wanted negative impacts on others, specifically imagery of estranged family members to experience distress and dishonour in relation to the participant's suicide. Revenge imagery has been previously documented and associated with increased suicidality (Selby et al., 2007).

Deterring Imagery

We suggest that Discouraging Imagery and Consideration Imagery warrant investigation as potential deterrents of suicide behaviour. Both themes explicitly focused on barriers to suicide and were rarely associated with suicide behaviour. We do not propose Mental Exercise Imagery as a potential deterrent because this imagery portrayed suicide as desirable and did not depict barriers.

The dimension of deterrence from suicide is related to but distinct from the established phenomenon of 'Wish to Live'. Previous work has highlighted possession of rich reasons to live as a potential protective factor against suicide (for a discussion, see Ernst et al., 2024). Indeed, some of the Discouraging Imagery described in our sample included depictions of the negative consequences of suicide upon reasons for living (e.g., distress for family, friends, or pets). However, the focus of deterrence imagery was dissuading away from suicide, rather than engagement towards life. This delineation between considering the reasons for and against two choices (in this case, suicide versus living) as separate decisions is clinically common, and part of the key DBT skill of 'Pros and Cons' (Linehan, 2015) where the reasons for and against both acting and not acting on crisis urges are charted separately. Thus, we suggest that the dimension of deterrence from suicide is distinct from the concept of Wish to Live.

Ambivalence as an Interaction Between Facilitation and Deterrence

We chose to represent facilitation and deterrence as distinct but interacting dimensions, as variability in one was not entirely dependent on the strength of the other. Within a single episode, participants could experience a combination of both facilitative and deterring imagery. We suggest that the interaction between facilitating and deterring imagery warrants investigation as a potential contributor to ambivalence, wherein the co-occurrence of strongly facilitative imagery (e.g., Determination Imagery) alongside strong deterrence imagery (e.g., Discouraging Imagery) might interact to produce high levels of ambivalence, similar to the tensions between Wish to Live and Wish To Die (Ernst et al., 2024). Given emerging evidence that ambivalence between co-occurring wish to live and wish to die may present a vulnerability factor for suicide behaviour (Ernst et al., 2024), future research might investigate whether ambivalence between facilitation and deterrence imagery is similarly implicated.

Broader Characteristics of Suicide Imagery

Imagery prior to attempt was often deliberately constructed, wanted/welcome, and contained content derived from media or personal experience. Although some participants reported brief imagery flashes of less than a minute, the median imagery duration prior to attempt was 90 minutes. This suggests that suicide imagery may be acceptable, comforting, or even sought after for some individuals prior to an attempt. This aligns with findings that prior history of suicidality is associated with greater comfort and less distress during worst-point suicide imagery (Crane et al., 2012). Some suicide imagery may be less closely related to intrusive and distressing trauma imagery than initially posited (Holmes et al., 2007). However, other participants in our sample experienced imagery that was distressing, intrusive, and unwanted prior to attempting suicide, suggesting that clinicians should be cautious not to discount the severity of risk based on individuals' emotional experience of their imagery, because presentations prior to attempt may vary. Finally, one-quarter of participants in this study had little-to-no verbal ideation.

Implications for clinical practice

Although the impact of assessing and modifying suicidal mental imagery on subsequent behaviour is yet to be tested, our findings may hold important implications for supporting people experiencing suicide imagery. Firstly, it may be valuable for clinicians to assess for suicide imagery to ensure that individuals who do not experience verbal ideation and would not otherwise spontaneously report imagery are detected and supported. Existing tools for suicide risk assessment such as the C-SSRS (Posner et al., 2011) and SIDAS (van Spijker et al., 2014) assess only verbal ideation. As a result, individuals who experience mental imagery of suicide with minimal verbal ideation may remain undetected. Secondly, it may be beneficial for clinicians to assess for both facilitating and deterring imagery as distinct dimensions that may separately inform case formulation. Example questions have been provided in Table 8 to assist clinicians in doing so. The impact of such imagery on suicide behaviour is yet to be validated prospectively and these questions do not assess risk of behaviour.

Given calls to develop interventions for suicide imagery (Paulik et al., 2023), these findings demonstrate that suicide imagery is not homogenous. There may be variability in the extent to which subtypes are facilitative or deterring of suicide so interventions should be applied cautiously to avoid blanket targeting of suicide imagery as a unitary construct. Finally, suicide imagery should be re-evaluated regularly as it can change over time.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although our focus on qualitative interviews allowed for a rich understanding of suicide imagery types and their relationship to suicide behaviour, limitations must be considered.

First, while there was some heterogeneity in this sample in gender, sexuality, and neurotype, the use of online recruitment and participation methods may limit generalizability to groups most susceptible to digital access barriers such as older, lower income, minority, and non-English speaking participants (Alkureishi et al., 2021).

Second, the findings in this study rely upon retrospective data collected at a single timepoint, which limits causal inference. Real-time longitudinal research is needed to objectively map the relationships between types of imagery, how they may change over time, and the functional impact of this imagery on suicide behaviour and coping. Future studies might utilise Ecological Momentary Assessment to reduce reliance on retrospective recall.

It is also important to note limitations with respect to predictive utility. Although we provide a description of suicide imagery themes that depict potential facilitators and deterrents to suicide, we do not argue that imagery alone is predictive of suicide behaviour. The prediction of suicide behaviour is inherently complex, impacted by a variety of factors, and notoriously inaccurate (Kessler et al., 2020). Instead, we present a preliminary account of suicide imagery themes that warrant further evaluation as potential contributors within the broader context of factors which may influence suicide behaviour.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides a description of varied suicide imagery themes and a framework for conceptualising suicide imagery themes along dimensions of facilitation and deterrence to suicide. Findings hold important implications for existing theories of suicidality as well as clinical care for people who experience this distressing and risk-increasing symptom.

However, further longitudinal research with larger samples is required to allow for causal inferences to be made regarding the functional impact of imagery types on suicide behaviour.
