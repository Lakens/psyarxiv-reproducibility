Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p ≥ .117 Age and length of inpatient stay were not significantly associated with profile membership at T1 or with transitions from T1 to T2 (all p ≥ .117; Supplementary Table S1).
p ≥ .228 Similarly, the number of attended AAT sessions was not significantly associated with transitions from T1 to T2 (all p ≥ .228).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .482 The joint Wald test indicated no significant differences in profile-specific means across T1 and T2, χ²(6) = 5.50, p = .482.
p ≥ .117 Age and length of inpatient stay were not significantly associated with profile membership at T1 or with transitions from T1 to T2 (all p ≥ .117; Supplementary Table S1).
p ≥ .228 Similarly, the number of attended AAT sessions was not significantly associated with transitions from T1 to T2 (all p ≥ .228).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Background and objectives: Adolescents receiving inpatient mental health care differ considerably in their symptom burden, psychological resources, and patterns of change. This study identified latent profiles based on depressive symptoms and mindfulness and examined transitions between these profiles among adolescents participating in animal-assisted group psychotherapy.

Methods: The sample comprised 108 adolescent inpatients aged 12-18 years (M age = 15.84 years, SD = 1.26) who participated in a five-week animal-assisted group psychotherapy program alongside standard inpatient treatment. Depressive symptoms and mindfulness were assessed before and after the intervention using the Beck Depression Inventory-Fast Screen and the Freiburg Mindfulness Inventory. Profiles for each time point were identified using latent profile analysis and linked longitudinally using latent transition analysis.

Results: Three profiles were identified at both measurement points: reduced burden (T1: n = 14, 14.0%; T2: n = 28, 26.7%), characterized by low depressive symptoms and high mindfulness; moderate burden (T1: n = 46, 46.0%; T2: n = 33, 31.4%); and elevated burden (T1: n = 40, 40.0%; T2: n = 44, 41.9%), characterized by high depressive symptoms and low mindfulness. Estimated transition probabilities indicated that 100% of adolescents in the reduced-burden profile and 86.9% of those in the elevated-burden profile at T1 remained in their respective profiles at T2. Among adolescents in the moderate-burden profile at T1, 27.7% transitioned to the reduced-burden profile over the five-week intervention period.

Conclusions: Adolescents showed distinct depression-mindfulness profiles with different patterns of stability and change. Adolescents with moderate initial burden were most likely to transition toward a more favorable profile, suggesting that they may represent a particularly responsive subgroup.

These findings highlight heterogeneity in adolescents' patterns of functioning and change during multimodal inpatient treatment incorporating animal-assisted group psychotherapy.

[div-01]

Research on animal-assisted approaches in youth mental health has examined a range of outcomes, including symptoms of anxiety, depression, trauma-related difficulties, and ADHD, as well as psychological well-being and functioning. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest potential benefits across these domains, although findings remain mixed and methodological limitations persist (Brandão et al., 2025; Dumouchel & Soubelet, 2026; Hoagwood et al., 2017; Jones et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2025). Evidence from psychiatric inpatient settings, however, remains limited and heterogeneous with respect to clinical populations, intervention formats, treatment dose, outcome measures, and study design (Fornefeld et al., 2025). Moreover, studies of animal-assisted approaches in youth psychiatric care have primarily examined mean-level pre-post changes, between-group differences, immediate session-related changes, or feasibility and acceptability (Fodstad et al., 2019; Stefanini et al., 2015 Stefanini et al., , 2016)). These approaches provide important information about overall outcomes but offer limited insight into whether adolescents enter treatment with different configurations of symptoms and psychological resources and whether these configurations remain stable or change over time in response to treatment.

Person-centered methods provide a complementary approach for examining such heterogeneity. Rather than focusing primarily on associations or average differences between variables, person-centered approaches identify subgroups of individuals who show similar patterns across multiple characteristics (Lanza & Rhoades, 2013). Latent profile analysis (LPA) can be used to identify unobserved subgroups based on patterns across continuous indicators, whereas latent transition analysis (LTA) extends this approach longitudinally by estimating the probabilities of remaining in the same profile or transitioning to another profile across measurement occasions (Collins & Lanza, 2009; Nylund-Gibson et al., 2023). In treatment research, these methods can therefore characterize heterogeneity in functioning at a given time point as well as different patterns of stability and change over a treatment period (Thompson et al., 2011).

Applications in child and adolescent mental health illustrate the potential of this approach.

LPA studies have identified subgroups differing in symptom severity, emotion dysregulation, and transdiagnostic psychological processes, with some evidence that these profiles are associated with subsequent clinical outcomes (Caporino et al., 2016; Evans et al., 2021; Kennedy et al., 2022).

Longitudinal person-centered analyses can additionally examine how individuals move between profiles. For example, Melero et al. (2022) identified low-and high-anxiety/depression profiles among children participating in a transdiagnostic prevention program and found that some children transitioned from the higher-to the lower-symptom profile over time. Together, these studies demonstrate how person-centered approaches can complement analyses of average change by describing clinically meaningful heterogeneity and patterns of change within treatment samples.

Depressive symptoms and mindfulness provide useful indicators for examining such heterogeneity among adolescents receiving inpatient mental health care. Depressive symptoms affect a substantial proportion of adolescents and constitute an important dimension of mental health burden in this age group (Shorey et al., 2022). Mindfulness, in contrast, refers to present-moment attention combined with an open and accepting orientation toward ongoing experience (Bishop et al., 2004) and has been associated with several aspects of psychological functioning, including emotion regulation and psychological well-being (Keng et al., 2011). Mindfulness may also be conceptually relevant to animal-assisted work because interaction with animals can involve attending to immediate, often nonverbal cues and continuously adapting to the present situation. Animal-assisted mindfulness approaches have explicitly drawn on this rationale (Schramm et al., 2015). Examining depressive symptoms together with mindfulness therefore allows symptom burden and a potentially adaptive psychological resource to be considered simultaneously rather than in isolation.

The current study

The present study examined heterogeneity in depressive symptoms and mindfulness among adolescents participating in a five-week animal-assisted group psychotherapy program alongside standard inpatient psychosomatic treatment. Separate LPAs were used to identify profiles based on depressive symptoms and mindfulness at the beginning and end of the five-week period, and these profiles were subsequently linked longitudinally using LTA to examine profile stability and transitions. The first aim was to identify latent profiles characterized by different patterns of depressive symptoms and mindfulness at the beginning and end of the five-week period. The second aim was to examine stability and transitions between these profiles over time. Based on previous person-centered research in youth mental health, we expected to identify distinct profiles reflecting different combinations of symptom burden and mindfulness (Evans et al., 2021; Kennedy et al., 2022; Melero et al., 2022). Given the limited evidence on person-centered patterns of change during AAT, profile stability and transitions were examined without specific directional hypotheses. By applying a longitudinal person-centered approach, the study aimed to provide a more differentiated understanding of heterogeneity and change during animal-assisted group psychotherapy embedded within adolescent inpatient care.

Methods

Participants

The data were drawn from a larger clinical study conducted with adolescent inpatients receiving treatment at the Schön Klinik Bad Arolsen, Germany. The sample initially consisted of 111 patients. Three patients were excluded because of extensive missing data, resulting in a final analytic sample of 108 adolescents. Patients were eligible if they were between 12 and 18 years old at the beginning of treatment, had sufficient German language proficiency, had received a diagnosis of a mental health disorder according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10; World Health Organization, 2004). Eligible diagnoses included major depressive episodes, eating disorders, anxiety disorders, and obsessivecompulsive disorder. Although the registered protocol initially specified an upper age limit of 17 years, this criterion was extended to 18 years to correspond to the age range treated at the clinic. Exclusion criteria comprised acute suicidality, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, bipolar disorder, substance use disorder, organic mental disorder, a current Axis I disorder other than the primary diagnosis, severe physical illness, reduced physical fitness due to anorexia nervosa, and ongoing therapeutically supervised eating within the clinic among patients with eating disorders.

Additional exclusion criteria for participation in the AAT program were pronounced emotional instability, fear of animals, and allergy to animal hair.

Study Design and Procedure

The present study employed a pretest-posttest design, examining adolescents before and after a five-week animal-assisted group psychotherapy program. The AAT program was offered as part of inpatient psychosomatic care and was intended for patients who were expected to benefit from therapeutic work involving animals. Goals included promoting mindfulness and self-awareness, strengthening self-esteem and self-efficacy, developing assertiveness and boundary-setting skills, addressing self-image and identity, increasing activation, and supporting treatment engagement.

Patients were referred to AAT by their treating psychotherapists and physicians. Before enrollment, the clinical team assessed whether each patient was physically and emotionally able to participate safely in group sessions outside the clinic. Particular attention was given to the physical condition of patients with eating disorders. Suitability for AAT was determined in consultation with the head physician and/or the responsible ward physician. The AAT program was provided in addition to the standard multimodal inpatient treatment received by all participants. Standard inpatient treatment included pharmacotherapy, individual and group psychotherapy, sports therapy, art therapy, supervised group meals, mindfulness-based therapy, and other therapeutic services.

Participants completed questionnaires immediately before the start of the AAT program (T1) and after completion of the five-week intervention period (T2). Before participation, patients and their legal guardians gave informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable German legal and regulatory requirements. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the legal department of Schön Klinik. The study was registered in the Dutch Trial Register on March 1, 2021; the registration is currently available through the Research with Human Participants registry.

Animal-assisted Group Psychotherapy

The intervention was delivered by a child and adolescent psychotherapist in training who was The intervention was intended to use structured human-animal interactions to support emotional stabilization, activation, intrinsic motivation, mindfulness, and self-efficacy. It consisted of five weekly sessions, each lasting approximately 150 minutes, and was conducted on the therapist's farm. Groups included no more than seven adolescents. Each session combined theoretical input, practical exercises involving animals, and guided reflection. The theoretical material covered topics such as human-animal relationships, mindful awareness, nonverbal communication, body language, emotional expression, personal boundaries, and experiences in relationships. Practical exercises involved observing, approaching, communicating with, and mindfully guiding dogs and horses. The therapist provided individualized feedback to help participants recognize how their internal emotional states were communicated through their behavior and how changes in body language affected the interaction.

The sessions increased progressively in complexity over the course of the program. Initial sessions focused on becoming acquainted with the animals and observing nonverbal communication.

Later sessions addressed mindful leadership, boundaries, relationship patterns, and the transfer of experiences from the animal interactions to everyday situations. The final session included the application and integration of these skills during a group walk. An overview of the content and focus of each session is provided in Table 1. Attendance was documented as the total number of completed sessions.

Animals

Dogs, horses, and ponies participated in the intervention. The program involved six mixedbreed dogs between 4 and 11 years of age. Four of the dogs belonged to the therapist and lived on the farm, whereas two belonged to another staff member and were present on most intervention days.

The group included three female and three male dogs. All dogs had been trained by the therapist and evaluated by a veterinarian to determine their suitability for therapeutic work.

Three horses and two Shetland ponies were housed at the farm in a spacious, natural openstable environment. The horses consisted of an English Warmblood mare, a Zweibrücker mare, and a Haflinger-Quarter Horse cross gelding, ranging in age from 10 to 22 years. The two Shetland mares were 4 and 5 years old. A certified animal-welfare practitioner was present throughout the sessions and monitored the animals continuously. Participation by the animals was voluntary, and each animal could move away from or discontinue an interaction at any point.

Measures

Depressive Symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed before (T1) and after (T2) the intervention using the German version of the Beck Depression Inventory-Fast Screen (BDI-FS; Beck et al., 2000). The BDI-FS comprises seven cognitive and affective items derived from the BDI-II. Items are rated from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptom severity. Scores of 0-3 indicate minimal, 4-8 mild, 9-12 moderate, and 13-21 severe symptoms. A score of 4 or higher has been proposed as an indicator of clinically relevant symptoms.

In a German population sample, the BDI-FS demonstrated good internal consistency (α = .84) and satisfactory discriminant validity (Kliem et al., 2014).

Mindfulness

Mindfulness was assessed at the beginning (T1) and end (T2) of the intervention using the German version of the Freiburg Mindfulness Inventory (Freiburger Fragebogen zur Achtsamkeit; FFA; Walach et al., 2006). The 30-item FFA assesses mindful awareness and acceptance of presentmoment experiences, including attention to current experience, nonjudgmental acceptance, and openness toward internal states. Items are rated from 1 (rarely) to 4 (almost always) and summed to yield a total score ranging from 30 to 120, with higher scores indicating greater mindfulness. The 30item version has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .93) and construct validity in meditating and nonmeditating samples (Walach et al., 2006).

Statistical Analysis

The analyses were conducted in Mplus Version 8.10 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998 -2017) using robust maximum likelihood estimation. Missing data on the profile indicators were handled using full-information maximum likelihood under the missing-at-random assumption, allowing participants with missing data on one indicator to contribute to model estimation. Depressive symptoms and mindfulness were used as continuous profile indicators. The models were estimated using the original, unstandardized BDI-FS and FFA scores, z-standardized values were used only for graphical presentation. Latent profile models were first estimated separately at T1 and T2. At each occasion, solutions containing one to five profiles were examined under different variance-covariance parameterizations (Masyn, 2013; Pastor et al., 2007). Model selection was based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC; Akaike, 1998), Bayesian information criterion (BIC; Schwarz, 1978), sample-size-adjusted BIC (SABIC; Sclove, 1987), and the adjusted Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (aLMR-LRT; Lo et al., 2001), together with profile size, solution stability, parsimony, and substantive interpretability (Masyn, 2013; Nylund et al., 2007; Weller et al., 2020). Lower information-criterion values indicated better relative fit, while a significant aLMR-LRT indicated that a k-profile solution provided better fit than the corresponding k -1 profile solution. Entropy was used to assess classification certainty, with higher values indicating clearer separation between profiles (Celeux & Soromenho, 1996).

After selecting the best-fitting latent profile solution at both measurement occasions, a longitudinal LTA was estimated using the selected number of profiles at T1 and T2. The model estimated the probabilities of remaining in the same profile or transitioning to another profile from T1 to T2. Longitudinal invariance of the profile-specific indicator means was examined to assess whether corresponding profiles had comparable substantive meaning across occasions. An unrestricted model, in which profile-specific means were estimated freely at T1 and T2, was compared with an invariant model in which corresponding means were constrained to equality across time. The equality constraints were evaluated using a joint Wald test and by comparing AIC, BIC, and SABIC values.

To examine covariates while accounting for classification uncertainty, we used the manual maximum likelihood three-step approach (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014; Nylund-Gibson et al., 2014; Vermunt, 2010). Profile assignments and classification-error probabilities were obtained from the invariant model and incorporated into the subsequent covariate models. Age and length of inpatient stay were examined as predictors of profile membership at T1 and of transitions from T1 to T2.

Because the number of attended AAT sessions was measured during the intervention, it was examined only as a predictor of transitions from T1 to T2. Associations between these covariates and initial profile membership and subsequent profile transitions were estimated using multinomial logistic regression and are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals, with one latent profile serving as the reference category.

Discussion

The present study used a person-centered approach to examine heterogeneity in depressive symptoms and mindfulness among adolescents participating in animal-assisted group psychotherapy during inpatient psychosomatic treatment. Three profiles with similar patterns of depressive symptoms and mindfulness were identified at the beginning and end of the five-week period and were characterized by reduced, moderate, and elevated burden. The moderate-burden profile showed greatest change in response to treatment, primarily toward the reduced-burden profile. The reducedand elevated-burden profiles however showed high estimated stability and limited response to treatment. These findings illustrate how longitudinal person-centered analyses can complement mean-level analyses by identifying heterogeneity in patterns of functioning and change within a treatment sample.

The three profiles primarily reflected an ordered pattern of burden: depressive symptoms and mindfulness were inversely patterned across profiles, with higher depressive symptoms accompanied by lower mindfulness and lower depressive symptoms accompanied by higher mindfulness. This inverse pattern aligns with previous research linking greater mindfulness with fewer internalizing symptoms and more adaptive psychological functioning in adolescents (Keng et al., 2011; Royuela-Colomer et al., 2021). Thus, the profiles appeared to represent different levels of overall psychological burden rather than qualitatively distinct combinations of depressive symptoms and mindfulness. Nevertheless, this finding is consistent with previous person-centered research demonstrating heterogeneity in psychological functioning among children and adolescents receiving psychological interventions (Evans et al., 2021; Kennedy et al., 2022; Melero et al., 2022).

The transition findings provide a highly relevant and more differentiated picture of change over the five-week period. Adolescents in the moderate-burden profile showed the greatest variability as illustrated by transitions of 28% of adolescent transitioning to the reduced-burden profile, whereas transitions to the elevated-burden profile were uncommon. Thus, a subgroup of adolescents who initially showed intermediate levels of depressive symptoms and mindfulness moved toward a more favorable pattern characterized by lower depressive symptoms and higher mindfulness. One possible explanation is that adolescents with moderate initial burden may have been particularly amenable to change over a relatively brief treatment period. They had sufficient symptom burden to allow meaningful improvement, while at the same time their difficulties may not have been severe enough to substantially limit their capacity to engage with and benefit from treatment. Adolescents with moderate burden may therefore have had both sufficient scope for improvement and sufficient psychological resources to participate actively in therapeutic processes.

Previous research on adolescent depression has similarly indicated that less chronic depression, better functioning, less hopelessness, and fewer comorbid diagnoses are associated with more favorable short-term treatment outcomes (Curry et al., 2006). These characteristics may have been specifically suitable for engagement in the animal-assisted program.

In contrast, the elevated-burden profile showed pronounced stability: approximately 87% of adolescents initially characterized by high depressive symptoms and low mindfulness remained in this profile at T2. Greater psychological burden may make it more difficult to engage fully with and benefit from therapeutic activities over a relatively short period. Adolescents with greater initial burden may therefore require more intensive, longer, or differently tailored support before changes are large enough to result in a transition to another profile. Consistent with this interpretation, previous research among adolescent psychiatric inpatients has shown that more severe clinical presentations are associated with greater symptom severity at discharge, suggesting that adolescents with higher levels of burden may require longer or more intensive treatment to achieve comparable improvement (Zambrowicz et al., 2019). Nevertheless, remaining in the same profile does not imply an absence of improvement. Changes in depressive symptoms or mindfulness may have been too small to result in a profile transition, or participants may have improved in domains not represented in the present profiles, such as engagement, self-efficacy, social interaction, or general functioning.

Previous research has shown that improvements across different outcome domains do not necessarily occur together during adolescent mental health treatment (Krause et al., 2022). The stability of the elevated-burden profile should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that therapy was ineffective for these adolescents.

At the other end of the spectrum, the reduced-burden profile was highly stable, with all adolescents assigned to this profile at baseline remaining in it at T2. This indicates that adolescents who entered the program with comparatively low depressive symptoms and high mindfulness generally maintained this favorable pattern during the intervention period. These adolescents already showed comparatively low depressive symptoms and high mindfulness, leaving less room for a transition toward a more favorable profile. Taken together, the comparatively greater movement observed among adolescents with moderate initial burden may reflect a combination of sufficient scope for improvement and sufficient psychological resources to engage with and benefit from treatment over the five-week period.

Age and length of inpatient stay were not significantly associated with baseline profile membership or transitions, and the number of attended AAT sessions was not significantly associated with transitions. However, the number of attended sessions captures only the quantity of exposure. Future studies should examine therapeutic alliance, engagement with the animals, treatment expectancies, perceived relevance of the intervention, and active participation as potential predictors of profile membership and transitions.

Implications

The findings have potential implications for understanding heterogeneity among adolescents receiving inpatient treatment that includes animal-assisted group psychotherapy. First, they suggest that adolescents participating in the same treatment context should not be assumed to follow a uniform trajectory. Adolescents with moderate psychological burden may represent a group that is particularly likely to show favorable change over a relatively brief treatment period, whereas those with high depressive symptoms and low mindfulness may require additional preparation, greater individualization, or more intensive support. For example, the pace and complexity of therapeutic activities could be adapted to adolescents' current capacity to engage, sustain attention, and reflect on their experiences. Second, monitoring both symptoms and adaptive psychological resources may provide a more informative picture of change than assessing symptom reduction alone. While symptom measures provide information about adolescents' symptom burden, assessing resources such as mindfulness may offer additional insight into their capacity to cope with psychological difficulties and engage in therapeutic processes. Repeated assessment during treatment could help clinicians identify adolescents who remain in an elevated-burden pattern and consider whether additional or adapted support may be needed.

Strengths and Limitations

Several strengths should be noted. The study examined adolescents in a naturalistic inpatient setting. The use of LTA extended previous AAT research, which has predominantly focused on average pre-post changes or immediate session effects (Brown et al., 2020; Stefanini et al., 2015).

Accounting for classification uncertainty further reduced the risk of treating profile assignments as directly observed categories. The joint consideration of depressive symptoms and mindfulness also provided information about both symptom burden and an adaptive psychological resource targeted by the intervention.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, depressive symptoms and mindfulness were assessed in adolescents participating in AAT and inpatient treatment. The observed profiles and transitions therefore reflect patterns occurring during multimodal inpatient treatment that included animal-assisted group psychotherapy along with other modalities and thus cannot be attributed specifically to AAT. A randomized controlled design comparing treatment effects by modality is needed to establish such evidence. Second, the relatively small sample size may have limited the precision and reliability of parameter estimates, particularly for transitions involving smaller profiles and for covariate analyses. Third, the predominantly female sample (93.5%) limits generalizability, particularly to male adolescents. A similarly female-predominant sample has been reported in comparable adolescent psychiatric AAT research (Rodrigo-Claverol et al., 2023).

Conclusion

In conclusion, adolescents participating in animal-assisted group psychotherapy within multimodal inpatient psychosomatic treatment showed three distinct depression-mindfulness profiles with different patterns of stability and change over the five-week period. Adolescents with moderate initial burden showed the greatest movement toward a more favorable profile characterized by lower depressive symptoms and higher mindfulness, suggesting that this group may represent a particularly responsive subgroup during relatively brief treatment periods. In contrast, the stability of the elevated-burden profile suggests that adolescents with greater symptom burden and fewer psychological resources may require more time or more individualized support. Controlled studies with larger and more diverse samples, repeated assessments, additional indicators, and longer-term follow-up are needed to determine whether these patterns are specifically associated with AAT and to identify processes underlying more and less favorable change.
