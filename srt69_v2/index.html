How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Depression incidence in adolescents is increasing, yet most do not access treatment. Policies requiring parental consent to initiate care may influence adolescent treatment access. In a convergent mixed methods study, we investigated adolescent perspectives toward accessing treatment with and without parental consent (i.e., selfconsent) across four modalities: therapy (in-person and online) and self-guided supports (in-person and online). We explored differences in attitudes amongst sub-samples, demographics, and depression severity. An online survey and qualitative interviews were conducted with 43 adolescents; one sub-sample required parental consent (n = 29), and one self-consented (n = 14) (i.e., parental consent requirements waived). We used a multi-step analytic approach.

Using content analysis, we qualitatively coded a positive, negative, or mixed valence to indicate attitudes toward parental consent or self-consent, and calculated frequencies. Using thematic analysis, themes were informed by selfdetermination theory. Three themes were identified: Factors that Influence Adolescent (1) Autonomy, (2) Competence, and (3) Relatedness. Generally, adolescents viewed self-consent positively, seeing it as preserving their autonomy and decision-making. Self-consent attitudes were consistently positive for self-guided supports and more mixed for therapist-delivered treatments. The 'waived parental consent' sub-sample expressed more positive self-consent attitudes overall; younger adolescents described more negative attitudes and adolescents with less severe depression symptoms described more negative and mixed attitudes. Perspectives on parent consent requirements were not monolithic, and were shaped by developmental needs for self-determination. Results suggest supporting adolescents' competence to navigate treatments; preserving autonomy in decision-making; and strengthening parent-teen communication and relatedness to encourage help-seeking.

Introduction

Depression among adolescents is a critical public health concern. In 2023, 18.1% of all adolescents in the United States (U.S.) experienced a major depressive episode (MDE) in the past year (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA], 2024), with MDE often associated with increased anxiety, substance use, and suicidal ideation in young people (Kalin, 2021; Marino, E. N., et al., 2024). However, fewer than half (44.9%) of U.S. adolescents with major depression accessed any form of treatment in 2024, and untreated depression is associated with a threefold increase in the likelihood of recurrent depressive episodes in adulthood (Reinert et al., 2024; Tharpar et al., 2022). These patterns emphasize the need to better understand and address modifiable barriers that limit adolescents' engagement with mental health services.

Barriers to Adolescents' Access to Treatment

Adolescent-reported barriers to accessing mental health services have been well-documented across numerous reviews. These barriers include low mental health literacy, fear of judgment and stigma associated with seeking help, and a preference for self-reliance (Meredith et al., 2009; Aguirre Velasco et al., 2020; Radez et al., 2021). Additionally, systemic issues such as a shortage of trained mental health professionals that accept insurance, a lack of or unstable insurance coverage, treatment cost, and structural stigma further hinder access to care (Roulston et al., 2022; Mora Ringle et al., 2024; Carbonell et al., 2024). Over the past two decades, there have been innovations in therapeutic service delivery formats (e.g., digital mental health interventions [DMHIs]), with selfguided and teletherapy options emerging as potential solutions to some of these barriers (Lattie et al., 2022). This advancement highlights the importance of further examining underlying systemic barriers to treatment accessibility.

One such barrier that warrants investigation involves legislation or policies that explicitly or inadvertently restrict equitable access to care. A salient example of this barrier is the requirement for parental consent to initiate mental health treatment-an obstacle that can delay or prevent adolescents from initiating care altogether (Schleider et al., 2025).

Parental Permission Requirements and Their Impacts on Adolescent Treatment Access

Questions regarding adolescents' ability to self-consent to mental health services raise complex ethical and practical considerations for in-person and digital (e.g., self-guided online) services alike. On the one hand, parental involvement in treatment can enhance safety, engagement, and adherence, particularly for younger adolescents or those experiencing a crisis (Pine et al., 2024). However, some adolescents frequently cite their parents as a barrier to accessing mental health support, especially LGBTQ+ and adolescents of color, who have expressed concerns about dismissal rooted in identity-based rejection, beliefs about seeking mental health treatment, or both (Samargia et al., 2006; Wilson & Deane, 2012; Mora Ringle et al., 2024; Roulston et al., 2025). When youth anticipate invalidation or negative reactions from parents, they may choose not to disclose their symptoms (Fox et al., 2022), limiting parents' knowledge of their children's symptoms (including suicidal ideation), and in turn their ability to seek support (Jones et al., 2019). Some parents also report difficulty gauging the severity of their child's mental health symptoms, which can further prevent timely access to care (Reardon et al., 2017). Consequently, the question of whether adolescents should be allowed to self-consent to mental health care (versus such care requiring active parental consent) carries significant policy and public health implications.

Across the U.S., state policies regarding parental consent requirements for adolescent mental health care are highly inconsistent. Some state-level laws permit youth as young as 12 to self-consent to mental healthcare (e.g., California, Illinois). However, one-third of U.S. states limit adolescents from independently consenting to any kind of mental health treatment (e.g., Idaho, Georgia; Schleider et al., 2025). In states that always mandate parental consent, treatment use is significantly lower, with only 37.2% of adolescents who experienced a depressive episode in 2024 receiving any treatment compared to 46.7% of adolescents residing in states with policies that allow independent minor consent in some capacity (Schleider et al., 2025).

Notably, restrictive parental consent laws can undermine access to services designed to overcome access barriers, such as DMHIs. Many DMHIs are initially disseminated via research studies, and most research in the U.S. requires adolescents under the age of 18 to obtain parental consent to participate (Liu et al., 2017). Researchers may be able to obtain a waiver of parental consent for minimal risk research through their Institutional Review Boards (IRB), thereby increasing adolescent participation rates and diversifying participant samples in clinical trials (e.g., increased representation of LGBTQ+ and adolescents of color; Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2020; Crane & Broome, 2017; Liu et al., 2017). Studies have demonstrated that adolescents as young as 12 possess the cognitive ability to independently consent to mental health research, per validated tools such as the MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool (MacCAT-T, MacCAT-CR) to determine decision-making capacity (Hein et al., 2014; Marino, L.G., et al., 2024). However, one-off IRB exceptions are insufficient to ensure adolescents' DMHI access outside the context of specific studies.

Adolescents' perspectives on parental permission requirements for mental health supports are rarely explored, with most studies focusing solely on parents' perceived barriers to accessing care for their child (Iskra et al., 2018; Hansen et al., 2021). There is a notable lack of research on how these policies, especially in the context of DMHIs (which may be self-guided by youth, presenting different potential risks than therapist-delivered treatments), are viewed by adolescents. Given that waivers of parental consent for research have been identified to diversify participant samples, it is imperative to investigate whether waiving the parental consent requirement to research or not may lead to a diversity in attitudes regarding self-consent to treatment. The absence of formal investigation into adolescent perspectives on accessing treatment with or without parental consent may hinder the development of public health strategies that align with the needs and preferences of young people. As demonstrated by Schleider and colleagues (2025), adolescents' depression treatment access is low across all U.S. states; thus, understanding their views is essential for developing youth-centered policies that support safe, acceptable, and equitable pathways to care. The purpose of this study was to explore adolescents' perspectives on parental consent requirements, with a focus on self-consent. We also examined how adolescents' perceptions of parental consent requirements might differ based on treatment modality. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1); characterize adolescents' attitudes toward self-consenting to mental health care, descriptively exploring attitudinal differences across demographic groups and depression severity levels; (2); explore differences in attitudes between adolescents whose parents were involved in the study's consent process and those whose parents were not (i.e., adolescents who self-consented to participate in this research); and (3); examine what adolescents perceive as the benefits and risks of accessing different types of mental health support with and without parental consent.

Study Theoretical Framework

The mixed-methods study was grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT), (Deci & Ryan, 2012) which offers a person-centered framework for understanding the psychological mechanisms that influence whether and how adolescents seek and engage with mental health support. According to SDT, three fundamental psychological needs-autonomy (a sense of volition and personal agency), competence (confidence in one's ability to succeed), and relatedness (a sense of meaningful connection to others)-are necessary for fostering intrinsic, self-directed motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2012). When these needs are supported, individuals are more likely to engage in healthpromoting behaviors voluntarily, persist over time, and experience enhanced well-being and behavioral effectiveness (Hui & Tsang, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2011; Simpson, 2021). SDT was selected as the guiding framework because it is developmentally aligned with adolescence: a period in which the need for autonomy intensifies, beliefs of competence vary, and relationships with others are central to decision-making (Hui & Tsang, 2012). SDT also provides a theoretically coherent way to understand how policy contexts shape youths' motivation and behavior by structuring the extent to which institutional contexts support or undermine their basic psychological needs. The use of SDT allowed us to generate actionable insights into how adolescents interpret and navigate treatment consent and access within the intersecting contexts of family relationships, service systems, and legal structures.

Methods

Positionality Statement

The study team comprised ten researchers with varied disciplinary training and lived experiences, including diverse racial/ethnic, gender, and LGBTQ+ identities, and experiences with depression and treatment-seeking. A constructivist paradigm was used throughout the study to account for researcher positionality, minimize bias, and prioritize understanding participants' perspectives rather than assessing a single objective truth (Daly, 2007).

Procedure

The study used a convergent parallel approach, and the methods and findings are reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Data (COREQ) checklist which is included in the supplementary information. (Creswell & Clark, 2011; Tong et al., 2007). All study procedures were approved by Northwestern University's Institutional Review Board (IRB) prior to the study's launch. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling from two participant pools. The first group was drawn from an ongoing NIH-funded longitudinal study that required parental consent for participation; parents of adolescents were recruited via Facebook advertisements, and adolescent eligibility criteria were previously published. Notably, adolescents were eligible if they reported elevated depressive symptoms (i.e., their CDI-II T-score was ≥ 60 for their assigned sex and age) (Pinder et al., 2024). For the present study, previous participants were recruited through email communications sent to parents from February 2024 to April 2024 that expressed an opportunity for their child to participate in an interview study. Interested parents and their children provided electronic consent and assent before the child began the interview.

The second group of participants were recruited on social media from April 2024 to June 2024 via an Instagram advertisement [https://osf.io/683qz/files/4dymk]. The Instagram advertisement included an invitation to participate in a brief online survey and an interview over the Zoom video conferencing platform. Participants were required to meet the following eligibility criteria: aged between 13 and 17 years old (as adolescents younger than 13 are not permitted to create an Instagram account); demonstrated proficiency in English; a reliable internet connection; resided in the United States via IP address; and reported elevated depressive symptoms via a score of ≥ 2 on the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (Kroenke et al., 2003). If individuals did not meet the eligibility criteria, they were directed to a link to free, anonymous, online mental health information and resources that adolescents could access with or without parental involvement. Eligible adolescent participants self-consented to the research before they completed the survey measures. Adolescent participants from both samples were compensated with a $20 Amazon gift card upon completion of study procedures. We obtained a waiver of parental consent from the University IRB to recruit the sample of adolescents via social media, as one of the study's aims was to compare responses among adolescents whose parents consented to the study and adolescents who did not-an aim that would be infeasible without the waiver.

Qualitative Interviews

A total of 43 individual, semi-structured interviews were conducted with adolescents (n = 29 required parental consent; n = 14 were granted a parental consent waiver) from February to July 2024. Interviews were recorded and ranged from 20 to 85 minutes (mean length = 47.01 minutes; SD = 12.03 minutes), depending on the length of adolescent responses. Team members who interacted directly with study participants (AS and AR) received training in qualitative interviewing and worked under the supervision of mixed-methods experts.

Participants had no prior relationship with the authors outside of email communication for recruitment and scheduling purposes and were blinded to the study's purpose. All participants completed their interviews independently. Interviews were transcribed by three sources: the first author, an undergraduate research assistant supervised by the first author, and professional transcription services. Researchers did not engage in memberchecking practices, and the interviewers did not write field notes.

Measures

Demographic Variables

We collected the following demographic data from participants: age, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, gender identity, and geographical region via zip code. Sexual orientation data was only obtained from the subsample of participants that self-consented to the study. Demographic data collected from the subsample of adolescents whose parents consented to the study was parent-reported, while demographic data from the subsample of adolescents who self-consented to the study was adolescent-reported.

Depression Symptom Severity

Depression symptom severity was measured through the full (for the sub-sample that required parental consent) and short form (for the sub-sample that self-consented to this study) of the Children's Depression Inventory-2, as both are reliable and valid measures of adolescent depression symptoms (Kovacs, 2015). Depression symptom severity was self-reported by adolescents from both samples. The Cronbach's alpha for this measure within this sample was statistically acceptable (α = 0.92).

Semi-Structured Interview Guide

The interview guide was developed and refined through consultation with an experienced qualitative researcher (BSL) and piloted with two undergraduate students. Interviews began with rapport-building questions, which prompted participants to share background information about themselves and their communities' views on mental health needs and treatment. The guide then focused on adolescent perspectives regarding parental consent requirements for access to mental health treatments. Participants were asked about seeking mental health support with or without parental consent, irrespective of existent laws or regulations. Four types of support were discussed: in-person, therapist-delivered support (e.g., going to a therapist office); online therapist-delivered support (e.g., telehealth); material self-guided support (e.g., skill-building worksheets or journals); and digital self-guided support (e.g., website or online self-help program). Participants were also asked to consider whether their perspectives were influenced by contextual factors (e.g., their age, experiences of social marginalization, and relationships to their communities). Additional prompts and clarifying questions were used as needed. To view the interview guide, see the supplementary materials.

Data Analytic Plan

Qualitative Coding Procedures

Text data from the transcribed interviews were loaded into MAXQDA (VERBI Software, 2025), a qualitative data analysis software. In MAXQDA, the interview data were coded through individual coded segments -usually a paragraph of text-with each participant having varying amounts of coded segments per interview transcript. We employed a multi-step approach to our qualitative analysis, encompassing content and thematic analytic approaches. First, we used a combination of an inductive and deductive approach, the latter informed by SDT, to our applied thematic analysis to identify codes and themes from interview segments (Guest et al., 2012). As we were interested in how participants naturalistically framed their perspectives on self-consent, we used a content analysis approach to assign positive, negative, or mixed valence codes to participant interview segments that discussed self-consent or parental consent to treatment. The coding team consisted of four authors (AS, TW, AR, SH) and initially two authors (AS and AR) independently read all transcripts to identify preliminary codes and constructed the first version of the codebook. Codebook development was an iterative process with all versions of the codebook available at [https://osf.io/683qz/files/yh2wk]. After reviewing all 43 interview transcripts, no new insights were gleaned from the data, indicating that data saturation had been reached (Guest et al., 2020).

Afterwards, the coding team jointly coded two transcripts and refined the codebook as necessary to result in a reliable codebook. The remaining transcripts were double-coded by two coders on the four-person coding team.

Within each pair, coders independently coded a transcript and then met to resolve any discrepancies, with rates of agreement ranging from 69-79% agreement across teams initially until reaching 100% agreement on their codes across all transcripts. Any discrepancies that could not be resolved within the pair were brought to the full team for consensus.

Mixed-Method Analyses

All quantitative data processing and analyses were conducted using R software (R Core Team, 2025), with the code available at [https://osf.io/683qz/files/osfstorage]. To characterize patterns in adolescents' attitudes, we transformed coded interview segments into quantitative summaries. For each participant, we calculated the number of valence codes applied toward self-consent or parental consent across their interview segments that discussed their attitudes toward each when asked about accessing the four different modalities of treatment. We also tabulated the frequency of qualitative themes identified through thematic coding. Interview segments that could not be assigned to a valence code (e.g., vague or uncertain responses) or that were unrelated to a participant's attitudes towards consent across treatment modalities were excluded from these summaries (n = 165 coded segments [15.2%]; across 42 participants). To visualize our data and descriptively compare across participants, we calculated the proportion of each participant's coded segments that reflected these valence codes (i.e., positive, negative, or mixed attitudes) towards self-consent for each treatment modality, relative to the total number of segments in which self-consent for that modality was discussed.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 43 adolescents (ages 12-17) participated in this study, with a mean age of 15.5. Most (65%, n = 28) participants identified as cisgender girls/women. Regarding racial and ethnic identity, 58% (n = 25) of the participants identified as youth of color (i.e., did not identify as "White non-Hispanic"). For participants that reported sexual orientation, (n = 14), 57% (n = 8) identified as heterosexual, and 43% (n = 6) identified with a minoritized sexual orientation.

Via zip code data, 30 participants resided in states that sometimes or always permitted independent minor consent for mental health treatment, while 13 participants resided in states that mandated caregiver consent to access treatment. Given participants in both studies were screened for eligibility based on elevated depressive symptoms (i.e., PHQ ≥ 2, CDI-2 t-score ≥ 60 for their assigned sex and age), a majority of the participants across both subsamples (67%, n = 29) reported clinically significant depressive symptoms consistent with the cut-off (i.e., CDI-2 sum score ≥ 10). Quantitative Results

Aim 1. Characterize Adolescents' Attitudes Toward Self-Consent to Mental Health Care and Descriptively Explore Attitudinal Differences Across Demographic and Depression Severity Groups.

Throughout their interviews, participants expressed various attitudes towards self-consenting to different forms of care (see Table 2 for detailed results). Support was moderate and more variable for modalities that included a therapist, with 52% of segments containing positive attitudes towards self-consent for in-person therapy and 72.6% toward online therapy. Support was higher for self-guided modalities: 96.8% of segments discussing self-help tools and 87.1% discussing online self-help tools contained positive attitudes towards self-consent. There were 29 segments (30.2% of participants, n = 13) excluded due to insufficient clarity. Three participants discussed attitudes toward only a subset of modalities (two did not report attitudes toward online therapy; and one did not report attitudes toward online self-help).

Participants across sociodemographic groups and depression symptom severity displayed varying attitudes towards seeking care across treatment modalities. No clear patterns in the attitudes from participant interview responses were observed based on gender identity, sexual orientation, or race and ethnicity. Descriptively, younger participants (i.e., youth under 15) proportionally described more negative attitudes towards self-consent, relative to older youth. Participants without clinically elevated depressive symptoms described a greater proportion of negative and mixed attitudes about self-consent relative to those with elevated symptoms; however, this pattern was less clear given the small sample size. Detailed frequencies and visualizations of Aim 1 results are presented in Table 2 and the supplementary materials.

Aim 2: Descriptively Explore Differences in Participant Attitudes Between Adolescents Who Needed Parental Consent to Participate and Those Who Self-consented to the Study.

Compared to the subsample of adolescents whose parents consented to this study, the subsample of adolescents that self-consented to this study descriptively expressed more positive attitudes toward seeking care without parental consent. When discussing self-consent to in-person therapy, the subsample of adolescents whose parents consented to this study descriptively showed greater variability in their attitudes, describing proportionately more mixed and negative attitudes than the subsample of adolescents who self-consented to research. Additionally, adolescents whose parents consented to research more frequently expressed multiple attitudes toward self-consent for in-person therapy within the same interview, relative to their attitudes toward self-consent toward other modalities and compared to the subset of adolescent who self-consented to this study. For online teletherapy delivered by a clinician, attitudes towards self-consent were generally positive across both samples, though adolescents whose parents consented to research were observed to describe more mixed and negative attitudes. Both groups most frequently expressed positive attitudes toward self-consenting to physical self-help tools (e.g., using a therapeutic workbook). When discussing self-consent for online self-help tools, adolescents whose parents consented to research described mostly positive attitudes, with slightly more instances of negative attitudes than the subsample of adolescents who self-consented to the study. Qualitative Results

Aim 3: Adolescent Perceived Benefits and Risks of Accessing Mental Health Care with and without Parental Consent

We organized our major themes under SDT (autonomy, competence, and relatedness), each capturing clusters of factors shaping adolescents' positive, mixed, and/or negative attitudes toward self-consent and parental consent for mental health treatment. Within each theme, several sub-themes reflected more specific constructs identified from the analysis. Table 3 summarizes these findings with illustrative examples.

Theme 1: Factors that Influence Adolescent Autonomy to Access Treatment. When reflecting parental consent requirements for mental health services, adolescents consistently emphasized the importance of feeling like they had control over whether to seek and access care. This theme represented 50.3% of all coded responses (461 of 917) across nearly all participant interviews (n = 41 of 43 participants). This theme was most often identified in discussions of self-guided supports (e.g., DMHIs, worksheets, journals), underscoring how opportunities for independent decision-making shapes adolescents' engagement with mental health resources.

Sub-Theme 1.1 Independence. Independence emerged as a central dimension of adolescents' autonomy, reflected in 75.9% of coded responses (n = 350 of 461) across all participants (n = 41) associated with Theme 1.

When discussing the possibility of self-consent, many adolescents emphasized the importance of being able to seek support on their own terms. One participant explained, "I'll feel a lot better because it's not my parents who are dealing with this problem it's me myself who's dealing with it so I should be the one to choose how I should fix that problem" (age 17, parent consented to participate). Participants frequently described independent consent as aligning with their desire for privacy, personal boundaries, and respect for their developing agency. Some also noted preferences towards specific modalities (e.g., online vs. in-person) and expressed that directing their own care based on their preferences, rather than deferring to caregiver choices, made them more willing to seek help.

Additionally, participants highlighted the importance of having control over when and how they access care, emphasizing autonomy from caregiver schedules and logistical limitations. Approximately 6.8% (n = 23) of coded segments across 29.3% of participants (n = 12) described treatment as generally easier to access when parental permission was not required, particularly for online services. Participants noted that online modalities removed the need for transportation or coordination with parents, thus expanding their ability to seek help on their own terms. As one participant explained:

Online therapy I mean you don't have to drive anywhere or go anywhere in person... but with online therapy you can always just go to another room and put your headphones on or even just go somewhere else, like to a different location (age 16, parent consented to participate).

Autonomy over the timing and modality of care also shaped attitudes toward self-guided tools. A substantial proportion of responses (55.7%, n = 195) across all participants within this sub-theme (n = 41) supported selfconsent and expressed negative attitudes toward parental permission requirements for self-help resources, including both physical materials and online platforms (e.g., websites, apps).

Sub-Theme 1.2 Barriers to Engagement that Undermine Adolescent Autonomy to Access Treatment. This sub-theme was reflected in 25.8% of coded responses (n = 119 of 461) across 82.9% of participants (n = 34 of 41).

One barrier involved administrative rules or legislation, particularly parental permission requirements broadly, that prevented youth from self-consenting to treatment. Nearly 19.3% of coded segments (n = 23) across 38.2% of participants (n = 13) under this sub-theme mentioned that these policies might be especially autonomy-restricting for youth with unsupportive parents (e.g., parents who do not believe their child should receive mental health support).

One participant stated: But I think that just for other people it wouldn't be a good idea because a lot of people's parents aren't as like accepting about the topic of being open about mental health, so it would kind of prevent them from getting the resources that they really need, and it would kind of like hinder the mental health of just a lot of people worldwide (age 15, parent consented to participate).

Financial constraints also emerged in discussions of autonomy-restricting barriers. Some adolescents noted that if treatment was not covered by insurance-in the case of therapy services-or if they lacked the means to independently seek care, their ability to access treatment would be dependent on their parents' financial support.

Additionally, participants cited transportation to in-person services as difficult for youth to access independently due to 41.9% of participants (n = 18) being aged under 16 and lacking a personal vehicle, with several others reporting limited access to public transit.

Theme 2: Factors that Influence Adolescent Competence to Access Treatment. Theme 2 was represented across 95.3% (n = 41 of 43) participants and 37.6% (n = 345 of 917) coded responses. When asked for their perspectives on parental permission requirements for mental health services, nearly all participants described factors that shaped their sense of competence, both in having the perceived skills to navigate mental health systems and feeling effective in managing their own well-being.

Sub-Theme 2.1 Confidentiality and Safety Concerns.

This sub-theme was reflected in 51.9% (n = 179 of 345) of the coded responses across 85.4% of participants (n = 35 of 41) associated with Theme 2. Participants described myriad confidentiality and safety considerations (e.g., data sharing and privacy) that undermined their perceived competence to self-consent to treatment. Under this sub-theme, 74.9% (n = 134) of coded segments across 97.1% of participants (n = 34) expressed positive attitudes toward parental permission requirements-across treatment modalities-grounded in feelings of safety and reassurance. Participant positive attitudes towards parental permission requirements persisted with little variability across modalities apart from physical self-help tools (e.g., workbooks, journals), as many viewed these resources as lower risk. Across modalities, participants mentioned that parents could identify credible providers, ensure secure environments and compensate for adolescents' perceived gaps in maturity or experience. As one participant discussed, "Because I feel like people that are underage, they don't really know how to make good decisions for themselves. I feel like their parent needs to be a part of that." (age 17, self-consented to participate).

Some responses, 25.1% (n = 45) across 62.9% of participants (n = 22) expressed negative attitudes towards self-consent. Participants cited trust in their parents' judgment as a key reason for welcoming parental involvement, particularly toward online modalities to minimize the risk of scams or dangerous encounters, as demonstrated by this participant: I totally think it's good the parents are signing off, especially online with Internet because you never know who you're going to run into on the Internet, so they know what's going to happen, they know who I'm talking to, just so that they feel safe themselves (age 14, parent consented to participate).

These participants perceived their parents as more able to identify trustworthy treatments, determine the best options, and provide maturity and knowledge to support decisions surrounding consenting to treatment.

Sub-Theme 2.2 Case-dependent Attitudes in Adolescent Perceived

Readiness. This sub-theme was reflected in 48.1% (n = 166 of 345) of coded responses across 92.6% of participants (n = 38 of 41) within Theme 2. Under this sub-theme, 36.1% of coded segments (n = 60) across 71.1% of participants (n = 27) provided responses reflecting "mixed" (i.e., ambivalent) perspectives on whether parental permission should be required. Additionally, 18.7% of coded segments (n = 31) across 50% of participants (n = 19) provided responses on whether adolescents should be able to self-consent. 45.2% of coded responses (n = 75) across 57.9% of participants (n = 22) provided responses reflecting a neutral perspective on parental permission requirements or self-consent. Many participants' attitudes were shaped by factors that influence their underlying readiness to navigate these treatment decisions independently and believed consent requirements should be flexible and reflective of one's capacity, rather than supporting a universal consent requirement. Some participants noted that older adolescents would be more ready to self-consent due to greater life experience and exposure to decision-making. One participant explained: I feel sixteen-year-olds have some sense of maturity and ground of the response. But.. not fully because they're still under age... I also feel like a sixteen-year-old would know how to respond to a situation better than, say, a thirteen-year-old (age 15, parent consented to participate).

The same participant further discussed how the severity of mental health symptoms may affect an individual's competence to independently consent: "But it also depends on how severe their mental health is. Like in the example you mentioned depression, but there's multiple levels of it." In scenarios where adolescents view themselves or others as well-equipped to seek treatments without parental consent, they may feel an increased sense of competence to consent. However, some responses highlighted adolescents' concerns about younger or more vulnerable youth, who may lack the resources or maturity to engage meaningfully in treatment without caregiver support.

Theme 3: Factors that Influence Adolescent Relatedness in Accessing Treatment. Theme 3 was identified in 86% (n = 37 of 43) of participant interviews and 14.5% (n = 133 of 917) of coded responses.

Participants described how relationships with parents or their community shaped their sense of connection and belonging when considering whether to access mental health support on their own. Sub-Theme 3.1 Stigma. This sub-theme was reflected in 31.6% (n = 42 of 133) of the coded responses across 51.3% of participants (n = 19 of 37) associated with Theme 3. Stigma surrounding mental health limited participants' ability to meet their need for belonging and connectedness within their community. Participants (42.1%, n = 8) directly referenced parents' stigmatizing views in these coded interview segments (23.8%, n = 10), which included dismissing the legitimacy of mental health concerns, belittling individuals with mental health issues, or framing help-seeking as shameful. As one participant shared:

A lot of the older generation, they kind of invalidate how you feel and like what's going on. So... having them know that you need to access this, it's kind of like a weakness and it's something that you don't want to admit to your parents (age 15, self-consented to participate).

A few participants referenced their parents or family members' mistrust of therapist-delivered treatment across modalities and how they hold differing views in coping with mental health difficulties, "Because I feel like they don't really believe in therapy and talking to other people about your problems. Especially someone online, I feel like they'd think it's a scam." (age 17, self-consented to participate).

Another participant shared: I feel like a big part of it is just how it's viewed as kind of silly around my community and the people I know. Like, it isn't a real issue. So even though that I believe that that isn't true, that it is important, it's still something I would rather not have other people know that I need assistance for (age 16, self-consented to participate).

These participants cited feeling disconnected in their familial relationships in those moments and led to a lack of willingness to want parental involvement in treatment decisions or to seek support from others about their mental health.

Sub-Theme 3.2 Comfort in Conversations About Mental Health.

This sub-theme was reflected in 69.2% of the coded responses (n = 91 of 133) across 95% of participants (n = 35 of 37) associated with Theme 3. Participants discussed whether they feel accepted or judged by others when approaching conversations regarding mental health, as it is essential to have these conversations to initiate mental health treatment with parental permission. Under this sub-theme, participant responses skewed more towards discomfort, with negative responses representing 59.4% of all coded responses (n = 54) across 60% of participants (n = 21). Particularly, participants reported feeling awkward, embarrassed, or dismissed when engaging in conversations about mental health, primarily with their parents.

Discomfort had led to ruptures in the some of the parent-child relationships and made participants less likely to want to initiate these conversations with them, as was demonstrated within this participant's response: ..if I could just do it on my own without my parents' consent, I wouldn't have to worry about like, okay, I have to go talk to them now, ask them for it.

And like that anxiety that arises and the fear, and panic, and just all the emotions that come with that, because you don't know how they're going to respond. (age 17, self-consented to participate).

Some participants expressed discomfort over their parents' reactions and felt unprepared to answer follow-up questions about initiating treatment, "I'd be hesitant to talk to my parents about this because I wouldn't want to bother them or have them ask a ton of questions about why they need permission or what the website is." (age 17, self-consented to participate). The existing relationship that some participants had with their parents influenced their comfort in initiating these conversations as well, "... I well I feel like it's just awkward like to talk with your parents about that..., or at least like what relationship where I have established with them that just wouldn't feel comfortable with that" (age 16, parent consented to participate). Their need for relatedness is undermined when they experience intense discomfort, such as fear or anxiety, regarding these conversations about seeking mental health treatment.

Responses that indicated participant comfort in engaging in conversations surrounding mental health accounted for 27.5% (n = 25) of coded segments and 48.6% of participants (n = 17) under this sub-theme. Within these responses in the sub-theme, participants pointed to an open communication style with their parents and positive experiences with disclosure of mental health concerns to promote a preference for establishing parental involvement in their treatment. These participants shared that their comfort stemmed from their parents previously supporting their needs to access mental health treatment, "I'm in therapy online. It used to be in-person...if I were to be doing any of this, my family would know about it...they wouldn't have any issue...and it wouldn't really hinder me receiving that care." (age 16, self-consented to participate). A few of these participants also felt that their parents' support in mental health treatment would extend to different modalities of treatment, as demonstrated by this one participant, "in the past, I wanted therapy...so we did it online, but it definitely transfers. For many adolescents, feeling supported and understood by their parents fosters a sense of security and connection, which could increase their comfort in involving parents in their decisions in seeking mental health resources.

Discussion

This study examined adolescents' perspectives on engaging with different modalities of mental health treatment with and without parental consent, with a focus on their attitudes towards self-consent. Adolescents were drawn from two samples: those whose parents consented to their participation (n = 29) and those that self-consented to participate in the study (n = 14). Responses were descriptively compared across demographic groups and depression symptom severity as well as between those who required parental consent to participate and those who were granted the waiver. We avoided conducting formal hypothesis testing, which can be misleading in very small samples where statistical power is low (Lakens, 2022).

Across both samples, adolescents expressed generally positive attitudes towards self-consenting to lowintensity treatment options (e.g., self-help tools). Further, across all treatment modalities, adolescents expressed positive attitudes towards self-consent more commonly than mixed or negative attitudes. No clear attitudinal patterns emerged across race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, or gender identity; younger participants seemed to express negative attitudes at a higher frequency than older participants; and those with subclinical depressive symptoms expressed more negative and mixed attitudes than those with clinically elevated symptoms. Although most adolescents expressed greater proportions of positive sentiments than negative or mixed sentiments about selfconsent, many adolescents also described complex or ambivalent feelings (i.e., mixed attitudes) about navigating care independently, citing both benefits and challenges. Taken together, these patterns illustrate both consistency and notable complexity in adolescents' perspectives on self-consenting to mental health care. These findings suggest that while adolescents value and want opportunities to access mental health care independently, their attitudes are shaped by broader developmental, relational, and structural contexts.

When considering parental consent requirements for mental health treatment, nearly all participants (95.3%, n = 41) and half of the coded interview segments (50.3%, n = 461) in this study discussed the importance of preserving adolescent autonomy-highlighting the need for privacy, convenience, and control over their decisions.

Many adolescents described self-consent as empowering, contextualizing the high frequency of positive attitudes (versus mixed and negative attitudes) towards self-consent. Adolescents' attitudes towards self-consenting to online and self-guided mental health supports, which were generally positive, largely centered around these supports' capacity to preserve adolescent autonomy (e.g., the ability to initiate treatment per their own schedules would make these supports easier to access and reduce dependence on their parents' availability). At the same time, adolescents identified several factors (e.g., lack of transportation, policies mandating parental consent) that limited their autonomy to initiate treatment-especially in-person therapy, which aligned with the descriptive pattern of greater proportions of mixed and negative attitudes towards self-consent to this modality.

Additionally, adolescents' perceptions of their own competence to access mental health treatment played a significant role in how they evaluated parental consent requirements. Many expressed uncertainties regarding their own and others' ability to manage treatment decisions safely and effectively without parental support. These adolescents emphasized that parents often had more experience evaluating the safety and credibility of treatment options and could help ensure that treatments were trustworthy. This viewpoint was more common among younger participants, who endorsed a greater proportion of negative attitudes toward self-consent relative to older adolescents; yet across the sample a negative attitude towards self-consent was considered a minority viewpoint.

While many adolescents may desire autonomy when initiating mental health care, their sense of competence often depends on the presence of guidance in unfamiliar or complex situations. Adolescents who question their competence to ensure their own safety in the context of mental health care may be less likely to independently seek support. A few participants that reported mixed attitudes towards self-consent simultaneously acknowledged concerns about low competency to navigate treatment independently, while also expressing a desire for autonomy and general support for self-consent. These responses emphasize variability between and within adolescents' attitudes towards self-consent, and the ways in which variability may be grounded in their perceptions of competence and autonomy.

For some adolescents, their sense of relatedness to parents and other adults shaped how they perceived parental consent requirements. Some adolescents reported feeling pressured to hide their mental health concerns from those in their community who perpetuate stigma, which led to a dampened sense of belonging. The adolescents experiencing this stigma explained that they would be less willing to seek treatment if parental permission were required. Accordingly, adolescents across both samples generally endorsed more positive attitudes toward selfconsenting to self-guided resources, an option that did not require discussing concerns with a parent or interacting with a provider. Overall, adolescents who felt connected, understood, and supported by their communities (i.e., those higher in relatedness) were more likely to view parental permission as appropriate or necessary when seeking mental health care, while those who experienced strained relationships or stigma against help-seeking were likely to view parental consent requirements as a barrier to accessing care. These findings align with previous literature that suggests the strength of the parent-child relationship influences adolescent disclosure of their mental health struggles (Pickens et al., 2025). Thus, emphasizing the importance of strengthening adolescents' relationships with supportive adults in their lives, and expanding pathways towards adolescent self-consent in circumstances with unsupportive parents to remove barriers to treatment access.

Limitations

The findings of this study must be understood in the context of its limitations. Regional representation of our participant sample was uneven and should not be interpreted as generalizable to all U.S. adolescents. In addition, there was an underrepresentation of adolescents who identified as boys/men (n = 13, 30.2%), and this gender imbalance may have limited the range of perspectives on help-seeking and parental involvement, given research suggesting gender differences in mental health experiences and willingness to use mental health services (Chandra & Minkovitz, 2006). The small sample size limited our ability, beyond descriptive patterns, to examine endorsement of attitudes towards self-consent across subgroups. Thus, the descriptive patterns observed of younger participants and participants without clinically elevated depression sharing greater proportions of mixed or negative attitudes should be interpreted with caution; these patterns are slight and would benefit from a larger sample. Second, information was collected differently across both samples. For the sub-sample of adolescents whose parents consented to research, adolescents' demographics were reported by parents, while for the sub-sample that selfconsented to research, demographics were self-reported. Notably, sexual orientation data was only available for the sub-sample of adolescents that self-consented, as parents were not asked to report this information. Additionally, depression symptom severity was collected through the long and short form of the CDI-2. This approach was intentional to reduce burden on adolescents recruited online, but it limited comparability across samples.

Conclusion

This study was among the first to explore adolescents' attitudes towards parental consent requirements for mental health treatment, particularly self-consenting to mental health treatment. Across demographic groups, depression symptom severity levels, and samples (self-consenting versus parent-consenting to the study), findings suggested that adolescents reported generally positive attitudes towards self-consent across the four treatment modalities. Notably, there were zero negative attitudes towards self-consenting to physical self-help tools observed.

At the same time, nuance emerged in adolescents' views: more negative and mixed attitudes were observed towards self-consent to in-person therapy in relation to other modalities, younger adolescents endorsed slightly more negative attitudes towards self-consent, and adolescents with sub-clinical symptoms expressed more negative and mixed attitudes towards self-consent. Future studies should investigate whether the attitude trends and the perspectives that adolescents raised would be consistent in a larger, national sample. Collectively, results highlight needs for parent consent policies that can improve adolescent access to care; protect adolescents' safety per their developmental, relational, and social contexts; and preserve adolescents' autonomy in help-seeking when appropriate-particularly for certain self-guided and online support, and when competence to navigate resources is high. Active policies in other countries may inform steps towards these goals within the U.S. For example, Gillick Competence (GC), a reflexive policy used in the United Kingdom's healthcare system, allows youth to self-consent to treatment at younger ages than the observed age of competence (16 years), if they demonstrate sufficient knowledge and understanding of the treatment being offered (Loades et al., 2025). Moving forward, populationlevel investigations that integrate perspectives from more adolescents, parents, bioethicists, and policymakers can inform the utility and implementation of new parental consent policies that may improve adolescents' access to needed care.
