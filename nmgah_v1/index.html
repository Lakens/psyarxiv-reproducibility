What we found

We found 11 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

In a dynamic world, events unfold and disappear, often providing only fleeting or incomplete sensory evidence. Information from another sense may help us interpret these encounters, but what determines how strongly it influences what we later remember? Across two audiovisual working-memory experiments, we tested whether auditory context exerts greater influence when visual information is reconstructed less reliably. Participants viewed a face morphed along a feminine-masculine continuum for 100ms and, after a 1,900-ms retention interval, reproduced it using a continuous face-morph response wheel. On cue trials, a feminine-or masculine-sounding voice was presented 1,000ms before face onset (prediction) or 900ms after face offset (postdiction). In Experiment 1 (N=84), voice cues shifted reconstructions toward the cued gender in both tasks. Across observers, weaker visual sensitivity was associated with greater auditory influence; and within observers, faces reconstructed less consistently without cues showed stronger bias. Experiment 2 (N=170) extended this relationship to externally imposed visual uncertainty through resolution reduction and spatial occlusion. Across both manipulations and cue timings, less reliable reconstruction was associated with greater auditory cue influence.

Despite this consistency in uncertainty-sensitive auditory influence, the way voices influenced reconstructions depended on whether they were heard before or after the face. Together, these findings suggest a recurring functional principle of crossmodal inference: The less reliably visual information can be reconstructed, the more strongly contextual information influences the resulting judgment. Differences in how auditory bias was expressed suggest prediction and postdiction may address the same inferential challenge through different underlying processes.

Public Significance Statement

When visual information is uncertain, people rely more heavily on information from other senses. We found that this principle also shapes how people reconstruct past visual experiences:

Voices had a stronger influence on reconstructions of briefly seen faces when visual evidence was less reliable, even when the voices were heard after the faces had disappeared. This relationship held across naturally occurring differences in visual reliability, arising from both individual differences between observers and variability in the stimuli, as well as uncertainty introduced by external factors, such as blurred images (as when viewing someone through a foggy window) or obscured facial features (as when wearing a mask or sunglasses). However, the way voices influenced reconstructions depended on whether they were heard before or after the face. Our results reveal that the same adaptive principle of integrating information across senses to resolve uncertainty may operate through distinct underlying processes.

Uncertainty Weighting as a Time-Invariant Principle of Crossmodal Inference

Recognizing someone can feel effortless, as though all we need to do is look. Yet even when someone is before our eyes, recognition can depend on information beyond what we see.

More than a century ago, Marcel Proust described a mysterious visitor approaching through the darkness of a garden. His face was difficult to distinguish, but his hosts could eventually recognize him by the sound of his voice. Even "seeing some one we know," Proust wrote, is "to some extent an intellectual process" (Proust, 1913 (Proust, /1922, "Overture" section).

The reliability of visual evidence can vary across encounters, as can our ability to reconstruct it later. Suppose now you catch a fleeting glimpse of the visitor between the branches, his features softened by dim light and portions of his face concealed by foliage. Even two observers witnessing the same moment may differ in how reliably they can later reconstruct what they saw. A voice heard before the visitor comes into view, or only after he disappears from sight, might shape what we later report seeing. The question, then, is whether auditory information has greater influence on what we later reconstruct when visual information is less reliable. Probabilistic accounts of perception offer a principled framework for understanding how observers draw conclusions from uncertain sensory evidence. These accounts propose that sensory evidence is interpreted in light of prior expectations, with their relative influence depending on the precision of the available information (De Lange et al., 2018; Kersten et al., 2004; Knill & Pouget, 2004). When sensory evidence is reliable, perceptual estimates are constrained more strongly by the signal itself; when it is uncertain, prior expectations can exert greater influence. This trade-off offers a principled explanation for the systematic perceptual biases observed in visual motion perception (Stocker & Simoncelli, 2006; Weiss et al., 2002).

Contextual influences also arise in audition, in which lexical knowledge can bias the identification of ambiguous speech sounds (Ganong, 1980). Importantly, reliability-sensitive weighting extends across sensory modalities. In visual-haptic and audiovisual tasks, the contributions of sensory signals depend on their relative reliability (Alais & Burr, 2004; Ernst & Banks, 2002). Cue weighting can also adjust dynamically as sensory reliability changes, as demonstrated with visual and vestibular information (Fetsch et al., 2009). Together, these findings suggest that flexible information weighting is a recurring feature of perceptual inference rather than a phenomenon confined to one sensory modality or task.

The multisensory nature of perception makes this flexibility particularly consequential.

Information conveyed through one sense can alter the interpretation of information conveyed through another, as demonstrated by visual influences on speech perception (Mcgurk & MacDonald, 1976) and auditory influences on face-gender judgments (Smith et al., 2007).

However, many foundational demonstrations of multisensory integration involve signals presented together or in close temporal proximity, allowing them to contribute to an immediate perceptual estimate. In the natural environment, potentially relevant information need not be available simultaneously, nor must it provide another measurement of precisely the same physical property. A voice, for example, may convey gender-related information relevant to interpreting a face without constituting a second visual measurement of that face. Can the influence of auditory context remain sensitive to visual uncertainty when a face must be reconstructed from memory, whether the voice is heard before or after the visual event?

These two temporal arrangements pose distinct inferential challenges. Context encountered before an event can generate expectations that guide the interpretation of incoming sensory evidence (De Lange et al., 2018). Information arriving afterward cannot play that anticipatory role, but may still influence how an earlier event is remembered, interpreted, or reconstructed. Postdictive effects demonstrate that later sensory information can influence reports of earlier events (Shimojo, 2014), including in audiovisual perception (Stiles et al., 2018).

In visual working memory, subsequently encountered visual information can systematically bias memory reports for simple features (Fukuda et al., 2022) and faces (Teoh et al., 2024). Related work has shown that visual items presented before and after a memory target can bias its subsequent report, with stronger influence from more reliable items (Zerr et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that the consequences of uncertainty-dependent inference may extend beyond the moment of sensory encoding. The critical question, then, is not simply whether later information can bias a report, but whether uncertainty in the earlier visual representation predicts how strongly a newly presented auditory cue biases its reconstruction.

In the current study, we examined whether uncertainty-sensitive contextual influence extends beyond initial sensory encoding and whether its expression across predictive and postdictive contexts points to similar or distinct underlying processes. We investigated these questions in two audiovisual experiments in which gender-related auditory cues were presented either before or after briefly displayed faces. Rather than making categorical gender judgments, participants reconstructed the appearance of each face using a continuous response wheel spanning the face-gender continuum. This approach allowed us to measure not simply whether auditory information changed a judgment, but the magnitude and direction of its influence on visual reconstruction. Importantly, continuous responses allowed us to quantify how consistently visual information was reconstructed in the absence of auditory cues, providing a basis for examining whether less reliable reconstruction was associated with greater auditory influence.

In Experiment 1, we asked whether auditory influence was stronger in observers with poorer visual sensitivity and for faces that individual observers reconstructed less consistently. If auditory influence varies as a function of visual uncertainty, cue-related biases should increase as reconstruction becomes less reliable. Crucially, if this relationship extends beyond the anticipatory interpretation of incoming sensory information, it should also emerge when the auditory cue arrives only after the face has disappeared. Whereas Experiment 1 indexed uncertainty through naturally occurring differences in reconstruction performance, Experiment 2 examined uncertainty imposed on the external sensory evidence. We reduced facial resolution or occluded diagnostic facial regions to test whether the same relationship persists across qualitatively different forms of information loss.

Together, these experiments tested the scope of uncertainty-sensitive crossmodal influence across different sources of visual uncertainty and temporal arrangements of contextual information. Auditory influence increased when visual information was reconstructed less reliably, whether uncertainty reflected differences across observers, variations in reconstruction consistency within observers, or degradation imposed on the external sensory evidence. This relationship emerged during both prediction and postdiction, suggesting a domain-general functional principle of uncertainty-sensitive contextual influence across sources of uncertainty and time. At the same time, prediction and postdiction revealed a dissociation in the expression of auditory influence, which varied more strongly along the face continuum when the voice followed rather than preceded the face. Thus, the persistence of a common relationship with uncertainty may reflect different processes through which contextual information shapes reconstruction depending on when that information becomes available.

Experiment 1 Method

Sample Size Justification and Participants

Experiment 1 was designed to examine uncertainty-sensitive auditory cue weighting during prediction and postdiction and to assess the consistency of individual differences across temporal contexts. Specifically, we examined uncertainty-cue relationships within each task and cross-context correlations in visual sensitivity and overall auditory cue bias. An a priori power analysis using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) indicated that 84 analyzable participants would provide 80% power to detect a moderate correlation (r = .30, two-tailed α = .05). Participants were recruited on a rolling weekly basis until this target analytic sample was achieved.

Eighty-five undergraduate students from the University of Toronto Mississauga received course credit for participating. All reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. Data from one participant were excluded because they failed to complete the second task, yielding the targeted final sample of 84 participants (66 women, 13 men, 5 unreported; age range = 17-24 years; M = 18.5). All participants provided informed consent under a protocol approved by the University of Toronto Research Ethics Board.

Apparatus

The tasks were programmed in MATLAB R2023b (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA) using Psychtoolbox extensions and presented on a laboratory computer. Stimuli were displayed on an LCD monitor (24-inch display) at a fixed viewing distance of 60 cm. Auditory stimuli were presented through over-ear headphones at a comfortable, fixed listening level. Testing was conducted in a quiet laboratory setting, with the experimenter present to monitor task compliance and address technical issues.

Stimuli

Visual stimuli. The stimulus set comprised 10 identity exemplars, each parameterized at 25 evenly spaced morph levels ranging from 1 (most feminine) to 25 (most masculine). Thus, each stimulus was defined by an identity exemplar and a morph level, with identity held constant while facial gender varied parametrically across the continuum. The faces were generated using FaceGen Modeller Pro, Version 3.18 (Singular Inversions, n.d.). For each identity exemplar, 25 face morphs were created by systematically varying the gender parameter from the most feminine to the most masculine setting while holding the remaining facial characteristics constant. This procedure produced a continuous feminine-to-masculine morph continuum separately for each of the 10 identities. We refer to the morph level of the presented face as the Face Gender Index (Face GI). Face GI ranged from 1 (most feminine) to 25 (most masculine), with higher values indicating a more masculine face.

To map the linear gender continuum onto the circular response space, the most feminine morph (Face GI = 1) and most masculine morph (Face GI = 25) were positioned at opposite points on the response wheel. The intermediate morphs were spaced equidistantly between these endpoints along one half of the circle. This mapping was then mirrored across the other half of the circle. Consequently, moving around the wheel from the most feminine face progressively increased facial masculinity until the most masculine face was reached, after which the sequence reversed and progressively returned toward the most feminine face.

On each trial, a target face was presented for 100 ms. Following a retention interval, participants reconstructed the target face by navigating the circular response wheel until the displayed face best matched their memory of the target. To prevent participants from associating fixed spatial locations with particular gender values while preserving the ordered relationship among morph levels, the angular position of the response wheel was randomly rotated on each trial so that absolute spatial position did not consistently correspond to a particular morph level.

We refer to the morph level corresponding to the participant's final selection as the Response Gender Index (Response GI), which ranged from 1 (most feminine) to 25 (most masculine).

Auditory stimuli.

The auditory stimuli consisted of 20 synthesized voice recordings (10 masculine-sounding, 10 feminine-sounding) producing the identical 500-ms spoken utterance ("Ready!"). First, 10 masculine voice exemplars were generated via text-to-speech using ElevenLabs with uniform synthesis parameters. We then manipulated the pitch and formant structure of these original recordings to generate 10 corresponding feminine versions. All 20 recordings were root-mean-square (RMS) normalized to 0.018. This approach minimized unintended acoustic differences across stimuli, ensuring that any influence of auditory gender on face reconstruction could not be attributed to variations in lexical content, duration, or overall intensity. During the experiment, these 20 voices were randomly paired with visual face stimuli across trials. This randomization prevented learned stimulus-specific associations and ensured that auditory gender served as an independent source of information.

Procedure

After providing informed consent to the procedures approved by the Research Ethics Board of the University of Toronto, participants completed two experimental tasks, each consisting of 300 trials: A predictive-cue task and a postdictive-cue task. The order of the two tasks was counterbalanced across participants, such that half completed the predictive task first and half completed the postdictive task first. The full experiment lasted approximately 2 hours, including instructions and practice trials.

On each trial, participants viewed a face for 100 ms. Face stimuli were presented at a resolution of 400 × 400 pixels on a 24-inch monitor (1920 × 1080 resolution), subtending approximately 10.54° × 10.54° of visual angle at a viewing distance of 60 cm. After a 1900-ms retention interval, they recreated the face they had perceived using a continuous circular response wheel. The face stimuli varied continuously along a morph dimension that captured variation in facial gender; however, participants were never instructed to categorize the faces by gender.

Instead, they were instructed to reproduce the face as accurately as possible. The response wheel therefore provided a continuous measure of the reconstructed face, allowing auditory information to be detected as a graded shift in face reconstruction rather than as a change in a discrete gender judgment.

In 66.7% of trials in each task, an auditory gender cue (nominal duration: 500 ms) was presented either before or after the face. In the predictive-cue task, auditory cue onset preceded face onset by approximately 1,000 ms, with the cue ending approximately 500 ms before the face appeared. In the postdictive-cue task, the auditory cue began approximately 900 ms after face offset and ended approximately 1,400 ms after face offset. These separations were intended to distinguish the face and voice as temporally separate events, rather than simultaneous components of a single audiovisual event (Vroomen & Keetels, 2010). The interval between face offset and the onset of the response wheel was approximately 1,900 ms in both tasks, although the auditory cue preceded face presentation during prediction and occurred during retention in postdiction (Figure 1).

Each task included congruent-cue, incongruent-cue, ambiguous-face cue, and no-cue trials (Table 1). On congruent-cue trials (50% of all trials), the auditory gender cue was consistent with the gender category of the presented face (GI 14-25 for masculine cues; GI 1-12 for feminine cues). On incongruent-cue trials (10%), the auditory and visual gender information conflicted (GI 1-12 for masculine cues; GI 14-25 for feminine cues). On a further 6.7% of trials, an auditory cue accompanied the gender-midpoint face (GI 13). Incongruent trials were intentionally infrequent and prevented the auditory cue from being a perfectly reliable indicator of facial gender. No-cue trials (33.3%) established baseline performance in the absence of auditory information, against which auditory cue-induced shifts could be quantified.

Faces at the midpoint of the gender continuum (GI 13) appeared on 10% of all trials, including 6.7% of trials with an auditory cue and 3.3% without one. These trials allowed us to test whether auditory information exerted a greater influence when the visual face provided less diagnostic evidence about gender. Because participants continuously reconstructed the face rather than explicitly reported the gender, the same response measure could capture both the baseline representation of a gender-ambiguous face and any systematic shift toward the gender conveyed by the auditory cue.

Together, the design manipulated the gender of the face stimulus, the presence of the auditory information and if present, the temporal availability of auditory information (pre or post the presentation of the face). The predictive-versus postdictive-cue manipulation tested whether auditory information could influence face reconstruction before versus after visual encoding, while the gender-ambiguous face trials tested whether auditory influence increased when gendercategory evidence was less diagnostic. Finally, the no-cue trials provided an auditoryindependent measure of each participant's visual face representations, enabling subsequent analyses of whether individual differences in visual response reliability predicted susceptibility to auditory cueing.

3. Does auditory bias vary with reconstruction uncertainty within observers?

An association across observers does not establish whether auditory influence also varies with the reliability of individual face reconstructions within the same observer. To examine this possibility, we quantified visual uncertainty from response variability across repeated presentations of each Face GI on no-cue trials, with greater variability indicating less consistent reconstruction. Only trials surviving the response-time exclusion criteria described above were considered (98.02% of all trials). For each participant, a face GI was included only if at least two no-cue responses were available to estimate its response SD and at least two observations spanning two distinct cue conditions were available to estimate its auditory cue coefficient.

These criteria yielded an average of 22.37 eligible GIs per participant in prediction (SD = 1.45) and 22.45 in postdiction (SD = 1.34), out of 25 possible GIs. For each participant, eligible face GIs were ranked from lowest to highest response variability (SD rank), with identical SD values assigned average ranks. Auditory cue bias was then estimated separately for each GI using the following linear regression: Response GI = β₀ + βCue(Cue) + ε. For each participant, the Pearson correlation between SD rank and the corresponding Cue coefficients across GIs indexed the relationship between reconstruction uncertainty and auditory bias. Participant-level correlation coefficients were tested against zero using two-tailed one-sample t-tests.

Interim Discussion

Experiment 1 demonstrated that auditory gender cues systematically biased continuous face reconstructions toward the cued gender during both prediction and postdiction. Because participants reproduced faces rather than explicitly categorizing their gender, these effects emerged as graded shifts in reconstruction responses, extending previous demonstrations of face-voice gender interference in categorization tasks (Huestegge & Raettig, 2020). More importantly, auditory influence varied systematically with the quality of visual information.

Across participants, weaker visual sensitivity was associated with greater auditory cue bias; within participants, faces that elicited more variable reconstructions in the absence of a cue also showed stronger auditory influence. The latter finding is particularly informative because it suggests that cue weighting was not solely a stable characteristic of individual observers, but also varied across visual representations that the same observer reconstructed with differing degrees of consistency. Together, these relationships are consistent with broader principles of uncertainty-sensitive multisensory integration (Alais & Burr, 2004; Rohe & Noppeney, 2015), while extending the question beyond the integration of temporally proximate sensory signals.

During prediction, auditory information could establish expectations before visual encoding.

During postdiction, however, the auditory cue arrived 900 ms after the face had disappeared.

Whereas previous work on audiovisual postdiction has focused on influences of later sensory events within rapid audiovisual sequences (e.g., Stiles et al., 2018; see also Stiles et al., 2022), the present findings show that uncertainty-dependent auditory influence is also evident during the continuous reconstruction of an earlier visual event.

The shared sensitivity to uncertainty does not, however, establish that prediction and postdiction rely on the same processes. Whereas individual differences in visual sensitivity were strongly correlated across tasks, auditory cue weighting showed weak correspondence, although limited measurement reliability prevents a definitive interpretation of this contrast. Moreover, mixed-effects analyses revealed a modest difference in how auditory cue influence varied along the face-gender continuum, with a steeper positive linear relationship during postdiction than prediction. Together, these findings suggest that temporal context may shape how auditory influence is expressed, even when both contexts exhibit the same broad relationship between reconstruction reliability and auditory bias. However, neither finding establishes distinct underlying mechanisms or identifies the processing stage at which auditory information acts. In particular, a cue presented after visual encoding may influence memory, reconstruction, or response selection without necessarily altering the earlier perceptual experience itself (Luu & Stocker, 2021). This distinction is important because research on visual-history effects has demonstrated that biases expressed in behavioural responses can emerge or become stronger during postperceptual processing (Bliss et al., 2017; Fritsche et al., 2017).

Experiment 2

Suppose the visitor in Proust's garden had approached through a thick veil of fog, his features difficult to resolve, or that overhanging branches had concealed parts of his face from view. Both circumstances would limit the available visual evidence, but in different ways. In the former scenario, facial information remains available but indistinct; in the latter one, potentially important features are missing altogether. How reliably an encounter can be reconstructed therefore depends not only on the observer's own internal processing, but also on the quality and completeness of the sensory evidence available in the environment.

Experiment 1 demonstrated that auditory cue weighting increased when visual reconstructions were less reliable, both between observers and across faces within individual observers. Experiment 2 asked whether this relationship would persist when uncertainty was introduced directly through changes to the visual evidence. We manipulated facial information in two qualitatively different ways: 1) reducing spatial resolution, which made fine-grained facial features increasingly difficult to distinguish, and 2) occluding facial regions, which removed potentially diagnostic information from specific locations. If auditory influence is systematically related to reconstruction reliability rather than to a particular source of visual uncertainty, we would expect cue-related biases to increase with empirical reconstruction uncertainty under both forms of degradation. Comparing these relationships would further allow us to examine whether the strength of uncertainty-sensitive auditory influence depended on the type of visual information loss.

We also examined whether auditory bias depended on which visual alternatives participants encountered during reconstruction. Because participants searched through a continuous range of faces before selecting their final response, one possibility was that auditory information influenced reconstruction only when the search failed to display a sufficiently close match to the original target. Alternatively, auditory bias might remain detectable even when a target-like face had appeared during the response process. To distinguish these possibilities, we continuously recorded participants' response-wheel trajectories and classified trials according to whether the search visibly encountered the target region (Pass) or did not (No-Pass). Under a strict fallback account, auditory bias should be restricted to No-Pass trials. Detecting auditory bias on Pass trials, by contrast, would demonstrate that its influence is not confined to reconstructions in which an appropriate visual alternative was never displayed.

Method

Transparency and Openness

The same as Experiment 1.

Sample Size Justification and Participants

Sample size was determined a priori at the level of temporal context. A power analysis conducted in G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) indicated that 84 participants would provide 80% power to detect a correlation of r = .30 at α = .05 (two-tailed).

We therefore targeted a minimum of 84 analyzable participants for each temporal context (prediction and postdiction), with participants divided approximately equally between the resolution-reduction and occlusion conditions, yielding a minimum target sample of 168 participants. This recruitment target was chosen to provide a well-powered sample within each temporal context while permitting complementary tests of the uncertainty-cue-weighting relationship within each form of visual degradation and across degradation types.

Experiment 2 included 171 undergraduate students from the University of Toronto Mississauga who received course credit for participating. One participant assigned to the prediction-resolution condition was excluded because of a technical issue, yielding a final sample of 170 participants (139 women, 24 men, 7 chose not to respond; age range = 17-40 years, M = 19.30; age data available for 169 participants). Of the final sample, 86 participants completed the prediction task and 84 completed the postdiction task, with approximately equal numbers assigned to the resolution-reduction and occlusion conditions. All reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. All participants provided informed consent under a protocol approved by the University of Toronto Research Ethics Board.

Apparatus

The same as Experiment 1.

Stimuli

Visual stimuli. The same as Experiment 1 with additional changes to the face stimuli detailed below.

Resolution.

Faces were presented at one of three spatial resolutions: High (100%), medium (20%), or low (10%). To manipulate spatial resolution while maintaining a constant physical stimulus size, images were first downsampled to the corresponding proportion of their original dimensions and then rescaled to the fixed 400 × 400-pixel display size using nearestneighbor interpolation. Thus, the resolution manipulation progressively reduced the availability of fine-grained visual information while holding image size, position, and overall stimulus format constant.

Occlusion.

We further manipulated the availability of facial information by selectively obscuring different facial regions. Faces were presented under one of three conditions: no occlusion, mouth occlusion, or eye occlusion. In the no-occlusion condition, the entire face remained visible. In the mouth-occlusion condition, a black rectangular mask (200 × 80 pixels) covered the lower facial region, obscuring the mouth while leaving the eyes and surrounding facial information visible. In the eye-occlusion condition, an identically sized mask covered the upper facial region, obscuring the eyes while leaving the mouth and surrounding facial information visible. The distinction between these regions was theoretically relevant because previous research has identified the eyes as particularly informative for face recognition, relative to the mouth (Haig, 1986; McKelvie, 1976). By varying the position of identically sized masks, we manipulated which facial information remained available while holding overall stimulus size, position, and exposure duration constant.

Auditory stimuli. The same as Experiment 1.

General Discussion

The central finding of these experiments is not simply that auditory context can bias the reconstruction of a previously encountered face, but that the extent of this influence is systematically related to how reliably the original visual information can be reconstructed.

Across two experiments, gender-related information conveyed by voices shifted continuous face reconstructions more strongly when visual reconstruction was less reliable, whether this reflected naturally occurring differences in reconstruction performance or degradation imposed on the visual evidence. Importantly, this relationship persisted when the auditory information preceded the face and when it arrived after the face had disappeared. This convergence is particularly informative because the voice provided contextual information rather than another direct measurement of the face's visual appearance, and because the two temporal arrangements offered different opportunities for auditory influence. Together, the findings suggest that sensitivity to sensory reliability may be a recurring feature of reconstructive inference, extending beyond the integration of concurrent sensory signals without necessarily requiring identical underlying processes.

Beyond conventional sensory integration

Many influential studies of multisensory integration have examined sensory signals presented together that provide separate estimates of a shared physical property. The present experiments depart from this arrangement in two important respects. First, the auditory and visual information were encountered as temporally distinct events. Second, the voice conveyed gender-related information relevant to interpreting a face, rather than providing another sensory estimate of its physical features. The importance of contextual information for visual interpretation is well established. In visual object recognition, surrounding scene information can help constrain the interpretation of ambiguous objects (Oliva & Torralba, 2007). Across senses, semantic relationships can influence audiovisual processing (Doehrmann & Naumer, 2008), and facial and vocal information can interact in person perception (Campanella & Belin, 2007). More directly, previous research has demonstrated that auditory pitch cues presented concurrently with faces can influence face-gender judgments (Smith et al., 2007). The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating that gender-related information conveyed by a voice can systematically bias continuous reconstructions of previously encountered faces, even when the voice becomes available only after the face has disappeared.

The distinction between sensory estimates and meaningful contextual information is also important for understanding how auditory influence varied with visual uncertainty. In conventional multisensory integration, the contribution of each signal depends on its relative reliability, with more reliable signals generally exerting greater influence on the resulting judgment (Alais & Burr, 2004; Ernst & Banks, 2002). These contributions can change as sensory reliability varies (Fetsch et al., 2009). Inference also depends on whether the signals are inferred to originate from a common source or cause (Körding et al., 2007). Together, these findings establish that crossmodal influence is conditional on the reliability and inferred relationship of sensory signals. The present experiments reveal a related sensitivity to uncertainty even when the voice provides gender-related context rather than another sensory estimate of the face. Auditory influence was greater when visual information was reconstructed less reliably. Thus, uncertaintysensitive crossmodal influence extends beyond combining concurrent measurements of a shared physical property to the reconstruction of earlier events in light of additional, meaningfully related information.

The Persistence of uncertainty-sensitive contextual influence

An important test of this broader relationship is whether it persists when the source of visual uncertainty changes. Experiment 1 established that auditory influence varied with naturally occurring differences in reconstruction performance, both across observers with different levels of visual sensitivity and within observers across faces reconstructed with different degrees of consistency. Yet uncertainty is not determined solely by how reliably an observer processes or reconstructs information. It can also arise from limitations in the sensory evidence available in the environment itself. A face glimpsed through fog may be difficult to distinguish, while one partially hidden by branches may leave important features entirely unseen.

Experiment 2 therefore asked whether uncertainty-sensitive auditory influence would also emerge when the quality of the external visual evidence was directly manipulated.

Critically, the two forms of visual degradation posed qualitatively different challenges to reconstruction. Reducing spatial resolution preserved information across the face while making fine-grained features more difficult to distinguish (Costen et al., 1996; Goffaux et al., 2005), whereas spatial occlusion removed information from particular facial regions altogether (Chen et al., 2009; Kellman & Shipley, 1991). Research using spatially restricted views of faces has also demonstrated that different facial regions contribute differentially to judgments of gender, expression, and identity (Gosselin & Schyns, 2001). These manipulations can be understood as presenting different inferential problems. Sharpening an estimate when visual information remains available but indistinct, versus filling in an estimate when potentially diagnostic information is completely missing.

Despite these different forms of information loss, the relationship between reconstruction uncertainty and auditory influence persisted. Across both resolution reduction and occlusion, auditory context generally exerted greater influence when visual reconstruction was less reliable, with converging support from participant-level and mixed-effects analyses. Neither approach detected a significant difference in this relationship between degradation types. Together with Experiment 1, these findings suggest that uncertainty-sensitive contextual influence is not tied to a particular source of unreliable visual information. The source of uncertainty changed, yet its systematic relationship with auditory influence remained.

The persistence of auditory bias across these conditions makes it important to consider whether its influence was restricted to a particular circumstance of the reconstruction process.

Participants searched through a continuous range of faces before selecting their final response, raising the possibility that auditory information biased reconstruction only when this search failed to encounter a sufficiently close match to the original face. Alternatively, auditory influence might remain detectable even when a target-like face had appeared among the visual alternatives encountered during reconstruction. To distinguish these possibilities, we recorded participants' continuous response-wheel trajectories in Experiment 2, allowing us to determine whether the target region had been visibly encountered during each reconstruction. Importantly, auditory bias remained evident even on trials in which participants encountered a close visual match, with particularly clear evidence during prediction. Although encountering a target-like face does not establish that participants recognized it as the appropriate response, this finding challenges a strict fallback explanation in which auditory influence arises exclusively because the response search never presents a suitable visual alternative. Auditory bias was therefore not confined to reconstructions in which a target-like face had never been displayed.

Perhaps most strikingly, the relationship between visual reliability and auditory influence persisted even when the voice followed the face. An auditory cue presented beforehand can establish expectations that guide the processing of upcoming visual information, whereas a cue arriving after the face has disappeared cannot have shaped initial encoding through those anticipatory expectations. Yet even when the auditory cue arrived approximately 900 ms after the visual event, its influence on subsequent reconstruction remained systematically related to visual uncertainty. This consistency suggests that uncertainty-sensitive contextual influence is not limited to the anticipatory interpretation of incoming sensory information, although it does not imply that identical processes operate across temporal contexts. Across differences in both the source of visual uncertainty and the timing of contextual information, the same functional relationship persisted: The less reliably visual information could be reconstructed, the more strongly auditory context influenced the resulting judgment.

Prediction and postdiction: Shared sensitivity with distinct processes?

The persistence of uncertainty-sensitive auditory influence across prediction and postdiction is particularly informative because the timing of contextual information creates different opportunities for shaping reconstruction. Information encountered before an event can establish expectations that guide the processing of incoming sensory evidence (Fischer et al., 2025; Summerfield & Egner, 2009), consistent with findings that expectations can sharpen visual representations (Kok et al., 2012) and shape visual representations even before stimulus onset (Kok et al., 2013 (Kok et al., , 2017)). Evidence from research on comprehension and recall similarly illustrates the importance of contextual timing. Information provided before a passage can be substantially more beneficial than the same information provided afterward (Bransford & Johnson, 1972). Yet in a dynamic environment, information relevant to interpreting an event may become available only after it has occurred. Previous research on postdiction has demonstrated that later sensory information can influence reports of earlier events (Eagleman & Sejnowski, 2000; Shimojo, 2014; Stiles et al., 2018). Incorporating later context into subsequent reconstruction may therefore offer another means of making sense of incomplete evidence.

Prediction and postdiction may thus serve complementary functions in interpreting uncertain events, even if the processes through which contextual information exerts its influence differ.

Although auditory influence remained sensitive to reconstruction reliability across temporal contexts, its expression was not identical in every respect. In Experiment 1, a significant Cue × Face GI × Temporal Context interaction revealed that auditory influence varied differently along the face-gender continuum during prediction and postdiction. Specifically, within the fitted model, auditory influence increased more steeply with facial masculinity during postdiction than prediction. This interaction concerns how auditory bias varied with the characteristics of the presented face, rather than a difference in its relationship with reconstruction uncertainty. Nevertheless, it demonstrates that the influence of auditory context depended not only on the original visual stimulus, but also on when the auditory information became available. Individual-differences analyses offered another potentially informative contrast. Visual sensitivity was strongly correlated across prediction and postdiction, whereas participants' auditory cue-weighting estimates showed only a weak, nonsignificant correlation.

However, the limited reliability of these estimates prevents us from determining whether the weaker correspondence reflects genuinely different individual susceptibilities to auditory influence across temporal contexts.

These findings suggest that a recurring relationship with sensory reliability need not entail an identical pattern of contextual influence. Predictive auditory cues may establish expectations that shape the processing of upcoming visual information, whereas postdictive cues must exert their influence after the original sensory encounter, potentially affecting retained information, its retrieval, or subsequent judgment formation. The present findings do not establish which processes are responsible, nor whether prediction and postdiction rely on distinct mechanisms; importantly, influences on subsequent reconstruction should not be equated with changes to the earlier perceptual experience (White, 2026). What they reveal is a notable combination of commonality and difference: auditory influence remained systematically related to reconstruction reliability across temporal contexts, even as its expression varied along the face-gender continuum. A broader account of reconstructive inference must therefore explain both why contextual influence is sensitive to the reliability of the original evidence and how its expression may depend on when that context becomes available.

Reconstructing the past: Flexibility and bias

The ability to draw on information beyond an original sensory encounter can be both a resource and a source of bias. When retained sensory evidence is imprecise, prior knowledge and expectations may help constrain an otherwise uncertain reconstruction (Hemmer & Steyvers, 2009; Huttenlocher et al., 1991 Huttenlocher et al., , 2000)). Research on visual working memory has likewise demonstrated that reports of individual items can be biased toward ensemble statistics of the surrounding display (Brady & Alvarez, 2011). But information that helps make sense of a past event need not faithfully reflect what was originally encountered. Studies of post-event information have demonstrated that subsequently encountered verbal material can bias later memory reports about an earlier visual event (Loftus et al., 1978). The same constructive flexibility that may support judgments from incomplete evidence can therefore introduce systematic biases, reflecting a longstanding tension between the adaptive functions of memory and its susceptibility to distortion (Schacter, 1999; Schacter et al., 2011).

The present findings add an important dimension to this tension by demonstrating the influence of context conveyed through a separate sensory event. Auditory information systematically shifted reconstructions of previously encountered faces, with greater influence when visual information was reconstructed less reliably. During postdiction, moreover, the auditory context responsible for these shifts became available only after the face had disappeared. Although the findings do not establish that the maintained visual representation itself was altered, or that auditory influence necessarily reduced reconstruction accuracy, they illustrate how the adaptive potential of reconstructive memory and its susceptibility to bias may arise from the same flexibility. Incorporating additional information may help resolve uncertainty, but it also means that what is ultimately reported about a past event need not be a direct readout of the original sensory evidence. Instead, reconstruction can reflect both what was originally encountered and information acquired beyond that encounter.

Constraints on generality

The present experiments involved predominantly young adult university students with self-reported normal hearing and normal or corrected-to-normal vision, using parametrically generated faces, synthesized voices, and a continuous visual reconstruction task. Although we varied the sources of visual uncertainty and the timing of auditory information, the contextual cues consistently conveyed gender-related information relevant to the dimension being reconstructed. We expect the relationship between reconstruction reliability and contextual influence to extend beyond the sampled population to other adults who can distinguish the relevant sensory attributes, as well as beyond these particular stimuli and procedures when contextual information bears meaningfully on the attribute being reconstructed. This expectation rests on the proposed importance of sensory reliability and contextual relevance, rather than on characteristics specific to our sample or task. However, greater uncertainty need not increase susceptibility to all forms of context indiscriminately. The reliability, informativeness, and relevance of contextual cues may also determine how strongly they influence reconstruction, and whether that influence improves or compromises the fidelity of the resulting judgment.

Consistent with the importance of contextual relevance, previous audiovisual research has demonstrated that semantic congruence between signals can influence multisensory performance (Laurienti et al., 2004). An especially revealing extension would be to reverse the direction of crossmodal influence, examining whether visual context shapes auditory reconstruction more strongly when the original auditory evidence is less reliable. If the same relationship emerged in both directions, it would strengthen the case for a shared principle of reconstructive inference extending across sensory domains. An asymmetry would be equally informative, suggesting that the expression of this principle also depends on the particular sensory systems involved and the relationships between them.

Conclusion

In Proust's shadowed garden, the sound of a voice brought meaning to an encounter that vision alone could not resolve. Everyday situations like this pose a broader challenge for perception, as sensory evidence varies in clarity and completeness, and contextual information may arrive before or after an event. Uncertainty-sensitive weighting may provide a recurring, potentially adaptive principle of crossmodal inference that helps observers navigate this variability, even as reliance on context can bias what is ultimately reconstructed. Yet this common functional principle need not imply identical underlying processes. Differences in how auditory influence was expressed across prediction and postdiction raise the possibility that these temporal arrangements address a shared inferential challenge through distinct processes.
