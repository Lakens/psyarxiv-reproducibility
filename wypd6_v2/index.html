Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Self-other evaluations play a crucial role in understanding psychiatric disorders and improving collaboration between service users and providers. However, experimental paradigms of such relational processes tend to have low reliability and ecological validity. Quantitative analyses can validate the task's measurement properties while qualitative analyses can empower service users to provide novel insights about task development and interpretation. In this study, we use both quantitative and qualitative analyses to develop the "Helping Task" based on the iterated prisoner's dilemma. In this task, help-seeking participants knowingly engaged in an asymmetric relationship with a computerized helpprovider, in which both parties invested effort (points). Initial qualitative feedback from 17 individuals (15 'neurotypical', 2 having lived experience of relational difficulties) was used to refine key task properties (like task instructions, interface labels, and general navigation). Subsequent thematic analyses revealed that these participants interpreted helping exchanges through expectations about reciprocity, moral obligations, and underlying mental intentions, mirroring descriptions of real helping relationships. Moreover, quantitative analyses of 30 online participants validated the task's sensitivity to helper behaviour and the within-session reliability of mean trust, decision satisfaction, and intended effort. Finally, computational analyses revealed probabilistic states in which trust, decision satisfaction, and effort were jointly lower or higher, indicating that self-other evaluations were linked to willingness to invest in the relationship. These findings support the reliability and validity of the Helping Task and pave the way for computational studies of self-other evaluations in helping relationships.

Introduction

Help-seeking involves translating a privately perceived need into something interpersonal, reaching out to another person for support (Rickwood et al., 2005). Help-seeking is a highstakes form of social interaction. In many cases, help-seeking presents an asymmetric dynamic; the help-seeker, e.g. an ill patient, enters a more vulnerable position and bears more risk. Hence many who need help do not engage with it, or do so unsuccessfully. This may be due to lack of information, but also due to embarrassment or mistrust (Elshaikh et al., 2023; Gulliver et al., 2010; Huff et al., 2024). Despite much qualitative, quantitative, and more recently computational (Hula et al., 2021; Guennouni et al., 2026) research, our understanding of trusting in asymmetric relationships is still insufficient to help those most in need.

The psychology of trust in helping relationships

Help-seeking can be demanding; to receive help, individuals must expose the reasons that drove them to seek it, risking psychological pain or even exploitation. Help-seeking is relational and frequently asymmetrical. Professionals may gain experience and develop expertise through care, while carrying duties of competent attention, appropriate communication, and importantly for cases of serious mental illness, relational continuity (Engstrom et al., 2023).

When help-seekers are supported or let down, which happens often in helping professions, they form judgements, such as whether their helper is caring and trustworthy (Lerch et al., 2024). Making such judgements involves 'mentalizing': the capacity to understand oneself and others in terms of intentional mental states (Luyten et al., 2020). Mentalizing is a transdiagnostic, transtheoretical capacity, and different disorders show different imbalances in it (Luyten et al., 2020). It affects whether people initiate and maintain engagement with helpers. Is another person's input trustworthy and relevant to the help-seeker? This attitude, known as epistemic trust, depends in part on feeling understood (Fonagy & Allison, 2014). The people who find relationships hardest are often the ones for whom help-seeking matters most. Understanding mentalizing processes when someone is helped or let down is therefore important, but these interpersonal dynamics remain underexplored (Zavlis et al., 2025).

The promise of computational psychiatry

Computational psychiatry is a promising discipline which has recently sought to elucidate trust. It combines neuroscience and economics, which together reveal brain processes behind behaviour and decision-making (Rilling and Sanfey, 2011). It clarifies how people learn, pursue reward, and trade off exploration against exploitation (Daw et al., 2006). These processes become more complex in social settings, where a person controls only part of the outcome. Repeated economic games, such as the multi-round Investor-Trustee task (MRITT), show how cooperation is built and how it breaks down or is repaired (Guennouni et al., 2026; King-Casas et al., 2005). These 'games' recruit mentalizing and phenomena such as self-serving bias (Moutoussis, Trujillo-Barreto, et al., 2014). Mentalizing can be rigorously modelled as inference (Moutoussis, Trujillo-Barreto, et al., 2014). A prior expectation about a person is combined with their observed behaviour to produce a judgement about what they are like. Inference is also applied to the self, so that a person infers their own worth in much the same way as they infer the trustworthiness of others (Moutoussis, Fearon, et al., 2014; Will et al., 2017).

The drawbacks of rigorous measurement

Creating rigorous tasks is only half the work; the settings used to elicit mental states matter just as much for ecological validity and real-world relevance. Neuroeconomic tasks have been critiqued to lack ecological validity and in some cases also reliability (Colman, 2003; Schilbach et al., 2013; Zavlis et al., 2025). Some rely on deception (the deceive-thendebrief approach), which soon becomes known and hence fair game to 'game'. At present, neurocomputational studies of trust, especially those about being helped, remain limited in ecological validity and in relevance to the people who use services. Little work has integrated qualitative and quantitative data of mentalizing and trust in interpersonal dynamics. Pioneering work from our team (Lau et al., 2024) asked whether and why people trusted professionals with their social evaluation learning task (SELT), but the task was neither interactive nor designed to account for the greater vulnerability of help-seekers.

Contribution of the current study

To address these issues and examine self-and other-evaluations during help-seeking interactions, we developed a new task based on the iterated prisoner's dilemma, but involving a realistic framing and assessments of subjective experience. Rigorous understanding of trust as emotion and decision-making is very challenging, as it involves setting, hot (emotion-rich) cognition and decision-making under risk. Indeed, individual differences shape expectations, the environment, and even what 'trusting a helper' means. One's definition of trust can be a discursive representation of one's track record of betrayal (Driver, 2015; Trotsuk, 2016). In help-seeking situations, the relationship presupposes some interdependence that entails a degree of risk and freedom to disappoint the other's expectations (i.e., wasting time/effort; Gambetta, 1998). From this interdependence, we may expect persistent efforts to build and maintain the trusting relationship, to increase the expectation of something good to happen from it, but also to minimize the risk of being exploited (Guennouni et al., 2026; Guennouni et al., 2024; Rousseau et al, 1998).

To select the aspects of this complex landscape which would make the 'Helping Task' most relevant and valid, in recent work we consulted with Experts by Experience of help-seeking for Functional Neurological Disorder (FND). They largely corroborated the above. They advised us to ask participants to rate their trust in their helper, and their satisfaction with themselves, as well as their decisions, within the 'Helping Task'. We proceeded to collect and model ratings of trust and decision satisfaction, alongside qualitative structured recall narratives.

Study aims

We first examined whether detailed qualitative study of our novel 'Helping Task' would not only make it meaningful and user-friendly to potential participants, but would help uncover modes of experience and thinking of help-seekers that have not as yet informed rigorous laboratory studies of trust. Second, we examined whether attending to the epistemic standpoint of individuals with lived experience of relationship difficulties (LE) would substantially enrich this understanding. Third, we assessed whether the key outputs of the task, that is, decisions to invest effort, decision satisfaction and trust, were sensitive to helper behaviour and reliable within a session. Finally, we explored whether patterns in help-seekers' evaluations and behaviour could be described using hidden Markov models. To address these aims, we conducted: 1. thematic analyses of interviews with selfreported 'neurotypical' participants to elucidate key elements of trust, such as the object (who's being trusted), domain (what they're being trusted for), and sources of trust (i.e., traditions, rational reasons, etc. ; Trotsuk, 2016). 2. Qualitative comparison with two LEs. 3. Sensitivity and validity (responsivity) analyses of the key task measures, and correlation analyses with personality and mentalizing questionnaires. 4. An online study of a further 30 participants, and computational modelling using the approach of Guennouni and coworkers (Guennouni et al., 2026).

Methods

Participants and design

We conducted two studies with the Helping Task. In Study 1, we used qualitative methods to elucidate key experiential perspectives in help-seeking and develop the task (n = 17). We first administered the task to a convenience sample, which we call '(neuro)typical participants' (TP, n = 15), on the basis that they did not report serious relationship difficulties and reported being physically and mentally healthy. They played the task in full on their computers or laptops and then discussed it over Zoom. The first five provided task feedback; the remaining 10 completed interviews. Nine interview recordings were available for thematic analysis because one recording was lost. After implementing the changes based on the task feedback of the TP group, we sought feedback from two individuals with lived experience (LE) of relationship difficulties, accompanied by full playthroughs of the task.

In Study 2, we recruited a total of 30 participants from the Prolific Academic platform (prolific.com). The participants gave written informed consent and were paid at an hourly rate approved by the Ethics Committee (110% of the minimum wage at the time of study). All participants were adults aged 18-65 who could speak, read and write English fluently; self-reported demographics are given in Table 1.

Experimental and Self-Reported Measures

Helping Task

Before beginning the experiment, participants were informed that they would be paired with a computerized helper agent, and asked to interact with it as if it were a real person, while role-playing as the help-seeker. They first went through a set of instructions explaining the task, and were given time to explore the asymmetrical reward consequences of the various combinations of effort invested between the helper and themselves.

The Helping Task is described in Figure 1 and was adapted from a probabilistic iterated prisoner's dilemma used in a prior study (Moutoussis et al., 2025). Participants were asked to immerse themselves in the role of seeking help from a helper (known to be computerized). Helpers and help-seekers alike could 'invest' effort into the helping endeavour, but the help-seekers were more vulnerable to costs if let down. Intended effort was translated probabilistically into realised contributions (Figure 1B). Having been shown these probabilities, participants were shown only the realised contributions and resulting returns (Figure 1C) of the two partners, and could only infer the helper's intended effort. Finally, each trial was followed by ratings of the self (satisfaction with their decision) and the other (trust in the helper). Intended effort had four response levels (1-4); decision satisfaction and trust were each rated on six-point scales (1-6). In the caring/uncaring version, participants engaged with three helpers (Figure 1D), each for 8-12 'visits' or trials. Blocks ended pseudo-randomly to reduce end-anticipation effects. In the online study, the helper sequence was randomly assigned as caringuncaring-caring (CUC) or caring-uncaring-uncaring (CUU).

The (neuro)typical participants in Study 1 encountered randomly acting helpers. The two LE participants and the online participants encountered caring and uncaring helper policies. These task versions were distinguished in the analyses described below.

Questionnaire measures

Personality traits were assessed using 30 items from the Personality Inventory for DSM-5 Brief Form Plus Modified (PID5BF+M; Bach et al., 2020), with psychoticism items omitted. The five assessed domains were negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition and anankastia, with six items per domain. Items were scored from 0 to 3; domain scores were item means, with higher scores indicating greater trait endorsement.

Mentalizing was assessed using the 12-item Mentalization Scale (MentS-12; Stefana et al., 2024). Four items assessed each of self-related mentalizing, other-related mentalizing and motivation to mentalize. Responses were scored from 1 to 5. Negatively keyed items were reversed before calculating subscale and total item means; higher scores indicated greater self-reported mentalizing capacity. Scoring of other measures administered in the wider battery is reported in the supporting information.

Task and questionnaire records were linked by exact participant identifiers for 27 of the 30 online participants. Three task records and three questionnaire responses could not be linked and were excluded only from task-questionnaire analyses. No imputation or matching by row order was used.

Qualitative Analyses

Task feedback

We were interested in whether participants could put themselves 'in the shoes' of the real situation, or whether they treated it as an impersonal game; whether they found the task easy to understand, immersive (i.e., in the sense that they were genuinely compelled to think about their helper as caring, helpful, etc.), engaging (i.e., whether they could fully attend to the needs of the situation), and ecologically valid (both the interpersonal dynamics and helper behaviours). We were also interested in specific elements of the task design and initial instructions and practice rounds, e.g., whether bar graphs were the best way to illustrate the noisy effort distributions. Participants were given space to discuss any additional concerns.

Participant interviews

We asked participants to note important feelings and moments with the helper. After each block of trials, they recalled these notes and evaluated the helper. The interviews were semi-structured, guided flexibly by questions (e.g., "What do you think of the helper?"), and designed to understand the participant's subjective experience, for example, how participants made sense of events like being let down or helped. Interviews were intended to elicit participants' own thoughts and feelings without suggesting interpretations. Interviews were conducted over Zoom, and participants consented to recording for transcription.

Thematic analysis

We implemented the thematic analysis framework (an inductive, semantic, and realist approach) described by Braun and Clarke (2006; Table 2). Participant interviews were first transcribed and reviewed repeatedly to develop an in-depth understanding of the data. The initial round of coding was completed, annotating semantic features regarding the participant's experience. After coding, the entire list was compiled to identify patterns and similarities and review the coded extracts in each group, considering whether they formed a coherent pattern. Where codes could be separated into distinct themes, or candidate themes were closely related, they were reassessed as separate themes or subthemes of a broader pattern. This refining process drew on Patton's (1990) dual criteria for judging categories (internal homogeneity and external heterogeneity). Then, the themes were assessed in relation to the entire interview to evaluate their coherence with the data as a whole, and to search again for themes that may have been missed earlier. After identifying what each theme is about and what it entails (the boundaries of that theme), we returned to the collated data extracts to develop a coherent and internally consistent account with the accompanying narrative. These processes were repeated iteratively by our team, alongside considerable discussion and deliberation to identify and categorize the potential patterns.

Quantitative Analyses

Data preparation

Analyses were exploratory. We studied trial and block identifiers, response ranges, missingness and recorded payoffs. Behavioural analyses used same-trial intended choices, realised contributions and ratings; next-trial effort alignment was specific to the hidden Markov models (HMM). No online participants or trials were excluded. Participant means, resampling and clustered standard errors were used to account for repeated observations. All reported confidence intervals are pointwise 95% intervals.

Sensitivity to helper behaviour

We compared participant-level mean trust, satisfaction and intended effort between caring and uncaring helpers in block 3 using bootstrap confidence intervals, two-sided permutation tests and Holm correction across the three outcomes. Realised helper effort served as a separate manipulation check. Sensitivity analyses adjusted for block-2 scores using regression with HC3 standard errors. Paired comparisons between blocks 1 and 2 used bootstrap intervals and sign-flip tests, recognising that helper behaviour was confounded with order and experience. Trial-level models examined associations of trust and satisfaction with realised helper effort, realised participant effort, intended choice and normalised trial position, using participant-block intercepts and participant-clustered standard errors with small-sample correction and t-based inference. We also recorded how often trust and satisfaction differed and modelled their difference to test whether their associations with helper and participant effort differed, applying Holm correction across these two contrasts.

Within-session reliability

Within-session reliability was evaluated for mean trust, satisfaction and effort across blocks 1-2, weighting blocks equally, and for each block-1-minus-block-2 difference. We restricted these scores to the common blocks to hold policy exposure constant across participants. Across 500 random partitions, trials were divided into approximately balanced halves within each participant, block and early/late phase. We correlated corresponding half-scores across participants, applied the Spearman-Brown correction and reported the median coefficient. Intervals used 2,000 participant bootstrap samples. A fixed odd-even partition provided a sensitivity analysis with 10,000 participant bootstrap samples. Thus, each modelled observation paired the post-outcome evaluations with the subsequent trial's effort selection. The first decision in each block was therefore dropped.

We estimated 2 to 5 states by maximum likelihood via the expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm. Candidate solutions were evaluated using BIC, stability across initialisations, participant coverage within each state, and posterior decoding certainty. Fits in which any state had an emission standard deviation below 0.15 were classified as degenerate. A candidate model was retained when at least 3 of 15 initialisations were non-degenerate and each state included data from at least two participants; among retained models, we selected the model with the lowest Bayesian information criterion (Schwarz, 1978).

We then compared a baseline model with fixed transition probabilities against inputoutput HMMs in which transitions varied with properties of the preceding exchange: helper effort, helper minus participant effort, and participant minus helper points.

For the best-fitting covariate model, we used global (Viterbi) decoding to identify each participant's most likely sequence of states within each block. States are numbered from lowest to highest expected trust; each state was characterised by distributions of the satisfaction and trust ratings on one trial and the effort chosen on the next.

Participant interviews

Typical participants

Analysis of the '(neuro)typical participants' interviews (N = 9; one TP's interview data was lost) identified four themes: relational dynamics, strategies for managing the interaction, evaluations of the helper, and their emotional responses to exchanges (Table S11). They explained their own and the helpers' behaviour not only with respect to trial-to-trial outcomes but also through beliefs about intentions, mutual obligations, and the likely future of the relationship.

The theme 'relational dynamics' was characterized by expectations held for helpseekers and helpers, and their potential influence over each other. Participants often described normative beliefs about what a helper should do and what they themselves were entitled or obliged to do when asking for help. These expectations were sometimes learned within the task (i.e., "I realized that I probably have to play nice") or drawn from more general beliefs about deservingness and reciprocity in help-seeking (i.e., "I'm the one asking for help [...] I don't feel entitled to anyone's help"). In some cases, these expectations were used as a reference to explain why they felt the helper was a good or bad person. Participants also considered how their own actions might shape the helper's subsequent behaviour, for example by coaxing the helper.

Participants described their decision making with several strategic orientations. We identified five main types that differed in tolerance to loss and degree of self-and otherregard: coaxing, investing conservatively, barely reciprocating, not cooperating, and freeriding. Participants used three characteristics to evaluate the helper's 'helpfulness' or trustworthiness: cooperation, consistency, and conscientiousness. These accounts distinguished observed behaviour from inferred motivation: participants could value a helper's good intentions even when outcomes were less than optimal, while repeated high investment was not always interpreted as genuine cooperation. Participants described how these evaluations related to their expectations of the helper and their emotions about the exchanges, their helpers, or themselves.

Finally, emotional responses often reflected a violation of helper expectations. For example, disappointment followed unmet expectations of effort; betrayal involved the perceived violation of an assumed cooperative agreement. Frustration arose when participants felt unable to act according to their preferred level of investment or unable to change an unresponsive partner. Regret and pride were self-evaluative, often concerning whether one had acted 'appropriately' toward a helper. Together, these accounts suggest that participants evaluated outcomes in relation to expectations about themselves and their partners' intentions and actions.

Individuals with lived experience of relational difficulties

We asked whether perspectives from individuals with lived experience of relationship difficulties would enrich our understanding of the experience and thinking of help-seekers. Key themes from these interviews are illustrated in Table S12. Both LE participants drew inferences about the helper from small changes in contributions. They described being reminded of previous relationship problems, making character or moral evaluations, or turning inwards on themselves and their self-worth.

One participant maintained trust in a generally cooperative helper after a single, modest reduction in contribution, while becoming dissatisfied with her own decision to trust: "I felt like I'm a bit stupid because I just trust other people too easily". She described feeling betrayed, while also considering whether the helper "had a bad day" and maintaining that they were still reliable. She also distinguished between the two caring helpers: one appeared to be insufficiently caring because they did not seem engaged or invested in her. Conversely, the other helper was deemed more caring but less helpful. This distinction separated evaluations of competence or reliability from evaluations of care and moral intention. A helper could be judged imperfectly helpful while still seeming engaged and therefore trustworthy; alternatively, a helper could be producing positive outcomes but be experienced as emotionally disengaged or uncaring.

The second participant, similarly, characterized even a caring helper as insufficiently attentive to her needs. She emphasized reciprocity as the cornerstone of cooperation. In a block with an uncaring helper that she initially described as effective and cooperative, she interpreted the growing inequality in contributions through the experience of a previous friendship and reduced her investment when reciprocity appeared unlikely. Reflecting on this helper interaction, she also attributed some blame to herself: "Maybe it's also like my issue… I also think I have some trust issues".

Together, these interviews add examples of relational inferences that could be understood in terms of mentalizing. Participants drew conclusions about helper reliability, character, reciprocity, and their own self-worth, sometimes after small fluctuations in helper behaviour.

Shared themes across participant groups

The TP and LE interviews revealed several expectations shared across participants from both groups. Help-seekers emphasised reciprocity, moral obligations, and the importance of feeling that the helper cared. Participants often described helping in moral rather than purely descriptive terms, discussing what helpers and help-seekers should do and evaluating themselves and others as good or bad people. Self-directed emotions, including guilt and shame in the LE interviews, arose when participants felt they had violated their own standards, such as by failing to reciprocate, trusting too quickly, or contributing unwillingly to relationships.

Quantitative results

Data completeness and response distributions

All 30 online participants completed three recorded blocks, yielding 90 blocks and 899 trials. Choices, contributions and ratings were complete and within their expected ranges. Recorded returns agreed with the payoff matrix on every trial. Participants used all four effort categories and all six rating categories. Maximum intended effort occurred on 379 trials (42.2%), and the highest trust and satisfaction categories occurred on 164 (18.2%) and 169 (18.8%) trials, respectively. Across the common first two blocks, participant mean trust ranged from 1.36 to 4.90 (between-person SD 0.82), mean satisfaction from 2.80 to 5.00 (SD 0.54), and mean intended effort from 1.63 to 3.78 (SD 0.56). Full descriptive statistics are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Sensitivity to helper behaviour

As intended, the final caring helper delivered greater effort than the final uncaring helper (difference 1.30, 95% CI 1.11-1.47). Participants allocated a caring helper reported higher trust and decision satisfaction and selected greater intended effort (all Holm-adjusted p<0.001; Table 4). Adjustment for the preceding uncaring block produced similar differences: 2.31 for trust (95% CI 1.67-2.95), 2.23 for satisfaction (1.63-2.83) and 1.10 for intended effort (0.63-1.57). Across the common first two blocks, trust was 2.17 points higher in the caring block than in the uncaring block (1.75-2.57). Corresponding differences were 1.49 for satisfaction (1.14-1.87) and 0.67 for intended effort (0.49-0.84), with all adjusted p values below 0.001. This comparison between the first two blocks also reflects their fixed order and participants' experience of the task.

Differentiation of trust and decision satisfaction

Trust and satisfaction differed on 428 of 899 trials (47.6%), and every participant provided different ratings on at least one trial. Within participant-blocks, higher helper effort was associated with higher trust (coefficient 0.62, 95% CI 0.49-0.74) and satisfaction (0.84, 0.71-0.98). Satisfaction showed a stronger association with helper effort than trust (difference 0.22, 0.07-0.38, adjusted p=0.012). Its association with own realised effort was also stronger (difference 0.22, 0.04-0.41, adjusted p=0.019). These results identify overlapping but distinguishable response patterns.

Within-session reliability

Mean trust, satisfaction and intended effort showed corrected split-half coefficients of 0.96, 0.76 and 0.90, respectively (Table 5). Consistency was therefore strongest for the average trust and effort scores, while effort change was more sensitive to partitioning.

Questionnaire associations

Acknowledging the limited statistical power of this study, we explored associations between task responses and personality and mentalizing scores. We found zero-order associations between antagonism and smaller reductions in decision satisfaction (r=-0.44, p=0.023), and between disinhibition and smaller reductions in decision satisfaction (r=-0.39, p=0.045), following the change from a caring to an uncaring helper. Both associations were similar using rank correlations and remained negative when individual participants were omitted. While these associations did not survive correction for multiple comparisons (q=0.612 and 0.808, respectively), these exploratory patterns raise the possibility that personality traits relate to how help-seekers evaluate their own decisions after being let down. Full correlations and sensitivity analyses are reported in the supporting information.

Discussion

In this study, we validated the Helping Task, an experimental procedure for assessing how help-seekers engage in constructive risk-taking from a vulnerable position as well as how they appraise a simulated 'helper' and themselves. Importantly, our participants were not deceived about their helpers being computerized avatars but were asked to role-play as help-seekers. We first sought to improve ecological validity, endorsed by individuals with lived experience with relational difficulties. Interviewing our lived experience and 'neurotypical' participants suggested that they were immersed in the situation, engaging as if it were real and comparable to real social interactions from their experience. Moreover, they described engaging in mentalizing processes, described the helper's mental states and/or thinking in more complex ways than they are programmed to behave.

In more detail, our qualitative analyses showed that help-seekers held various expectations of moral, role-based, and relational responsibilities, which shaped their emotions and decision-making. These findings were paired with exploratory quantitative analyses which identified patterns of trust, self-satisfaction, and engagement suggesting that participants did not "gamify" but rather perceived the task as if it were a real social interaction.

In a recent systematic review from our team, no theory-driven empirical study of interpersonal dynamics in psychopathology reported task reliability (Zavlis et al., 2025).

Moreover, as Hedge and colleagues (2018) implied, many popular task paradigms produce robust and easily replicable experimental effects simply due to low between-subject variance. This can make large differences in the qualitative interpretations that can be made from such findings, which may seriously undermine many published conclusions.

Here, we've addressed these problems by demonstrating the consistency within the average scores of our task, while also the differentiation in individual differences from across trust, satisfaction, and effort on a trial-to-trial basis. Higher effort was strongly associated higher trust and satisfaction scores within session blocks (though more so for the latter). Indeed, participants were sensitive to helper behaviour, engaging more and reporting higher across both scores with caring helpers than uncaring helpers. However, as our qualitative findings also suggest, these measures should be separate. For example, separate measures of trust and satisfaction have demonstrated participants' ability to evaluate their own decision separate from the helper, sometimes to the detriment of their own self-esteem. Together, these pieces of evidence support the within-session measurement and responsiveness of our task (but leave unvalidated other aspects of the task; see limitations for details).

In our Helping task, participants were not deceived about being paired with artificial helpers and still reported their interactions to be realistic (though see also our limitations).

As we've shown, participants often described their interactions in relational terms, and as if interacting with a conscious being, i.e., reciprocity, helper/help-seeker responsibilities, betrayal, helpers 'having a bad day', countless attempts to make sense of underlying intentions, and quite interestingly, using moral evaluations (e.g., "I wouldn't think he's a bad person or something because, based on the previous evidence, I think he's quite reliable"). Our results are consistent with a wealth of findings indicating that humans apply social expectations (i.e., politeness and reciprocity) to machines without requiring a belief that the computer possesses a mind (Nass & Moon, 2000); that moral judgements depend on perceived intentions, obligations, and the capacity to prevent an outcome (Malle et al., 2014); and the dimensions of experience (relating to moral concern) vs agency (relating moral responsibility) predicate different moral judgments to human and nonhuman characters (Gray, Gray, & Wegner, 2007). Indeed, our results suggest that participants anthropomorphize their artificial helpers with a moral typecasting that Gray and Wagner (2009) suggest stems from the nature of dyadic morality. Moreover, they behave and reflect on their helper as if it has intentions-and try to infer what they are-as a basis for their moral judgements (i.e., whether the helping agent is caring or not). Together, these findings align with the wide literature, in support of the ecological validity of our task, and suggest that deception is not necessary to elicit behaviors as one would with other people.

Finally, our hidden Markov analyses were able to recover interpretable latent states from help-seekers' self-and other-evaluations and decisions. Across samples, the states formed a graded scale ordered by trust, effort and satisfaction together, with trust separating them most clearly. Participants moved along this scale in response to their helper, rather than playing the task as an impersonal points game. Again, specific helpertypes reliably shaped the trajectory of our recovered mental states; participants moved towards high-trust states with caring helpers and towards low-trust states with uncaring helpers, whereas participants with random helpers spread across states without any consistent direction. Trust also built gradually, but broke down sharply, as expected, according to definitions of trust as a (gradually developing) state of accepted vulnerability based on the positive expectations of another's intentions (Rousseau et al., 1998). This account is in line with participant accounts of "betrayal" and may explain why feeling betrayed was so devastating to trust, compared to other cases of low effort contributions from a helper. In recent work, participant-level HMMs were used to identify return behaviors in a repeated trust game. Guennouni and colleagues (2026) showed that a cognitive intervention reduced the tendency to remain in low-return starts following trust withdrawal. Adapted from a prisoner's dilemma task, the Helping task shares with the repeated trust game, an emphasis on the persistence, breakdown, and recovery of trust.

However, the application of a cognitive intervention for trust in help-seeking may be more challenging for the same reasons that it is prescribed in the first place. Nonetheless, as we have seen, interpersonal interactions in help-seeking significantly shape the trajectory of help itself, so some improvement or organization of interpersonal skills is likely to be helpful. This was the documented case of Hale and Aarts (2023) who found that patients that improved in depression scores from psychotherapy were characterized by longer time spent in states of focused listening. Thus, while response profiles (i.e. average effort investment) may be informative, further psychological mechanisms, specifically of interpersonal processes, such as mentalization, require further testing.

Limitations and future directions

Despite these strengths of our study, several limitations must be acknowledged. Here, we highlight three, particularly the constraints of a small sample size, the need to extend this study with a theory-driven approach, and the ecological differences of interacting with artificial agents compared to real people.

Firstly, this was a pilot study, using a convenience-sampled majority in the case of our qualitative study, and only a pair of lived experience individuals. The contributions from these groups may be informative for task validity and development, but further support is needed. In a previous quantitative study from our team (Moutoussis et al., 2025), a power analysis suggested a sample size of 63 participants, but our online sample here was only half of that and thus they need replication in larger samples. As a next step, therefore, larger and more diverse samples are needed to further examine the relational processes of our task.

Secondly, as we've discussed previously, our quantitative analyses were datadriven approaches. While these methods are useful for uncovering patterns within the data, they lack the explanatory depth of other theory-driven approaches. Given the high prevalence of relational dynamics (like mentalizing and moral inferences) in our task, more theory-driven modeling is required to shed light on their underlying mechanics.

Interestingly, simulation papers have shown promising results in reproducing some interesting patterns that may explain some of patterns, including idealization/devaluation of others (e.g., Story et al., 2024) or mentalizing patterns (e.g., Zavlis et al., 2026). Our aim for future research is to apply these modeling approaches to our task and examine the relational processes our task elicits more comprehensively in order to shed light on relational dynamics from both neuro-typical and neuro-diverse populations.

Finally, we acknowledge that letting participants know they are interacting with artificial agents, may limit our ecological validity with regards to understanding real relational interactions. Moreover, this applies to the idea of what 'realistic' means (i.e., if a participant affirms the task feels ecologically valid, it may be unclear whether they mean it feels 'realistic to real life' or 'realistic online', and they may not be aware of the difference either). Although they put themselves in the shoes of a real-life situation, the difference between interacting with humans and role-playing without the unique stakes involved in real help-seeking may make a substantial difference. Nevertheless, as a next step, we plan to extend our task to include real, dyadic interactions further improving its validity.

Conclusion

To conclude, we have developed and validated the Helping Task using a mixed qualitative and quantitative approach. Our results showed how participants in a vulnerable position as help-seekers described meaningful relational experiences with their helpers (despite knowing they are artificial agents). Repeated ratings of trust and satisfaction, as well as engagement throughout repeated interactions with helpers helped captured how they evaluated their helper and their own choices separately, and their willingness to continue investing. Future work with the Helping task should include larger samples with theorydriven modelling, and real dyadic interactions to extend these preliminary findings.
