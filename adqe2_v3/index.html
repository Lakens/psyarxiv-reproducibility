To improve Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: While endogenous attention is known to influence visual metacognition, it remains unclear whether these effects occur without altering perceptual performance, and what specific role the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) plays in mediating them.

Objective: We investigated whether spatial attention modulates subjective visibility and metacognitive efficiency independently of perceptual sensitivity (d′), and whether the DLPFC contributes to these effects.

Methods: Across two experiments, participants completed a two-alternative forced-choice discrimination task embedded within a Posner cueing paradigm, utilizing adaptive staircases. We assessed reaction times, signal detection metrics, subjective visibility ratings, and metacognitive efficiency. In Experiment 2, this task was performed following continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS) targeting either the bilateral DLPFC or an active control site (primary somatosensory cortex, S1).

In Experiment 1, invalid cues prolonged reaction times, shifted response bias, and impaired metacognitive efficiency, without affecting overall accuracy, subjective visibility, or perceptual sensitivity. In Experiment 2, whereas these attentional effects persisted following control S1-cTBS, they were significantly disrupted following DLPFC-cTBS, despite objective perceptual performance remaining stable. Furthermore, trial-bytrial analyses indicated that DLPFC disruption impairs adaptive metacognitive updating.

Conclusions: Endogenous spatial attention modulates visual metacognitive efficiency independently of objective perceptual sensitivity. Notably, our neuromodulatory findings suggest that the DLPFC does not generate metacognitive contents directly, but rather regulates the executive and attentional processes that inform metacognitive evaluations.

1. Introduction

Visual perception is assessed through both objective measures of perceptual performance and subjective reports of visual experience or confidence. Objective perceptual performance can be quantified by discrimination sensitivity (d′), whereas subjective visibility refers to the perceived clarity or strength of a stimulus (e.g. seen/unseen judgments). Importantly, subjective visibility and decision confidence are related but distinct constructs: visibility captures the quality of the perceptual experience itself, whereas confidence reflects the observer's subjective estimate of their decision's accuracy (Rausch & Zehetleitner, 2016; Mazor, Dijkstra & Fleming, 2022). Metacognitive sensitivity, in turn, captures the extent to which subjective judgments distinguish correct from incorrect perceptual decisions, while metacognitive efficiency evaluates this sensitivity relative to underlying perceptual capacity (d') (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012; Fleming & Lau, 2014; Fleming, 2017). Notably, these measures can dissociate: changes in subjective reporting do not necessarily entail corresponding shifts in objective performance, and metacognitive sensitivity can vary independently of first-order visual sensitivity (Maniscalco & Lau, 2016; Rausch & Zehetleitner, 2016). Distinguishing subjective visibility from metacognitive sensitivity and efficiency is therefore important for determining whether an experimental manipulation alters the strength of subjective visual experience, the fidelity of metacognitive monitoring, or both.

Manipulating spatial attention provides a direct way to test these different aspects of vision. While attending a stimulus location typically enhances objective perceptual performance (Posner, 1980; Posner, 2016; Chica et al., 2011), attention can also modulate subjective reports independently of changes in sensitivity. For instance, observers may be more likely to report seeing a stimulus when attention is directed toward its location, even in the absence of improved detection accuracy, potentially reflecting a shift in response criterion (Kurtz et al., 2017; Rahnev et al., 2011 Rahnev et al., , 2016;; Sherman et al., 2015; Summerfield & de Lange, 2014; Vernet et al., 2019). Conversely, prior expectations can enhance metacognitive sensitivity while objective performance is held constant (Sherman et al., 2015). Consequently, attention may change subjective visual experience, the efficiency with which subjective experience tracks objective performance, or both.

Here, we focus on endogenous or top-down spatial attention, in which centrally presented cues voluntarily direct attention toward an expected stimulus location. While recent evidence indicates that endogenous attention can influence both perceptual performance and subjective judgments (Jurkiewicz et al., 2026; Krug & Huckauf, 2025; Fernández, Okun & Carrasco, 2021; Recht, Gardelle & Mamassian, 2021), the extent to which it distinctly modulates subjective visibility and metacognitive efficiency remains unresolved. A persistent methodological challenge is that attention typically improves objective performance, making it difficult to determine whether changes in subjective measures reflect true attentional modulation or simply a byproduct of improved perception (Pruitt, Knotts & Odegaard, 2024). To overcome this, we utilized adaptive staircasing to equate d' across valid and invalid cue conditions, allowing attentional effects on subjective visibility and metacognitive efficiency to be examined without altering basic perceptual performance.

A primary candidate mechanism for this top-down modulation is the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). The DLPFC is central to executive control, supporting the maintenance of task goals and the top-down regulation of visual regions to select behaviorally relevant information (Miller & Cohen, 2001; Paneri & Gregoriou, 2017). Concurrently, the DLPFC is heavily implicated in confidence judgments and metacognitive monitoring (Brosnan & Wiegand, 2017; de Lange, Heilbron & Kok, 2018; Lobanov et al., 2014; Bolton & Staines, 2011; Liu et al., 2016; Fleming et al., 2010; Rounis et al., 2010; Vaccaro & Fleming, 2018; Di Bello, 2026; Iba & Sawaguchi, 2003). These dual functions position the DLPFC as a critical nexus between top-down attentional control and the modulation of metacognitive awareness. For example, Rounis et al. (2010) reported that disrupting the DLPFC reduced metacognitive sensitivity without affecting discrimination performance, though this interpretation has sparked ongoing debate (Bor et al., 2017; Ruby, Maniscalco & Peters, 2018; Martin, Lane, & Hsu, 2023). It remains unclear whether DLPFC involvement extends from metacognitive monitoring to subjective visual experience, and whether it actively contributes to the attentional modulation of these processes. This present pilot study provides an initial test of whether top-down spatial attention modulates subjective visibility and metacognitive efficiency independently of objective performance, and whether these effects depend on DLPFC function. We tested these predictions across two experiments. Experiment 1 utilized an endogenous Posner cueing task paired with metacontrast masking and adaptive staircasing. We hypothesized that valid cues would facilitate attentional orienting, reflected in shorter reaction times, while objective sensitivity (d') would remain matched. By equating basic performance, we aimed to determine whether spatial attention selectively enhances the efficiency of metacognitive monitoring, elevates overall subjective visibility, or both.

In Experiment 2, we applied continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS) over the bilateral DLPFC (or an active S1 control site) to establish a causal link between DLPFC function and these attention-dependent effects. If the DLPFC mediates this top-down modulation, we predicted that cTBS disruption would attenuate cueing-related changes. Finally, building on evidence that recent decision history shapes perceptual and metacognitive processing (Benwell et al., 2023), we conducted trial-by-trial analyses to explore how attentional history shapes these dynamics. While drawing conceptual parallels to sequential conflict adaptation such as the Gratton effect (Gratton et al., 1992; Egner, 2007), we specifically investigated whether current cue-validity effects on reaction times and metacognitive efficiency depended upon previous-trial validity.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Experiment One

2.1.1 Participants and apparatus

Nineteen healthy adults (12 females; age 21-41 years, M = 29.37, SD = 5.02) participated in this behavioural experiment. Four participants were excluded according to the criteria described below, leaving a final sample size of 15. All participants had normal or correctedto-normal vision, provided written informed consent, and received monetary compensation. The study was approved by the joint Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University (N201910050). Stimuli were presented using PsychoPy3 (v2021.2.3) on a 60-Hz LCD monitor in a dark room. Participants viewed the display from 50 cm using a chin rest.

2.1.2 Task and procedure

Participants performed a two-alternative forced-choice visual discrimination task (Figure 1). Each trial began with a 1000 ms fixation period followed by a spatial cue (600 ms) that validly predicted the target location on 70% of trials. A briefly presented target (33 ms; square or diamond) appeared 3° to the left or right of fixation and was followed after a 50ms interstimulus interval by a metacontrast mask (50 ms). Participants were asked to report if they had consciously seen the figure.

Participants identified the target shape while maintaining central fixation and simultaneously reported whether they consciously perceived the stimulus using a combined four-key response that encoded both discrimination accuracy (square/diamond) and subjective visibility (seen/unseen). Participants were instructed to guess the target identity whenever they reported not seeing it.

The experiment began with two 100-trial practice stages using adaptive staircase procedures (Kaernbach, 1991). During the first practice stage, target duration was adjusted to familiarize participants with brief stimulus presentations. During the second stage, target contrast (opacity) was adjusted to achieve 75% discrimination accuracy. Separate staircases were maintained for both valid and invalid cue trials to equate objective task performance across attention conditions. The main experiment consisted of five blocks of 100 trials in which staircase adjustments continued to maintain stable performance. Participants were asked to identify the shape of the target stimulus and report if they had seen it. During the response window, they pressed 'm' for a seen square, 'k' for an unseen square, 'c' for a seen diamond, or 'd' for an unseen diamond. Participants were informed that the spatial cue (dot) predicted the target location on most trials.

2.1.3 Data analyses and exclusion criteria

Visual and metacognitive performance was quantified using signal detection theory. Type 1 response bias (c') and metacognitive sensitivity (meta-d') were calculated following Maniscalco and Lau (2012), and metacognitive efficiency was defined as meta-d' -d' (Fleming & Lau, 2014).

One-sided paired-sample t-tests were used to compare valid and invalid cue conditions for stimulus opacity, accuracy, visibility, reaction time, response bias, and metacognitive efficiency.

Participants showing unstable SDT estimates (type 1 or type 2 hit or false alarm rates <5% or >95%; Bor et al., 2017) were excluded. Blocks of trials presenting with discrimination accuracy below 60% or visibility reports below 5% or above 95% were also removed. Overall, four participants and ten blocks of trials (across 8 participants) were excluded from analysis, resulting in a final sample of 15 participants and 65 blocks of trials in total (a necessary loss of 31% of unstable datasets).

2.2 Experiment Two

2.2.1 Participants

Eight healthy adults (6 females; age 25-41 years, M = 33.13, SD = 5.38) participated in this two-session cTBS experiment. All of these participants had already agreed to participate in Experiment 1 and thus were familiar with the task. One participant was excluded because of ceiling performance, leaving a final sample size of 7 for this pilot study. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, gave written informed consent, and received monetary compensation. As for Experiment 1, the same IRB approval applied and the same apparatus were used.

2.2.2 Stimulation and Procedure

Participants completed the same visual discrimination task described in Experiment 1. Following the practice stages, bilateral cTBS was applied over either the DLPFC or the S1 in two counterbalanced sessions performed over two consecutive weeks. MRI-guided neuronavigation, stimulation parameters, target localization, and intensity adjustment followed the protocol described in Martin et al. (2023). Participants then completed the five-block main experiment.

2.2.3 Data analyses and exclusion criteria

Data preprocessing and exclusion criteria were identical to Experiment 1 unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses employed aligned rank transform (ART) repeated-measures ANOVA (R package ARTool, version 0.11.1), followed by one-sided Wilcoxon signedrank tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Potential outliers were identified using Grubbs' test.

Data quality protocols resulted in the exclusion of two experimental blocks in which discrimination accuracy fell below 60%, leaving a total of 33 blocks for subsequent analysis. In contrast to the criteria applied in Experiment 1, one participant demonstrating extreme false-alarm rates was retained in the primary analyses to preserve statistical power given the smaller sample size. To rigorously address extreme values, Grubbs' tests were conducted prior to each statistical comparison. If a statistically significant outlier was identified, a maximum of one participant was excluded from that specific analysis.

Additional non-parametric analyses of metacognitive sensitivity (A′; Zhang & Mueller, 2005) were conducted to further corroborate the findings. In addition, trial-by-trial analyses of metacognitive efficiency were performed. Following Gratton et al. (1992), a trial-bytrial analysis of reaction times was conducted to quantify the Gratton effect, assessing whether the effect of current-trial cue validity on reaction times was reduced when the preceding trial involved an invalid cue compared with a valid cue. 3.1 Experiment One

The adaptive staircase procedure effectively controlled task difficulty by adjusting target opacity levels, resulting in different opacity thresholds between valid and invalid trials (t(14) = 2.94, p < 0.05, Cohen's d = 0.76). Discrimination accuracy and visibility ratings did not differ between valid and invalid cueing trials and remained stable across task blocks (all p > 0.60; Greenhouse-Geisser correction applied where appropriate). Discrimination sensitivity (d′) did not differ between cue conditions (p = 0.22).

Although task performance was stable, reaction times were significantly faster on valid compared to invalid trials (634 ± 137 vs. 725 ± 159 ms; t(14) = 3.44, p < 0.01, Cohen's d = 0.89; Figure 2A). Although cueing did not affect overall visibility, participants became more conservative at invalid cue trials (i.e. response bias; t(14) = 2.38, p < 0.05, Cohen's d = 0.62; Figure 2B), and they also displayed a reduced metacognitive efficiency (t(14) = 1.89, p < 0.05, Cohen's d = 0.49; Figure 2C).

3.2 Experiment Two

As in Experiment 1, discrimination accuracy, visibility ratings and perceptual sensitivity did not differ with cue validity, stimulation site, or task block (all p > 0.05; Greenhouse-Geisser corrections applied where appropriate). Unlike in Experiment 1, opacity levels and response bias did not differ between cue conditions or stimulation sites (all p > 0.10), indicating comparable task difficulty and familiarization with the task.

Reaction times showed a main effect of cue validity (F(1,15) = 6.72, p < 0.05, η² p = 0.58), but not stimulation site (p = 0.89). Interestingly, valid cues speed responses following S1-cTBS (V = 6, p < 0.05, rrb = 1), whereas this cueing effect was absent after DLPFC-cTBS (p = 0.07; Figure 3A). Also consistent with Experiment 1, invalid cues reduced metacognitive efficiency following S1-cTBS (V = 6, p < 0.05, rrb = 0.81), whereas this effect disappeared after DLPFC-cTBS (p = 0.42; Figure 3B). Nonparametric metacognitive sensitivity (A′) likewise decreased for invalid cues following S1-cTBS (V = 3, p < 0.05, rrb = 0.77), but not after DLPFC-cTBS (p = 0.53). Trial-by-trial analyses showed no significant effects following S1-cTBS (all p > 0.40), whereas following DLPFC-cTBS, two consecutive invalid-cue trials significantly reduced metacognitive efficiency (V = 27, p < 0.05, rrb = 0.93; Figure 4A). Finally, the Gratton effect on reaction time was significantly smaller following DLPFC-cTBS than following S1-cTBS (V = 21, p < 0.05, rrb = 0.90; Figure 4B).

4. Discussion

Across two experiments, we investigated whether endogenous spatial attention modulates subjective visibility and metacognitive efficiency while objective perceptual performance is controlled, and whether these effects depend on DLPFC function. Experiment 1 showed that invalid spatial cues increased reaction times and reduced metacognitive efficiency without detectable changes in objective perceptual sensitivity or subjective visibility ratings. These findings indicate that endogenous attention influences response speed and metacognitive performance beyond its effects on perceptual discrimination. Experiment 2 provided preliminary evidence that the effects of cue validity on reaction times and metacognitive efficiency differed following DLPFC versus S1 stimulation. Given the established role of the DLPFC in executive control and top-down attentional regulation (Miller & Cohen, 2001; Paneri & Gregoriou, 2017), these findings raise the possibility that prefrontal control processes mediate the influence of spatial attention on metacognitive performance. The findings from Experiment 1 extend previous evidence that attention and conscious perception are dissociable yet closely interacting processes (Wyart & Tallon-Baudry, 2008; Tallon-Baudry, 2012). Despite matched perceptual sensitivity, valid cues were associated with faster responses and greater metacognitive efficiency. Together, these findings suggest that endogenous attentional orienting influences conscious reports through mechanisms extending beyond early sensory encoding, supporting the view that metacognitive judgements incorporate higher-order information in addition to perceptual evidence (Peng & Tullis, 2021; Sherman et al., 2015). More broadly, our findings indicate that endogenous attention does not alter the overall conscious visibility of stimuli, but rather modulates how accurately observers' visibility reports track their own perceptual accuracy. This aligns with prior work showing that spatial attention can improve visual performance without altering confidence ratings (Wilimzig et al., 2008), further highlighting the distinction between metacognitive performance and visual awareness (Jachs et al., 2015).

Experiment 2 provides preliminary causal evidence that this attentional modulation depends on the DLPFC. Despite preserved objective performance and subjective reporting across stimulation conditions, DLPFC-cTBS attenuated the behavioural facilitation and metacognitive advantage associated with valid attentional orienting, which remained intact following control S1-cTBS. This suggests that the DLPFC is necessary for translating topdown attentional cues into behavioral and metacognitive advantages, and for managing the trial-by-trial adjustments through which cognitive control adapts to changing attentional demands (MacDonald et al., 2000; Pulopulos et al., 2022). This is aligned with the literature which suggests a causal role of DLPFC in attention, executive control, planning, and goaldirected decision-making (Lowe et al., 2018; Ngetich et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2013). For example, applying TMS to the right DLPFC eliminates the location cueing effect in a variant of the Posner task (Jin, 2021). Notably, our stimulation was bilateral, whereas previous studies often suggest hemispheric specificity, sometimes yielding divergent results for the left versus right DLPFC (Ngetich et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2013). While the observed behavioral and metacognitive facilitation in our study appears causally dependent on the DLPFC, the precise underlying neurocognitive processes require further characterization.

The role of the DLPFC as an executive controller, rather than a mere sensory conduit, is further supported by our sequential analyses. Under control S1-cTBS conditions, we observed robust trial-by-trial adaptations: an invalid cue on the preceding trial temporarily blunted the metacognitive cueing effect and modulated current-trial reaction times. This reflects a dynamic adjustment where violated prior expectations trigger a recalibration of cognitive control to handle changing attentional demands (MacDonald et al., 2000; Pulopulos et al., 2022). Following DLPFC-cTBS, however, these sequential adaptations were abolished. The cueing effect on metacognitive efficiency persisted indiscriminately even after invalid trials, and reaction time history effects disappeared. Although preliminary, these data suggest that the DLPFC actively drives the trial-by-trial adjustment of attentional control based on recent cueing history. When the DLPFC is disrupted, the brain fails to update its preparatory executive processes in response to prior errors.

By combining these main and sequential effects, our findings provide a potential framework to contextualize the mixed results regarding the DLPFC and visual metacognition. Whereas Rounis et al. (2010) reported reduced metacognitive efficiency following DLPFC-cTBS, subsequent studies failed to replicate such a generalized impairment (Bor et al., 2017 (Bor et al., , 2018;; Martin et al., 2023). Our preliminary findings help bridge these accounts: we observed no main effects of stimulation site on overall metacognition, suggesting the DLPFC is not a dedicated computational module for metacognition per se. Instead, it may specifically support the executive processes through which top-down attentional cues influence the efficiency of metacognitive evaluations. This perspective fits with broader evidence implicating the DLPFC in cognitive control, top-down attention, and cognitive flexibility, rather than in generating conscious perceptual contents or acting as an isolated metacognitive module (Gbadeyan et al., 2016; Ryali et al., 2016; Uddin, Yeo & Spreng, 2019; Lowe et al., 2018; Bor & Seth, 2012).

Consequently, this study complements a growing literature mapping the distributed neural architecture of metacognition. While evidence increasingly implicates prefrontal areas in metacognitive abilities (Saccenti et al., 2024), their exact functional roles remain unresolved. Some studies propose that the DLPFC simply reads out the strength of the sensory evidence before relaying it to the anterior PFC (Shekhar & Rahnev, 2018), while others assign specific roles in metacognitive bias to the dorsomedial PFC (Mattes & Soutschek, 2025). Furthermore, recent studies highlight a causal role for parietal areas in metacognition-adjacent functions, including memory, expectations, and choice bias (Sengupta et al., 2024 , Tarasi et al, 2026; Ye et al., 2018). It is likely that parietal regions interact dynamically with frontal and visual cortices within a distributed metacognitive network (Di Luzio, 2022; Leitão et al., 2017). Given the complexity of metacognition, future research must prioritize investigating its interactions with other cognitive systems to delineate the exact role of prefrontal nodes in perception, confidence, and consciousness. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, Experiment 2 was designed as a pilot study and included only seven participants. The small sample inherently limits statistical power, resulting in considerable uncertainty regarding the estimated effects and their magnitudes. The findings from Experiment 2 should be interpreted as preliminary, particularly for the effects assessed using the ART ANOVA. The limited samples also complicates the interpretation of certain discrepancies between the two experiments. For instance, in Experiment 1, participants exhibited a more conservative response bias (higher c′) following invalid cues. This effect did not reach significance in Experiment 2, leaving it unclear whether the effect reflects a reliable modulation of the decision criterion or sampling variability. Because participants in Experiment 2 had previously completed Experiment 1, task familiarity may also have contributed to this discrepancy. Ultimately, the small sample of Experiment 2 limits the reliability of both null and positive findings, highlighting the critical need for replication in larger, independent cohorts. Second, we quantified metacognitive efficiency using the method described by Maniscalco and Lau (2012), primarily to facilitate comparison with Rounis et al. (2010). However, newer measures of metacognitive performance have been proposed (e.g. Fleming, 2017), each carrying distinct assumptions and potential sources of bias (Rahnev, 2025). Our estimates should thus be contextualized within the specific metric employed, and future studies should assess the robustness of these effects across alternative modeling approaches. Finally, because reaction times varied despite constant discrimination performance, our reaction time findings warrant cautious interpretation. In research on unconscious processing and priming, reaction time is often utilized as an objective measure of sensory processing (Meyen et al., 2022). Thus, observing response speed modulations without d' changes raises important questions regarding the precise boundary between conscious and unconscious performance metrics in metacognition research. This questions the distinction between conscious and unconscious performance in metacognition research.

In summary, our findings indicate that endogenous spatial attention modulates both reaction time and metacognitive efficiency without altering objective perceptual sensitivity. Furthermore, our pilot cTBS experiment data provide preliminary evidence that this modulation depends on intact DLPFC function. Rather than directly generating conscious perceptual contents or performing metacognitive evaluation, the DLPFC may contribute to visual metacognitive awareness through executive control processes that regulate attentional demands and the evaluation of perceptual decisions.
