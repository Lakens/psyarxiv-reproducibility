Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

Generative AI is now embedded in how coworkers, leaders, and organizations communicate, yet its effects on the relationships among them are still poorly understood. This review synthesizes recent research on how an individual's AI use changes the way others perceive, judge, and behave toward them, and shows that these effects can be explained by established theories of human-human interaction, including attribution theory, computers-as-social-actors, the integrative model of organizational trust, social exchange theory, and resourcebased theories. The evidence suggests that observers often read AI use as a sign of disposition rather than circumstance, judging AI users as less competent, motivated, warm, and authentic, and that disclosure does not repair the resulting loss of trust. These perceptions carry over into behavior: coworkers reciprocate less, share less knowledge, and show less prosocial behavior toward AI users, while AI-related job insecurity, loneliness, and work alienation further strain ties among colleagues. AI use also loosens moral standards and shifts how blame is assigned, making people more critical of others' work but more lenient toward misconduct. These effects are conditional rather than uniformly negative. AI can reduce loneliness, improve coordination, and prompt employees to invest more in emotionally supportive ties, and relational buffers such as leader-member exchange, shared leadership, and supervisor support can offset its costs. The review concludes that AI's costs to workplace relationships run mainly through indirect pathways, not through the technology itself. It outlines organizational implications, including clear norms for when and why AI is used and protection of socially valued tasks, and identifies priorities for future research: longitudinal designs, team-and network-level studies, intervention research, and more diverse samples.

1. Introduction

As generative artificial intelligence (AI) becomes integrated into the workplace, from drafting emails and offering advice to managing performance, its impact extends beyond task assistance to the relationships people form with one another. AI is now positioned between coworkers, between leaders and followers, and between employees and their organizations, mediating the exchanges that have traditionally defined workplace relationships (Boyd & Markowitz, 2026; Jung et al., 2026). The central claim of this review is that an individual's AI use indirectly changes how other people perceive, judge, and behave toward them in systematic and predictable ways, and these changes can be understood through existing psychological theories of human-human interaction. People who do not use AI trust AI users less and see AI users as less competent, less motivated, and less authentic (Cardon & Coman, 2025; Reif et al., 2025; Schilke & Reimann, 2025). People judge AI users more harshly: AI-assisted effort is read as less warm, less empathetic, and less personally motivated, and interacting with AI can itself make people harsher judges of others (Buss et al., 2025; Dishop et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023; Tey et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026b). Additionally, people change how they behave toward AI users, reciprocating help less, sharing less knowledge, and being less prosocial toward those who rely on AI (Dang & Liu, 2026b; Dishop et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2025). Over time, such shifts may weaken people's motivation to invest in relationships with colleagues, make interactions more transactional, erode relational skills and tacit knowledge, and contribute to incivility and reduced wellbeing at work (Cebulla, 2025; Connell, 2025; Keeler & Murphy, 2026).

However, AI use in the workplace is not uniformly negative. Under some conditions, AI use can lead to more favorable performance evaluations (He et al., 2024). Additionally, when AI reduces the need for task-based exchange, employees may compensate by investing more in emotionally supportive ties with coworkers (Zhang & Yan, 2026) and by helping coworkers more to meet a heightened need for affiliation (Tang et al., 2023). The social presence of AI can reduce workplace loneliness under some conditions (Liu et al., 2025); AI-generated support messages can be rated as more sincere and actionable than human ones (Swain et al., 2025), and can improve coordination among human groups (Shirado & Christakis, 2017). Overall, the literature suggests that AI's relational effects are conditional. AI can support coordination and reduce routine work, but it also changes how employees interpret one another's effort, care, competence, and responsibility.

2. Theoretical Foundations

People interpret others' behavior in systematic, predictable ways, and much of AI's effect on workplace relationships can be understood within existing psychological theory. Attribution theory offers the most useful starting point, because the primary question an observer asks when a coworker uses AI is why they used it, and what that says about them (Reif et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; He et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2026; Jago et al., 2024). Two well-established attribution biases help explain why AI use so often counts against the user in the eyes of others, and why people tend to see their own AI use as reasonable while viewing others' use of the same tools as a problem.

The first is the fundamental attribution error, the tendency to overemphasize internal traits and underweight situational factors when explaining other people's behavior (Ross, 1977). Applied to AI, an observer who sees a coworker use AI is likely to read it as a sign of disposition, such as laziness, low competence, or low commitment, rather than as a response to workload, deadlines, or organizational expectations. This is consistent with evidence that AI users are rated as less competent and less motivated than non-users (Reif et al., 2025), that people deny humanness to those who seek advice from AI (Dang & Liu, 2026b), and that observers assign responsibility for both success and failure in human-AI collaboration to the person rather than the AI (Hu et al., 2026). The second is the self-serving bias, the tendency to attribute one's own successes to internal qualities such as skill and effort and one's failures to external circumstances (Miller & Ross, 1975). For AI users, this bias allows good AI-assisted outcomes to be claimed as one's own work while poor outcomes can be blamed on the tool. For observers, the same logic runs in reverse: a coworker's AI-assisted success is discounted as the AI's contribution while their failures remain their own. Together, these biases suggest that the same act of AI use carries very different meaning depending on whether one is the user or the observer.

Several other frameworks recur across this literature and are drawn on where relevant in the sections that follow. Understanding how people interact and relate to computers through the paradigm computers are social actors is akin to how people interact with each other; when people are interacting with AI, all the same social scripts and heuristics are triggered that would normally happen in a human-human interaction despite the lack of human connection. In other words, humans' interpersonal social rules, norms, and expectations are ingrained in how they behave, which comes naturally and automatically without the need to actually view computers (or AI) as human (Nass et al., 1994). Thus, it can be expected that AI interactions can help inform later human-human interactions. The integrative model of organizational trust (Mayer et al., 1995) separates trustworthiness into ability, benevolence, and integrity, and has been used to explain why AI use erodes per-ceptions of a user's benevolence and integrity even when their ability is unaffected (Dang & Liu, 2026a; Cardon & Coman, 2025; Schilke & Reimann, 2025). The social exchange theory as a cost-benefit analysis of human interaction explains why AI-assisted help is reciprocated less than unassisted help because people may perceive people who use AI as dampening the ''cost'' of the help (Dishop et al., 2025; Zhang & Yan, 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Harrell & Traeger, 2025a). Dang and Liu's (2026a) model of AI-use-induced dehumanization and resource-based theories, including the conservation of resources theory and the job demands-resources model, explain loneliness, alienation, and conflict as products of AI-driven resource depletion (Tang et al., 2023; Hai et al., 2025; Jeong & Jeong, 2025; Liu et al., 2026). Across all of these frameworks, AI's relational effects are rarely direct; they are filtered through attribution, trust, exchange, and resource processes that were well understood in human-human contexts before AI entered the picture.

3. Relational Wellbeing: Loneliness, Alienation, and Dehumanization

One of the most direct ways AI changes workplace relationships is by reducing the need to interact with coworkers. When AI can easily carry out tasks that once required a colleague's help, the everyday exchanges that build and maintain coworker relationships become less necessary (Dishop et al., 2025; Zhang & Yan, 2026). Whether this matters for employees' sense of connection depends on whether AI can adequately stand in for the human contact it replaces, and the evidence on this point is mixed. In some cases, AI is an adequate substitute for human interaction. For example, Ho et al. (2018) find that the emotional and relational benefits of self-disclosure are equivalent whether people believe they are disclosing to a chatbot or to another person, supporting the computers are social actors paradigm (Nass et al., 1994). Liu et al. (2025) find that the social presence of AI reduces employee workplace loneliness through AI rapport and interpersonal closeness, although this effect depends on the employee's underlying need for human interaction. Swain et al. (2025) tested an LLM-based ''empathetic coworker'' designed to help client-service representatives regulate emotion after uncivil client interactions; representatives rated its support as more sincere and actionable than human-generated messages. Yet, the same representatives emphasized the indispensability of shared experience, suggesting that AI can supplement but not fully replace a coworker. The limits of substitution are also clear in initial interactions, where people are less open, agreeable, and self-disclosing with AI than with humans (Mou & Xu, 2017). Similarly, AI-generated messages made recipients feel more heard than human-generated ones, but only when recipients believed the message came from a person; once they learned it came from AI, the feeling of being heard diminished (Yin et al., 2024). Feeling heard therefore appears to be a relational function that cannot be replaced by AI even when AI performs the task equally well or better.

In other cases, AI contributes to loneliness and alienation.

As employees interact more with AI in pursuit of work goals, they experience both a compensatory need for social affiliation and a sense of loneliness, which worsens after-work insomnia and alcohol consumption especially among employees high in attachment anxiety (Tang et al., 2023). AI can threaten the psychological need for relatedness at work, alongside the needs for competence and autonomy, prompting employees to adopt maladaptive coping strategies (Hermann et al., 2025). Daily employee-AI collaboration also increases work alienation, a disconnection from work and colleagues when digital job demands are high (Hai et al., 2025), and AI use more broadly is linked to psychological distress through the dual paths of job insecurity and workplace loneliness (Liu et al., 2026). These effects are not evenly distributed; AI use is associated with more loneliness overall, but the effect is stronger for older adults than for younger ones (Dong et al., 2025). Although loneliness and alienation are measured as individual-level traits, they reflect how connected people feel to those around them, so rising loneliness alongside greater AI use may signal that workplace relationships are under strain. However, relationships depend not only on how employees feel when they use AI but also on how others interpret that use, and AI use changes those perceptions as well.

4. How Does AI Change the Way People Perceive Others?

Trust and perceived authenticity are foundations of a cooperative and productive workplace (De Jong et al., 2016; Mayer et al., 1995; McAllister, 1995); employees need to trust their coworkers and leaders to be confident in shared decisions, but AI use appears to weaken both aspects. When people doubt the authenticity of others' communication, they also report feeling a loss of agency in the exchange, which can undermine communication between coworkers (Connell, 2025; Wenker, 2023). People tend to distrust others who use AI and perceive them as less warm, even when AI users explicitly disclose their use (Schilke & Reimann, 2025; Yin et al., 2024). People also question others' authorship, confidence, and sincerity when they know AI was involved (Buss et al., 2025; Cardon & Coman, 2025). Consistent with the integrative model of organizational trust (Mayer et al., 1995), this affects perceived benevolence and integrity rather than ability; AI users as a class are judged as less benevolent than non-users regardless of their actual competence (Dang & Liu, 2026a). Even outside of the workplace, using AI advice for relational maintenance in friendships is perceived as low effort and is related to lower relationship satisfaction and higher uncertainty (Liu et al., 2023), which translate into harsher judgments. People who use AI at work both anticipate and actually receive lower ratings of competence and motivation, and they are seen as lazier than those who refrain, with downstream consequences for hiring evaluations (Reif et al., 2025). Furthermore, people who have just completed a task with an AI subsequently judge an unrelated human's work more harshly than people who worked with a purported human partner, suggesting that interacting with AI recalibrates the standards people apply to others (Tey et al., 2024).

How AI affects relationships is predicated on both the task type and the relationship context. People prefer AI when it is used for functionally relevant tasks and prefer humans when a task is socially or empathically relevant (Höddinghaus et al., 2021; Li & Bitterly, 2024; Lindgren, 2024). Additionally, employees who have strong relationships with their coworkers interpret those coworkers' AI use as a challenge, whereas those with weak relationships interpret it as a threat and orient toward avoiding negative job outcomes rather than pursuing positive ones (Qu & Xu, 2026). Thus, relationships can change how people interpret AI use, which is seen in supervisor relationships as well. Supervisors inflate performance ratings for employees who use AI, attributing AI-assisted output to the employee's own skill (He et al., 2024), yet being managed by an algorithm, rather than assisted by one, lowers the perceived status of the managed employee because it signals that the underlying job lacks complexity (Jago et al., 2024). Furthermore, people assign this credit and blame asymmetrically, attributing both success and failure in human-AI collaboration to the person rather than to the AI (Hu et al., 2026). Yet, disclosure, the remedy most often proposed to offset these costs, does not restore trust. Disclosing AI use lowers trust in the discloser regardless of how the disclosure is framed or whether it is voluntary (Schilke & Reimann, 2025). AI use therefore does not carry a single, fixed social meaning at work; its effect on how a person is trusted, judged, and credited depends on the task, the relationship between observer and user, and whether AI is understood as replacing the person's judgment, augmenting their effort, or managing them.

4.1 Socially Relevant Moral Perception Change

AI use also shifts moral standards more broadly. People tend to apply different moral beliefs to robots compared to the moral beliefs they apply to humans (Malle et al., 2015). Employees' AI usage triggers more flexible moral beliefs, which increases their own workplace deviance and produces more lenient moral judgment of other people's misconduct (Zhao et al., 2025). Akin to human interactions, AI-generated advice that promotes dishonesty increases dishonest behavior in the person who receives it (Leib et al., 2023). Employees who self-identify as AI users report higher psychological entitlement, which increases unethical behavior toward others; this effect is strongest when AI identity is perceived as rare within the organization, suggesting that being an early or unusual adopter amplifies the sense of being special (Cao et al., 2023). Indeed, AI can loosen the moral standards people hold, both for themselves and for others, which may make unethical behavior easier to cognitively justify, thus increasing moral flexibility at work.

When people turn to AI to make sense of social and moral situations, AI generated responses can change the conclusions they would otherwise have reached on their own or through conversation with another person. Large language models (LLMs) tend to resolve ambiguous interpersonal situations, such as unclear signals from a supervisor or an ambiguous exchange with a colleague, into a single, confident narrative rather than preserving the uncertainty that may be warranted (Yuan et al., 2026). The result is a more black-and-white view of the situation, delivered with more confidence than the evidence supports, so people may act on a false sense of interpersonal clarity. Where a conversation with a coworker might surface alternative interpretations or situational explanations, an AI's single answer can shortcut that process, which may reinforce the tendency to attribute others' behavior to fixed traits.

Beyond loosening the moral standards people apply to themselves, AI can also alter the standards they hold others to. People are less likely to blame AI for unfair treatment compared to blaming humans for the same treatment, and this effect has consequences for subsequent behavior; people are less likely to punish humans for unfair behavior, even when that behavior is unrelated to AI (Zhang et al., 2025). Moral perception is therefore not only shaped by what AI says but by who people believe is responsible; when AI is involved, blame is redistributed in ways that can lower the standards people hold others to. Whether these shifts make people more lenient or more harsh depends on the domain. For example, AI exposure can make people more demanding judges of others' work quality (Tey et al., 2024) while making them more lenient toward others' ethical misconduct (Zhao et al., 2025). This may be especially harmful for workplace relationships, as colleagues could become more critical of each other's work but less willing to hold each other accountable for unfair or unethical behavior.

5. How Does AI Change the Way People Behave

Toward Others?

Because attitudes are predictive of behavior, changes in how people perceive and judge AI users matter since they shape how people behave toward others. People are less likely to show prosocial responses to those who seek advice from AI than to those who do not (Dang & Liu, 2026b). From a social exchange perspective, when AI replaces or mediates human interaction, it lowers the effort those interactions require, which may lead people to value them less and reciprocate less (Dishop et al., 2025). When employees use AI to help coworkers, recipients perceive the helper as exerting less effort and having less benevolent intentions, which reduces the gratitude recipients feel and makes them less likely to reciprocate (Zhang et al., 2026b). AI-assisted help can thus weaken coworker relationships even when AI is useful, because it is read as more instrumental and less personally motivated. Harrell and Traeger (2025a, 2025b) extend this to networks of reciprocity and cooperation: adding bots to a reputationbased reciprocity network makes people less generous overall, in part because people do not believe bots deserve help the way humans do and extend that belief to humans who help bots. Delegating tasks to AI may therefore dilute the social connections that are essential to workplace functioning and collaboration.

Knowledge sharing follows a similar pattern, showing that higher AI usage is related to less knowledge sharing with coworkers (Xu et al., 2025). This effect may be mediated by self-esteem. For example, an employee's perception of a coworker's AI usage lowers their own organization-based self-esteem and raises anxiety, which increases knowledge hiding toward others (Xu et al., 2026). AI usage at work is also associated with more time theft and job insecurity, and AIinduced job insecurity reduces the psychological safety needed for open knowledge sharing (Xu et al., 2025a; Kim & Kim, 2024). Frequent AI interaction is further linked to workplace incivility through job insecurity and work alienation (Yam et al., 2022; You et al., 2026). At the organizational level, AI investment itself, independent of any single employee's AI use, is associated with job insecurity across the workforce, which increases both coworker conflict and work-family conflict (Jeong & Jeong, 2025). These findings suggest that AI often harms coworker relationships not through the technology itself, but by threatening employees' sense of security and worth, which leads them to guard their knowledge, withdraw from colleagues, and treat one another less civilly.

These behavioral effects are not uniformly negative, and several conditions determine their direction. Zhang and Yan (2026) find that AI usage reduces instrumental ties, coworker relationships centered on task support and information access, which in turn reduces help seeking and reciprocity; but AI usage is also associated with stronger expressive ties, relationships centered on emotional support and belonging, which are linked to more help seeking and reciprocity. Shared leadership among colleagues weakens AI's negative effect on both kinds of ties. Employees may therefore compensate for fewer task-based interactions by investing more in emotional connection, just as the heightened affiliation need that follows AI interaction can increase helping toward coworkers (Tang et al., 2023). Positive AI interactions can also carry positive spillover: expressing gratitude directed at a robot promotes prosocial behavior toward people (Xu et al., 2025b), and wellplaced bots can improve coordination among human groups (Shirado & Christakis, 2017). AI does not simply reduce the total volume of coworker exchange; it reallocates which kinds of exchange happen, toward some relationships and away from others.

6. How Does AI Change Team and Leadership Dynamics?

The perception, judgment, and behavior effects described above take on additional features in teams and leadership relationships, where AI affects not only dyads but group processes and the distribution of influence. In three-person teams using virtual reality, the role assigned to an AI system (i.e., advisor/unsolicited advice vs. assistant/solicited advice) and its voice (i.e., male vs. female) affected who submitted the team's decisions. When the AI was assigned an advisor role, it shaped how team members related to one another, with the advisor's voice shifting which gender took charge of submitting decisions, even though teams followed its suggestions less (Traeger et al., 2026). This suggests that AI does not need to be trusted or followed to affect workplace relationships. Much of this effect is driven by belief rather than by AI's actual behavior; in teams ostensibly composed entirely of humans, simply believing that a teammate was an AI changed team sentiment, team process, and the emergence of shared cognition among the human members (Musick et al., 2021). This mirrors the dyadic findings on disclosure and source beliefs (Yin et al., 2024; Schilke & Reimann, 2025), what people think AI is doing matters as much, if not more, than what it actually does.

When leaders adopt the use of AI, it contributes to whether employees feel threatened or supported. Transformational leadership increases employee AI usage through perceived organizational support, though this indirect effect is weaker in highly competitive workplace environments (Wang et al., 2025). Nanda (2026) introduces the construct of algorithmic deference, the tendency to defer to algorithmic recommendations, and finds that it lowers psychological safety and innovation; positive leader-member exchange buffers this effect, suggesting that a strong leader-follower relationship can protect against some of AI's negative effects. Shared leadership among colleagues similarly weakens AI's negative effect on coworker ties (Zhang & Yan, 2026). Supervisor and colleague support can also determine whether AI-delivered feedback is effective; negative feedback from AI improved employees' occupational self-efficacy only when they also perceived high emotional support from supervisors and colleagues, whereas positive feedback from AI improved self-efficacy regardless of how much support employees perceived (Watanabe et al., 2025). Therefore, the human relationships surrounding an AI tool may matter more than the tool's design in determining its effects.

AI also creates new sources of conflict and changes what is needed from leaders. Gonzalez et al. (2024) argue that as AI takes over routine communication and reporting, people who lead organizational change will rely more on distinctly human skills, such as building strong relationships and creating meaningful in-person connection, which they call communal co-presence. Similarly, Shanmugam et al. (2026) argue that AI-related conflict, such as conflict caused by algorithmic bias, surveillance, or unclear AI decisions, cannot be managed through technical fixes alone, and that HR policy, ethical oversight, and employee training all shape whether these conflicts escalate or are resolved. Together, these perspectives suggest that as AI takes on more tasks, the human side of leadership becomes more important.

7. Organizational and Practical Implications

These findings carry direct implications for how organizations approach AI adoption. Because AI's relational costs run through attribution, trust, and reciprocity rather than through output quality, organizations cannot treat AI adoption as a purely technical or productivity decision. AI's relational risks, including lower trust between colleagues, reduced reciprocity and knowledge sharing, greater loneliness and alienation, less civil treatment of one another, and a weaker willingness to hold one another accountable, should be treated as a work health and safety concern rather than left to informal norms, and addressed through participatory job crafting that positions workers as co-designers of AI-related change rather than passive recipients of it (Cebulla, 2025). Industrial-organizational and consulting psychology practitioners have a parallel ethical duty to anticipate and mitigate AI's interpersonal and well-being harms, not only to deliver its productivity benefits (Connell, 2025).

People may judge AI users harshly partly because they cannot see why the AI was used. Without that context, observers tend to assume the person is lazy or less capable, rather than considering reasons such as heavy workloads or tasks that AI is well suited for. Supporting this, the judgment given for using AI disappears when AI is described as clearly useful for the task (Reif et al., 2025). Organizations could therefore reduce this judgment by setting clear, shared norms about when and why AI use is expected, so that AI use is seen as part of the job rather than a personal shortcoming. This may matter more than disclosure, since simply revealing AI use does not restore trust (Schilke & Reimann, 2025). Since disclosure alone does not restore trust (Schilke & Reimann, 2025), relational remedies might be more promising: investing in leader-member exchange (Nanda, 2026), shared leadership (Zhang & Yan, 2026), supportive supervisors and colleagues (Watanabe et al., 2025), and HRM practices could reduce AI-driven job insecurity (Jeong & Jeong, 2025). Organizations should also consider protecting the tasks where human contact is most valued, such as socially and empathically relevant exchanges (Li & Bitterly, 2024), rather than automating them by default.

These shifts sit within a broader change to the employment relationship itself; generative AI and autonomous agents are rewriting the social contract of employment, changing how expertise is recognized, how authority is distributed, and how relationships form at work (Valentine, 2025). Makarius et al. (2020) offer a practical framework for this transition, describing AI integration as a process of building sociotechnical capital along cognitive, relational, and structural dimensions. The organizations best positioned to adopt AI successfully may be those that invest as deliberately in the relational conditions surrounding its use, including usage norms, leader support, and team climate, as they do in the technology itself.

8. Limitations and Future Directions

Several methodological patterns qualify how confidently these findings can be generalized. Much of this literature relies on multi-wave employee surveys, often collected in China, South Korea, or Taiwan, paired with scenario-based experiments and analyzed with moderated mediation models. Selfreport measures are often used, and AI usage is operationalized inconsistently across studies, sometimes as frequency, sometimes as collaboration intensity, and sometimes as organizational investment. The stronger causal evidence, drawn from field experiments (Tang et al., 2023; Tey et al., 2024), incentivized behavioral games (Leib et al., 2023; Harrell & Traeger, 2025a , 2025b), and large-scale archival analysis (Safadi et al., 2024), is concentrated in a smaller subset of studies and would benefit from replication across more industries and cultural contexts. Several studies rely on student rather than employee samples (Zhang et al., 2026a), and robots, chatbots, and generative AI are often treated as interchangeable despite likely differences in the relational signals each conveys.

Longitudinal work is needed to understand whether these effects persist, intensify, or habituate as AI use becomes normalized, since most current designs capture a single point in time or a short panel. The literature is heavily skewed toward documenting the costs of AI; the conditions under which AI can be designed to support rather than substitute for human connection remain underexplored, despite promising exceptions such as Shirado and Christakis (2017). Intervention research testing specific organizational policies is similarly scarce, as is research at the team and network level rather than the dyad. Since disclosure does not appear to restore trust (Schilke & Reimann, 2025), future work should test what does: which usage norms avoid the degradation of trust without concealing AI use, how credit and blame should be allocated for AI-assisted work, and what relational-skill training should accompany AI literacy training, given the risk that relational skills may erode without use (Keeler & Murphy, 2026). Taken as a whole, this literature suggests that AI's costs to workplace relationships run mostly through indirect pathways, through how coworkers attribute motive, how they calibrate trust and moral judgment, and how they choose to reciprocate, rather than through any inherent property of the technology itself.
