Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Replication studies are essential for building cumulative knowledge in second language acquisition (SLA) research because they allow researchers to revisit, contextualize, and extend the findings of earlier studies. Rather than arguing for the value of replication, this chapter focuses on the replication research process itself. It begins with two core issues: deciding what study to replicate and what kind of replication to conduct (conceptual, approximate, close, or exact). It then considers the place of replication in qualitative research. The chapter reviews methodological considerations for planning a replication, supported by a set of planning questions, and for disseminating it through presentations and journal articles, including reporting standards. It then describes the practical relevance of replication for building confidence in findings before they are applied and for testing lab-based results in classrooms. The chapter closes with future directions: integrating replication into research programs, extending it beyond quantitative methods, improving planning and reporting, and adopting open research practices.

What to replicate

One of the biggest challenges at the beginning of a replication research project involves deciding what prior study to replicate. First and foremost, not every study needs to be

Similarly, a study that has been replicated before can be replicated again (for examples, see McManus et al., 2026; Morgan-Short et al., 2018). Consistent with all types of research, a replication study should be motivated based on its potential contribution to knowledge and not the absence of research alone.

Turning attention to some of the reasons that might favor the selection of a study to be replicated, Porte and McManus (2019) recommended researchers to consider the following factors: (a) value/impact, (b) continued relevance, (c) access to data and materials, (c) unexpected or unusual results, and (d) statistical and methodological rigor (see also Isager et al., 2023; Marsden et al., 2018; McManus, 2024a). Based on this, studies that are valuable and impactful for the field (e.g., theory, academic, social) and relevant today represent a good starting point. In addition, researchers should determine if their identified study provides access to the data and materials. It is extremely difficult to replicate a study without access to the materials. Developing new materials from scratch can also introduce new sources of error and variation in study outcomes, thus preventing a clear comparison to the initial study. Access to data is becoming increasingly important to draw meaningful comparisons between studies (see McManus et al., 2025). Researchers should also review the candidate study for its findings, including whether the results are unexpected, surprising and/or they appear inconsistent with the rest of the field. Lastly, methodological rigor is an important consideration as well. Researchers should identify

What kind of replication study

We generally see labels like "close", "approximate", and "conceptual" to describe the types of replications conducted in SLA (Porte & McManus, 2019). Although labels such as "exact", "direct", "partial", "systematic" exist, they are less common today in SLA. We can think about these different kinds of replication studies in terms of (a) amounts of intentional change and (b) the aims of the replication study. We will review these different kinds of replication, starting with conceptual, followed by approximate and close, and then exact. We will see that each type of replication is defined in terms of its comparability with the initial study and its aims.

The label "conceptual replication" is likely the most familiar. Schmidt (2009) defines conceptual replications as the "repetition of a test of a hypothesis or a result of earlier research work with different methods" (p. 91). From a quantitative methods perspective, we can think of conceptual replications as a way to revisit the internal and construct validity of a finding by modifying the study's operationalizations of key theoretical concepts (Finkel et al., 2017; Nosek & Errington, 2020; Slough & Tyson, 2024).

In this way, conceptual replications address the same research question(s) as the initial study, but often by using different methods. Wu and Fricke's (2025) conceptual replication of Jacob et al. (2018), for example, maintained the same research focus as the initial study (early-stage processing November 5, 2026 Routledge Handbook of Second Language Acquisition Kevin McManus, kevin.mcmanus@pitt.edu of derived vs. inflected words) but changed L1, L2, and aspects of the stimuli design. In this way, Wu and Fricke's conceptual replication intentionally varied the study's core constructs to investigate the extent to which Jacob's findings applied to a different kind of L1-L2 pairings (Chinese-English vs. Russian-German). Approximate and close replication studies include smaller amounts of modification than conceptual replications when compared to the initial study. In close/approximate replications, researchers often seek to contribute to the external validity of the initial study's conclusions. This is achieved by modifying elements of the initial study's procedures (population, contrasts, measurement strategies) to examine the extent to which specific and intentional variations shape the effect of interest. In other words, close and approximate replications allow us to learn something about the external validity or generalizability of a finding by intentionally modifying specific elements of the initial study's procedures (Finkel et al., 2017; Porte & McManus, 2019; Slough & Tyson, 2024).

The difference between close and approximate replication concerns the amount of intentional variation integrated into the design. For example, Ryan et al. (2024) reported a close replication of Tavakoli and Foster's (2008) investigation of narrative task design in L2 oral performance. Their close replication varied the L1 literacy level of the learner (advanced in the initial study, and low in the replication) while keeping other aspects of the initial study stable (e.g., L2 proficiency, independent variables, tasks).

In this way, their close replication investigated to what extent the initial study's findings additionally applied to L2 learners with low levels of L1 literacy. We can contrast this approach with November 5, 2026 Routledge Handbook of Second Language Acquisition Kevin McManus, kevin.mcmanus@pitt.edu approximate replication where up to two key variables of interest are modified in the replication (e.g., population and task). Pelzl et al. (2024), for example, conducted an approximate replication of Van Hell and Dijkstra (2002) in which two variables were modified. First, the population was modified to include Dutch-English bilinguals whereas the initial study included Dutch-English-French trilinguals. Second, the replication study was conducted online whereas the initial study was conducted in a lab. All other aspects of the initial study were retained and not intentionally modified. In this way, the replication examined to what extent the initial study's findings extended to (a) a different population, (i.e., bilinguals vs. trilinguals) and (b) differences in data collection modality.

The last approach to replication reviewed here is exact replication, in which the exact procedures of the initial study are precisely repeated without modification. The research aim goal in exact replication is to ascertain the replicability of the initial result.

That is, to what extent does exactly and precisely repeating the initial study's method produce the same result? This approach of replication is not possible in SLA and is presented for information. We cannot exactly repeat a previous study's method without there being modifications since in SLA we study people (Guttinger, 2020; Schmidt, 2009) and people are complex and time-variant.

Replication and qualitative methods

In the preceding sections, I introduced replication as a process of doing research to better understand prior studies that have been impactful (e.g., academically, theoretically, socially). To what extent is replication a viable, appropriate, and useful research method in empirical studies using qualitative methods?

In general, replication has not really been encouraged in qualitative research and is often seen as incompatible with the foundational assumptions of qualitative research (Casanave, 2012; Markee, 2017; Porte & Richards, 2012; Willig, 2019). There are several reasons for this position, including differing positions on "truth", reality, interpretation and researcher subjectivity. Interpretation, for example, is a key tool in qualitative research, which is informed by the researcher's stance and their approach to interpretation. Pownall (2024) argues that subjectivity and positionality are so intentional and inherent to the

November 5, 2026 Routledge Handbook of Second Language Acquisition Kevin McManus, mailto:kevin.mcmanus@pitt.edu research process that replication is neither feasible nor desirable.

In addition, qualitative research often aims to study "exceptional phenomena" (Tuval-Mashiach, 2021, p. 368) in which circumstances appear unique in order to learn about individuals with particularly distinctive qualities or life experiences (Duff, 2025; Sandelowski, 1986).

Overall, these concerns appear appropriate. However, I don't think they are unique to qualitative SLA research. Claims of replication incompatibility are at least partly rooted in a traditional view of replication as referring to exact replication only. In other words, replications that seek to provide identical findings and context-free generalization (i.e., the replicability aim). These understandings of replication are (a) rarely practiced in SLA and (b) not achievable (Porte & McManus, 2019). Rather, in contemporary SLA, replication refers to the systematic revisiting of one or more aspects of an earlier study (its methods, participants, sites, or claims) in order to corroborate, elaborate, contrast, and clarify. On this account, the value of qualitative replication lies in clarifying where interpretations converge, diverge, become more differentiated, or reveal their contextual limits, thereby supporting the cumulative development of knowledge.

Indeed, some of the aims of replication research as defined here are related to several important concepts in qualitative research, including transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Bitsch, 2005; Krefting, 1991; Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Markee, 2017). First, transferability is the degree to which qualitative research findings can be transferred to other contexts or settings conducted among other respondents.

The focus here is on whether the results of a previous study apply to a new setting. Second, dependability is November 5, 2026 Routledge Handbook of Second Language Acquisition Kevin McManus, kevin.mcmanus@pitt.edu 11 defined as the stability of findings over time, with the expectation that what should be expected is variability over consistency of findings. This is because qualitative research emphasizes the uniqueness of the human situation, so that variation in experience rather than identical repetition is sought. Third, confirmability is defined as the degree to which the findings of study can be confirmed by other researchers and are clearly derived from and embedded in the data. Indeed, these principles have entered into some proposals for how replication studies can play a role in qualitative research. Talkad Sukumar & Metoyer (2019) suggest, for example, that a qualitative replication study repeats one of more aspects of a prior study (e.g., methods, participants) and embeds within its findings an interpretive comparison with a view to corroborate, elaborate, contrast, or clarify the elements corresponding to the replicated aspects with those of the earlier study (see also East, 2021; Kim, 2025; Markee, 2017).

Taken together, replication serves to broaden the perspective of prior findings to facilitate accumulation of knowledge.

Dissemination

Reporting and sharing your replication project are essential parts of doing research too, so we need to build in time for dissemination. SLA researchers regularly participate in different kinds of dissemination, including conference presentations and journal articles.

Presentations

Giving a presentation about your research is the most recommended and useful first step for dissemination. Indeed, about 75% of participants in McManus' (2022a) survey of replication practices reported presenting a replication study at a meeting or conference.

Moving on to the presentation itself (paper or poster), attention to communicating the replication study's aims, research design and methods, findings, and interpretations is still needed. Practical Relevance

This section reviews the ways in which replication studies are relevant to instruction and other domains such as assessment, a topic that goes beyond the scope of this chapter.

Throughout this chapter, I have discussed replication studies as being essential to the growth of the discipline because they allow us to ask many different kinds of questions that are not available to most other types of research (e.g., revisiting a prior result, building confidence, adding nuance, exploring additional factors). Replication studies encourage us November 5, 2026 Routledge Handbook of Second Language Acquisition Kevin McManus, kevin.mcmanus@pitt.edu to focus on a single study in a line of research with the aim to carefully consider its research design and methods, the types of results it generated, what those results mean, as well any implications arising from the study's results. Furthermore, replication studies are key to cumulative knowledge building because no single study can possibly examine all relevant variables on a finding, so they allow researchers to build knowledge, refine and test theories, and further understanding in principled ways.

Building confidence

Replication studies are practically relevant because they allow us to build confidence around results before resources are used to invest in them (Tarone et al., 1976). Indeed, the field has witnessed debates about the relevance or impact of SLA research findings on classroom teaching for decades. It has been repeatedly noted that replication studies are needed before we can begin to extend or apply SLA results to the classroom. Why is this so? Replication studies allow us to tease apart chance findings from observations that appear more stable. SLA researchers are constantly developing new instructional approaches that build on prior work in diverse ways. Replication studies allow us to build a case around a particular instructional technique. In other words, replication allows us to better understand an instructional technique, when it appears to work, when it does not, and how it might vary across contexts. These are critical steps to building confidence in a known finding or approach before extending and/or applying it.

For example, VanPatten (1990) asked whether L2 learners of Spanish can attend to form and meaning during L2 input processing, a result that has important implications for classroom learning. The study indicated that processing for meaning and form at the same time is difficult for learners. Instructors should consider modifying the input in some ways.

Given the relevance of this study for classroom learning, VanPatten's ( 1990) study has been replicated several times, with each one adding new understanding to this initial result. For example, replicating VanPatten (1990) in aural and written modalities but with L2 English did not lead to the same results (Wong, 2001). Greenslade et al. (1999) found that attending to aural forms interfered with comprehension, but not in writing. Leow et al.'s (2008) findings were consistent with Wong (2001), indicating no impact on comprehension.

However, Leow et al. (2008) identified several methodological concerns with VanPatten's (1990) study that were addressed in the replication. When these artifacts were ironed out, no between-group differences were found. Another replication confirmed Leow et al.'s (2008) findings (Morgan-Short et al., 2012), suggesting that methodological limitations in the initial study had likely resulted in several misleading conclusions for classroom instruction (see also Morgan-Short et al., 2018). Thus, replication of this important and instructionally relevant line of research has better informed research. It suggests that methodological limitations in the initial study may have impacted those early conclusions, but when these were ironed out different patterns of results began to emerge.

It is still the case that many types of SLA research are conducted in labbased contexts (e.g., outside of regular classroom time, without instructors). While these studies hold important implications for L2 learning, the realities of authentic classrooms are quite different from the tightly controlled experimental conditions of the research lab. Replication studies that repeat lab-based studies in the classroom have strong potential to tell us in what ways lab-based findings extend and can be used in the classroom. What we tend to see is that instructional effects are much less pronounced in the classroom than in the lab, for several reasons. For example, the populations studied in lab-based studies tend be relatively homogenous by design, whereas classroom studies include populations than are more diverse in all senses. Similarly, lab-based research tends to reduce realworld distractions (e.g., completing a task in a sound-proof both), whereas classrooms can be full of many kinds of distractions. These factors can make very clear effects from lab-November 5, 2026 Routledge Handbook of Second Language Acquisition Kevin McManus, kevin.mcmanus@pitt.edu 21 based research less impactful in the classroom, which highlight the relevance of replications for instruction. A relevant to example to illustrate this point is Gass, Mackey and Ross-Feldman.

While not a replication study, the study set out to examine the ways in which L2 learners' task-based interactions compare in the classroom with the lab. The study included university-level English-speaking learners of L2 Spanish who completed three interactive information exchange tasks (picture differences task, consensus task, and a map task) in dyads in a research lab context or an intact classroom context. The results showed very few differences between the two contexts for three interactional features (negotiation for meaning, language-related episodes, and recasts), disagreeing with claims that the types of interactional features used in lab and classroom settings are not the same (e.g., Foster, 1998).

Future directions

In this chapter , I have discussed and reviewed what replication studies are, what roles they play in the growth and development of the field, as well as recommended practices for replication study design and dissemination.

In this section, I outline future directions for replication studies in SLA. First, replications should be integrated into existing programs of research program rather than one-off studies. Similarly to research synthesis, replications can be November 5, 2026 Routledge Handbook of Second Language Acquisition Kevin McManus, kevin.mcmanus@pitt.edu 22 most useful at the beginning of a new line of research, including as part of graduate student training. Replications play a central role in revisiting a key study to better understand its methods and aims, contextualize its findings, and inform future work in that line of research. Through replication, researchers take away a much firmer understanding of work in that area and the nature of a previous study's methods and limits to the findings. Doing replication research allows us to contribute to cumulative knowledge building in a principled way. In addition, our decisions about what to replicate become intentional and principled and less opportunistic or idiosyncratic. As reviewed in this chapter, the motivations for replication study designs are not always clear, but they need to be in order to better contribute to the field. We can address this weakness by integrating replications into the regular workflow of research program development.

Second, there has been debate about the relevance and utility of replication studies outside of quantitative methods (Kim, 2025; Liu et al., 2026), which may have harmed the conduct of mixed-methods and qualitative replication studies. It has been claimed that the aims of replication research are in tension with some of the aims and epistemological bases of qualitative and mixed-methods research. For example, Casanave (2012) has argued that replication is suited to phenomena that are "relatively fixed or stable" (p. 297), adding that "if a replication is not as exact as possible, then it can hardly be called a replication" (p. 296). In addition, Matsuda (2012) has argued that replication presupposes a world "that can be observed without any biases" (p. 300). As discussed in this chapter, replication studies allow one or more aspects of an earlier study to be revisited as way to corroborate, elaborate, contrast, or clarify. Since all SLA research involves people, places, and objects that vary in space and time, there is no part of SLA that is fixed or stable. On this view, intentionally varying or modifying one or more features of an earlier study is a defining part of the replication approach. Aiming for exactness in replication is now widely understood to be unhelpful, unrealistic, and not achievable in human-subject research (Liu et al., 2026; Nosek & Errington, 2020). For this reason, no replication study in SLA should expect or seek identical findings or context-free generalization. What replication studies do allow, therefore, is an opportunity to study how a previous result or observation takes shape when it is studied again. Going forward, it will be healthy and beneficial for the field to expand the use of replication beyond quantitative methods only, with appropriate recalibrations in line with a study's epistemological commitments. Third, researchers should direct careful attention to replication study planning and reporting. Both study planning and reporting have been reviewed in this chapter since both have strong potential to improve the quality of replication studies in the field. When it comes to replication study planning, researchers are recommended to begin with the replication study planning questions provided in Appendix A. These questions can help researchers work through major components in the design of their replication, especially with regard to the value and impact of both the initial study and the replication. In terms of study reporting, the reporting standard for replication studies in McManus (2024b) is a useful starting point. The goal here is to assist researchers to more clearly (a) identify the study as a replication, (b) communicate the unique contributions of the study replication study, (c) describe all relevant similarities and differences between the studies, including design, methods, results, and (d) discuss the value and potential impact of the replication for the field. The reporting standard walks researchers through each part of a study report so that the unique contributions of the replication can be clearly shared with the field. Fourth, researchers are urged to fully share all materials, all data, and all analysis protocols and code. Researchers were once encouraged to share their data collection materials, the research data, and their analysis protocols and/or code using trusted repositories like the Instruments for Research in Language Studies database (https://www.iris-database.org). Today, however, these open practices are now an expected component of the research process. In other words, it is how we do research. It is thus important that researchers take the necessary steps at study planning so that this important part of dissemination proceeds unhindered. A study published today without open materials, open data, and open analysis protocols and/or code reduces its contribution to future scholarship. For one, it is extremely difficult to replicate a study without open materials. While some materials could be recreated, most cannot. From my perspective, a study that does not have the potential to be replicated contributes little to the scientific record, no matter how unexpected or revolutionary the results. In addition, I think that decisions about what to replicate should be based on data availability. I see potential here more studies are making data available. This will be an important step forward because it will allow replication studies to integrate analysis of the initial study's data into the full analysis of the replication. This is a powerful resource for replication because it allows clearer statements and understandings about similarities and differences in the results, not only in terms of statistics, but also in terms of visualizations and even data coding. The field has come a very long way in the conduct of replication studies in the last ten years. We are beginning to see more published replications in the field. More resources and methodological discussions about replication exist, which has likely made an important contribution to this change. By engaging in this work, it is clear to see that we are beginning to be more rigorous and accountable not only in the topics we research, but also in how and why we do research.
