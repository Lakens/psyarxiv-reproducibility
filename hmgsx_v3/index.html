Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

How Data Check works

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Auditing the measurement quality of societal progress: A 6-criterion framework applied to 99 indicators

Global governance runs on indicators, be it by governments to identify gaps and set goals, by international agencies to target and evaluate interventions, or by researchers to measure and track societal change. Pragmatically, indicators must also reliably track change across populations to drive timely intervention in critical domains. The United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were in part designed to address related shortcomings in the Millennium Development Goals, expanding from 48 to 233 indicators co-selected by national and international agencies, to measure change more precisely [1, 2]. However, it remains an open question if public measures of progress (such as the SDGs) meet the minimum criteria needed before their validity can even be assessed: whether they can be inspected, whether data exists, and if this data is kept current to track the world's most pressing challenges. Here, we aim to start filling this gap.

Assessing whether an indicator tracks change validly depends on two prerequisites: how it is conceived, and the data it relies on. First, an indicator may inadequately capture the theoretical construct it claims to measure. Gross domestic product (GDP), for instance, is often treated as a holistic marker of progress despite its narrow scope and warnings by critics and its own architect [3, 4]. Similarly, early versions of the Human Development Index were constructed to replace the GDP's misuse by capturing broader dimensions of human well-being (e.g., longevity, education, income; [5]. Yet aggregation of distinct dimensions into a single index obscured the relations between them by weighting them equally. To assess an indicator's validity, social science methodologists have long advocated for the conceptual clarity guided by adequate quantification and transparent documentation on how indicators are composed or estimated [6, 7]. Yet such debates presuppose that an indicator is defined, documented, and publicly available; whether commonly used indicators meet even these prerequisites is rarely examined. Second, a well-constructed indicator cannot reliably measure global progress if its data is incomplete or too sparse. How is one to assess the validity of SDG indicators when country coverage varies from 80% for clean energy to under 20% for climate action, with nearly a third of indicators not updated in the last few years [8]?

These concerns are not necessarily novel nor unique to global governance. Consider the flawed demographic projections of runaway population growth in China, which provided the technical justification for the disastrous one-child policy [9]. Or consider how modern public and private sectors alike often rely on inadequately specified sustainability targets and poorly operationalized performance metrics, constraining program efforts [10]. Others have discussed how weak national statistical systems [8] and administrative decisions to curtail data infrastructures may undermine assessment of the validity for global progress indicators [e.g., 11-13]. However, these common critiques have chiefly focused on single indicators or issues [e.g., [14] [15] [16], leaving open how a broad set of commonly used indicators perform against the basic prerequisites outlined above.

Several existing frameworks have attempted to address these prerequisites. For instance, the OECD/JRC Handbook [17] addresses the construction of composite indices, including metadata, comparability and robustness. In turn, the FAIR principles [18] concern data infrastructure standards, and the Cape Town Global Action Plan [19] has addressed gaps in national statistical systems. Finally, European Statistical System (ESS) and IMF frameworks , 21] set comprehensive quality criteria largely for their own assessment (which may preclude valid global comparisons of indicators), whereas IAEG-SDG tier system [2] grades SDG indicators on methodological maturity and data availability.

Because each of these frameworks addresses only part of the problem, we sought to adapt their central principles into a compact audit framework to assess whether indicators meet minimum criteria to assess validity as global measures of progress (see Table 1). Drawing from publicly accessible data, scoring each criterion separable, and applying the same rules to heterogenous produces allows informative yet quick comparison across diverse sets of indicators.

From the aims and principles of existing frameworks, we have derived six prerequisites to address issues in assessing indicator validity: definability, quantifiability, public data availability, population coverage, documentation and annual updating. First, the construct must be definable, so that everyone is talking about the same phenomenon and the indicator can be quantifiable, with specified units and procedures. Data must be available in practice, since specified procedures are only as useful as the numbers they produce. An indicator aspiring to measure humanity's progress ought to adequately sample from at least half the world's population (similar to the IAEG's criterion, [2]) so that conclusions about "global progress" do not simply reflect the experience of a handful of nations. Documentation must be transparent, so that independent analysts can audit estimation methods and understand indicator composition. Finally, indicators require temporal resolution of at least annual updates within the last decade (since indicator coding in 2024), providing a feedback loop policymakers can use to detect emerging risks or verify whether an intervention is working. This threshold follows from the monitoring purpose rather than from measurement quality per se: an indicator updated every five years cannot register a shock, or a response to it, until long after decisions have been made. We consider these criteria to be minimal but necessary requirements for beginning to evaluate whether an indicator may be a valid measure of global progress.

To evaluate our audit's utility, we applied the framework to 99 candidate indicators spanning eight domains of progress and six additional domains proposed by leading field experts. We assembled them from two sources: 62 nominated by 24 experts primarily from social and economic sciences, augmented by 37 indicators pre-selected by the research team based on common policy and research use. Two coders independently identified the best online data source for each indicator, scoring definability, quantifiability, and data availability sequentially before assessing the remaining data-level criteria in parallel. In doing so, the audit extends to societal indicators the scrutiny recently directed at validity and measurement practice within psychology [22], and provides an openly available, reusable assessment of the metrics on which claims about human progress rest. Discussion

Questions about how to track societal progress are central to concerns about the present and the future. Despite concerted efforts by individual nations and the global community to understand the direction of societal progress, our systematic audit reveals that fewer than onethird of 99 expert-nominated or commonly-used indicators satisfy the six basic prerequisites for assessing measurement validity.

Indicators that did meet these criteria appear to share institutional and methodological advantages. Many originate from long-established institutions (e.g., NASA, NOAA, IMF) with existing mandates for standardization, transparency, and cross-national comparability. Metrics from these organizations also reflect clearly defined, quantifiable constructs with welldocumented procedures. Many also draw on near-continuous monitoring systems with extensive global coverage. Even political indicators from Freedom House and V-Dem Institute benefit from standardized definitions and annual updates covering most states, suggesting even more "human" and holistic markers may achieve the same rigor.

Perhaps most importantly, documentation and temporal resolution criteria appeared as major bottlenecks in indicator success in our audit. Institutions such as NASA, the World Bank, and Freedom House routinely publish open-access data, metadata, and methodological documentation, necessary for open critique, targeted usage for intervention, or replication. Though temporal resolution may be more difficult to achieve, given resources and time, a measure of progress must consistently track temporal change, which may be achieved from routine data collection and/or sound estimate extrapolation. Assessing the validity of these timepoints in turn requires freely accessible documentation, so that anyone can evaluate an indicator's methods and composition.

Steps forward to indicator development for actionable policy

The audit points to concrete improvements that producers and funders can make to improve indicators. Given most failures appeared for documentation and temporal resolution criteria, the most direct improvements producers can make are providing more accessible methods documentation (in estimation and composition) and improving update schedules to capture trends from noise [31, 32]. At the same time, clearer definitions, population coverage reporting, and source-selection rules may reduce failures of the other criteria. These requirements could be checked routinely against public materials, as in our audit. Where an indicator claims to predict future outcomes, it could also be tested against simple forecasting baselines. Descriptive indicators call for different evidence, such as agreement with reference measurements, consistency across sources, or sensitivity to meaningful change.

Beyond these steps, we offer two more speculative recommendations. Given political interference [11] [12] [13], an independent body could register indicator protocols in advance and publish periodic scorecards against criteria like ours, which may matter most for indicators controlled by private firms with little incentive to disclose their methods. To improve data production, emerging big data sources, such as satellite imagery, administrative data partnerships, and low-cost sensor networks, may also extend coverage where traditional surveys are infrequent, such as nations with weak national statistical systems. Open-data licenses and version-controlled repositories attached to funding for such sources would lock transparency into the infrastructure, allowing at-will data auditing and discouraging retreating from this system. However, such systems necessitate intergovernmental commitments to long-term maintenance to ensure systematic temporal resolution of critical societal phenomena.

Limitations

It is possible that our team, dominated by psychologists and forecasting scientists, may have lacked the specialized expertise and knowledge needed to access and evaluate some technical indicators fully. If so, the audit may underestimate the share of indicators that meet our criteria; conversely, retaining the higher-scoring data source when coders disagreed may have inflated it. Future research and audit evaluations should directly engage with domain-specific experts to refine these evaluations further and move toward direct assessment of indicator validity.

We deliberately set the temporal threshold at annual updating because our audit asked whether indicators could support timely responses to societal challenges and shocks; by this standard, a series updated every three to five years would arrive too late to inform action, however well it would be measuring its construct. Some indicators that failed this criterion, particularly for slow-moving constructs such as educational attainment or values measured in periodic surveys, may therefore be adequate for describing long-run change but inadequate for rapid response. Conversely, update frequency is not the only barrier to timeliness: an annually updated series published several years after its reference period offers little more feedback than a five-yearly one. Because we did not assess publication lag, the audit may overstate the share of indicators that can support rapid response.

Another caveat concerns the effort expert participants devoted to nominating domains and indicators. Greater deliberation might have produced more reliable suggestions, but it is unclear whether additional time would have reduced bias (systematic differences such as favouring climate over economic indicators due to one's political ideology) or merely noise (random variability in judgments; [33]). Noise can also originate in the indicators themselves when the societal phenomena they track fluctuate in ways that current instruments cannot capture.

Conclusion

In an era where data integrity is increasingly threatened by political pressures and misrepresentation, transparent, rigorous, and globally inclusive measurement frameworks become indispensable. Only by building on that foundation can progress indicators move from pundits' talking points to trustworthy guides for human welfare.
