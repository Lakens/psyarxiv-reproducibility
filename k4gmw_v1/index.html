Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p > .25 None of the four wave × predictor interactions tested for two rights was significant (all p > .25; Appendix D2).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .11 Behavioural restraint did not change (d = -0.07, p = .11).
p = .66 Social media and web-use data did not change (d = 0.02, p = .66). B3.
p > .25 None of the four wave × predictor interactions tested for two rights was significant (all p > .25; Appendix D2).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

European data protection law gives people rights over their personal data, but what motivates us to exercise those rights? In three surveys of Estonian adults (2023-2025), we examined whether people who care about privacy also know their data protection rights. Respondents valued privacy far more than they felt they had it and regarded all seven types of personal data asked about as sensitive; valuing privacy, attention to privacy, experienced privacy and security, and perceived data sensitivity rose slightly over the period. Knowledge of data rights, by contrast, was low, did not rise, and was unrelated to privacy and data sensitivity. The same five combinations of sensitivity and knowledge recurred in all three waves, and sociodemographic characteristics explained little of them. Caring about personal data and privacy were thus unrelated to knowing one's rights, so efforts should be made to explain and bridge the gap between privacy/data concerns and the individual opportunities to mitigate them.

Introduction

The large-scale collection and use of personal data shifts power towards those who collect it (van Dijck, 2014; Zuboff, 2019): they set the rules for how data is accessed and analysed, while privacy protection tends to treat individuals at best as passive data subjects (Mällo et al., 2026). People increasingly take part in this arrangement under compulsion rather than by choice: providing personal data has become a condition for using schools, health care, banks and public services (Barassi, 2019). As these services move online, the law that protects citizens becomes invisible to them, and those who cannot see it are less able to use their rights on equal terms (Ranchordás, 2024).

European data protection law responds largely by giving individuals rights: to access their data, to correct and delete it, to restrict and object to its processing, and not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 15-22). This places much of the work of protection on individuals. Solove (2013) argues that such self-management, though necessary, is asked to do more than it can: people cannot weigh every data relationship, and many harms arise only when data is combined. Yet as more decisions about people are based on data, their ability to see, understand and contest its use becomes increasingly important (Lehtiniemi and Haapoja, 2020), and the regulation itself aims to give people control of their own data (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Recital 7). Rights that people do not know about, however, cannot be used (Potter et al., 2025). The rights-based model therefore depends partly on people knowing their rights. We ask whether this knowledge grows over time and whether people who care about privacy are more likely to have it. We examine these questions in three surveys of Estonian adults conducted between 2023 and 2025.

Caring and knowing

Using your rights requires a reason to act and the means to act or, in terms of protection motivation theory, a threat appraisal and a coping appraisal (Rogers, 1975). In privacy research the first is conceptualized as privacy concern and the second as online privacy literacy, which includes knowledge of privacy rights and law as well as of protective strategies (Prince et al., 2023). More concerned people have been found to have greater privacy literacy (Prince et al., 2023; Weinberger et al., 2017) and to know more about privacy law (Hoofnagle and Urban, 2014). These studies, however, measured privacy literacy broadly. Knowledge also need not bring control: GDPR awareness has been negatively associated with perceived control over personal data (Prince et al., 2024). Concern may also be general rather than informed: people tend to see abstract privacy risks as likely but know little about the specific harms that collected data can cause (Gerber et al., 2019).

Studies of digital resignation, privacy apathy and privacy cynicism describe people who are concerned about the collection of their data but feel unable to act (Draper and Turow, 2019; Hargittai and Marwick, 2016; Hoffmann et al., 2016), including towards the state, whose digital services are hard to opt out of (Bagger et al., 2023). When a threat is perceived but the means to act are not, people tend to respond defensively, for example by avoiding the issue (Witte, 1992).

The digital resignation literature also suggests that people may value privacy without experiencing it. This attitude-experience gap concerns whether people feel that their privacy is respected in the environments they use, and differs from the privacy paradox, a gap between attitudes and behaviour (Barth and de Jong, 2017).

We study three constructs. Privacy sensitivity is how much people value privacy and act on it. We measure it together with the experience of privacy, how secure and private people feel in digital environments, to capture the gap between the two. Data sensitivity is how sensitive people consider different kinds of personal data; it is measured separately for each data type, because sensitivity varies by data type and context (Chen et al., 2025). Rights knowledge is whether people know which data protection rights are in force, a basic layer of the capability to act on one's data that a survey can measure. Below, caring refers to the two kinds of sensitivity and knowing to rights knowledge.

Because using rights requires both, what matters is how privacy and data sensitivity and rights knowledge combine within individuals. Westin's typology of privacy fundamentalists, pragmatists and unconcerned uses attitudes alone and assumes that people know how their data is protected (Hoofnagle and Urban, 2014). When tested, most respondents misunderstood these protections, and Urban and Hoofnagle (2014) proposed separating a more knowledgeable "privacy resilient" group from a less knowledgeable "privacy vulnerable" one. European typologies have combined GDPR awareness with digital experience (Rughinis , et al., 2021) and the importance of privacy with GDPR knowledge (Hanneke et al., 2023), but each was estimated in a single survey only.

Caring and knowing in a highly digital society: Research gaps and the Estonian case

Previous research leaves four gaps. First, repeated population-level measurement is limited. Eurobarometer surveys have tracked GDPR awareness since 2019, but with changing wording and survey modes, and mostly ask whether people have heard of the regulation rather than which rights they know (European Commission, 2019a , 2024). National studies of rights knowledge are usually single cross-sections (Sideri and Gritzalis, 2020; Strycharz et al., 2020); four Norwegian surveys over five years found little change after the GDPR took effect (Presthus and Sørum, 2024). Second, the link between caring and knowing has rarely been tested for knowledge of rights. Third, privacy typologies are rarely estimated in more than one sample.

Fourth, findings on who cares and who knows are partly mixed. GDPR awareness is predicted by education, occupation and age but little by gender, and sociodemographic characteristics together explain only a modest share of it (Rughinis , et al., 2021; Sideri and Gritzalis, 2020). Age effects vary by context: younger Estonians more often noticed individual online privacy violations (Männiste and Masso, 2018), but were also more tolerant of corporate surveillance (older ones were more tolerant of state surveillance (Kalmus et al., 2024).

Estonia is a useful test case: everyday contact with personal data is more routine there than in most countries. Nearly all public services are available online (e-Estonia, n.d.), and more than nine in ten internet users interact with public authorities online (Eurostat, 2024). An effectively mandatory digital identity (Tsap et al., 2017) and a common data exchange layer (Solvak et al., 2019) make contact with state-held personal data routine. Hence, if such contact builds knowledge of data rights, this should show in Estonia. Use, however, often outpaces understanding: Estonians knew little about their rights regarding automated decision-making while being the most enthusiastic of three countries about automating public administration (Kaun et al., 2024). In 2024, 29% of Estonians had heard of the GDPR and knew what it is, against 40% across the EU (European Commission, 2024), and their awareness of specific rights was close to the EU average, although they were more than twice as likely to report having exercised their right of access (39% vs 18%; European Commission, 2019a). The Data Tracker, which shows which public institutions have accessed one's data in selected registries (Solvak and Lauringson, 2024), had by late 2023 been heard of by 41% of residents and used by 13%, fewer among non-Estonians (Ministry of Economic Affairs and Communications [MEAC], 2023). In short, Estonians use digital services extensively but know relatively little about the rules that govern personal data.

To address these gaps, we formulated five research questions (RQ): RQ1. How much do Estonians value privacy compared with how private and secure they feel online, how sensitive do they consider their personal data, and how well do they know their data protection rights?

RQ2. Did privacy sensitivity, data sensitivity and rights knowledge change between 2023 and 2025?

RQ3. Are privacy sensitivity and rights knowledge related: do those who care more about privacy know more about their rights?

RQ4. What combinations of sensitivity and rights knowledge characterise the population, and do the same combinations recur across the three waves?

RQ5. How are these measures and combinations associated with gender, age, ethnicity, education and income?

Methods

Study design

We conducted three online surveys of Estonian adults aged 18 and over: Wave 1 in June 2023 (n = 1,503), Wave 2 in October 2023 (n = 1,702) and Wave 3 in December 2025 (n = 1,005), all conducted by Saar Poll, an Estonian social and market research company, in the Norstat Estonia online panel, with quotas for gender, age group, ethnicity, education and county of residence (Appendix A2). The questionnaire was available in Estonian and Russian.

In late summer 2023, a research project used Estonian population register data to contact childless women about the reasons for their childlessness, prompting wide public debate (Pärli, 2023). We brought Wave 2 forward to October 2023 to study its possible effects. All Wave 1 respondents were invited to Wave 2 and 947 took part; new respondents filled the quotas (Appendix A2). Wave 3 was a new sample.

We treat the waves as cross-sections: change is tested only between the independent Waves 1 and 3, collected 30 months apart, and Wave 2 is used descriptively and in the latent class models. Longitudinal analyses of Waves 1 and 2 and the effects of the incident are reported separately (Authors, in preparation).

The items analysed here were developed for these surveys (the data types were adapted from Puusalu and Marnot, 2021) and worded identically in all waves; the full questionnaires are in Supplementary File S2.

Sample

Waves 1 and 2 were similar in composition (Table 1). Wave 3 had more respondents in the lower income group (42% vs 31% in Wave 1) and slightly fewer with higher education (32% vs 36%). Its income question was also worded differently, with different bands (Appendix A1), so part of the income difference may be due to the question. All comparisons between Waves 1 and 3 were therefore repeated with sociodemographic characteristics controlled. People who rarely use the internet are not represented, but over 90% of Estonian adults aged 16-74 use it regularly (Eurostat, 2025). All analyses are unweighted (Appendix A2).

Measures

Established multidimensional instruments of privacy concern (Malhotra et al., 2004; Smith et al., 1996) have not fully held up in independent validation (Groß, 2021), and as the surveys also covered other topics, we used a shorter set of items. Privacy sensitivity was measured with five statements on a four-point agreement scale (1-4): privacy is important to me in general (PRIV1); I pay close attention to ensuring my privacy and security in digital environments (PRIV2); I have sometimes refrained from doing something online or on my phone because it leaves a trace (PRIV3); I feel secure in all kinds of digital environments (PRIV4); and I feel private in all kinds of digital environments (PRIV5). PRIV1 to PRIV3 measure privacy sensitivity and PRIV4 and PRIV5 the experience of privacy. Because their means differ by up to one scale point, they are analysed individually. A sixth statement (Waves 1 and 2 only) is not analysed.

Data sensitivity was measured by asking respondents to compare seven types of personal data and rate how sensitive each is for them (1-4): financial, communication, health, family and contact details, basic identifiers (name, personal identification code and date of birth), location, and social media and web-use data. The items formed a reliable scale in every wave (Cronbach's 𝛼 = .86, .84 and .79; McDonald's 𝜔 similar) and are analysed individually and as a mean composite. Genetic data (Waves 1 and 2 only) is excluded.

Rights knowledge was measured by asking which of six GDPR rights respondents believed to be in force in Estonia: to access one's data, to restrict its processing, to receive a copy, to correct and delete it, to object to conclusions drawn from it, and not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing (Articles 15-18, 21 and 22). All six are in force, so "in force" is the correct answer, and the knowledge score counts the rights identified as in force (0-6). For the rights, "don't know" was kept as a separate answer, because uncertainty is part of what we measure; for the sensitivity items it was treated as missing.

The sociodemographic characteristics were gender, age, ethnicity (self-reported, Estonian or other), education and income. Waves 1 and 2 also measured risk-taking propensity and general trust (single items, 0-10) and satisfaction with five life domains (four-point scale), analysed for Wave 1 to check whether the patterns reflect general dispositions rather than orientations towards privacy.

Data analysis

Comparisons and associations. Means of the privacy items and data sensitivity were compared between Waves 1 and 3 with independent-samples t-tests (Cohen's d), and answers for each right with 𝜒 2 tests (df = 2; Cramér's V). Because the samples differed in composition, each comparison was repeated in a linear model controlling for age (continuous), gender, ethnicity, education and income, with identifying a right as in force as the outcome for the rights measure (Appendix D1).

Relations among the constructs were estimated within each wave with Pearson correlations and 95% confidence intervals, using pairwise deletion (Appendix B6). Privacy sensitivity was represented by importance (PRIV1) and behavioural restraint (PRIV3), its attitudinal and behavioural expressions. Attention to privacy (PRIV2) and the experience items (PRIV4, PRIV5) were correlated with knowledge separately.

Associations with sociodemographic characteristics, and in Wave 1 with the psychological measures, were tested within Waves 1 and 3, one characteristic at a time, with t-tests, one-way ANOVAs (𝜂 2 ) and, for the rights, 𝜒 2 tests (Cramér's V). Differences between waves in these associations were tested only for the two rights whose associations with gender and income differed most (wave × predictor interactions; Appendix D2); other such differences are not interpreted.

With samples of 1,000 to 1,700, small effects reach significance, so we interpret only effects of at least d = 0.20 or 𝜂2 = .01. All tests use 𝛼 = .05 without correction for multiple comparisons. Analyses were conducted in JASP 0.95.4 (JASP Team, 2025) and IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 31 (IBM Corp., 2026).

Latent class analysis. Latent class analysis (LCA) identified groups with distinct combinations of sensitivity and rights knowledge. Models were estimated separately for each wave with 18 categorical indicators: the five privacy items, the seven data types and the six rights (model specification in Appendix C0). We used poLCA (Linzer and Lewis, 2011) in R (4.1; R Core Team, 2025), retaining respondents with incomplete answers, and checked fit indices with snowRMM (Seol, 2023) in jamovi (2.4; The jamovi project, 2025).

Models with one to seven classes were compared on AIC, BIC, sample-size-adjusted BIC, entropy, smallest class size, convergence, interpretability and reproducibility across waves (Appendix C1). Some criteria kept improving beyond five classes in Waves 1 and 2, but the five-class solution was retained because its classes were distinct, interpretable and recurred in every wave. Classes were matched across waves by their conditional response profiles; measurement invariance was not tested formally (Appendix C2).

Respondents were assigned to the class with the highest posterior probability (modal assignment), which ignores classification uncertainty, so results based on it are interpreted cautiously. In each wave, class membership was regressed on the five sociodemographic characteristics in a multinomial logistic regression in JASP (likelihood-ratio tests, Nagelkerke R² ; Appendix C4), and associations with the psychological measures (Wave 1) were tested with Cramér's V.

Use of generative AI. We used Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic) and GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI) (by Author 1 and 2, respectively) in preparing this manuscript. Statistical output from JASP (aggregate results only; no raw data were shared) was entered into the tool to extract estimates and arrange them into tables and figures. The tool was also used to check the consistency of the Results and Discussion, to edit the text for language and brevity, and to format the references. The authors conducted all analyses, checked all AI-assisted output against the original output and sources, and take full responsibility for the content.

Results

We first describe results on privacy sensitivity, data sensitivity and rights knowledge (RQ1, RQ2), then how sensitivity and knowledge combine (RQ3, RQ4), and finally how they vary across sociodemographic groups (RQ5). Comparisons over time are made between Waves 1 and 3.

Privacy sensitivity

In every wave, respondents valued privacy more than they experienced it in digital environments (Figure 1). Importance of privacy (PRIV1) averaged 3.38 to 3.55, while feeling secure (PRIV4: 2.48 to 2.61) and feeling private (PRIV5: 2.33 to 2.50) were rated lowest, averaging close to the midpoint of the four-point scale (2.5), between 'rather disagree' and 'rather agree'. Attention to privacy and behavioural restraint fell in between. The gap between valuing and experiencing privacy, about one scale point (0.86 to 1.07), was larger than any change between waves (at most 0.28) and more than twice the largest difference between sociodemographic groups on any item (0.44; Appendix B2). B1.

Between Waves 1 and 3, attention to privacy (d = 0.41) and its importance (d = 0.29), feeling private (d = 0.22) and, slightly, feeling secure (d = 0.12) increased, also with sociodemographics controlled (Appendices B1, D1). Behavioural restraint did not change (d = -0.07, p = .11).

Data sensitivity

All seven data types were rated as sensitive (Figure 2): every mean was above 3, and the types differed by at most half a scale point. Financial, communication and health data were rated highest (3.35 to 3.58) and social media and web-use data lowest (3.06 and 3.08). Health data moved from third place in Wave 1 to first in Wave 3 (3.58, ahead of financial and communication data at 3.54).

Between Wave 1 and Wave 3, the composite rose from 3.24 to 3.40 (d = 0.28), also with sociodemographics controlled (Appendices B3, D1). Six of the seven data types increased in perceived sensitivity: most for health (d = 0.32), location (d = 0.30) and communication data (d = 0.23), and less for contact and family data (d = 0.20), financial data (d = 0.16) and basic identifiers (d = 0.14). Social media and web-use data did not change (d = 0.02, p = .66). B3.

Knowledge of GDPR rights

Knowledge varied widely by right (Figure 3). In Wave 1, most respondents knew the rights to access their data (62%), to restrict processing (59%) and to receive a copy (54%); fewer knew the rights to correct and delete their data (46%), to object to conclusions drawn from it (37%) and not to be subject to solely automated decisions (28%). Respondents who did not identify a right as in force mostly answered "don't know" rather than "not in force" (Appendix B5.1). On average, respondents identified fewer than three of the six rights (2.86 in Wave 1 and 2.65 in Wave 3).

Rights knowledge did not increase between Wave 1 and Wave 3. Five rights did not change (Cramér's V ≤ .04; Appendix B5.1). Knowledge of the right to restrict processing fell from 59% to 44% (𝜒 2 (2) = 55.13, p < .001, V = .15), also with sociodemographics controlled (Appendix D1). The lost share moved mainly to "don't know" (31% to 40%) and partly to "not in force" (10% to 16%).

How sensitivity and knowledge combine

Within each wave, importance of privacy (PRIV1), behavioural restraint as a privacy strategy (PRIV3) and data sensitivity were weakly to moderately related to one another (r = .20 to .37), but not to the number of rights identified (r = -.07 to .05; Appendix B6). All confidence intervals lay within ±.13, making larger correlations unlikely, and only one correlation reached significance (importance in Wave 3, r = -.07, p = .03). Attention to privacy (PRIV2), the item that rose most, was also unrelated to knowledge (r = -.05 to .04). Feeling secure and feeling private, by contrast, were positively correlated with knowledge in every wave (r = .19 to .26 and .14 to .20, all p < .001).

A five-class solution was retained in each wave (Appendix C1), with good classification quality (entropy = .88, .86 and .87) and no class below 9% of the sample. The same five combinations recurred in all three waves (Appendix C2). Three classes combined high data sensitivity with uncertain (CLASS-2), misinformed (CLASS-3) or accurate knowledge of rights (CLASS-4), and two combined moderate sensitivity with uncertain (CLASS-1) or accurate knowledge (CLASS-5); the privacy items differed less across the classes (see below). The uncertain and misinformed classes identified similarly few rights, but CLASS-1 and CLASS-2 mostly answered "don't know" and CLASS-3 "not in force". CLASS-1 in Wave 1 differed most from its later counterparts, with lower data sensitivity and more rights identified (Appendix C5), but it still matched CLASS-1 most closely.

Two measures separated the classes in every wave: class membership explained 56 to 64% of the variance in data sensitivity and 65 to 71% in rights knowledge, but only 4 to 18% in any privacy item. CLASS-4 and CLASS-5 identified around four of the six rights and CLASS-1, CLASS-2 and CLASS-3 fewer than two. On data sensitivity, CLASS-2, CLASS-3 and CLASS-4 averaged 3.2 to 3.8 (highly sensitive) and CLASS-1 and CLASS-5 2.1 to 2.9 (moderately sensitive). The two divisions cut across each other (Figure 4): CLASS-2 and CLASS-4 rated data almost equally sensitive in Waves 2 and 3 (3.66 vs 3.69; 3.62 vs 3.72), yet differed by more than three and a half rights in every wave. The privacy items differed in the same way in every wave: the three highly sensitive classes (CLASS-2 -CLASS-4) rated privacy as more important, paid more attention to it and more often refrained from online activities than CLASS-1 and CLASS-5, so privacy sensitivity followed the same division as data sensitivity. Feeling secure and private was lowest in CLASS-3, second lowest in CLASS-2 and highest in CLASS-5 (Figure 4; Appendix C5).

Class shares differed by no more than five percentage points between waves, and CLASS-4 and CLASS-5 were the two largest classes throughout (together 56 to 58%).

Sociodemographic patterning

Effect sizes are summarised in Appendices B2.0, B4.0 and B5.2, and full results are in Appendices B2.1-B2.5, B4.1-B4.8 and B5.3. All associations were small. The strongest explained about 6% of the variance (age and the sensitivity of basic identifiers in Wave 3, 𝜂2 = .06), the largest group difference was d = 0.45 (gender, basic identifiers), and the strongest association with any right was V = .23 (age, right to restrict processing).

Rights knowledge was associated with age and ethnicity for all six rights in both waves (V = .09 to .23): respondents under 45 and ethnic Estonians identified more rights. Gender was weakly associated with all rights in Wave 1 (V = .07 to .09), mainly because women more often answered "don't know" and men "not in force", while the shares answering "in force" differed by at most five percentage points. In Wave 3, women less often identified the rights to object to conclusions (32% vs 40%) and not to be subject to automated decisions (22% vs 28%), answering "don't know" instead (V = .16 and .18).

Women rated personal data as more sensitive than men (composite: d = 0.25 in Wave 1 and 0.30 in Wave 3; five of seven types in both waves) without identifying more rights. On the privacy items, ethnic Estonians reported more behavioural restraint in Wave 1 and felt more secure in both waves, women rated privacy as more important than men, and the youngest (18-24) rated it least important. Education was associated with almost nothing: 6 of 38 tests reached significance, all with negligible effects (𝜂2 ≤ .01, V ≤ .08).

In both waves, every subgroup rated the importance of privacy at least half a scale point above feeling secure or private (0.50 to 1.33 points; Appendix B2).

Sociodemographic characteristics explained little of class membership (Nagelkerke R² = .13, .16 and .18 in Waves 1 to 3). Age, gender and ethnicity contributed in all waves (p < .001), income weakly (p = .01 to .04) and education only in Wave 2 (Appendix C4). In every wave (Appendix C3), CLASS-4 was the youngest class (roughly a third under 35) and CLASS-1 and CLASS-2 the oldest (30 to 39% aged 65 or over); the classes with accurate knowledge, CLASS-4 and CLASS-5, had fewer respondents of other ethnicities (19 to 29%) than the other three (32 to 51%); and women were overrepresented in CLASS-2 and CLASS-4.

The psychological measures (Wave 1) were associated mainly with feeling secure and private (𝜂2 ≤ .03) and only weakly with class membership (Cramér's V = .06 to .10). None of the four wave × predictor interactions tested for two rights was significant (all p > .25; Appendix D2).

Discussion

Data and privacy sensitivity and data rights knowledge

Respondents cared about privacy and their personal data, but those who were more sensitive did not know their rights any better, and high data sensitivity occurred both with and without knowledge of rights (CLASS-2 and CLASS-4). Because the sensitivity measures were related to one another but not to knowledge, this is unlikely to reflect weak measures.

The concern was also general rather than informed: respondents rated all seven data types as sensitive, within half a scale point, and their ratings formed a single scale. People may thus regard their data as sensitive without knowing what each kind of data can reveal (Gerber et al., 2019) or what they can do about it. In terms of protection motivation theory (Rogers, 1975), respondents had a reason to act but often lacked the means, a situation in which people tend to respond defensively (Witte, 1992). Two results, discussed below, fit this reading, (although we did not measure efficacy beliefs or fear directly): those who knew fewer rights felt less secure and private, and between 2023 and 2025, privacy and data sensitivity rose while rights knowledge and behavioural restraint did not.

The decoupling also appeared at group level: the groups that knew more were not the groups that cared more. Respondents under 45 identified more rights, yet the youngest (18-24) rated privacy as least important; women rated personal data as more sensitive than men but did not identify more rights. This differs from earlier studies, in which people more concerned about privacy also knew more about it (Hoofnagle and Urban, 2014; Prince et al., 2023; Weinberger et al., 2017). Those studies measured knowledge broadly, as online privacy literacy or knowledge of privacy law and business practices; for knowledge of one's own data protection rights, we found no such link.

Knowledge of data rights

Five and seven and a half years after the GDPR came to full effect (Waves 1 and 3, respectively), respondents identified fewer than three of the six data rights on average, with no rise between 2023 and 2025, as in Norway (Presthus and Sørum, 2024). Because all rights were in force and presented for recognition, our measure is more likely to overstate than understate knowledge; recalling a right unprompted, or knowing how to use it, is harder.

As in other European surveys, the right not to be subject to solely automated decisions was the least known (European Commission, 2019a; Rughinis , et al., 2021; Strycharz et al., 2020), matching Estonians' limited knowledge of these rights despite their enthusiasm for e.g. automated public administration (Kaun et al., 2024a). This matters because automated decisions are becoming more common, including in public administration.

Only knowledge of the right to restrict processing changed, and it fell between Wave 1 and Wave 3. This may reflect growing acceptance of routine processing as a norm that cannot be contested, which this right most directly challenges, and would fit the resignation discussed below; but as only one item changed and the order of question blocks differed in Wave 3, this interpretation remains tentative.

Knowledge of rights was also unequal across sociodemographic groups. Younger respondents and ethnic Estonians knew more rights. As non-Estonians are also less aware of the Estonian Data Tracker (MEAC, 2023), information about data rights may reach them less well. Because the rights-based model protects those who know how to claim their rights, rights that formally apply to everyone are in practice less available to older and non-Estonian residents (Ranchordás, 2024). Education hardly mattered, although it predicts GDPR awareness in Europe (Rughinis , et al., 2021) and Data Tracker awareness in Estonia (MEAC, 2023), perhaps because we asked about specific rights rather than familiarity with a regulation or service.

We expected routine contact with personal data in Estonia to be reflected in better knowledge of rights, but this was not the case. Estonians were more than twice as likely as the EU average to report having exercised their right of access, yet no more aware of specific rights (European Commission, 2019a), perhaps counting routine viewing of their records in e-services as an instance of using that right. Such general self-reports probably overstate formal use of the right: when Dutch respondents were asked about specific actions rather than about rights in general, only 3% had formally requested access to their data (Strycharz et al., 2020). Even people who obtain their data through formal requests often struggle to make sense of it (Bowyer et al., 2022). eeing one's data does not by itself seem to build knowledge of rights. It may help if services present access to one's data explicitly as the use of a right, and of course, if people regard data as theirs.

The gap between valuing and experiencing privacy

Respondents valued privacy about one scale point more than they felt secure or private in digital environments, in every wave and group. Part of this may reflect wording, as the experience items refer to all kinds of digital environments. However, a gap of at least half a scale point in every group is unlikely to be a wording effect alone.

This gap concerns experience rather than behaviour and is in line with findings on digital resignation and privacy cynicism (Draper and Turow, 2019; Hargittai and Marwick, 2016; Hoffmann et al., 2016).

Resignation may also arise towards the state, which is harder to opt out of than commercial services (Bagger et al., 2023). Estonians trust public institutions with their data more than companies (Puusalu and Marnot, 2021), but few check how the state uses it: by late 2023, 13% had used the Data Tracker (MEAC, 2023). As our items did not distinguish public from commercial environments, whether the gap concerns either or both remains open.

Feeling secure and private, however, was related to knowledge of rights: classes with accurate knowledge felt more secure and private than classes with similar sensitivity but uncertain knowledge (CLASS-4 vs CLASS-2, CLASS-5 vs CLASS-1), and the misinformed CLASS-3 felt least so (Figure 4; Appendix C5). In a Europe-wide survey, by contrast, GDPR awareness was associated with lower perceived control over personal data (Prince et al., 2024). The findings need not conflict: learning how one's data is used can raise unease (Weinshel et al., 2019), while knowing what one can do about it may reduce it, as protection motivation theory would predict.

Part of this association may reflect ethnicity, although CLASS-3 felt least secure even in Wave 3, when its ethnic composition resembled that of the full sample. The direction of the association is unknown: knowing one's rights may make people feel protected, or feeling unprotected may make them doubt that rights exist, as CLASS-3's "not in force" answers suggest. Over time the two did not move together: between Waves 1 and 3, feeling private rose while rights knowledge did not, so the rise in feeling private cannot be attributed to better knowledge of rights.

Change and stability between 2023 and 2025 measurements

Between 2023 and 2025, most measures of privacy and data sensitivity rose while knowledge of rights did not. Attention to privacy rose most, but behavioural restraint, the only item about something respondents had actually done, did not change. Among data types, the perceived sensitivity of health and location data rose most, and health data moved from third to first place, having ranked below financial and communication data in 2021 (Puusalu and Marnot, 2021) and in Wave 1.

Several data cases were widely reported in Estonia between Waves 1 and 3. After the 2023 debate about the use of register data (see Methods), Wave 2 means, collected about seven weeks later, were close to those of Wave 1 (Figures 1 2 3). Later, a breach exposed the identification numbers, contact details and purchase histories of about 700,000 pharmacy customers (ERR, 2024), and the Chancellor of Justice questioned the legal basis of police number-plate cameras that record vehicle movements (ERR, 2025). Health and location data, which rose most, correspond to these two cases. Our design cannot show causation (see Limitations), but during repeated public data cases, sensitivity rose without any gain in knowledge of rights. Media coverage of such cases may make risks more visible, but it does not necessarily tell people what they can do.

The latent class structure, by contrast, did not change. The same five classes recurred in three waves over two and a half years, with no class share changing by more than five percentage points, which addresses the gaps noted in the Introduction. Whether individuals kept their class membership is examined in a a separate manuscript (Authors, in preparation).

The classes extend earlier typologies. CLASS-2, the largest class with poor knowledge, resembles the "privacy vulnerable" group of Urban and Hoofnagle (2014) but rates personal data as highly sensitive. Compared with earlier typologies, ours measures knowledge of specific rights rather than awareness of the GDPR and distinguishes uncertainty from mistaken belief (see below).

Not knowing one's data rights took two forms. Most respondents with poor knowledge were uncertain (CLASS-1 and CLASS-2, about a third of each sample), while about a tenth (CLASS-3) answered that rights were not in force, which may reflect wrong information or doubt that rights work in practice, or simply a guess. In practice, information campaigns may help the uncertain group, while the misinformed group may also need examples of rights being enforced.

Finally, sociodemographic characteristics explained little of any measure or of class membership, and the classes were only weakly related to trust, risk-taking or life satisfaction, so they reflect orientations towards privacy rather than general traits. Group differences in knowledge were real but small next to the deficit that all groups shared, suggesting that the lack of a link between sensitivity and rights knowledge is not specific to particular groups and may reflect how people encounter their data in everyday services.

Implications

European data protection law and policy rely on individuals using their rights to take control of their data (European Commission, 2019b), assuming that people who care about their data and privacy will find out what they can do about it. Our results do not support this assumption: knowledge of rights was limited and unequally distributed, and it was not related to sensitivity, which was widespread and rising. Raising concern, whether through public cases or campaigns, is therefore unlikely by itself to make people better able to use their rights.

This does not mean giving up the individual's role, which becomes more necessary as more decisions are based on data (see Introduction). But a deficit that is shared across the population and stable over time is unlikely to be closed by individual effort or information campaigns alone. Knowledge of rights needs to be built into the places where people view or use their personal data, such as e-government portals, and it needs to reach older and non-Estonian residents in particular.

In Estonia, services such as the Data Tracker (Solvak and Lauringson, 2024) could tell people which right they are using when they view their data, and what else they can do: correct the data, restrict its processing or object to conclusions drawn from it. Identifying and understanding one's own data are the first steps towards acting on it (Pangrazio and Selwyn, 2019), and such services could make these steps part of ordinary use.

Future research could test whether contact with or knowledge of one's own data builds knowledge of rights and links it to concern; and whether knowing a right leads to using it, by linking surveys to records of rights exercised.

Limitations

The surveys used a quota-based online panel rather than a probability sample, and all analyses are unweighted. Change over time rests on two independent samples, so we cannot distinguish a trend from a fluctuation or attribute the changes to any particular cause. Wave 3 also differed in composition and in the wording of the income question; all differences held when composition was controlled (Appendix D1), but the adjustment for income is imperfect.

All measures are self-reports. Privacy sensitivity was measured with single four-point items, and importance was rated close to the top of the scale, leaving little room for increase. Because all six rights listed are in force, a general tendency to answer "in force" would raise knowledge scores; future surveys could include rights that do not exist. The translation of the rights items from Estonian to Russian may have affected answers, in particular the ethnic difference in knowledge. Knowing a right is not the same as using it, and the surveys did not observe privacy behaviour or the exercise of rights. The order of question blocks differed in Wave 3, but a general shift towards agreeing is unlikely, because the share of "in force" answers did not rise.

Because many tests were run without correction for multiple comparisons, isolated small effects should be read with caution. Comparisons of classes across waves are descriptive, as classes were matched by inspection, and CLASS-1 in Wave 1 differed most from its later counterparts. The independence of sensitivity and knowledge was not tested within subgroups. Finally, whether the patterns hold in societies less digitalised than Estonia remains open.

Conclusion

In a highly digitalised society, caring about privacy and personal data and knowing one's data protection rights turned out to be separate. Across three waves over two and a half years, privacy and data sensitivity were high and rose, while knowledge of rights remained low and unrelated to them. Where such knowledge is low and unequal, rights place little practical constraint on data processing: the rights-based model leaves control with those who hold the data and protects mainly those who already know how to claim their rights. Building this knowledge into the services where people use their data is therefore a responsibility of the institutions that provide them, and a condition for people to take part in decisions about their data.
