Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

The concept of Umwelt refers to the phenomenal world of an organism, defined by its senses and what it does to stay alive, thrive and reproduce. Umwelt had a powerful influence on how the scientific study of animal behavior under natural conditions developed in the middle decades of the 20th century and currently is enjoying renewed interest in the field of comparative cognition. Traditionally, animals have been the exclusive focus of Umwelt-based analysis. This chapter focuses on Myxococcus xanthus, a predatory bacterium with an unusual, complex social lifestyle. The presentation is intended to show that the Umwelt concept can be useful in unpacking, in radically alien forms of life, processes regarded as cognitive in animals and in a context (predator-prey interactions) long important to ethology and comparative psychology, where Umwelt has done most work.

Roland Thaxter (1892; pp.393-4) A concept for connecting the dots in an evolutionary science of cognition For the past several years I and others have been arguing for the utility of basal cognition for understanding the evolution of biological capacities typically associated with mentality [see, in particular, special issues of Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B (2021, vol. 376, issues 1820 and 1821) and the special article collection in Animal Cognition ].

These capacities include differential responding to signs and cues, memory, appetitive and aversive responses (valence), learning, decision making, error correction, anticipation/prediction, and communication. "Basal" here mirrors usage in evolutionary biology. 'Basal metazoans' refers to the earliest evolved constitutively multicellular organisms known as animals. A more-or-less traceable continuum is assumed to exist on a variety of levels between basal metazoans and animals with nervous systems, as well as between animals with simple nervous systems and animals with highly complex brains. Basal cognition hypothesizes that a more-or-less traceable continuum exists in a basic toolkit of behaviorgenerating cognitive capacities found in microbes to Homo sapiens (Lyon et al., 2021). I define cognition as the processes by which an organism becomes familiar with, values, and exploits or evades features of its environment to meet existential goals (e.g., survival, wellbeing, reproduction) (Lyon et al., 2021). How these basic capacities are realized in different types of organism varies markedly in complexity and flexibility-the result of evolutionary history, ecology, ways of life (solitary, social), and so on-albeit with many common features, functionally and mechanistically. All are tools evolved for adaptation, serving lived imperatives.

The most successful approach for uncovering fundamental processes in the life sciences for the past century has been to start with the simplest tractable model system, derive principles, then scale up to more complex instances. This is not often because the phenomena of interest were first identified in these simple models-such phenomena are typically common to life-but because the physiological complexity of the animals in which they did first attract and hold attention, including humans, is so great as to inhibit understanding. The logic of transgenerational trait transmission was first inferred in pea plants, the physical basis of biological inheritance discovered in bacteria, the basic elements of genetic expression learned in fruit flies, and the findings applied to more complex animals, including us. This start simple-derive principles-scale up methodology provided fundamental insights into the energetic economy of organisms (respiration), the mechanism of ion channels (the basis of bioelectricity), physiological regulation in adverse conditions (e.g., stress responses), signal transduction, sensorimotor behavior, development, and much more. For instance, the current leading molecular model for investigating the mechanism by which anaesthesia works its wondrous effects in animals is, in fact, bacterial (Nury et al., 2011). There is no principled reason why this approach shouldn't apply to constrained, well-defined issues in comparative cognition and psychology.

In this chapter I will show how a conceptual framework introduced more than a century ago by an Estonian-German aristocrat, Jakob Johann von Uexküll (1909), can be a useful tool for getting a grip on the phenomenon of predation, a behavior typically considered cognitive in animals, in a way that could productively contribute to a deeply evolutionary, phyletically more inclusive comparative science of cognition and behavior. The central concept of this framework is Umwelt, literally "surrounding world". In von Uexküll's usage, Umwelt refers not to the environment (Umbegung) as commonly understood 1 but, rather, to the phenomenal world of an organism generated by its sensory capacities in contact with features of its surroundings (Gamble, 1910). The conjunction of senses and features, given the organism's current status, leads to behavior to meet needs and preferences, inherently goal-driven states. 1 Translation from one language to another is always tricky. It is clear from his earlier work that von Uexküll's concept of Umwelt was characterized by irreducibly subjective elements: perception and the effects of behavior, which distinguished it from what was then and is now standardly referred to as 'environment' (Gamble, F.W. (1910) "The comparative physiology of response in animals". Nature 83). Nevertheless, the English translator of the 2010 publication of von Uexküll's 1934 'travelogue', chose to render Umwelt as environment throughout and refers to Umbegung as "surroundings". The search function on my software turned up not a single mention of Umwelt in the entire body of the translated book, although it is an element of the original title-Streifzüge durch die Umwelten von Tieren und Menschen-and despite its frequent appearance in the introduction, written in original English. That said, 'environmental protection' in German is Umweltschutz.

The term phenomenology is used here in its established scientific sense, as it was by von Uexküll. In physics, phenomenology refers to models or descriptions that capture observable regularities without specifying underlying mechanisms. In biology it refers to the systematic description of observable organism-level dynamics independent of specific mechanistic accounts. Phenomenological descriptions characterize the organized appearance of biological processes at the scale where functional coherence emerges, while mechanistic explanations account for their realization. The two are complementary rather than competing explanatory modes. This usage differs profoundly from philosophical phenomenology concerned with firstperson subjective experience, which is not what von Uexküll, a marine biologist (albeit a philosophical one), was concerned with when he introduced the concept.

Umwelt denotes the organism-environment coupling that gives rise to functional perceptual worlds, species-specific meaning structures, and perception-action loops. As per Lewontin, environment here does not mean external sets of circumstances acting upon passive living systems but, rather, milieux actively constructed and modified by organisms in a reciprocal causal relationship that shapes selection and behavior (Lewontin, 2000). Organism and environment co-define, or co-construct, one another. This perspective aligns closely with von Uexküll's, but does not stand in for it, because Lewontin does not privilege perception.

Perceptual capacities, action systems, and environmental structure are all critical for niche construction, another important concept in comparative cognition and psychology. A niche encompasses all the interactions an organism has as it makes a living, including food sources, habitat, activity patterns, and reproductive strategies. Niche construction emphasizes organism modification of environmental conditions through their activities and metabolism. Essentially, they construct the environment they not only experience but for which they are evolved by natural selection (Scott-Phillips et al., 2014). Umwelt figures in niche construction both implicitly, as the basis for behavioral choices, and explicitly, as the 'internal' counterpart to the 'external' ecological niche (Fontanille, 2025).

The Umwelt framework shaped the early development of ethology (Lorenz, 1935), the study of animal behavior in more or less natural settings, and is now enjoying a revival among practitioners in comparative cognition, comparative psychology and behavioral ecology (Bueno-Guerra & Amici, 2018; Burghardt, 2024; Menzel, 2018; Van Dyck, 2012). In these settings Umwelt gave rise to Gordon Burghardt's critical anthropomorphism (Burghardt, 1991), the idea that to fully understand an animal's behavior, and to test hypotheses, an investigator must combine detailed knowledge of the species, its perceptual world, ecology and evolutionary history. Such knowledge interpreted in the context of valid scientific understanding about human phenomenology and behavior (as against unverified subjective first-person imputation) has been shown to be capable of yielding productive cognitive insights in non-traditional, poorly understood and under-studied animal models (e.g., snakes and other reptiles), paradigmatically in Burghardt's own work. The aim of this chapter is to provide a glimpse of how an even more radically non-traditional, aneural model organism may nevertheless shed new light on long-standing issues in these comparative cognition-related fields: predator-prey behavior, collective dynamics, and eusociality. This is the first time widely dispersed behavioral information on this unusual myxobacterium has been brought together in this way. With one of the largest genomes in the bacterial world, much of it devoted to signaling-sometimes a proxy for sensing-M. xanthus presents as a reasonable candidate. Myxococcota (formerly Deltaproteobacteria (Li et al., 2023; Waite et al., 2020)), the phylum to which M. xanthus belongs, are in the top tier of adaptable prokaryotes (Lyon, 2017), and M. xanthus has "the highest 'bacterial IQ' for sensing the environment" (Bretl & Kirby, 2016; p. 3808), due largely to genomic duplication. A geographically widely distributed denizen of soils, probably the most complex environments on Earth (Fierer, 2017), M. xanthus has long been studied for a dramatic developmental sequence leading to sporulation that coordinates many hundreds of thousands of cells in the formation of three-dimensional structures called fruiting bodies and the transformation of vegetative cells into two different cell types as well as food for conspecifics.

These features are strikingly similar to aspects of multicellular development and have made M. xanthus a valuable model system for understanding the principles underlying multicellular organization, particularly from evolutionary and developmental perspectives. Here we will concentrate on M. xanthus as a cooperative predator that forms groups often likened to a "wolf pack" (Berleman & Kirby, 2009). Predator-prey interactions have been part of cognitive ethology since the beginning (see, for example, Burghardt, 1975; Giraldeau, 1997; Griffin, 1978; Griffin et al., 1960; Hauser & Wrangham, 1990; Seyfarth et al., 1980). This is due in part to Donald Griffin, the fields founder, who made a defining discovery: the role of echolocation in bats hunting flying insects (Griffin, 1978; Griffin et al., 1960). Focusing on the predatory behavior of M. xanthus helps to limit the discussion to an area with analogs in more familiar forms of life.

Von Uexküll illustrated his ideas with the reproductive lifecycle of a female castor bean tick (Iodes ricinus), presented in a sequence of three functional circles involving different sensory modalities and sets of effectors (2010). In functional circle #1 the tick is poised, clinging, on a blade of grass or a bough, awaiting the chemical sign of butyric acid, exuded by mammalian skin. When butyric acid is sensed, the tick releases its grip and falls. So ends functional circle #1. If the tick falls onto something warm, ascertained by temperature sensors, functional circle #2 begins. (If it does not, the tick ascends a new perch.) On the warm surface of the host's body the arachnid begins searching for a hairless spot on the skin. Once located, the tick uses its mouth to cut into the flesh to reach blood, releasing saliva to both numb the spot (staving off host expulsion efforts) and prevent clotting, then inserts a feeding tube.

Feeding may last several days. Functional circle #3 begins when distention of the tick's body is such that it stops sucking blood. The engorged tick detaches from the host and drops off. If the tick is an adult female that has mated, she lays eggs (thousands), to be nourished by the blood she holds. She then dies. The primary signs constitutive of the tick Umwelt in von Uexküll's account are three: butyric acid (chemical), temperature, and mechanical distention. (Lack of obstruction by hair is arguably a fourth.) Today, the Umwelt of a tick looks more complex. Ticks produce and respond to a wide variety of chemical signals (Sonenshine, 2006), and respond differentially according to their current state in the present context, which includes nutritive status and lifecycle stage (larvae, nymph, adult) (Tsunoda, 2008).

The Umwelt of M. xanthus, an organism that lives, hunts, kills, feeds, reproduces and sporulates in the microscopic domain, is nowhere near as neat as the castor bean tick's, not least because the ability to observe what it is doing at an individual level is so much more problematic. The M. xanthus lifecycle is relentlessly predatory, intensely social, intimately cooperative, indeed multicellular. The rod-shaped bacterium hunts collectively, preys and consumes collectively, then constructs extraordinary 3-dimensional, spore-containing structures that will seed the next population when conditions improve. On any measure, this bacterium has a more behaviorally complex lifecycle than von Uexküll's tick, which possesses central and peripheral nervous systems. This suggests a more complicated Umwelt, arguably much more complicated.

A word about multicellularity: All multicellular organisms are made up of individual cells.

Obligate multicellularity is what plants and animals display; they are whole organisms made up of individual cells that perform specialized jobs within the system. In tissue each cell is obliged to act with the ones that surround it because they are physically connected to one anotherobliged, except in the case of programmed cell death and cancer. Facultative multicellularity, exhibited by M. xanthus and other unicellular organisms, is the self-organization of individual cells into structured collectives to perform important tasks (e.g., predation, sporulation, biofilm construction). They behave like multicellular organisms.

Outlining the contours of the M. xanthus Umwelt thus often depends on data on social behaviors, not individual capacities, including sensing and signaling the nature of which cannot yet be specified.

The aim here, however, is solely to show how analytical methods that have guided the study of cognitively driven behavior in animals can apply to certain bacteria in the context of basal cognition as a project for connecting the dots in the evolution of cognition. The phenomenological data available is more than adequate for this purpose, despite the shortcomings. In M. xanthus efficient predation is a profoundly collective affair that depends fundamentally on distributed, population-level coordination of individual behavior-similar to animals that herd, flock and shoal-using the most complex system of locomotion yet discovered in a bacterium. This paradigm-shattering, dual-mechanism motility system defied explanation until this century. Improvements in microscopy and imaging methods enabled rapid progress. Several recent discoveries (Gloag et al., 2016; Han et al., 2025; Mas et al., 2025; Saulnier et al., 2024) are transforming the traditional picture of M. xanthus motility in unexpected ways that bring Umwelt, Lewontin's co-construction hypothesis and niche construction to the fore.

Functional circle #1: Foraging and collective predation 1. Hunting prey: scouting and swarming M. xanthus moves across solid surfaces using two distinct but coordinated motility systems. These systems allow cells to glide without flagella and to organize into highly structured swarms capable of efficient collective predation. The two mechanisms-A-motility (adventurous) and S-motility (social)-operate through different physical interactions with the environment but share a common regulatory architecture that allows cells to switch direction and reorganize dynamically within a moving group (Mas et al., 2025; Saulnier et al., 2024).

A-motility allows individual cells to move independently. The mechanism depends on distributed focal adhesion-like complexes that couple intracellular motors to transient attachment points along the cell body. In this system, a motor complex transmits force to adhesion proteins that anchor briefly to the substrate (Mignot et al., 2007). As these adhesion sites cycle along the rotating cell length, the organism glides forward in a smooth motion sometimes described metaphorically as movement on microscopic "sticky feet" (Mas et al., 2025). A-motility supports exploratory movement of individual cells or loosely packed groups, particularly during colony expansion and early encounters with prey.

S-motility commonly operates in large groups of cells (Hu et al., 2011) 7 and depends on type IV pili, long retractable filaments that extend from the leading cell pole. Type IV pili comprise an ancient nanomachine that has been dubbed "the prokaryotic Swiss Army knife" (Berry & Pelicic, 2014) due to the myriad critical uses to which it has been put in widely differing species. In S-motility the filaments function like molecular grappling hooks. The pili are extended from the leading pole and attach to nearby material-substrate, neighboring cells, and environmental objects-and then retracted, pulling the cell forward (Wu et al., 2009). Pili-mediated pulling works most effectively in dense populations where attachment opportunities are abundant. S-motility is known to promote coordinated group movement and helps maintain alignment within swarms (Kaiser, 2008).

Simultaneous propulsion by two motility systems

For many decades the two motility systems were assumed to operate separately in largely different contexts: A-motility for solitary cells and S-motility for dense swarms. Recent work combining enhanced imagining techniques and video has overturned this view. The first major discovery was that cells seamlessly switch between scouting and swarming (Rombouts et al., 2023). In follow-up work, using detailed single-cell tracking and focusing on behavior at the boundary of swarming and rippling, Mas and colleagues (Mas et al., 2025) show that both propulsion systems can operate simultaneously within the same cell. Wild type cells possessing both motors exhibit a distinct movement signature and are capable of locomotion twice as fast as genetic knock-out mutants with only one modality. This demonstrates that co-deployment generates enhanced locomotor capacity rather than simple redundancy. Cells at the boundaries between different collective dynamics (swarming and rippling), which manifest in adjacent patches of the moving mass, shifted easily between them. The finding also implies that motility is tunable and that cells adjust the relative contribution of the two propulsion systems depending on environmental conditions.

Cell reversals

Movement in M. xanthus is not continuous in one direction. Cells periodically reverse their polarity, causing the leading and trailing poles to exchange roles, which reverses travelling direction. Functionally, this produces something analogous to the run-and-tumble logic of flagellated bacteria, to keep the organism from traveling too far off-piste, although by a very different mechanism. Rather than tumbling to reorient in liquid, M. xanthus reverses direction on a surface. This process is governed by a regulatory system centered on the proteins MglA/MglB and the Frz chemosensory pathway. MglA localizes to the leading cell pole and promotes assembly of motility machinery, while Frz signaling periodically triggers reversals that relocate MglA-and the motility complexes-to the opposite pole (Dinet et al., 2021). Both mechanisms are sensitive to environmental cues.

Han and colleagues in the Shaevitz lab (2025) show that thin M. xanthus colonies contain transient patches of cells moving in the same direction, a phenomenon they term local polar order. By measuring cell orientation, velocity, polarity, and substrate forces simultaneously, they demonstrate that these polar domains generate large traction-force fluctuations that cannot be explained in terms of the characteristic parallel alignment of M. xanthus where local polar order is not maintained. Crucially, the magnitude of this polar order is controlled by cell reversal frequency. When reversals become less frequent, polar domains persist longer, mechanical stresses increase, and cells begin to stack into additional layers, necessary for building the 3-dimensional structures of later developmental stages. The study therefore identifies a direct physical mechanism linking polarity dynamics, collective motion, and colony morphogenesis, showing that changes in behavioral interaction dynamics can trigger major structural transitions in the colony without a change in genetic instructions. A small shift in behavioral timing changes the interaction field, which reorganizes the system, much as collisions do in a crowd.foot_3

Cell alignment and extracellular structure

The effectiveness of the motility system depends strongly on cell alignment and extracellular structure. M. xanthus cells are elongated rods that naturally align with one another in parallel when densely packed. (See Figure 3.) Recent research shows that a key role in cell alignment is played by capillary forces (Black et al., 2025), the water-based interplay of surface tension, adhesion and cohesion that enables plants to transport water from roots to leaves and blood to ascend a narrow tube for testing. Recent evidence suggests strong cell-cell alignment remains present in the developing mounds leading to fruiting body formation (Han et al., 2025). Pole-to-pole alignment, shown to be necessary for C-signaling during aggregation and sporulation (Farrugia et al., 2025; Kaiser, 2004), may require a means of orientation detection, however. As we will see, M. xanthus is now known to have a tactile sense that registers cell-cell contact, the first demonstration of touch in a bacterium (Herrou et al., 2024).

While this has been demonstrated with prey cells, how and in what circumstances M. xanthus registers physical contact with siblings, apart from the force of collision, remains to be seen. As colonies move across surfaces they also construct a complex extracellular milieu that facilitates gliding and much more. This extracellular matrix includes self-produced exopolysaccharides (EPS), which combines with constituents of killed prey and self-produced outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), pouches of organic materials pinched off from the cell's outer membrane typically containing proteins and nucleic acids. This attractive extracellular cocktail traditionally has been described as "slime trails" laid down by adventurous cells for others to follow (Burchard, 1982). Recent research shows that cells following adventurous scouts are moving along furrows carved into the substrate by the movement of other cells, furrows that fill with the viscous extracellular material and constitute a persistent network of channels that facilitates stigmergy (Gloag et al., 2016).

Stigmergy was introduced in the 1950s to describe how termites create complex ordered structure without central control (Grassé, 1959). It is defined as "a mechanism of indirect coordination in which the trace left by an action in a medium stimulates subsequent actions" (Heylighen, 2016; p.4). Stigmergy is widely found in the living world and is increasingly used to describe engineered systems (Heylighen, 2016). In M. xanthus these extracellular topographical structures are important because they provide attachment sites for S-motility, generate tracks that channel gliding cells, and help stabilize the collective alignment necessary for coordinated swarm dynamics (Gloag et al., 2016). Recent high-resolution imaging of predatory colonies shows that this structured environment plays a central role in organizing cooperative motility behaviors that enable efficient prey killing (Rombouts et al., 2023).

Not built for speed (but cunning)

Although M. xanthus is an efficient predator, it is not the speediest bacterium on the block. Indeed, it moves at almost glacial speed. The average M. xanthus vegetative cell is 5-10 µm (micrometres, or thousandths of a millimetre) long and ~0.5 µm wide, with a gliding speed-in two-dimensions on solid surfaces-of 4-7 µm per minute (Patra et al., 2016). By contrast E. coli, with a much smaller average length (1-3 µm and similar girth), swims an average of 20-30 µm per second, which can be increased depending on strain, temperature and viscosity of the medium (Mears et al., 2014).

Apparently to overcome this massive speed differential between predator and prey, M. xanthus swarms have been found to engage in "a form of ensnarement" (Shi & Zusman, 1993; p. 414). Myxobacterial cells secrete into the environment a substance (different from exopolysaccharide) that includes abundant amino acids, which attract E. coli into killing range. E. coli cells defective in chemotaxis were completely unresponsive. Mutants deficient in aminoacid sensing were still attracted to the unknown substance, but less so. This suggests the predator, which does not sense amino acids, was high-jacking the prey bacterium's chemosensing system. This work, reported in Nature by a leading investigator (David Zusman), remains inexplicably unexplored. 9 In the animal behavior literature, such behavior is called deceptive signaling (Maynard Smith & Harper, 2003). Examples include the dangling light of an angler fish and the deceptive illumination of predatory Photuris fireflies, which mimic the flash patterns of female Photinus fireflies to attract males, which are killed and eaten (Eisner et al., 1997).

From swarming to rippling

Together A-motility, S-motility, extracellular structuring, and reversal control produce the large-scale behavior known as swarming. Densely packed cells move outward in coordinated sheets that explore surfaces and initiate predation. Under certain conditions-as cell density increases during prey consumption-these dynamics can shift into rippling, the next functional circle. As we have seen, the underlying motility machinery remains active throughout this transition (Saulnier et al., 2024). Individual cells appear capable of toggling between locomotor regimes depending on environmental conditions, including cues generated by the colony itself during migration and predation. Through motility M. xanthus cells continuously modify the conditions that shape their own subsequent behavior in ways that feed back into the dynamics of behavior and collective organization, including morphogenesis.

Self-production of cues that guide subsequent action is a characteristic of niche construction.

2. Finding prey

Locating and utilizing prey in this functional circle is critically dependent upon adventurous cells, which alone or in small numbers act as lethal scouts (Seef et al., 2021), striking out beyond the boundaries of the mass of cells which form the main predatory force (Rombouts, 2021; Rombouts et al., 2023). These cells spearhead invasion of prey colonies by larger populations of M. xanthus by altering the environment via self-produced exopolysaccharide, historically described as slime trails, which other cells preferentially glide onto and follow (Burchard, 1982). Current understanding presents a more complex picture. Abundantly found in this extracellular matrix are molecular materials associated with the myxobacterial lifestyle, particularly lipids associated with prey and intraspecies cell-cell membrane fusion (Ducret et al., 2013).

Cell-cell membrane fusion is hypothesized to be the effector of complementation, the enigmatic, long-recognized capacity of M. xanthus cells to rescue siblings deficient in the means to engage in critical collective behaviors by exchanging physiological material of some kind, by some means (Kroos et al., 2025). M. xanthus recently has been shown to be capable of directly transferring to siblings a novel acquired phenotype, specifically, a response to detergent not normally found in the bacterium's environment that can otherwise be fatal (Subedi et al., 2024). How, specifically, this transfer is effected we do not know. We only know that the outer membranes of M. xanthus cells display chains of outer membrane vesicles (Remis et al., 2014), whose involvement remains to be conclusively demonstrated but is strongly suspected (Berleman & Auer, 2013).

Sensing: key signals but mostly unknown

Now we move into opaque territory: the bacterium's sensorium. M. xanthus' genome enables no fewer than eight chemotaxis systems similar to those originally discovered in flagellated bacteria, which use them to sense the environment and drive motility. In the early 1980s, M. xanthus was tested across a wide range of substances well known to attract and repel swimming bacteria. The myxobacterium failed to respond, suggesting a lack of capacity for chemotaxis-as-we-know-it (Dworkin & Eide, 1983), even as it responded to chemically inert objects (latex or glass beads) in ways other bacteria do not (Dworkin, 1983). 10 Researchers suggested M. xanthus' seeming chemotactic incompetence might be due to the vastly different time scales on which it moves relative to prey.

Of the eight chemotaxis systems in the M. xanthus genome, only two-Frz and Dif-have been characterized to a satisfying degree. Both are sensitive to environmental cues and population density. The sensing target of Frz, which regulates polarity switching and cell reversals, is unclear. While the Frz system is not classically chemotactic in structure, or entirely in output, it has the capacity for signal amplification, signal integration and nonlinear responses to stimulation (Bretl & Kirby, 2016) that have been counted among the "cognitive features" typical of chemotactic systems (Lyon, 2015). Frz enables "sharp transitions from slow reversals to rapid reversals" (Dinet et al., 2021; p. 6) in M. xanthus. While Frz does not have the mechanisms for phosphorylation (protein tagging and untagging) that instantiates memory in typical chemotaxis systems, the molecular system that controls polarity switches in M. xanthus (MglA) displays an adjustable refractory period, during which further activation is inhibited. This refractory period acts as a memory of the last cell reversal. Together, these features suggest that individual cells involved in swarming motility "can switch dramatically from nonreversing to fast oscillatory-like reversals based on narrow signal changes" (Dinet et al., 2021; p. 6).

Dif, which is required for the synthesis of exopolysaccharide, has been shown to be involved (somehow) in M. xanthus' response to the only chemoattractant so far identified for the myxobacterium: phospholipid phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) (Bonner & Shimkets, 2006).

An important constituent of cell membranes, PE not only is the first paradigm-compliant chemoattractant identified in a surface-gliding bacterium, it is the first lipid signal identified in any bacterium (Kearns & Shimkets, 1998).foot_5 PE adheres to surfaces, diffuses slowly, and is released into the environment by lysis of prey. M. xanthus has genes associated with breaking down lipids and fatty acids, which suggests PE is a nutrient source, but cells are also 100-fold more sensitive to PE during development, indicating a strong signaling role (Bonner & Shimkets, 2006). PE or a derivative is hypothesized, but not yet shown, to be a constituent of the extracellular matrix necessary for M. xanthus S-motility. Post-kill adventurous cells tend to be sloppy eaters, leaving the majority of available nutrients behind as they continue searching for prey (see Figure 4) (Zhang et al., 2020). These unconsumed scraps would include PE as a membrane constituent of prey, which could help to recruit more cells (Kearns & Shimkets, 2001). PE thus may also be an indicator of prey.

From the standpoint of basal cognition, which is concerned with connecting the dots in cognitive evolution across phyla, M. xanthus responsiveness to PE is interesting for two reasons. First, three types of predatory eukaryotic cells that move by gliding motility also are attracted to PE derivatives or chemical relatives: human leukocytes, human monocytes and the social amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum (Kearns & Shimkets, 1998), the leading eukaryotic model of multicellular development in a unicellular. Second, one of the pheromones ants use for trail-following uses a derivative of PE to which M. xanthus also responds (Kearns & Shimkets, 2001).

In addition to PE, an entire class of molecular signals has been shown to stimulate M. xanthus: N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs), the dominant quorum sensing signal of diderm bacteria but which M. xanthus (a diderm bacterium) cannot synthesize (Lloyd & Whitworth, 2017). Quorum sensing, ubiquitous across the bacterial world, is a mechanism by which cells determine if sufficient numbers are present to coordinate gene expression and protein synthesis for collective behaviors (e.g., bioluminescence, releasing virulence factors, scavenging iron, taking up DNA from the environment, biofilm formation) (Waters & Bassler, 2005). Quorum sensing molecules are proxies for population density, so AHLs at a threshold concentration indicates a large population. AHLs added to M. xanthus populations have been shown to delay sporulation, stimulate myxospore germination, and increase both predatory activity and colony expansion rates (Lloyd & Whitworth, 2017). In animals, sensation of a signal by an organism other than the intended recipient is called 'eavesdropping', and such signals are known as kairomones. AHLs could be considered kairomones for M. xanthus (Lloyd & Whitworth, 2017).

While a large number of species-specific signals associated with motility and multicellular development have been identified in M. xanthus through meticulous genetic knock-out studies (Jogler et al., 2012), which is to be expected given its massive genomic endowment, the endogenous signaling picture in M. xanthus remains partial, highly complex and too nuanced to be presented straightforwardly (by this writer, anyway) given an absence of definitive findings as to their precise role and the mechanisms by which the bacterium processes them. These remain open questions.

3. Killing prey

Despite the centrality of predation to the M. xanthus lifecycle, how the cells kill other cells-and such a phylogenetically wide range of prey (Thiery & Kaimer, 2020)-remained unclear until, well, until now. Given the remarkable array of antibiotic and degradative substances synthesized by the bacterium, it was long assumed secretion of metabolites into the environment and detection by individual cells could be enough to do the job. Quorumsensing signals operate in just such a fashion (Mukherjee & Bassler, 2019). Chemical signals are secreted and diffuse into the environment. When a threshold density of signal is detected, cells collectively alter gene expression and engage in new behaviors. M. xanthus has toxin-antitoxin systems that would immunize itself and its siblings from the adverse effects of its own poisons if secreted in this manner. Convincing evidence did not follow, however. The discovery in M. xanthus of toxin-containing outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) seemed a promising killing strategy (Berleman et al., 2014), but these increasingly appear mainly directed at eliminating non-kin, not killing prey for food (Herrou et al., 2024). Direct evidence of OMVs as a predation strategy also has been slow in coming.

Predation research became much more focused after the game-changing finding in 2020 that solitary M. xanthus cells kill by direct contact with prey cells, which die within minutes by plasmolysis (Zhang et al., 2020). The cell envelope is penetrated (lysed), water drains out, contact between cell contents and cell membrane is lost and with it vital function. There rapidly followed a series of three remarkable reports of surprising discoveries. First was a novel, contact-dependent prey-killing system-the Kil system-which employs a mechanism similar to Type-IV pili (Seef et al., 2021). The precise antibiotic(s) M. xanthus uses in the Kil system are unknown. While Kil is sufficient to kill prey, it does not fully lyse the cell to release nutritive contents for consumption, which is accomplished by another, multitasking molecular apparatus similar to a Type-III secretion system.

Kil system proteins form where contact occurs between M. xanthus and prey cell. At the contact site a filamentous Kil pilus, a mechanism for transferring toxic agents, is constructed and inserted into the prey cell. Genetic evidence shows the killing function of this major pilus is linked to four minor pilin complexes that appear to form "interchangeable 'Tips'", which work in combination "to detect and kill phylogenetically diverse bacterial species" (Herrou et al., 2025; p.1). This finding goes some way to explaining how M. xanthus is such a surprisingly generalist predator. One size doesn't fit all, because it doesn't have to.

While these discoveries are already impressive, imaging of green fluorescent-labelled Kil proteins revealed an unexpected discovery: "a bona fide sense of touch" in a bacterium that extends to its entire body length (Herrou et al., 2024; p. 8). Functional circle #2: Rippling fuels the drive to development

Functional circle #2 begins when M. xanthus invades a prey colony sufficiently abundant to trigger large-scale reorganization of motility, predation/consumption, and reproduction in a visually arresting phenomenon called rippling. As Berleman and colleagues observe: "Rippling behavior coordinates cell movement across areas of several square millimeters and can involve >10 8 cells working as a unit" (Berleman et al., 2008; p. 17127)-an extraordinary feat of coordination by any measure.

Many thousands of moving cells self-organize into travelling bands of high-density wave

crests and low-density troughs (Zhang et al., 2012). Waves travelling in different directions appear to pass through each other but actually reflect off one another in 'accordion waves', due to the alignment of synchronously reversing cells perpendicular to the wave direction (Zhang et al., 2012). These waves, coordinated through the organism's adjustable motility parameters, are distinct from waves generated by Turing instability, diffusion-reaction patterns observed in chemical systems and the collective dynamics of D. discoideum (Zhang et al., 2012). Although a precise functional characterization of rippling is still evolving, one goal of the behavior appears to be to swell the population sufficiently (via predation, feeding and cell division) to sporulate (Berleman et al., 2008).

These striking collective patterns pose a long-standing problem familiar to the study of animal cognition: How can coordinated collective behavior, in this case facultative multicellular organization, arise in the absence of centralized control? Quorum sensing long seemed to be the answer. Rippling was believed, based on mutant studies, to be driven by C-signal-mediated regulation of reversal frequency. While C-signaling clearly contributes to coordination, the precise mechanism by which large-scale waves arise nevertheless remained unresolved, although it was becoming increasingly evident that cell alignment is critical (Zhang et al., 2012), including, in the case of C-signaling, pole to pole alignment (Farrugia et al., 2025; Kaiser, 2004).

New research provides a unifying explanation for this long-standing puzzle, but not in the way long expected.

Recent work by the Mignot lab, using combined experimental observation and modelling, show that transitions between swarming and rippling can arise from local interaction dynamics rather than from changes in genetic regulation or specialized signaling programs (Saulnier et al., 2024). The key observation is that cells preferentially reverse direction in regions where their forward movement becomes constrained by the surrounding collective. In dense populations, collisions and alignment forces often prevent cells from moving along their preferred trajectories. This mismatch between intended and realized motion is quantified as a "frustration index": reversals occur disproportionately in the most frustrated regions of the swarm, where individual movement is most constrained.

In practical terms, this means that rippling emerges as cells continuously negotiate local traffic conditions created by their neighbours and the extracellular environment. When congestion increases-particularly in areas with significant quantities of prey cells, alive or dead-cells reverse more frequently, generating oscillatory waves that propagate through the population. Crucially, these patterns do not require genetic reprogramming or centralized signaling: they arise from the modulation of reversal dynamics and the tunable refractory period governing when a cell can reverse again (Saulnier et al., 2024). Collective patterning thus reflects the regulation of interaction constraints within the swarm itself, transforming what might otherwise be chaotic crowding into ordered, travelling waves of coordinated motion.

Seen through the lens of Umwelt, this finding is particularly illuminating. The bacterium's functional circle is not defined solely by internal regulatory programs but by the relational field created by neighbouring cells, extracellular matrices, and prey-derived substrates. Saulnier and colleagues show that different environmental structures impose distinct alignment regimes on moving cells: short-range alignment along extracellular slime trails promotes swarming, whereas long-range alignment within prey-derived matrices favours rippling (Saulnier et al., 2024). The same organism, deploying different facets of the same motility machinery, generates different collective behaviors because the structure of its world changes. The environment is not merely a background condition but an active constraint field shaping the organism's available actions and the organization of its behavior. How precisely the relative abundance of prey cells affects the mix-beyond the obvious fact that it does-is yet to be determined.

This perspective aligns closely with Lewontin's argument that organisms and environments are mutually constructed rather than independently specified. In predatory swarms of M. xanthus extracellular matrix, inscribed furrows, lysed prey, and dense cell populations continuously reshape the physical and informational landscapes in which subsequent behavior unfolds. Rippling can thus be understood as an emergent regulatory regime within this co-constructed environment-one that redistributes resources, maintains coordinated interaction, and stabilizes collective organization during periods of declining but still exploitable nutrients. In niche-construction terms, the bacterial community actively generates the very environmental structures that organize its own activity, illustrating how organism-environment coupling can produce complex behavioral order without the need for centralized control or new genetic programs.

In sum, imaging at the single-cell level demonstrates that collective reorganization by rippling emerges from congestion-related reversal dynamics. Whatever the role of specific chemical signals or external gradients may prove to be-and they may be substantial-the mechanical forces of congestion arising from organism-environment relations may prove to be the dominant perceptual constraint shaping collective action in this stage. In Umwelt terms, congestion becomes a dominant perceptual cue within the functional circle of rippling, which reorganizes collective behavior through altered possibilities for action rather than through new internal programs.

What happens next

Multiple functional circles exist after rippling and commencement of the decline of available prey, with starvation the inevitable result. These involve even more dramatic multicellular behavior, which cannot be addressed here. The declining prey environment restructures the Umwelt, directing activity toward aggregation and sporulation. The following, briefly, is the sequence of events. First, reproduction is down-regulated and aggregation begins. Likened to the great animal herd migrations of the Serengeti in Africa (Shimkets, 1999), many hundreds of thousands of cells come together, first to form large mounds and then organized, dropletshaped, 3-dimensional structures called fruiting bodies.

Inside the fruiting bodies, about 10% of the rod-shaped cells transform into round, more compact myxospores. Outside the fruiting bodies, another 20-30% of the population transform into peripheral rods, so-called because they form around the periphery of the fruiting bodies. Peripheral rods are also metabolically down-regulated, although not as stringently as myxospores, and are unresponsive to an endogenous signal associated with sporulation. The remaining cells in the population-the overwhelming majority-autocide: 13 they lyse themselves. The prevailing consensus (although not universally accepted) is that this phenomenon is what it appears to be-altruistic sacrifice-to ensure the energetically demanding sporulation process is completed despite vanishing nutrients (Popp & Mascher, 2019). In the scientific literature, altruistic suicide is defined as the genetically programmed, active termination of an individual's life that increases the fitness (usually defined in reproductive terms) of closely related kin or the overall survival of the group/colony (Aubin et al., 2013). In the M. xanthus case, a small minority of the population is preserved, growth can begin anew when conditions improve, and the colony continues. How autocide is induced, however, remains unclear.

The Umwelt of M. xanthus: What we can say

The first thing we can say with confidence about the phenomenal world of M. xanthus is that it is truly social, or eusocial. Every facet of its existence, including (very probably) feeding, 14 is lived in groups in which an individual cell's behavior intimately affects and is affected by others. This is not to say that M. xanthus meets most modern definitions of eusociality in evolutionary biology, which are derived exclusively from animals (ants, some bees, termites, some vertebrates) and which, in keeping with the Modern Synthesis, measures the phenomenon entirely in terms of reproduction, especially reproductive castes (Crespi & Yanega, 1995), which does not (so far) apply in prokaryotes. Because M. xanthus displays two important characteristics associated with eusocial animals-morphological divisions of labor and altruism-it does just about fit a definition of eusociality introduced by E.O. Wilson (Wilson, 2012). 13 Autocide is suicide in the biological context. In the human context, autocide is suicide by automobile. Thanks to my lawyer husband for uncovering the distinction.

The reason why this is important is because, as we know from extensive work in animals, eusociality has consequences for the kinds of sensing and behavior of which an organism is capable; also, discernible patterns exist in that way of life. A prominent pattern is the tight, colony-level co-regulation of physiology and behavior at the molecular level (Friedman et al., 2020), which is everywhere apparent in M. xanthus. Another is the necessity for tracking mechanisms in predatory eusocial species. We have already seen that a derivative of the M. xanthus chemoattractant PE is found in tracking mechanisms in eukaryotes, including social amoeba, phagocytic human immune cells, and omnivorous ants. From a basal cognition perspective, this would suggest that a closer look at PE-following in those distant phyla may hold clues for understanding aspects of M. xanthus behavior. Convergent evolution repeatedly shows that similar problems in making a living in particular ways are often solved in broadly similar fashion.

So, is eusociality a fair ascription?

As we have seen, M. xanthus is a form of cellular life that lives very much as a collective, functioning in many respects like a dynamically motile tissue. There are no observations, there is no literature, on the behavior of free-living M. xanthus, unlike in D. discoideum, in which the developmental cycle begins with the aggregation of free-living amoeba (Annesley & Fisher, 2009). Starvation signaling brings these solitary predators together for multicellular transformation and sporulation. M. xanthus, by contrast, appears to be effectively involved in functionally collective behavior at every life stage. Whether solitary, A-motile M. xanthus cells can sustain themselves without group support, given the proportion of the nutrient load they leave behind after killing, is arguably an open question. However, periodic reversals keep them close to the larger group. 15 Close observation of M. xanthus under ecologically valid conditions is necessary.

Also supporting the ascription of eusociality is the high degree to which cells tend to align themselves body-to-body and end-to-end, which very likely facilitates the intimate cell-to-cell exchange of existentially important goods of various kinds. Other indications of eusociality are rippling and how multicellular development proceeds. Killing is what M. xanthus does, relentlessly, but feeding and reproduction for reproduction's sake is not the sole driver of the behavior: continuation of the colony appears to be. Large-scale autocide of most of the (recently acquired) population during multicellular development is only the most shocking piece of evidence for such an ascription. Population numbers must be high enough to engage in sporulation; rippling may have a key role in accomplishing this. M. xanthus may thus provide a useful testbed for evolutionary theories of group-level adaptation in superorganisms (Gardner & Grafen, 2009; Wilson & Hölldobler, 2005).

Fruiting body formation, which begins with a decline in the availability of prey serious enough to signal starvation (Berleman & Kirby, 2007), is an anticipatory developmental process, as developmental processes are in multicellular organisms. If prey is declining, starvation and population collapse will follow. Starvation would be too late, although there is evidence M. xanthus cells store lipid bodies, 16 which would be an anticipatory behavior analogous to storing fat. Similarly, in E. coli, the transition from aerobic to anaerobic respiration, necessary to survive the oxygen-free environment of the stomach and intestines, begins immediately upon a rise in ambient temperature from entering the body of an animal host (Tagkopoulos et al., 2008). By the time the bacterium hits the stomach, it's too late to make the switch. What we don't know is how M. xanthus detects a step-down in prey availability, which would seem to require the kind of tunable sensitivity to something in the surroundings that chemotaxis systems evolved to do. Whether PE-sensing and Dif are involved, or something else-something more indirect but accurate and predictive of where circumstances are heading-remains to be seen.

Perhaps the M. xanthus sensorium remains obscure because much of it operates quite differently from the paradigms on offer, and that might be because its priorities, as a form of life, are also quite different from the norm. On the other hand, M. xanthus has not been studied as intensively as other bacterial model systems and what is obscure could perhaps be explained more classically than expected. 17

There is much here that conforms to the literature on predator-prey interactions in comparative cognition and comparative psychology as well as behavioral ecology. In the microworld, too, the evolutionary arms race between predator and prey is apparent, as prey species evolve defences against this apex predator of the prokaryotic realm. Bacillus subtilis, another sporulating bacterium that exhibits complex multicellular behavior, has evolved a toxin to retard the predatory onslaught of M. xanthus and can construct resilient megastructures to keep it at bay (Müller et al., 2016). There would be others mounting countering strategies.

From the standpoint of basal cognition, all of this promises down the track to open the field of comparative cognition in exciting ways.
