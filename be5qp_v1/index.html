How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Traditional psychometrics collapse rich psychedelic experiences into aggregate composite scores, masking the structural dynamics that drive therapeutic outcomes.

Methods: An unsupervised Natural Language Processing framework mapped a substance-specific corpus of psilocybin narrative reports (n = 5,498; ~5.19M tokens) into a continuous seven-dimensional thematic state space, using semantic embeddings and cluster-level differential TF-IDF feature extraction. Spatial topology metrics, multivariable logistic regression, and substructure analyses characterized its organization, dynamics, and functional roles.

Results: The resulting topology revealed four functional roles across the continuous state space: modulators (e.g., visual patterns, environment, body-object features), a threshold marker (temporal distortion), a peak state (ego dissolution), and a distributed state (unity).

Cognitive-emotional processing emerged as the dominant driver of transitions across this topology, promoting internally-oriented states while suppressing environmental tracking. Within unity, this topology resolves into two mutually inhibitory pathways: internal cognitive-temporal transcendence and external somatosensory-environmental merging. Because legacy composite scales like the MEQ30 sum across these opposing channels, feature cancellation masks divergent phenomenological trajectories behind identical composite scores.

Conclusions: Psilocybin phenomenology organizes into a structured, cognitive-emotionally anchored topology rather than unconstrained variation. Resolving unity into opposing pathways 1 exposes a mechanism of feature cancellation in legacy aggregate psychometrics, whereas the global state-space architecture aligns with neuroimaging evidence of network reorganization and provides a quantitative framework for optimizing dose, set-and-setting, and targeted clinical protocols.

INTRODUCTION

A growing body of clinical research highlights the therapeutic potential of psychedelic-induced subjective altered states of consciousness. Contemporary evidence demonstrates that long-term clinical efficacy depends directly on the nature and depth of the acute psychological experience itself (Yaden and Griffiths, 2021). While molecular neurobiological models suggest that pharmacological agents induce structural circuit repair (Olson, 2021), empirical evidence indicates that the lived subjective experience operates as a primary mediator of transformation in the patient's self-narrative (Letheby, 2021). Given the therapeutic role of subjective experience, a topology of its phenomenological architecture is a clinical necessity, without which clinicians cannot reliably leverage experiential patterns to drive therapeutic breakthroughs or prevent psychological distress.

Quantitatively modeling these complex and qualitative experiences remains a significant challenge to standardized psychometric instruments, such as the Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ30; MacLean et al., 2012) or the Altered States of Consciousness Rating Scale (5D-ASC; Studerus et al., 2010), which are commonly applied to gauge the intensity of altered states. While useful for baseline correlations, these aggregate scales introduce a systemic measurement challenge, compressing the vast, high-dimensional nature of subjective experience into predefined, low-dimensional scalar summary scores (Van Elk and Yaden, 2022).

Forcing rich phenomenology into fixed categories risks obscuring the qualitative variations that may drive distinct clinical outcomes and inhibit development of more reliable clinical support systems.

Prior research designs have attempted to navigate these measurement constraints through large-scale surveys and retrospective narrative collections, achieving moderate scale but remaining limited by the predefined architecture of questionnaire formats (Griffiths et al., 2019; Davis et al., 2020; Cox et al., 2021). Alternative neuroimaging and qualitative field interview designs seek higher physiological or phenomenological fidelity, yet both approaches are restricted by logistical demands and small sample sizes that limit broad generalizability.

Meta-analytic evaluations reveal that pooled experimental neuroimaging datasets (Dos Santos et al., 2016; McCulloch et al., 2022) and large-scale psychometric trial syntheses (Studerus et al., 2011) often encompass only a few hundred substance-specific (e.g., psilocybin) sessions, while naturalistic qualitative interview studies are typically restricted to fewer than 50 participants (Michael et al., 2023). Even when evaluating qualitative narratives, existing analytical frameworks frequently encounter the same data-reduction problem, compressing reports back into the low-dimensional scalar metrics of legacy questionnaires.

While traditional clinical frameworks have historically relied on rigid diagnostic categories or coarse, single-scalar survey metrics, modern computational psychiatry increasingly emphasizes continuous, multidimensional state mapping (Insel et al., 2010). Computational text processing offers a powerful framework for constructing such high-dimensional phenomenological spaces from self-report narratives (Ahuja et al., 2025), particularly within large curated repositories such as the Erowid Experience Vaults (Erowid Center, 2026). Early text mining relied on basic word counts, dictionary-based tools (e.g., LIWC), or traditional topic modeling (Cox et al., 2021; Hase et al., 2022; Mooseder et al., 2022). While simple keyword counts fail to capture continuous state-space dynamics or vector interactions, subsequent Natural Language Processing (NLP) applications have expanded into dense semantic text embeddings and transformer-based language models, providing an effective mechanism for capturing complex qualitative markers in psychedelic phenomenology. However, these efforts predominantly focused on contrasting diverse psychoactive classes (Al-Imam et al., 2025; Ballentine et al., 2022; Noah et al., 2024), necessarily reducing intra-substance variance into class-level averages, obscuring phenomenology specific to individual substances. Although inter-class models can isolate specific experiential elements (e.g., differentiating visual terminology), they are blind to intra-session topology, such as how emotional catharsis, somatic load, and spatial altered states correlate within a single experience, remaining inherently unsuited for deep intra-substance mapping. Because therapeutic application requires a complete, drug-specific profile of the clinical agent, mapping the continuous internal state-space of a single substance represents a crucial unaddressed need in psychedelic research. In comparison, previous studies isolating single-substance narratives were constrained by sample scale. Small cohorts, ranging from qualitative sentiment mining to detailed thematic coding on modest datasets (N = 36), lack the statistical power required to construct stable, continuous high-dimensional topologies (Al-Imam et al., 2025; Lawrence et al., 2022).

To address these limitations, this study developed a computational framework that treats altered states as a continuous landscape of experience, mapped directly from a large corpus of raw psilocybin-specific narrative reports (n = 5,498 reports; 5,191, 050

METHODS

The computational framework processes raw narrative corpora in a multi-stage pipeline designed to map qualitative text into a structured, continuous state space. First, narrative reports undergo standard text preprocessing before transformation into high-dimensional vector representations using dense transformer embedding models. These high-dimensional text embeddings are then refined through clustering to reveal stable dimensions of thematic grouping, representing each experience report not by a single label, but as a data point in a continuous mixture space where a narrative retains fractional membership across seven discovered themes (e.g., partitioning an individual experience across explicit ratios of, say, ego dissolution, visual patterns, or body features). To visualize this multi-dimensional mixture data without losing its inherent properties, the framework applies the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm (McInnes et al., 2018) to compress the high-dimensional thematic vectors into an interpretable 2D geometry. Combining text embeddings, unsupervised clustering, non-linear geometric mapping, and predictive models, the framework establishes a computational foundation that maps both the static structures of the altered state, and the directional trajectories inherent in the phenomenology.

Corpus curation and preprocessing

To build a foundational dataset of acute psilocybin experiences, the data utilized in this analysis were obtained from the Erowid Experience Vaults online repository (Erowid Center, 2026) according to the platform's automated access guidelines. Two different datasets, one comprising 40,254 user reports across multiple substances (including non-psychedelic), and another, 7655 psilocybin-specific reports, were merged and filtered down to a corpus of 10,979 unconstrained narratives of psilocybin experiences (of which 93 were removed as duplicates).

Raw self-reports frequently involve poly-substance combinations or extensive non-phenomenological context. To ensure the downstream analysis reflects pure psilocybin dynamics, a Llama 3.3 (70B) large language model (Grattafiori et al., 2024) was deployed to partition this combined dataset (N = 10,979) into subsets of strictly-psilocybin use (n = 5,498, precision 96%, recall 79%), those mixed with other hallucinogens (n = 2,053, precision ~100%, recall ~100%), and those co-experienced with cannabis (n = 3,357, precision 40%, recall 80%), with 71 reports deemed inconclusive. Classification accuracy was manually evaluated on random samples of 250 instances per class. Filtering out cross-substance information (both the mixed with cannabis and other hallucinogenic datasets were discarded), yielding 5,498 strictly-psilocybin experiences (comprising 5,191,050 total tokens, with a mean length of ~944 tokens per report), represents a high-confidence dataset that is specifically suitable for tracking the topology of a single chemical family by avoiding the compound blurring that is typical of multi-substance content.

Prior to vectorization, the Llama 3.3 (70B) architecture was further utilized to apply extractive semantic filtering to each report. This isolated the core phenomenological text while systematically removing non-experiential noise, after which the text was transformed into a 384-dimensional embedding vector representation, using all-MiniLM-L6-v2 Sentence Transformer, to quantitatively capture the semantics and tone for each experiential content in vector format.

From clustering to seven stable spatial dimensions

Exploring the empirical distribution of the corpus revealed key structural properties that guided downstream feature engineering, establishing a continuous seven-dimensional state-space as a foundational topology.

Revealing the composite nature of experience. Having projected each narrative into a 384-dimensional dense vector space ( ), exploratory iterative K-means clustering was ℝ 384 applied to evaluate the compositional structure of acute psilocybin narratives. Progressively increasing the cluster parameter from K = 2 to K = 10 revealed that the data do not cleanly separate into discrete, isolated partitions; rather, embedding vectors occupy a continuous space characterized by peripheral overlap and spatial interpenetration between neighboring domains (see Figure 3 in Results). This lack of clear spatial separation indicates that hard-clustering artificially divides what is fundamentally a shared mixture of co-occurring phenomenological states.

Seven stable dimensions of experience.

To identify distinguishing thematic signatures, statistical feature extraction was paired with qualitative synthesis across multiple cluster resolutions. First, a foreground-background Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) differential analysis (Salton and Buckley, 1988) was executed to identify the most salient terminology within each cluster. For every cluster, the mean TF-IDF vector of all in-cluster documents (foreground) was contrasted against the mean vector of out-of-cluster documents (background). The 50 terms (comprising unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams) displaying the highest positive differential scores, representing features with high mutual information and semantic specificity, were selected to define the cluster's thematic profile. This procedure was iteratively applied across K [2, 10] cluster configurations. Compared to a global ∈ TF-IDF baseline computed across the unclustered corpus, executing differential analysis at the cluster level exposed localized n-gram signatures strongly coupled with distinct experiential states. Because individual clusters represent unique density configurations of co-occurring phenomena, this two-stage approach ensured that the resulting thematic space emerged directly from the natural geometry of the data manifold, rather than being constrained by global term frequencies.

Following term extraction, candidate thematic labels were manually categorized and validated against corresponding narrative excerpts using established qualitative coding protocols (Braun and Clarke, 2006) to ensure semantic fidelity between isolated n-grams and their underlying human context. Descriptor assessment was guided both by established psychometric literature, describing previously identified domains of subjective experience, and by inductive identification of lexical commonalities. Domain-expert interpretation was applied to ground statistically elevated terminology in self-contained psychological constructs, verifying that thematic dimensions strictly reflect coherent phenomenological states. This synthesis revealed seven core experiential themes: ego dissolution, unity and connectedness, time-distortion, body-object features, environmental tracking, visual patterns, and cognitive-emotional processing (see Supplementary Information Table S6 for the complete dictionary of extracted n-grams and assigned thematic labels). Notably, varying clustering configuration K during the term extraction step shifted the resolution of data partitioning rather than the underlying vocabulary domains; differential n-gram extraction across all cluster granularities (K [2, 10]) reliably recovered descriptors spanning these same seven invariant ∈ thematic dimensions. Furthermore, these stable dimensions directly converge with validated psychometric frameworks in psychedelic science, establishing a robust architectural foundation wherein granular sub-vocabularies represent localized subsets of these baseline phenomenological axes (Table 1).

Vectorization in seven dimensional space.

Under this framework, an individual narrative is conceptualized as a composite of co-occurring themes manifesting at varying degrees of intensity. For example, a single report might simultaneously feature language describing visual distortions, blending with nature, and to a lesser degree elements of ego dissolution. To construct this continuous feature space, narrative texts and extracted thematic dictionaries were lemmatized using spaCy (Honnibal et al., 2020), retaining unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams (1-3-grams). Theme intensity scores were calculated for each report by matching token occurrences against the thematic dictionaries, applying a phrase-length weighting multiplier (1 x for unigrams, 2 x for bigrams, 3 x for trigrams) to account for multi-word specificity, and normalizing by the document's total token length. This scaling yields relative narrative density scores, projecting each report as a continuous coordinate within a seven-dimensional semantic feature space ( ). ℝ 7

Core analytical framework

Having established experiences as mixtures of themes, the framework investigates this seven-dimensional phenomenological landscape by leveraging three distinct methodological approaches: 1.

Geometric analysis: visualizing the spatial architecture of the phenomenological landscape.

2. Quantitative measurement: applying formal statistical metrics calculations.

3. Modeling: rationalizing and explaining thematic behavior.

Geometric analysis.

To visualize the structural landscape, UMAP was applied to compress the seven-dimensional feature space into a two-dimensional projection. This non-linear dimensionality reduction is uniquely suited for preserving the inherent topology of the data across three distinct scales, maintaining: (1) Local neighborhoods: such that if two reports are similar in 7D theme membership they remain close in a 2D geometry.

(2) Global structure: keeping the broader relationships between different thematic regions.

(3) Manifold continuity: preserving a topology that assumes locally continuous data.

Within this 2D space, three geometric and distributional properties were evaluated: • Thematic centroids: representing the mathematical spatial center of mass for each individual theme.

The centroids reflect the concentration point where narratives heavily dominated by a specific theme naturally cluster.

• Dominant theme distribution: mapping the locations of reports where a given theme exhibits the highest relative intensity vector. This highlights the localized spatial dominance of individual themes across the manifold.

• Mean-deviation gradients: capturing the gradual variance of each dimension across the topology by measuring how much a narrative's thematic score elevates (positive deviation) or is suppressed (negative deviation) relative to its dataset-wide mean.

This normalization converts raw intensity metrics into a spatial map of localized phenomenological highlights.

RESULTS

Continuous Topology of Psilocybin Phenomenology

Mapping acute psilocybin experiences across three complementary spatial resolutions-384D sentence embeddings, 7D thematic coordinates, and 2D manifold projections-resolves the global state-space into a continuous, multi-level topology. Across scales, cognitive-emotional processing emerges as a primary system-wide driver, with vector dynamics reflecting distinct divergent trajectories within unitive states. Quantifying these multi-resolution dynamics establishes empirical parameters to track state transitions, exposes limitations in static psychometrics, and informs target selection for clinical trials.

DISCUSSION

This work establishes a computational framework for extracting clinically useful patterns relevant to the psilocybin experience, demonstrating that the apparent complexity and heterogeneity of qualitative trip reports can be organized into a structured topology. Three findings emerge with direct clinical and methodological significance. First, global state transitions across this topology are systematically mediated by cognitive-emotional processing, which functions as a primary predictive driver guiding trajectories across the manifold. Second, topological decomposition reveals that unitive phenomena are not monolithic, but split into two distinct channels: an internal pathway (cognitive-temporal transcendence) and an outward pathway (somatosensory-environmental merging). Third, these channels exhibit strong reciprocal inhibition, demonstrating that aggregated psychometric scores can mask opposing experience dynamics. Together, these results advance psychedelic research from simple categorization toward an actionable, high-resolution geometry of experiential consciousness, establishing a data-driven foundation for refining therapeutic protocols.

Topological Mechanics: Cognitive-Emotional Drivers and Unitive Divergence

Multiple lines of evidence converge to identify cognitive-emotional processing as the primary driver of state dynamics across the manifold. In 2D state-space projections, this domain occupies a central region that directly overlaps with internally oriented themes. In 7D spatial analyses, it demonstrates unique global density and modulatory prominence. Finally, multivariable regression confirms its role as a primary statistical driver, simultaneously co-elevating with internally oriented states (temporal distortion, ego dissolution, and unitive processing) while downregulating externally focused environmental tracking. Rather than emerging from unstructured narrative entropy, these state transitions operate under systematic topological principles-a finding that echoes recent population-level neuroimaging showing organized neural remapping under psilocybin across varying contexts (Stoliker et al., 2026). Because the quality of the acute experience predicts therapeutic outcome (Roseman et al., 2018), concealing these distinct pathways may obscure clinically relevant variation and contribute to divergent outcomes among patients with similar aggregate scores, a possibility addressed below.

These two trajectories closely parallel the distinction between introvertive and extrovertive mysticism proposed by Stace (1960), who described introvertive unity as an inward-turning experience in which the sense of self, sensory content, time and space dissolve, and extrovertive unity as an outward-facing apprehension of oneness perceived through the senses in the external world. The internal pole identified here, defined by ego dissolution, time distortion and cognitive-emotional processing, corresponds to the former; the outward pole, defined by environmental, visual and somatosensory features, corresponds to the latter. This correspondence emerged from unsupervised analysis of naturalistic narratives, without reference to Stace's framework or any questionnaire structure, providing independent, data-driven support for a distinction that has largely rested on philosophical analysis and factor analyses of questionnaire items (Hood, 1975; Hood et al., 2001). The present findings also refine it: rather than two discrete types, introvertive and extrovertive unity form opposing poles of a single continuous manifold (K = 1), linked through a baseline unitive region and expressed in graded mixtures.

This structure has a direct consequence for the MEQ30. Although the instrument descends from Stace's framework, its Mystical factor combines items reflecting internal and external unity into a single score (MacLean et al., 2012; Barrett et al., 2015). Because the present data show these two forms to be inversely related, this combined factor is where feature cancellation would be expected to operate. These findings therefore reframe a long-standing debate over whether unitive states constitute a single construct (MacLean et al., 2012; Barrett et al., 2015) or discrete categorical types (Griffiths et al., 2006): unitive experience appears to be neither, but a continuous landscape organized along an inward-outward axis.

Clinical Implications for State Navigation and Protocol Optimization

The most immediate clinical implication concerns measurement. If composite instruments sum across inversely related experiential channels, then patients with matched composite scores may differ systematically in their underlying trajectories. This generates a directly testable prediction: in existing trial datasets with item-level MEQ30 or 5D-ASC data, participants with matched totals but divergent inward versus outward profiles should show different therapeutic outcomes. In parallel, the present pipeline can score written post-session reports, which many trials already collect, on the same seven dimensions, providing a complement to questionnaire-based measures. Translating the above topological mechanisms into clinical practice provides a framework for real-time state navigation, trial design, and protocol optimization, utilizing predictive models to anticipate individual treatment trajectories (Feuerriegel et al., 2024). Viewing altered states as traversable trajectories through a structured landscape implies that experiences follow probabilistic transition dynamics rather than arbitrary fluctuations. Within this framework, particular feature dynamics could function as real-time state-transition markers. For instance, a participant's report of temporal slowing may indicate that the session has crossed the threshold marked by time distortion, a region of the state space closely associated with ego dissolution in the present data. Furthermore, because ego dissolution is a highly localized boundary state, future trials could test whether doses sufficient to cross this threshold occasion ego dissolution more reliably than lower doses.

Moreover, the central topological role of cognitive-emotional processing provides an operational lever for steering session dynamics and engineering set-and-setting protocols. Because internal dimensions interconnect directly through this central core, modulating the participant's pre-dosing cognitive-emotional set via targeted preparation, therapist grounding, or curated music selection allows clinicians to guide overall manifold trajectories, while acute cognitive-emotional destabilization serves as an early-warning marker for emerging distress.

Crucially, disaggregating monolithic unitive states into bipolar experiential channels enables trajectory-targeted protocol design. Internal unitive trajectories (cognitive-temporal transcendence) represent the primary target vector for internalized affective conditions such as Major Depressive Disorder, where disrupting rigid self-referential narratives is essential (Carhart-Harris et al., 2016). To foster these internal synergies, protocols could systematically minimize external sensory inputs (e.g., eyeshades) while leveraging structured cues (e.g. curated music (Kaelen et al., 2018)) to deepen inward cognitive-temporal focus without competing external sensory distraction (Carhart-Harris et al., 2016). Conversely, outward unitive trajectories (somatosensory-environmental merging) offer a tailored target for relational trauma, existential anxiety, and end-of-life distress (Griffiths et al., 2016; Ross et al., 2016).

Because outward merging co-elevates with visual patterns and somatic alteration while suppressing internal narrative focus, relational modalities may benefit from sensory-grounded, open-eye, and nature-embedded environments. This topological divergence aligns directly with recent neuroimaging evidence demonstrating that psilocybin reorganizes brain network connectivity in a context-dependent manner, aligning neural dynamics with external environmental cues (Stoliker et al., 2026). Together, stratifying patients by these distinct topological trajectories transforms psychedelic administration into a precision-guided intervention capable of tailoring set, setting, and therapeutic support to targeted experiential states.

Limitations

Several methodological and observational bounds warrant consideration when interpreting these findings. At the observational level, constructing state-space coordinates from crowdsourced natural language reports introduces inherent recall bias, selective self-reporting, and post-hoc narrative smoothing (Mooseder et al., 2022). While naturalistic datasets offer unmatched sample sizes and real-world ecological validity, they also present systemic data gaps and unobserved environmental confounders that lack the standardized clinical controls, precise dosage, and setting consistency of laboratory trials (Ballentine et al., 2022).

At the analytical level, natural language embeddings serve as a conceptual proxy for underlying neurological activity. Although semantic term frequencies map subjective feature co-occurrence with high resolution, language captures the cognitive and narrative representation of an altered state rather than direct, real-time physiological or cortical dynamics. Methodologically, deriving report-level coordinates via normalized term frequencies relies on simplifying assumptions: unweighted summation assumes equal semantic contribution across cluster terms, whereas exact keyword matching fails to account for polysemous or context-dependent usage. Future refinements should incorporate dynamic, token-level contextual embeddings (e.g., transformer-based attention masks) to assign fractional thematic membership based on immediate syntactic context. Nevertheless, aggregating normalized term frequencies into multidimensional coordinates provides a transparent, interpretable framework for mapping the continuous topology of experience directly from unconstrained natural language reports. A geometric manifold framework is uniquely suited for such naturalistic corpora: rather than fitting global parametric functions across skewed sample distributions or forcing linear correlations on unobserved confounders, it projects noisy inputs into localized spatial coordinates that reveal continuous state relationships. Demonstrating exceptional statistical robustness, the directional stability observed across all primary multivariable models, reinforced by non-parametric bootstrapping and cross-stage alignment across two-dimensional observations, seven-dimensional spatial statistics, and predictive modeling, confirms that this state-space topology recovers reproducible phenomenological signals rather than fitting noise within crowdsourced data distributions.

Construct Validation and Methodological Scope

Without relying on rigid survey priors or supervised target labels, this framework autonomously recovers the foundational feature space of psychedelic phenomenology. Ego dissolution and unity-connectedness mirror constructs captured by the Ego Dissolution Inventory (EDI) (Nour et al., 2016) and the 30-item Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ30) (Barrett et al., 2015).

The visual patterns and body-object features align with the Elementary Imagery and Somatosensory subscales of the 11D-ASC (Studerus et al., 2010), while time-distortion captures the temporal transcendence documented across acute states (MacLean et al., 2012).

Furthermore, cognitive-emotional processing reflects psychological insight and affective dynamics (Davis et al., 2021), and environment aligns with environmental and nature-connectedness dimensions (Carhart-Harris et al., 2018). This alignment validates the external consistency of the state-space coordinates, establishing a baseline from which its novel topological discoveries can be evaluated.

Moreover, while the seven-dimensional coordinate system was specifically selected to capture system-wide patterns across psychedelic experiences, the underlying framework is fundamentally dimensional-agnostic. This architecture establishes a generalizable computational paradigm for converting unconstrained and unstructured qualitative reports into quantitative topologies-a scalable system for uncovering state-space patterns by projecting narratives into spatial coordinates over an adaptable number of dimensions. Consequently, the architecture extends beyond psilocybin or specific psychedelic states, offering a methodology for mapping reports across diverse altered states of consciousness, pharmacological interventions, and psychiatric phenomena.

Neurobiological Alignment

This framework connects the application of computational phenomenology in psychedelic research (Ramstead et al., 2022) with cognitive neuroscience. While functional neuroimaging demonstrates that acute psilocybin disrupts rigid network boundaries and increases global neural entropy, this framework shows that subjective experience remains systematically structured around underlying system mechanics.

System-wide reciprocal coupling demonstrates that despite cortical desynchronization under psychedelics, phenomenological information processing exhibits structural polarity: internal cognitive-temporal transcendence and outward environmental-sensory merging form distinct, competing trajectories that remain linked through a shared cognitive-emotional driver. At the mechanistic level, the divergent coupling of somatic processing aligns with predictive processing frameworks (e.g., REBUS) (Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019; Friston, 2010), neuroimaging observations of disrupted thalamocortical sensory gating (Preller et al., 2018) and altered connectome-harmonic dynamics (Atasoy et al., 2017). Additionally, recent large-scale neuroimaging results further substantiate this link, demonstrating that psilocybin systematically modulates network connectivity and sensory input filtering as a function of environmental context (Stoliker et al., 2026). Specifically, in narrative state-space, the downregulation of environmental tracking mirrors the psychedelic relaxation of high-level sensory priors (Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019). Conversely, increased body-object features reflect unresolved somatosensory prediction errors anchored to altered thalamocortical filtering, presenting a barrier that suppresses transitions into internal unitive states (Preller et al., 2018).

Within the REBUS framework, the relaxation of top-down priors frees higher-level processing from rigid sensory constraints (Letheby, 2021; Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019); under these conditions, the present topological model demonstrates that cognitive-emotional elevation operates as the primary statistical lever guiding the experience along internally oriented state trajectories. In quantifying these directional pathways, this work unifies qualitative human narratives with quantitative system mechanics to establish a foundation for personalized, trajectory-targeted psychedelic medicine.
