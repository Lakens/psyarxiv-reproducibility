Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Where Does Attention Go When AI Listens? Meta-Awareness of Mediated Attention

Consider a student attending a lecture in a language in which they are not fully fluent.

Smart glasses or an earpiece translate the lecturer's speech, organize the main points, and assemble notes in real time. The student is not distracted and may understand more than they would without assistance. Nevertheless, the object of attention has changed. Rather than encountering the lecture primarily as an unfolding event, the student increasingly encounters a textual or spoken representation generated from it. Voice, hesitation, gesture, spatial orientation, audience response, and the felt continuity of the room may recede even as comprehension improves. Ambient AI therefore presents a problem that cannot be reduced to whether technology increases or decreases attention: it can sustain attention while changing what attention is directed toward.

The features that recede are not merely incidental. Speakers' gestures can contribute to listeners' comprehension (Hostetter, 2011), and learners may rely differently on captions and audio depending on their proficiency (Li, 2025). A generated representation can therefore improve access to propositional content while redistributing attention away from other information through which the event is understood.

A systematic review of 83 higher-education studies documented the expansion of English-medium instruction but found that the evidence available at the time was insufficient to establish that it improved English proficiency or clearly harmed content learning (Macaro et al., 2018). Lecture comprehension is also a recurring difficulty. In an Australian university study published in 2000, only 9% of the 174 respondents from non-English-speaking backgrounds who answered an immediate postlecture comprehension question reported understanding the lecture very well, while an alarming 22% not understanding much of its content (Mulligan & Kirkpatrick, 2000). In another study involving European English-medium programs, 42% of Norwegian respondents and 72% of German respondents had self-assessment scores indicating difficulty comprehending lectures in English; prominent difficulties included unfamiliar vocabulary and taking notes while listening (Hellekjær, 2010). Given the prominence of such issues in classrooms when medium of instruction is a language students are unfamiliar with, many are now turning to technology to close the gap. At a transnational English-medium university in China, students described using machine translation for reading and writing, many of them daily, and instructors reported students using simultaneous speech translation during class (High et al., 2025). Ambient translation in lectures is therefore an existing practice in international and transnational education, and the psychological questions it raises are already practical ones.

The lecture example exposes a limitation in common accounts of technology and attention. Such accounts often ask whether AI reduces attention, as though attention were a single quantity. Research instead describes attention as a family of selection processes directed toward external and internal targets (Chun et al., 2011). The more precise questions are what becomes the object of attention, how that object is constituted, and whether the user recognizes the role of mediation in constituting it. A learner absorbed in an accurate transcript can be highly attentive to meaning while attending less to the event from which that meaning was extracted. Conversely, translation can make an otherwise opaque lecture available for the first time. The same interface can therefore narrow contact with an event in one circumstance and restore it in another.

The argument has a defined scope. The term ambient AI extends the ambient intelligence tradition in computing, which describes environments that are sensitive and responsive to people (Cook et al., 2009) and systems that are embedded, context aware, personalized, adaptive, and anticipatory (Aarts, 2004). Following this tradition, ambient AI here refers to systems that sense an ongoing situation and produce assistance during it, including live captions, translation, generated notes, summaries, and wearable prompts. Clinical ambient scribes and speech-enabled lecture translation illustrate real-time assistance, although only the former typically involves generative language models (Kanaparthy et al., 2025; Shadiev & Huang, 2020). Generative systems are distinctive because they can interpret, compress, organize, and explain an event while it continues. The present analysis concerns the individual user's relation to the ongoing event and the representation produced from it.

The proposed process is experiential reconfiguration: ambient AI reorganizes present experience by redistributing attention between an event and a generated representation of it.

Reconfiguration is a neutral term, consistent with postphenomenological accounts in which technologies shape how the world appears to their users (Ihde, 1990; Verbeek, 2000 Verbeek, /2005)). It may scaffold contact when the representation makes otherwise inaccessible aspects of the event available, support concurrent augmentation when event and representation remain jointly relevant, or produce representational substitution when the generated output becomes the primary attentional object. None of these relations can be inferred from task performance alone.

The central construct is therefore meta-awareness of mediated attention: awareness that a technological representation is shaping what one attends to and which features of the event are being selected, transformed, foregrounded, or omitted.

Several features distinguish ambient generative AI from older forms of mediation such as slides, handouts, and conventional transcripts. Its output is generated during the event and may interpret, select, and compress content rather than reproduce it. It can be private and personalized, so co-present people neither share the same representation nor see what it omits. It is fallible in ways that may be difficult to detect because language models can produce fluent claims unsupported by the source (Ji et al., 2023). It is also conversational and available on demand, inviting repeated consultation while the event continues. These features do not inevitably produce substitution, but they increase the importance of recognizing the output as a selective representation rather than as the event itself. This paper develops a relational model of that recognition, distinguishes it from neighboring constructs, reviews relevant evidence, identifies boundary conditions, and advances propositions for future research.

Meta-Awareness of Mediated Attention

Meta-awareness refers to explicit awareness of one's current experience or mental activity rather than experience alone (Schooler, 2002). In AI-mediated situations, the relevant object of meta-awareness is not only where attention has gone but also how a technological representation is organizing that attention. Meta-awareness of mediated attention is defined here as awareness that an interface is shaping the user's attentional relation to an ongoing event, including awareness of attention moving between the event and its representation and recognition that the representation is selective. The construct is episode specific: a user may recognize mediation in one situation yet fail to recognize it in another.

Meta-awareness of mediated attention is distinct from attentional breadth. An event includes semantic content, but it is not exhausted by that content. A lecture also has pace, prosody, gesture, interpersonal cues, bodily responses, interruptions, and a social and physical setting. These features need not all occupy focal attention, and broad attention is not inherently preferable to narrow attention. Attentional breadth describes which features of the event the user encounters; meta-awareness describes whether the user recognizes that an interface is shaping that encounter. A student may knowingly prioritize an accurate transcript and remain meta-aware despite attending narrowly. Another may alternate between the transcript and lecturer without recognizing that the text has become the practical center of the lecture. The construct therefore concerns recognition of mediation, not an ideal of comprehensive awareness.

Meta-awareness must also be separated from regulation. Recognizing that attention has moved toward a representation creates an opportunity to continue, alternate, verify, redirect, or disengage, but it does not determine which response the user will choose. A user may knowingly remain with generated notes because semantic comprehension is the immediate priority. Conversely, awareness may not support redirection when the display is difficult to dismiss or the underlying event remains inaccessible.

Mindfulness research helps explain this distinction by treating attention, monitoring, and regulation as related but separable processes. In Bishop et al.'s (2004) account, mindfulness involves both the self-regulation of attention and an orientation of openness and curiosity toward present experience. Focused-attention practice likewise depends on monitoring whether attention remains with the intended object, detecting movement elsewhere, and redirecting it when appropriate (Lutz et al., 2008). Dunne et al. (2019) further characterize mindful meta-awareness as an ongoing capacity to monitor attention and affect while attention remains directed toward an explicit object. Applied to ambient AI, these accounts suggest that users may remain engaged with a generated representation while also recognizing that it has become the primary attentional object. Such recognition is not equivalent to redirecting attention: regulation additionally depends on whether the user regards the shift as inconsistent with their purpose and has the capacity to respond.

Decentering offers a complementary explanation. It describes a change in one's relationship to mental content through meta-awareness, reduced identification with internal experience, and reduced reactivity to thought content (Bernstein et al., 2015 (Bernstein et al., , 2019)). Thoughts and feelings can consequently be experienced as events or representations rather than accepted unreflectively as direct reflections of reality. In an AI-mediated episode, an analogous shift would involve encountering a transcript, summary, or recommendation as a selective construction rather than as a transparent substitute for the event. Decentering may therefore help explain how users can remain engaged with AI output while preserving awareness of its representational status and limitations. This application extends a construct developed primarily for relating to internal mental events and should not be assumed to transfer unchanged to technological representations. Meta-awareness of mediated attention is accordingly proposed as a distinct construct whose relationships with mindfulness, decentering, attentional monitoring, and metacognitive control require empirical evaluation. ongoing event, and attention is distributed between the event and that representation. A Relational Model of AI-Mediated Attention

The model separates five elements that are often conflated. Mediation produces a representation; attention is distributed between that representation and the event; meta-awareness concerns recognition of this distribution and of the representation's selectivity; regulation concerns the user's response; and outcomes concern what is learned, remembered, noticed, or done. This separation prevents semantic success from being treated as evidence that the event remained the primary attentional object, and it prevents awareness from being treated as evidence that regulation occurred.

The three relational configurations are analytic positions rather than rigid categories.

Experiential scaffolding occurs when mediation increases attainable contact with an event.

Concurrent augmentation occurs when event and representation remain jointly significant and the user alternates between or integrates them. Representational substitution occurs when the representation becomes the principal attentional object and the event is encountered chiefly as its source. A single system may support different configurations across users, tasks, and moments.

Distinguishing the Model From Related Constructs

Cognitive Offloading

Cognitive offloading involves using bodily action or external tools to alter a task's information-processing requirements and reduce cognitive demand (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). A student may offload transcription to a recording device, organization to a language model, or retrieval to a searchable archive. Offloading can weaken encoding when the user no longer performs operations needed for learning, but it can also release capacity for interpretation, questioning, or participation. Both outcomes are documented. Participants who offloaded information during a copying task performed faster but remembered less of what they had copied (Grinschgl et al., 2021), whereas saving a file containing a word list before studying a second list improved recall of the second list (Storm & Stone, 2015). The saving benefit was not observed when saving was unreliable or when the first list contained only two words. Cognitive offloading concerns how processing demands are distributed across internal processes, bodily action, and external resources. Its broader framework also addresses metacognitive evaluations and the allocation of resources released by automation, including consequences for situation awareness (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). The present account focuses more specifically on which aspects of an event or its representation occupy awareness and whether users recognize the representation's selective role.

Experiential reconfiguration focuses on this relation between the event, its representation, and awareness. A student can refrain from offloading and still experience displacement. Consider a learner who reads every generated caption, checks the AI's organization of the lecture, and actively relates each point to prior knowledge. The learner remains cognitively engaged and may expend more effort than a student who simply listens. Yet attention may be organized around the generated representation. Conversely, a student may offload note production to an ambient system and use the released capacity to watch the lecturer, notice uncertainty in an explanation, and participate more fully. Studies of ambient scribes report improved clinician engagement and anecdotal accounts of renewed connection with patients (Kanaparthy et al., 2025). These examples illustrate why the extent of offloading need not determine the quality of experiential contact.

Divided Attention and Neighboring Constructs

Divided attention is also an incomplete description. Multiple-resource theory predicts greater interference when concurrent tasks compete for the same resources, which can include a shared sensory modality or processing code (Wickens, 2008). Instructional research reports comparable costs when learners must integrate spatially separated sources of information (Kalyuga et al., 1999). It does not distinguish an unrelated message from a live translation that carries the lecturer's meaning. Both may draw the eyes away from the lecturer, but the first interrupts the event whereas the second mediates access to it. Text on screen also attracts the eyes with little regard to need: viewers spend considerable time reading subtitles even when they understand the language of the soundtrack (d' Ydewalle et al., 1991). The relevant question is relational: does the secondary stream return the user to the event, operate alongside it, or become the event for practical purposes?

Three further literatures describe neighboring phenomena. Presence research defines presence as a perceptual illusion of nonmediation, in which users respond as though a medium were absent even though they can identify it when asked (Lombard & Ditton, 1997). Research on aviation displays describes attentional tunneling as persisting with an information source, hypothesis, or goal at the expense of other relevant demands. Across simulator studies, pilots using synthetic vision with trajectory guidance more often missed unexpected hazards visible only outside the display than pilots using synthetic vision alone (Wickens & Alexander, 2009).

This provides a perceptual analogy for smart glasses that overlay captions or summaries. Media multitasking research examines associations between habitual use of multiple media streams and cognitive performance, including attentional control. However, findings vary across studies, and the direction of causation remains unresolved (Ophir et al., 2009; Uncapher & Wagner, 2018).

The present model differs from these constructs in its unit of analysis and its separation of processes. Presence concerns responding as though mediation were absent, whereas experiential reconfiguration can occur when the interface is fully visible but its selective role is no longer salient. Attentional tunneling describes persistence with a source or goal at the expense of other demands, whereas the present model distinguishes whether a representation supplies access to the event, operates alongside it, or becomes its practical substitute. Media multitasking typically concerns concurrent media use and performance across tasks; the present account concerns an episode in which one stream represents the event that another stream presents. Its distinctive contribution is to explain how semantic engagement can remain strong or improve while contact with nonrepresented features changes, and to treat recognition of that mediation and regulation of attention as separate processes.

Evidence for the Redistribution of Experience Photo-Taking as a Mechanistic Analogy

The most informative evidence does not yet come from ambient generative AI. Phototaking experiments provide a stronger test of the underlying attentional proposition because they examine what happens when people mediate an experience while it occurs. The results do not support a uniform impairment account. They show selective changes in visual attention and enjoyment (Diehl et al., 2016), and in what is remembered (Barasch et al., 2017; Henkel, 2014).

Henkel (2014) led participants through a museum and instructed them to observe some objects and photograph others. Photographing an entire object was followed by poorer memory for the object, its details, and its location than observation alone. When participants zoomed in on a specific feature, however, the impairment disappeared, including for details outside the zoomed region. The finding is often cited as evidence for offloading to the camera. The zoom condition complicates that explanation, and later work points the same way. Soares and Storm (2018) found poorer memory for visual details of photographed paintings than for those only observed, even when participants used an ephemeral photo application or immediately deleted each photograph. These findings challenge an explanation based solely on consciously relying on the camera to remember. They are consistent with disrupted engagement or encoding, but the study did not isolate that mechanism and could not rule out automatic offloading. Barasch et al. (2017) showed more directly that mediation changes the distribution of attention. Across a field study and three laboratory studies, participants permitted to take photographs later recognized more visual information and less auditory information than participants who did not take photographs. They did not review the images before testing, and mentally taking photographs produced a similar pattern. The effect was therefore not simply reliance on an external archive or the motor act of operating a camera. Photo-taking oriented attention toward visually representable aspects of the scene and away from sound.

A separate series of nine experiments found that photo-taking often increased enjoyment because it increased engagement with photographable aspects of positive experiences (Diehl et al., 2016). The benefit was weaker when the activity was already highly engaging or when taking photographs interfered with participation. Eye-tracking in a museum showed greater visual attention to objects that participants might photograph. These results challenge the familiar claim that mediation necessarily removes people from experience. It can intensify contact with selected features while reducing the relative availability of others.

This literature is analogical rather than direct evidence about AI. A camera does not translate, summarize, or converse. The experiments nevertheless establish a principle that is directly relevant: an aid can improve engagement with the information it privileges without preserving the experiential profile of the unmediated event. Across these studies, photo-taking improved memory in one channel and impaired it in another (Barasch et al., 2017), while also increasing enjoyment under suitable conditions (Diehl et al., 2016). A study that measures only one of these outcomes can produce an unduly simple conclusion.

Evidence From Learning and Mediated Access

Evidence from captions and translation first establishes that mediation can change the route through which an event becomes accessible. When learners cannot process speech fluently, text may cease to function as a secondary aid and become their principal route to the content. Li (2025), for example, found that lower-proficiency learners relied more heavily on captions than higher-proficiency learners, whose processing depended more on audio. The significance of this finding is not simply that proficiency predicts caption use. It shows that the relation between event and representation depends on baseline access: the same caption stream may scaffold one learner's contact with the lecture while remaining supplementary or redundant for another.

Once a representation becomes a major route to content, attentional redistribution does not necessarily appear as poorer learning. Subtitle formats altered participants' gaze patterns in Liao et al. ( 2020), yet recall did not differ significantly across conditions, and bilingual subtitles were associated with lower reported cognitive load than the no-subtitle condition. This pattern supports the distinction between performance and attentional relation. Assistance may make meaning easier to obtain while simultaneously reorganizing how the audiovisual event is encountered. A stable recall score therefore cannot establish that mediation left the experiential route to learning unchanged.

Translation further clarifies why redistribution should not automatically be interpreted as displacement. In Shadiev and Huang's (2020) study, access to translated text was associated with lower reported cognitive load than listening to foreign-language lectures without support, as well as higher scores on two proprietary indices, labeled "attention" and "meditation," generated by a single-sensor brainwave headset (NeuroSky MindWave). Translated text also produced higher scores on both indices than same-language English transcription, suggesting that the benefit was not simply a consequence of displaying text. The "meditation" index was intended to reflect relaxation and should not be interpreted as a measure of meditation practice, mindfulness, or meta-awareness. The brief lectures, partly random, partly matched group allocation, and devicederived indices limit what can be concluded about learning or awareness. Nevertheless, the study illustrates the accessibility side of the framework: when the spoken event is not sufficiently attainable, directing attention toward a translation may restore contact with the lecture's meaning rather than displace an otherwise accessible experience. Together, the caption and translation findings suggest that attentional redistribution must be interpreted relative to what the user could access without assistance, not against an idealized unmediated baseline.

AI-supported note-taking extends the argument because the representation no longer merely reproduces speech; it interprets the event by selecting, organizing, and connecting ideas.

This additional transformation makes the user's relation to the output especially consequential. Pi et al. (2026) found that learners who participated in note production, either independently or with AI, acquired more knowledge than learners who received AI-produced notes alone.

Learners in the learner-AI condition also produced higher-quality, although less complete, notes and directed more visual attention toward the note area. These results do not show that AI collaboration preserved contact with the instructional event, but they indicate that learning outcomes and attentional distribution vary with how the learner participates in constructing the representation.

The theoretically important contrast is therefore not between AI and non-AI note-taking as fixed media, but between different relations to the representational layer. Note-taking research distinguishes the cognitive process of generating notes from the external product available for review (Kiewra, 1985). A finished AI summary supplies a product while removing much of the selection and organization normally performed by the learner. By contrast, editing, extending, or questioning AI-generated notes can preserve constructive engagement (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016), and contingent learner-AI exchange may support the interactive engagement described by the interactive, constructive, active, and passive (ICAP) framework, which orders these modes of engagement from highest to lowest expected learning (Chi & Wylie, 2014). Yet greater engagement with the representation is not equivalent to greater contact with the event. The representation may function as a means of returning to the lecture or become the destination of attention in its own right.

This relational interpretation also avoids attributing attentional quality to a medium by default. Claims that longhand note-taking necessarily produces deeper learning than laptop use were weakened when a preregistered replication reproduced differences in note quantity and verbatim overlap but not the original immediate conceptual advantage for longhand (Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014; Urry et al., 2021). The broader lesson is that neither the technology label nor a learning score identifies the user's attentional relation. Across captions, translation, and note-taking, the evidence supports a consistent conclusion: mediated support can improve access or learning while changing the route through which the event is encountered, but existing studies rarely establish whether users recognize that change. Meta-awareness of mediated attention adds explanatory value by addressing precisely this unresolved question.

Boundary Conditions: When AI Restores or Displaces Contact Accessibility and Attainable Experience

A theory of displacement that treats all representations as losses would be conceptually weak and practically exclusionary. For a learner who cannot hear the lecturer clearly, cannot process the language at conversational speed, or cannot sustain conventional note-taking, captions, translation, and generated notes may create access rather than dilute it. A review of more than 100 empirical studies concluded that captions improve comprehension of, attention to, and memory for video for hearing viewers as well as deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers (Gernsbacher, 2015). Research on deaf students in interpreted classrooms also documents substantially different patterns of visual attention allocation across interpreters, instructors, and displays, although it does not establish that those patterns cause differences in learning (Marschark et al., 2005).

The appropriate baseline is therefore the user's attainable experience without the aid, not an imagined fully accessible unmediated event. Without assistance, a person may devote most available capacity to decoding fragments; with it, they may recover the argument, anticipate transitions, and redirect attention to the speaker. Lectures are already mediated by microphones, slides, language, and the lecturer's framing, so the relevant comparison is among forms of mediation and their consequences. Evaluations should also incorporate the accounts of deaf, hard-of-hearing, and second-language users themselves rather than infer their experience from comparisons with hearing or fluent peers.

Outside education, ambient AI scribes provide a useful counterexample to the assumption that mediation displaces contact. These systems listen during clinical consultations and draft documentation so that clinicians need not divide attention between the patient and the electronic record. A rapid review found only six eligible real-world studies through 2024; most were observational, small, or survey based. Reported benefits included reduced documentation burden and improved clinician engagement, but objective measures of patient-clinician attention were scarce (Kanaparthy et al., 2025). The evidence is too weak to establish restored contact. It nevertheless illustrates a plausible design route by which offloading can increase direct contact with an ongoing interaction.

Function, Fit, and User Control

The boundary between scaffolding and displacement turns on function, fit, and control.

Assistance is more likely to scaffold when it supplies information the user could not otherwise obtain, arrives at moments chosen or anticipated by the user, and remains easy to set aside. It is more likely to displace when it continuously reformats an already accessible event, demands monitoring, or presents its output as a sufficient account of what occurred. Automation research identifies a further risk: under competing task demands, people may monitor consistently reliable automation inadequately and fail to detect its errors. Trust may contribute to this pattern, although its relationship with monitoring has not been firmly established (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Accuracy alone does not resolve the issue. A perfectly accurate transcript can still narrow attention to what can be transcribed.

Greater experiential breadth is not always desirable. A student may reasonably prioritize conceptual understanding over awareness of room ambience, and a surgeon may need a narrowed attentional field during a critical procedure. The framework does not prescribe indiscriminate openness or treat unmediated experience as inherently superior. Its normative claim is more limited: users should be able to recognize when an interface is selecting on their behalf, understand that the representation is partial, and regulate their relation to it when regulation is relevant and feasible.

Propositions and Future Research Directions

The model is intended to guide a program of research rather than prescribe a single experimental protocol. Its claims can be evaluated in lectures, consultations, meetings, conversations, and other situations in which AI represents an event while it unfolds. The following propositions state the principal expectations of the framework shown in Figure 1 while leaving their operational expression open to theoretical refinement and to the demands of particular settings.

Proposition 1: Representational orientation. As an AI representation becomes more continuous, salient, or interpretively complete, attention will be more likely to become organized around the representation rather than the event it represents.

Proposition 2: Conditional scaffolding. AI mediation will be more likely to scaffold contact when it makes relevant aspects of an event attainable that the user could not otherwise access or process effectively.

Proposition 3: Conditional substitution. AI mediation will be more likely to produce representational substitution when the representation repeatedly reformats an already accessible event, demands continued monitoring, or is treated as a sufficient account of what is occurring.

Proposition 4: Outcome dissociation. Representation-focused attention may improve access to semantic content while reducing attention to, or later memory for, relevant features that the representation does not preserve. The direction and value of this dissociation will depend on the user's goals and on the significance of the omitted features.

Proposition 5: Meta-awareness and calibration. Greater meta-awareness of mediated attention will be associated with more accurate recognition of where attention was directed and of the representation's selective role. This proposition concerns calibration rather than a presumed preference for broader attention.

Proposition 6: Regulation as a separate mechanism. Meta-awareness will reduce unwanted displacement only when users also have the intention, opportunity, and capacity to regulate attention. Awareness may therefore coexist with representational substitution when remaining with the representation is deliberate or when returning to the event is not feasible. Research should also examine variation rather than seek a single average effect of "AI use." The same support may scaffold one user, augment another, and substitute for the event for a third. Relations may also shift within a single episode as difficulty, fatigue, goals, or trust change. Perceptual load and expertise reversal provide useful starting points for explaining such variation (Kalyuga, 2007; Lavie, 2005), but the framework should remain open to additional moderators. The theoretically important task is to explain transitions in the user's relation to the event, not to classify a device as inherently beneficial, harmful, attentive, or distracting.

Design Implications

A more useful design goal than minimal AI is recoverable contact with the event.

Interfaces can support that goal by allowing users to reduce output density, postpone summaries, request clarification selectively, and review organized notes after the event rather than continuously during it. Published guidelines for human-AI interaction make similar recommendations, asking systems to time services to the user's context and to support efficient dismissal of unwanted services (Amershi et al., 2019). Translation may need to remain live, but it can be timed and positioned to reduce unnecessary competition with faces, demonstrations, or other relevant cues.

Systems should also communicate their selectivity. A generated outline is an interpretation, not a transparent copy of the lecture. Research on trust in automation suggests that interfaces should communicate system capabilities and reliability so that users can calibrate trust and reliance (Lee & See, 2004). Communicating uncertainty, preserving links to the relevant moment, and allowing rapid return to the original speech can help prevent the summary from becoming the sole record of what happened. User control must include the option to stop monitoring the AI without losing later access to its output.

User practice and interface design should be considered together. Users could be taught to recognize shifts between event and representation, to verify generated interpretations against their source, and to decide deliberately when the representation should remain primary. Such practices require direct evaluation and should not substitute for responsible design. If a system repeatedly makes its output the most salient object, advising users simply to monitor themselves places the burden of managing mediation on the individual while leaving the interface unchanged.

Conclusion

Ambient AI changes experience not only by adding information or reducing effort, but also by reorganizing the relation between a user and an unfolding event. Its output may open an event that would otherwise remain inaccessible, remain alongside the event as a complementary attentional object, or become the principal object through which the event is encountered. These possibilities cannot be distinguished by task performance alone. Improved comprehension may coexist with diminished contact with features that the representation does not preserve, just as narrowed attention may be fully appropriate when it serves the user's purposes.

The central theoretical issue is therefore not whether mediation is present, or whether attention has increased or decreased, but whether the user recognizes how mediation is shaping attention. Meta-awareness of mediated attention identifies this capacity without assuming that recognition will produce redirection. Awareness creates the possibility of deliberate regulation; intention, opportunity, and control determine whether regulation occurs. This separation allows the framework to acknowledge accessibility and performance gains while still asking what has been selected, transformed, or omitted. These distinctions are offered as theoretical claims, and the propositions advanced here are intended to make them open to empirical test.

On this account, ambient AI should be judged neither by the amount of assistance it provides nor by an ideal of unmediated contact. It should be judged by the attentional relation it creates and by whether that relation remains recognizable and governable. Assistance supports experience when it expands attainable contact with an event or serves as a representation the user can knowingly integrate, consult, and set aside. It displaces experience when the representation silently becomes sufficient for the event and its selectivity disappears from awareness. The decisive human capacity is therefore not resistance to mediation, but the ability to know when one is attending through AI, to understand that the resulting account is partial, and to determine whether that form of attention remains aligned with one's purposes. Well-designed systems can support that capacity by communicating their selectivity and keeping the event recoverable.
