Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) of cortical sites connected with the hippocampus improves scores on episodic memory tests and is being tested for memory decline, including in Alzheimer's disease. These gains are read as gains in intentional retrieval.

Objective: To examine whether standard memory outcomes can support this reading and how the contribution of repetition priming can be measured.

Methods: Narrative review of hippocampal-network stimulation studies, cognitive and electrophysiological research on priming and processing fluency, stimulation studies of priming and parietal cortex, and clinical trials of TMS for memory decline.

Results: Old/new recognition and cued recall present studied items or cues again at test. Repetition priming makes these items easier to process, and this fluency can raise a memory score with intentional retrieval unchanged. The fluency behind word-recognition decisions is largely conceptual, so it persists after a change of surface form. TMS can also alter priming directly, and stimulating the standard parietal target changes the subjective side of recollection.

Meta-analytic evidence shows that the gains are specific to episodic memory and largest for recollection tests after stimulation before encoding, but the priming contribution to these gains has not been estimated.

Conclusions: Process-level claims about stimulation-related memory gains require the priming contribution to be measured. Five measurement steps, centred on matched indirect tests and process-dissociation procedures, make it measurable in the same participants, including in clinical trials.

Introduction

Episodic memory involves the conscious recollection of a specific past event, whereas implicit memory is expressed when prior experience influences performance without conscious or intentional recollection [1, 2]. Here, this distinction is used to refer to how memory is expressed at test. Priming refers to the facilitation of subsequent processing produced by prior exposure.

Processing fluency refers to the subjective ease of processing that may result from this facilitation. Participants may interpret this ease as evidence that an item was encountered previously and use it to inform recognition judgements [3, 4]. Recent syntheses describe the subjective feeling of familiarity as fluency attributed to past encounters, a fluency that can have mnemonic and non-mnemonic sources [5].

In tasks that re-present studied material at test, performance may therefore reflect repetition priming and the processing fluency it can produce alongside episodic retrieval. A single outcome measure may contain contributions from multiple memory processes [6, 7]. A direct assessment of these implicit influences requires an indirect priming measure matched to the explicit memory test. Assessing explicit and indirect performance together makes their potential contributions more measurable and comparable within the same participants and materials. This measurement problem is particularly consequential in transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) studies, in which improvements in task performance are interpreted as improvements in episodic-memory function. TMS has been used for more than two decades to modulate episodic memory through stimulation of different cortical targets. Following early interference studies of the prefrontal cortex [8, 9], the literature expanded substantially. A systematic review included 59 TMS studies of episodic memory [10].

One influential approach within this research programme is the network-based stimulation paradigm introduced in 2014 [11]. That study applied multi-session repetitive TMS (rTMS) to a left lateral parietal site chosen for its resting-state functional connectivity with the hippocampus. It reported increased connectivity within the hippocampal network alongside improved face-cued word recall. Because conventional TMS cannot directly reach the hippocampus, the approach stimulates an accessible cortical node to influence the hippocampal network indirectly. This approach is now termed hippocampal indirectly targeted stimulation (HITS).

The paradigm was subsequently extended to older adults, individuals with amnestic mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's disease [12] [13] [14] [15], and psychiatric populations [16].

Subsequent studies from the original laboratory and from independent groups have largely reproduced the memory effect [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] [22]. A re-analysis of an independent multi-day study that had found no memory effect showed larger memory gains in participants whose stimulation site lay closer to an individually personalised target [23]. The programme has since moved into longer clinical trials, including a 52-week randomised trial of precuneus rTMS in Alzheimer's disease [24].

A quantitative synthesis of 38 HITS studies reported a small-to-moderate improvement in episodic memory (Hedges' g = 0.44) [25]. The effect on non-memory tasks was close to zero (g = 0.04). The effect was larger for tasks emphasising recollection than for recognition tasks, a pattern interpreted as evidence of cognitive and mechanistic specificity. The effect was also larger when stimulation preceded encoding, and it was largest when stimulation before encoding was combined with a recollection test (g = 0.66), the design of the original study.

Stimulation after encoding produced an average effect close to zero. The advantage for recollection tests argues against a simple item-fluency account, under which old/new recognition, the format most open to fluency, would show the largest gains. These findings support specificity across task domains. Establishing specificity at the process level additionally requires the separate measurement of episodic retrieval and priming contributions. Stimulation before encoding can shape both explicit and implicit traces, and cued recall still re-presents a studied cue, so neither moderator identifies the process by itself.

Here we set out the test formats through which priming can contribute to these scores. We also review evidence that stimulation reaches priming and alters recollective experience at the parietal target. The following sections then address why this contribution stays unmeasured and what it means in patient groups and clinical trials. They close with five measurement steps that separate priming from retrieval in the same participants.

Two processes behind a single score

Behavioural tasks separate explicit and implicit memory processes only in part. Performance on direct tests can be influenced by automatic processes, whereas performance on indirect tests can be affected by intentional retrieval and strategic responding. Both direct and indirect memory tasks are therefore process-impure [26]. Impure measures can still be separated. A single explicit score leaves the contributions confounded, with no means of estimating either.

A matched indirect measure or a process-dissociation procedure turns the assumption that the score reflects retrieval alone into an empirical comparison within the same participants and materials.

Masked primes flashed just before a recognition decision change both "old" responses and the brain potential linked to familiarity. Relative to primes that match the test word, orthographically similar primes produce fewer "old" responses and more negative potentials 300 to 500 ms after the word [27]. Reducing perceptual overlap while holding meaning constant leaves the effect intact, whereas removing conceptual overlap abolishes it, so the fluency driving these decisions is largely conceptual [28]. In a recognition test, repeated words produced a frontal old/new effect linked to familiarity [29]. When repeated words were judged for pleasantness, the old/new effect resembled an N400 effect. Recognition can also be accurate without reported awareness of retrieval, both behaviourally and in its electrophysiological signature [30, 31], although an attempt to reproduce the behavioural effect in three experiments was unsuccessful [32]. Together, these studies show that processing fluency can contribute to some recognition judgements and that the same behavioural outcome can be produced by more than one process. A single-system account reads these effects differently. In patients with amnesia, a formal model with one memory signal predicted the pattern of recognition and repetition priming for the same objects, including deficits in both [33]. On that account, priming and recognition are two readouts of one signal. The measurement argument holds under both accounts. In each case a recognition score combines contributions that a matched indirect measure can estimate separately.

Where test format decides

The opportunity created by re-presentation can be seen in one of the central tasks of this research programme. In the face-word task of the original HITS study, participants studied words paired with faces and later attempted to recall the associated word when the same face was presented again at test. Easier processing of a previously encountered face differs from remembering its associated word, and by itself it cannot produce the correct response. Fluency in processing the cue could still contribute to the explicit response through attention, decisionmaking or access to associated information. This mechanism is inferred by applying the priming literature reviewed above to the structure of the task. The original study included no measure of it. A matched indirect measure would allow this cue fluency to be assessed alongside intentional episodic retrieval.

Tasks that re-present studied material differ in how open they are to this contribution (Fig. 1).

In recognition tests that require old and new items to be distinguished, the relatively fluent processing of a studied item can directly support an "old" response. The contribution is less direct when a studied cue is re-presented to elicit an associated response. Cue fluency leaves the correct target unspecified, yet it may influence the process through which the response is produced. In forced-choice or temporal-order tasks in which all response options were previously studied, the options share a similar repetition history. Simple item fluency is then less diagnostic of the correct response. Even in these tasks, repetition can affect the processing of re-presented materials, item-response bindings or decision processes [34]. The scoring of a test also matters. Sensitivity measures such as d′ correct for a shift in response criterion. Item-specific fluency raises hits on studied items without raising false alarms to new foils, so it raises d′ as well. The lure-discrimination index of the Mnemonic Similarity Task, used in a HITS study of fear discrimination, works in the opposite direction [35]. A lure that feels fluent is more likely to be called old, so fluency lowers this index.

One study illustrates the importance of these distinctions [36]. Although studied objects were re-presented at test, stimulation increased the precision of memory independently of overall retrieval success. This pattern points to a more specific change than a simple item-fluency account would predict. The possible contribution of priming should therefore be evaluated against the presence of re-presentation, the structure of the task and the nature of the behavioural outcome.

Stimulation reaches priming and the parietal target

Stimulation can act on priming directly. Magnetic stimulation of the right visual cortex impairs form-specific object priming [37]. An interventional study dissociated priming-based from working-memory-based attentional guidance in early visual cortex [38]. Both studies used outcomes outside episodic memory. Together they show that priming is causally accessible to stimulation.

The parietal target also shapes how recollection is experienced. Continuous theta-burst stimulation was applied to the left angular gyrus, part of the lateral parietal region that HITS studies target. It kept the accuracy of source recollection high but selectively reduced confidence in it [39]. The authors attribute this pattern to the integration of memory features into conscious experience. The same stimulation reduced the retrieval of context features across sensory modalities and left the integration of features within one modality intact [40]. Online stimulation of the left angular gyrus during encoding left recollection and familiarity estimates unchanged and altered confidence in incorrect judgements [41]. An attentional account places the bottom-up capture of attention by retrieved information in ventral parietal cortex, which includes the angular gyrus [42]. A memory gain after parietal stimulation can therefore reflect a change in how memory signals are experienced and used, with or without a change in hippocampal retrieval.

Some studies have used control tasks from other cognitive domains to test the specificity of a TMS effect for episodic memory. One study administered a probabilistic-classification task alongside a face-word task [43]. This comparison tests for a general improvement across different tasks. The classification task uses different materials and test demands from the faceword task. It therefore informs selectivity across cognitive domains and leaves the facilitation from repeated faces and words within the memory task unmeasured.

Changes in connectivity within the targeted network address a different but complementary question. In the original study, its multi-day follow-up and the classification study, connectivity changes in the hippocampal network accompanied the memory gains, which shows that stimulation affected the intended neural system. Identifying the psychological processes expressed in the behavioural score additionally requires direct and indirect behavioural measures to be considered together. The neural target of an intervention and the cognitive processes through which that intervention is expressed in behaviour can thus be evaluated as related but distinct levels of evidence.

Why the priming contribution stays unmeasured

The outcome measures of this programme were designed to index hippocampal-dependent associative memory. They score the accuracy of explicit responses. Process-level claims are then drawn by comparing task formats, such as recollection-based with recognition-based tests.

Both formats are direct tests, so the comparison leaves the priming contribution to each format unestimated.

Priming enters the interpretation of stimulation studies as an explanation offered after the results. Reductions in evoked activity after stimulation have been read as efficient processing, by analogy with findings from priming experiments [44], as in the precision study discussed above. Higher false-alarm rates after cerebellar stimulation have been read as stronger activation of associative links in semantic memory, in line with stimulation effects on associative priming [45]. A study of semantic prediction in reading considered an associativepriming account and set it aside [46]. Another report proposed that implicit memory guided responses in an explicit recognition test, drawing on the electrophysiological evidence reviewed above [47]. An earlier study by our group read a gain in visual recognition after parietal rTMS in Alzheimer's disease as an improvement of implicit memory [48]. Each of these interpretations uses explicit scores alone.

One study measured the contribution directly [49]. It administered indirect word-completion and explicit recognition measures to the same participants and excluded the words used in the completion test from the recognition analysis. Disruption of the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex reduced the directed-forgetting effect on explicit recognition and left the effect on the indirect test unchanged. The design shows that explicit and indirect effects of stimulation can be compared within one experiment.

The field can therefore show that stimulation changes memory scores and network connectivity, and that the change is selective across task domains. It cannot yet show which memory process produced a given gain.

Patient groups and networks

This distinction is particularly important in clinical samples. Priming cannot be reduced to a single brain region or memory system. The coordinate-based meta-analysis cited above covered 65 functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies. Repetition suppression accompanying behavioural priming was concentrated in the left inferior frontal and fusiform gyri, and perceptual and conceptual priming showed no simple anatomical separation. A clinical disorder or a particular TMS target therefore cannot be assumed to affect episodic memory, priming and their supporting networks to the same extent. The role of the hippocampus remains contested. A 2024 discussion paper reviewed 13 fMRI studies and found a confound, such as explicit memory or unbalanced attention, behind every reported hippocampal effect in implicit memory [50]. Five commentaries disputed that conclusion, with arguments that included statistical learning, contextual cueing and findings with objectively invisible stimuli [51] [52] [53] [54] [55].

One commentary supported it, noting that 7 of 18 studies linking the hippocampus with implicit memory reported no correction for multiple comparisons [56]. The authors maintained their conclusion and called for conceptual clarity and more measures of awareness [57]. If the hippocampus supports implicit memory, stimulation of the hippocampal network becomes more likely to act on priming; if the critique holds, it becomes less likely. Current designs cannot test either possibility behaviourally.

Alzheimer's disease and amnestic mild cognitive impairment provide a clear example.

Recognition can be impaired from the earliest stages of Alzheimer's disease, including mild cognitive impairment [58]. In early Alzheimer's disease, impaired recognition of scenes has been reported alongside intact repetition priming for the same scenes, with different neural correlates for the two [59]. Alzheimer pathology has also been associated with some measures of conceptual priming, without the same relationship for certain perceptual or word-completion measures [60]. A meta-analysis of 32 studies found repetition priming lower, on average, in both amnestic mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's dementia than in healthy older adults [61]. The size of the difference varied across tasks and paradigms. Where priming is relatively better preserved than episodic memory for a particular task, a re-presentation-based score may carry a larger share of priming than the level of intentional retrieval would suggest.

Related dissociations have been reported in other clinical groups. In Parkinson's disease, free recall can be impaired in patients without dementia, and recall and recognition in patients with dementia, while word-and picture-fragment identification priming shows no impairment in either group [62]. Large-scale connectivity changes have also been described across the continuum of cognitive impairment in Parkinson's disease [63]. Meta-analytic findings in schizophrenia indicate a small average impairment in priming, with reduced conceptual priming and intact perceptual priming [64]. Altered cortico-hippocampal network connectivity has also been associated with cognitive performance in first-episode schizophrenia [65]. Post-stroke cognitive impairment is accompanied by connectivity changes involving the hippocampus, precuneus and cingulate regions [66]. Patients with occipital stroke have shown preserved conceptual category-exemplar priming alongside impaired perceptual picture-identification performance [67]. Priming therefore varies across clinical populations, and its contribution should be evaluated within the relevant sample, materials and task format.

What is at stake in trials

These dissociations bear directly on how stimulation trials are read. Recent randomised trials in Alzheimer's disease, including the 52-week precuneus trial described above, use composite scales as primary and secondary endpoints. These are the cognitive subscale of the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale (ADAS-Cog), the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB). Of these, the word-recognition items of the ADAS-Cog present studied words again at test [68]. Effects on these scales are small and can be transient. In an H-coil trial, real rTMS produced a mean decrease of 1.01 ADAS-Cog points per time point, a trend that was no longer evident two months after the end of treatment [69]. In a multi-site trial, active and sham coils both produced significant cognitive improvement up to two months after treatment [70]. Recently derived minimal clinically important differences for these scales exceed the average effects reported in recent trials of antiamyloid antibodies [71].

When an effect is this close to the threshold of clinical meaning, the process behind it decides what a trial has shown. A gain in intentional retrieval would be expected to transfer to the everyday episodic function that the trials aim at. A gain in facilitated processing of re-presented material would act on a process that one word-stem completion study found to match healthy older adults in Alzheimer's disease [72]. Such a gain has value as a different claim with a different therapeutic pathway. Its benefit also needs evidence of its own. Errorless learning, a rehabilitation technique thought to work through implicit memory, benefited young adults and gave older adults no advantage, and the effects of errorful learning related to explicit memory function [73]. Measuring both contributions allows a trial to say which of these it has produced.

No single ideal task removes this problem. Five steps make the possible contribution of priming visible and interpretable (Table 1). shape reproduction in the study of psychiatric patients described above. Story recall [74] and complex-figure recall [75] serve the same purpose. These formats reduce the direct contribution of repetition fluency because no studied item or cue is presented again at test. When instruments such as the Auditory Verbal Learning Test are used, the recommendation applies to their freeand delayed-recall outcomes, since a recognition component re-presents studied material. These tasks are also process-impure, yet they reduce the ambiguity that arises directly from item repetition at test.

Third, studies can combine outcomes with and without re-presentation in the same participants.

Reporting recognition alongside free-or delayed-recall measures tests if an effect generalises across test formats. Our earlier study described above shows the value of joint reporting. Its battery included immediate and delayed visual reproduction recall, visual recognition and verbal-memory measures, and the significant memory change appeared in visual recognition.

In clinical samples, an instrument whose structure contrasts free recall, cued recall and recognition is particularly informative. Under the controlled encoding of the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test, recall that cueing normalises indicates a retrieval deficit. Recall that stays impaired despite cueing and recognition indicates a storage deficit, the amnestic profile of the medial temporal type [76, 77]. Reporting these outcomes separately distinguishes an intervention that improves access to a stored representation from one that changes what is stored. A single composite score cannot support that distinction. Task scores can also have low reliability, and difference scores, such as change from baseline, depend on the reliability of their components and on the correlation between them [78]. The measures should preferably be administered at the same time points, and comparisons between them should be prespecified.

Fourth, studies can add a matched indirect test using the same materials. The clearest design administers an indirect priming measure alongside the explicit-memory test, in the same participants and from the same pool of studied materials. The matched design described above shows how non-overlapping item sets can be assigned to the two analyses. Where possible, items should be counterbalanced across test formats, test order should be arranged to minimise contamination between tests, and no item should contribute to both analyses.

Fifth, studies can apply the process-dissociation procedure, which estimates controlled and automatic contributions within the same task structure [79]. In the inclusion condition, participants complete a word stem with a word they remember from the study list, or with the first suitable word that comes to mind. Both intentional retrieval and automatic priming can therefore produce a studied response. In the exclusion condition, participants avoid words from the study list. A studied word produced despite this instruction indicates an automatic influence.

Modelling inclusion and exclusion rates gives separate estimates of the two contributions (Box 1). Both tools improve comparability without delivering process purity. Process-dissociation estimates depend on assumptions about the relationship between processes and on comprehension of and compliance with the instructions. Violations of the invariance assumption can bias the estimates and mislead their interpretation [80]. Studies should therefore report inclusion and exclusion rates alongside the estimates and check if the two estimates covary. Indirect priming tests have already been run in amnestic mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease, as the patient studies discussed above show.

The literature reviewed here is heterogeneous in stimulation protocols, cortical targets, tasks, outcome measures and clinical populations. Priming need not contribute to the same extent across studies, and its relationship with intentional retrieval has to be evaluated within each task and sample. The same template applies to other neuromodulation programmes with cognitive outcomes: name the process that the endpoint leaves unmeasured, add a matched measure of it, and estimate both in the same participants. Applied to memory, these steps would let the field state which process a stimulation-related gain reflects and which therapeutic pathway that gain opens.

Re-presentation at some stages only, or a repetition history shared by all response options, reduces the diagnostic value of item fluency. d Studied material absent at test: free recall, reproduction from memory and event reconstruction allow no direct item-fluency contribution. The panels show where a contribution is possible; its mechanism and size require measurement. The figure was drawn with code prepared with the assistance of Claude (Anthropic).
