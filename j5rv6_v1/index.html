To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Objectives: To examine whether objectively measured activity was associated with psychological well-being in older adults and whether social participation and digital connectivity were related to these associations.

Trends Study (2022-2024), this analysis applied multilevel models with within-between decomposition to examine three activity indicators (mean activity, relative amplitude, and behavioral fragmentation) and four well-being outcomes (loneliness, positive affect, meaning and purpose, and self-determination) in 543 participants. Between-person structural equation models examined social participation as a statistical correlate, and cross-level interactions tested digital connectivity as a moderator of within-person associations.

Results: At the between-person level, fragmentation was associated with three of four outcomes (positive affect, meaning and purpose, self-determination), whereas mean activity was associated with one (positive affect); seven of 24 coefficients survived Bonferroni correction. Mean activity showed within-person associations with loneliness and positive affect. Relative amplitude showed no association surviving correction. Exploratory indirect associations through social participation were significant for positive affect, meaning and purpose, and self-determination, but not loneliness. None of the 12 cross-level interactions was significant (all ps ≥ .069).

Discussion: Older adults with less fragmented daily activity, characterized by fewer shifts from active to sedentary states, reported higher well-being than their peers. In years when participants were more active than their own usual level, they reported less loneliness and more positive affect. Social participation statistically accounted for part of the between-person associations. These findings are observational and based on a selected subsample.

Introduction

The U.S. population 65 and older is projected to grow from approximately 58 million in 2022 to approximately 82 million by 2050, increasing from roughly 17% to 23% of the total population (Mather & Scommegna, 2024). The population 80 and older is projected to be the fastest-growing segment of the older population (Federal Interagency Forum on Aging-Related Statistics, 2020). This demographic shift, coupled with changes in retirement, mobility, and social networks that often accompany later life, places the maintenance of psychological well-being among older adults at the center of gerontological research (Kitsaki et al., 2024). Loneliness is one important manifestation of poor well-being. Recent evidence indicates that approximately 30% of older adults in North America experience significant loneliness (Salari et al., 2025), which is associated with an estimated 14% increase in all-cause mortality risk (Nakou et al., 2025).

Although loneliness represents one related but distinct dimension of psychological well-being (Gutierrez et al., 2025), the broader behavioral processes through which psychological well-being is maintained or undermined in later life remain incompletely understood. Everyday behavioral activity may be particularly relevant because it reflects how older adults engage with their physical and social environments across daily life.

Theoretical perspectives on behavioral engagement provide a basis for understanding how activity may be associated with psychological well-being in later life. Behavioral activation (BA) theory proposes that engagement in goal-directed and value-based activities sustains positive reinforcement, disrupts patterns of ruminative avoidance, and supports emotional well-being (Martell et al., 2001; Dimidjian et al., 2011). Although evidence supports the utility of BA for depression in older adults, its implications for psychological well-being among non-clinical, normatively aging populations have received comparatively little attention (Scott et al., 2022).

Activity theory similarly proposes that continued engagement in social and leisure activities is associated with well-being in later life (Lemon et al., 1972; Adams et al., 2011). These perspectives suggest that behavioral activity may be relevant to well-being not only through how much activity individuals engage in overall but also through how activity is distributed across the day. Regular and sustained engagement may provide more consistent opportunities for behavioral reinforcement and environmental engagement, whereas fragmented or highly irregular activity may reflect disruptions in daily routines. Thus, both the volume and temporal organization of everyday activity may be relevant to psychological well-being.

Several gaps in the literature have constrained the understanding of these relationships.

First, much of the existing literature has relied on self-reported measures of physical activity, which are subject to recall difficulties, social desirability bias, and disagreement with objectively measured activity (VandeBunte et al., 2022). Accelerometer-based measurement can provide a more direct assessment of everyday behavioral activity while also allowing examination of its temporal organization, including the extent to which activity is fragmented and the relative contrast between more and less active periods of the day. Second, the predominant use of cross-sectional designs limits the ability to distinguish between-person differences from within-person variation (Curran & Bauer, 2011). Older adults who are generally more active may differ in psychological well-being from those who are generally less active, while an individual may experience differences in well-being during periods when their activity is comparatively higher or lower than their typical level. These two sources of variation have rarely been examined simultaneously in studies of objectively measured activity and psychological well-being among older adults (Maher & Conroy, 2017; Leger et al., 2023). Because a person's habitual activity level reflects relatively stable patterns of health, mobility, and engagement accumulated over time, between-person differences in activity may show broader and more consistent associations with well-being than short-term within-person fluctuations (Curran & Bauer, 2011). Third, prior research has often emphasized negative psychological outcomes, particularly depressive symptoms, rather than examining multiple dimensions of well-being (Park et al., 2024). Behavioral activity may be relevant to both hedonic and eudaimonic aspects of psychological functioning, including positive affect, loneliness, meaning and purpose, and self-determination (Schilling et al., 2022).

Behavioral activity also occurs within social contexts, suggesting that social participation may represent one pathway through which activity is associated with psychological well-being (Lee et al., 2026). Many activities that constitute daily behavioral engagement among communitydwelling older adults, including visiting friends and family, volunteering, attending religious services, and participating in community organizations, involve social interaction (Townsend et al., 2021). Social participation is itself associated with health and well-being in later life (Adams et al., 2011; Townsend et al., 2021). Thus, older adults who are generally more behaviorally active may also participate more frequently in social activities, which may in turn be associated with better psychological well-being. However, the extent to which social participation statistically accounts for associations between objectively measured behavioral activity and multiple dimensions of psychological well-being has not been examined in a longitudinal multilevel framework, as prior work has relied on self-reported activity measures and cross-sectional designs (Ho et al., 2018). Examining this relationship at the between-person level may help clarify whether social participation represents one social context associated with differences in well-being between older adults who have different habitual levels of activity.

The social context of behavioral activity increasingly includes digital forms of connection (Kakulla, 2023). Over the past decade, the use of video calling, texting, email, and other digital communication technologies has expanded substantially among older adults, with smartphone ownership rising from 55% to 90% between 2016 and 2025, and adoption accelerated during the COVID-19 pandemic (Kakulla, 2023). These technologies provide additional opportunities for social contact that are less dependent on physical mobility and geographic proximity, potentially serving as a resource that compensates for functional or mobility limitations in old age (Schlomann et al., 2020). Digital connectivity may therefore be particularly relevant to day-to-day changes in the relationship between activity and well-being. For example, when an older adult is less active than usual on a given day, access to digital communication may provide an alternative opportunity for social contact and reduce the potential consequences of lower behavioral engagement. Conversely, digital connectivity may complement behavioral activity by strengthening the social and psychological benefits associated with being more active (Quinn, 2021). These possibilities suggest that habitual digital connectivity may condition the extent to which an individual's day-today deviations in activity from their own typical level are associated with psychological wellbeing. Despite the increasing relevance of digital communication to later-life social engagement, its potential role as a moderator of within-person associations between objectively measured activity and psychological well-being has not been examined. Although prior work has examined internet use alongside accelerometer-measured activity, that work has been limited to crosssectional analyses of direct associations rather than moderation (Chung et al., 2025).

The present study addresses these gaps using three waves of longitudinal data from the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), a nationally representative cohort of U.S. adults aged 65 and older (Freedman & Kasper, 2019). The study aim was to assess whether objectively measured behavioral activity is associated with multiple dimensions of psychological well-being and distinguish between differences in activity between individuals and fluctuations within individuals over time. Specifically, three hypotheses were tested: (1) greater accelerometermeasured mean activity and lower behavioral fragmentation would be associated with better psychological well-being across hedonic and eudaimonic dimensions; (2) between-person differences in activity would show broader and more consistent associations with psychological well-being than within-person fluctuations in activity; and (3) social participation would statistically accounts for part of the between-person associations between activity and psychological well-being, whereas digital connectivity moderates the within-person associations between activity and psychological well-being. Because prior work has linked relative amplitude to cognitive and functional outcomes, whereas its relationship with affective and eudaimonic dimensions of psychological well-being remains less clear, relative amplitude was examined in an exploratory capacity without a specific directional prediction (Rabinowitz et al., 2022).

Methods

Data Source and Study Design

Data were drawn from NHATS, a nationally representative longitudinal cohort of U.S. adults aged 65 years and older, initiated in 2011 with annual data collection and ongoing panel follow-up (Freedman & Kasper, 2019). NHATS employs a complex, stratified probability sampling design anchored to the Medicare enrollment file, which covers approximately 96% of U.S. adults aged 65 and older, to enable population-level inference for the U.S. older adult population (Freedman & Kasper, 2019). This population-representative sampling design applies to the parent NHATS cohort; as described below, the present analytic sample is a non-random subset defined by accelerometer participation and data completeness, and should not itself be assumed to be nationally representative. The study protocol was approved by the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 00002083; NHATS, n.d.), and all participants provided informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2013). This analysis linked data from Rounds 12 (2022), 13 (2023), and 14 (2024) at the participant level using unique NHATS identifiers, yielding a repeated-measures dataset spanning three waves and structured in long format, with wave-level observations at Level 1 nested within individuals at Level 2.

Participants and Inclusion Criteria

Of the 629 participants with valid Round 12 accelerometer data, 539 were included in the final analytic sample and contributed Round 12 data; 90 were excluded. Additional participants entered the accelerometer sub-study in Round 13 or 14 and did not have Round 12 accelerometer data, yielding a final analytic sample of 543 unique participants. Comparisons between the Round 12 accelerometer completers and the 90 participants excluded from the final sample showed that completers had higher mean activity, higher relative amplitude, lower behavioral fragmentation, greater positive affect, higher self-determination, higher social participation, lower PHQ-2 scores, and were in younger age categories (all ps < .05; Table 1); groups did not differ in loneliness, meaning and purpose, sex, or number of chronic conditions.

Participants were included in the analytic sample if they had valid accelerometer data at two or more waves, met the minimum accelerometer wear criterion of four valid wear days per deployment, and had complete information on the covariates included in the primary analyses. The 543 participants contributed 1,547 wave-level observations, with 466 contributing observations at all three waves. Given that the analytic sample was younger and more behaviorally and socially engaged than excluded participants, estimates should be interpreted as applying to this selected subsample rather than to the U.S. older adult population as a whole.

Wearable Accelerometer Data Acquisition and Processing

Accelerometer-derived behavioral activity was assessed using wrist-worn triaxial accelerometers (ActiGraph CentrePoint Insight Watch) deployed as part of the NHATS wearable sub-study, worn continuously for 24 hours per day across a 7-day monitoring period. Raw signals were recorded at 64 Hz and processed into minute-level activity counts using NHATS standardized processing procedures implemented in the R arctools package (Karas et al., 2021). Non-wear periods were identified as 90 or more consecutive minutes of zero activity counts, and a valid wear day required at least 90% wear time (≥1,296 minutes), consistent with NHATS accelerometry processing guidelines (Schrack et al., 2022 (Schrack et al., , 2025)). Analyses used the revised accelerometry files released in August 2026, which corrected time-zone misclassification affecting a subset of participants (Etzkorn et al., 2026). Only participants with four or more valid wear days per deployment were retained.

Measures

Accelerometer-Derived Behavioral Predictors. Three accelerometer-derived indices were calculated: overall activity volume, temporal continuity, and the robustness of day-night organization. Mean activity was calculated as the mean per-minute activity count across valid wear minutes, averaged across valid wear days. Relative amplitude (RA) was calculated as (M10 -L5)/(M10 + L5) following standard nonparametric circadian rhythm analysis (Van Someren et al., 1999), where M10 and L5 denote mean activity during the most active 10-hour and least active 5hour periods, respectively, averaged across valid days, with higher values indicating a more strongly consolidated day-active pattern. Relative amplitude was examined in an exploratory capacity, without a prespecified directional hypothesis. Behavioral fragmentation was operationalized as the active-to-sedentary transition probability (ASTP): the number of transitions from an active minute (≥1,853 counts/minute; Koster et al., 2016) to a sedentary minute, divided by the total number of active minutes in the day, following the calculation approach used in prior NHATS accelerometry research (Schrack et al., 2019). This computed value was similar to the NHATS-provided summary variable for the same construct.

For each predictor, between-person and within-person components were constructed following the within-between random-effects specification (Bell et al., 2019; Curran & Bauer, 2011): the between-person component represented each participant's mean across all available waves; the within-person component represented the deviation of each wave-specific value from the participant's own mean. Both components were standardized prior to analysis.

Psychological Well-Being Outcomes.

Four outcomes spanning hedonic and eudaimonic dimensions of psychological well-being were examined (Schilling et al., 2022), all drawn from the NHATS psychological well-being module. Loneliness was assessed using a single item measuring how often participants felt lonely during the past week on a 5-point frequency scale (1 = every day,5 = never), reverse-scored, so that higher values indicated greater loneliness (valid n = 540).

Positive affect was assessed using two items measuring how often participants felt cheerful and full of life, respectively, on the same 5-point frequency scale, reverse-scored, and averaged into a composite (Pearson r = .546). Meaning and purpose was assessed using a single item ("My life has meaning and purpose") rated on a 3-point agreement scale, scored so that higher values indicated greater endorsement. Self-determination was assessed using three items measuring perceived autonomy over daily decisions; items were scored according to their original NHATS response direction, with one item retained in its original direction while the remaining two were reversescored, so that higher composite values consistently indicated greater self-determination. Internal consistency for this composite was low (Cronbach's α = .331); accordingly, findings involving self-determination were interpreted cautiously. All four outcomes were standardized prior to analysis.

Social Participation.

Social participation was assessed using a six-item binary composite derived from NHATS items measuring visits with friends or family, attendance at religious services, participation in clubs or groups, going out for enjoyment, volunteer activities, and use of social networking sites (Townsend et al., 2021). The composite was calculated as the mean of nonmissing items, with participants retained if at least one item was non-missing; no minimum number of valid items was required. Internal consistency was modest (KR-20 = .504). Higher values indicated greater social participation. For the between-person structural equation models, social participation was represented by each participant's mean proportion score across available waves.

Digital Connectivity.

Digital connectivity was assessed using a single NHATS technology-use item measuring the frequency of email or text messaging, standardized and decomposed into between-person and within-person components using the same approach applied to the accelerometer-derived predictors. The between-person component was entered as a Level-2 moderator in the cross-level interaction models; the within-person component was included as a covariate.

Covariates. All models adjusted for age, sex, educational attainment, number of chronic conditions, pain, living arrangement, and depressive symptoms measured by the PHQ-2 (standardized), based on their established associations with both accelerometer-measured activity and psychological well-being in older adult populations (Prince et al., 2008; Schilling et al., 2022).

Age was measured using the NHATS interview-age category variable (5-year bands from 65-69 through 80-84, with an open-ended 85+ category) and was included as a categorical covariate (four indicator variables contrasting the 70-74, 75-79, 80-84, and 85+ groups against the 65-69 reference group). Sex was coded 1 for female, living arrangement was coded 1 for living alone, and pain was coded as a binary variable. Wave indicators for Rounds 13 and 14, with Round 12 as the reference, were included in the multilevel linear models; the between-person structural equation models included the same covariates but not the wave indicators, as all covariates were aggregated to participant-level means for these models, consistent with the behavioral predictors, social participation, and outcomes.

Sample Size Justification

The analytic sample size (N = 543) was determined by the availability of valid accelerometer data at two or more waves and complete covariate information rather than by a prospective power analysis; a prospective sample size calculation was therefore not applicable.

Unconditional models showed intraclass correlations ranging from .54 to .85 across study variables.

Statistical Analysis

Three analytic approaches corresponded to the three primary hypotheses. First, two-level linear multilevel models (LMMs) with random intercepts were estimated for each combination of behavioral predictor and psychological well-being outcome using the lme4 package in R (Bates et al., 2015), incorporating the between-person and within-person components of each predictor.

Eight primary models examined the two hypothesized behavioral predictors (mean activity and fragmentation) across four outcomes. For each predictor, the familywise significance level was controlled using a Bonferroni correction across the four outcomes (corrected α = .0125). Four additional exploratory models examined relative amplitude across the four outcomes; these models were treated as exploratory and were not subject to the same Bonferroni correction. Marginal and conditional R² values were calculated following Nakagawa and Schielzeth (2013).

Second, between-person structural equation models were estimated using lavaan in R (Rosseel, 2012) to examine whether social participation statistically accounted for part of the between-person associations between behavioral activity and psychological well-being (Lee et al., 2026), estimating the association between each behavioral predictor and social participation (Path a), the association between social participation and each outcome (Path b), the direct association (c′), the indirect association (a × b), and the total association. This specification represents an aggregated between-person structural model using multi-wave person-means; indirect effects were therefore interpreted as between-person statistical associations rather than temporally ordered mediation. Covariates were modeled with fixed.x = FALSE to allow full-information maximum likelihood estimation with partially missing covariate data; a preliminary specification using the default fixed.x = TRUE option resulted in listwise deletion of 14 cases and was not retained. Indirect effects were evaluated using 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals based on 1,000 resamples.

Third, cross-level interaction models were estimated by adding an interaction between each Level-1 within-person behavioral predictor and the Level-2 between-person component of digital connectivity to the corresponding base LMM. Within-person activity slopes were modeled as fixed effects rather than random slopes. Improvement in model fit after adding each cross-level interaction was evaluated using likelihood-ratio chi-square difference tests comparing each interaction model against its corresponding base model. Two sensitivity analyses were conducted: one excluding PHQ-2 from covariate adjustment, and one restricting the sample to participants who contributed observations at all three waves.

Analysis 1: Within-Person and Between-Person Associations With Well-Being

Table 2 presents results from the within-between multilevel models. Mean activity and fragmentation showed distinct patterns of association across levels of analysis. At the betweenperson level, fragmentation was associated with three of the four well-being outcomes after Bonferroni correction (α = .0125): positive affect, meaning and purpose, and self-determination.

Each 1-SD increment in within-person mean activity corresponded to a 0.048-SD reduction in same-wave loneliness (B = -0.048, 95% CI, -0.081 to -0.015) and a 0.036-SD increase in positive affect (B = 0.036, 95% CI, 0.008 to 0.064); between-person mean activity was additionally associated with positive affect (B = 0.108, 95% CI, 0.041 to 0.175). Between-person fragmentation showed the mirror-image pattern for positive affect (B = -0.116, 95% CI, -0.183 to -0.050), with an additional within-person association (B = -0.054, 95% CI, -0.082 to -0.026), and was further associated with meaning and purpose (B = -0.112, 95% CI, -0.182 to -0.042) and selfdetermination (B = -0.104, 95% CI, -0.173 to -0.036) at the between-person level only. Figure 1 illustrates this pattern, showing that fragmentation's associations were concentrated at the between-person level across three outcomes, whereas mean activity's associations were more evenly distributed across within-and between-person levels but showed significant associations with fewer outcomes after Bonferroni correction.

Analysis 2: Indirect Associations Through Social Participation

Table 3 presents results from all 12 predictor-outcome combinations from the betweenperson structural equation models. All three behavioral predictors were associated with social participation (Path a, all ps < .01), and social participation was independently associated with positive affect, meaning and purpose, and self-determination (Path b, all ps ≤ .002) but not loneliness. Indirect associations through social participation were significant for 9 of the 12 combinations: mean activity, RA, and fragmentation each showed a significant indirect association with positive affect, meaning and purpose, and self-determination; none showed a significant indirect association with loneliness. The fragmentation-self-determination combination was the only one in which both the direct (c′ = -0.081, 95% CI, -0.157 to -0.010) and indirect pathways were independently significant, yielding the largest absolute total association observed among the 12 combinations (B = -0.095, 95% CI, -0.167 to -0.025).

Sensitivity Analyses

Two sensitivity analyses were largely consistent with the primary findings. Excluding PHQ-2 from covariate adjustment produced larger-magnitude coefficients for mean activity and revealed nominally significant between-person associations of RA with loneliness and positive affect; because these RA associations emerged only in this exploratory check, they should not be interpreted as robust confirmatory findings. Restricting the analytic sample to the 466 participants with observations at all three waves yielded between-person mean activity coefficients that remained significant for loneliness (B = -0.095, p = .007), positive affect (B = 0.108, p = .004), and meaning and purpose (B = 0.110, p = .005); the between-person association with selfdetermination was attenuated to non-significance in this smaller subsample (B = 0.069, p = .067).

Discussion

This study examined how accelerometer-measured activity, encompassing both its overall volume and its temporal organization, was associated with psychological well-being in a subsample of older adults drawn from a nationally representative NHATS cohort. Three findings are highlighted. First, behavioral fragmentation showed broader between-person associations with well-being than mean activity: fragmentation was associated with three of the four outcomes (positive affect, meaning and purpose, self-determination) after Bonferroni correction, whereas mean activity survived correction for only one outcome at the between-person level (positive affect), with its corrected associations at the within-person level involving loneliness and positive affect. Second, within-person fluctuations in activity were associated with same-wave well-being primarily for the two affective outcomes, loneliness and positive affect. Third, social participation statistically accounted for part of the between-person activity-well-being associations for three of the four outcomes, whereas digital connectivity did not moderate any within-person association.

As an observational study, these findings describe associations rather than causal effects; reversedirection and bidirectional processes remain plausible throughout.

Distinct Associations of Activity Volume and Temporal Organization With Well-Being

A central finding of this study is the divergent pattern shown by mean activity and behavioral fragmentation after accounting for chronic conditions. Fragmentation, which indexes the continuity and regularity with which activity is organized across the day, showed broader associations with well-being (three of four outcomes), and, in the structural equation models, was the only predictor-outcome combination with a significant direct association after accounting for social participation. Mean activity, by contrast, showed a between-person association with only one outcome (positive affect) after correction, with its more consistent corrected associations emerging instead at the within-person level, where occasions of higher-than-usual activity were modestly associated with lower loneliness and greater positive affect in the same wave. This pattern parallels findings from frailty research using the same NHATS accelerometer infrastructure. Namely, when multiple activity metrics are entered simultaneously, fragmentation has been shown to remain associated with frailty even after adjusting for activity volume and intensity, whereas volume-based metrics alone have not consistently retained independent associations (Wanigatunga et al., 2022; Wingood et al., 2025). Together with the present findings, this suggests that how activity is organized across the day may capture aspects of well-being that are not fully reflected by activity volume alone.

Mean activity and fragmentation were themselves very strongly correlated at the betweenperson level (r = -.802; Supplementary Table S1), indicating that they capture closely related dimensions of daily behavioral engagement. Even so, fragmentation retained the more consistent between-person pattern after adjustment for chronic conditions, including a significant direct association with self-determination after accounting for social participation.

Within-person fluctuations were selectively associated with affective outcomes (loneliness, positive affect) rather than eudaimonic outcomes (meaning and purpose, selfdetermination). This selectivity is consistent with evidence that older adults' daily affective experiences are comparatively responsive to same-day contextual variation (Riediger & Rauers, 2024), whereas eudaimonic constructs such as purpose and autonomy may be relatively more stable appraisals over shorter observation windows (Wettstein et al., 2015). Relative amplitude showed no association with any outcome after Bonferroni correction. Prior work has linked circadian rest-activity rhythm strength primarily to cognitive outcomes (Rabinowitz et al., 2022); its null association with well-being in the present sample may indicate that RA captures behavioral information less relevant to affective or eudaimonic functioning specifically.

Social Participation as a Statistical Correlate

Social participation statistically accounted for part of the between-person association between activity and well-being for positive affect, meaning and purpose, and self-determination, and did so consistently across all three behavioral predictors. This pattern is directionally consistent with convoy model and social integration perspectives, which propose that behavioral engagement is linked to well-being partly through the preservation of social roles, contacts, and norms of reciprocity (Antonucci et al., 2014; Townsend et al., 2021). Because all structural paths were specified at the between-person level using cross-sectional indicators, these results represent a between-person indirect statistical association rather than a temporally ordered mediating process; a fully longitudinal test of mediation would require modeling within-person change across more waves than were available here.

For fragmentation and self-determination specifically, both the direct and indirect pathways were independently significant, yielding the largest absolute total association observed among the 12 combinations. This finding suggests that the between-person association between fragmentation and self-determination was only partly statistically accounted for by social participation, leaving room for other behavioral, psychological, or contextual pathways that were not directly measured here. The non-significant indirect association with loneliness across all three predictors, despite a significant between-person association between mean activity and loneliness in the three-wave linear multilevel model (LMM), may reflect differences between the two models: the LMM leverages within-person information across three waves, whereas the between-person structural equation model (SEM) relies solely on cross-sectional, person-mean indicators.

Accordingly, the association with loneliness should be interpreted more cautiously than the associations with positive affect, meaning and purpose, and self-determination.

No Evidence of Moderation by Digital Connectivity

None of the twelve cross-level interaction tests reached statistical significance (all ps ≥ .069), indicating that between-person differences in digital connectivity did not moderate withinperson associations between behavioral activity and well-being. This null pattern contrasts with survey-based evidence that digital communication can serve as a compensatory resource when physical mobility is constrained (Schlomann et al., 2020) and as a complement to in-person social engagement (Quinn, 2021). One possible explanation is that a single item measuring the frequency of email or text messaging does not capture the quality or reciprocity of digital contact that these compensatory and complementary mechanisms may depend on (Han, 2026).

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include objective accelerometer measurement, a three-wave longitudinal design drawn from a nationally representative parent cohort, explicit within-between decomposition, a temporal-organization index operationalized using the ASTP metric (Schrack et al., 2019), and an integrated framework combining indirect-association and moderation analyses.

This study has several limitations. First, the analytic sample is a selected, younger and more engaged subset of the parent NHATS cohort; findings should not be generalized to frailer or less active subgroups. Second, the observational design cannot establish causal direction; reverse causation and unmeasured confounding remain plausible. Third, the three-wave, two-year observation window limits examination of longer-term trajectories. Fourth, the between-person SEM and cross-level moderation analyses involved multiple uncorrected tests; therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and warrant confirmation in future studies. Fifth, the low internal consistency of the self-determination composite (α = .331) and moderate reliability of the social participation composite (KR-20 = .504) limit measurement precision. Sixth, analyses did not apply NHATS survey weights, so findings reflect associations within the analytic sample rather than population-representative estimates. Seventh, race/ethnicity and marital status were not included as covariates in the statistical models; future studies should consider these characteristics when examining heterogeneity in activity-well-being associations. Eighth, each well-being outcome was assessed with a brief measure of one to three items, which may limit measurement precision. Future studies could use more comprehensive instruments to assess these dimensions of psychological well-being.

Future research should employ ecological momentary assessment to examine within-day dynamics of activity, social contact, and affect; distinguish the quality from the frequency of digital communication; and prioritize more reliable measures of self-determination. Designs better suited to causal inference, such as natural experiments or randomized behavioral interventions, would be needed to test whether the associations observed here, particularly the more consistent betweenperson associations observed for activity fragmentation relative to activity volume, reflect a causal effect of activity organization on well-being.
