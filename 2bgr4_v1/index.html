Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Abstract

Purpose. Postpartum depression (PPD) disproportionately affects women experiencing socioeconomic hardship. We examined the association between exposure to Rx Kids, the nation's first universal and unconditional perinatal cash transfer program, and PPD risk among mothers of one-month-old infants.

Methods. This study used Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) data collected from electronic health records of 815 one-month well-child visits between 2022 and 2025, spanning two years before and after Rx Kids implementation. Difference-in-differences analyses compared changes in depression screening outcomes among mothers residing in Flint, Michigan, where Rx Kids was implemented, with changes among mothers residing outside Flint.

In the two years before Rx Kids, the prevalence of high risk PPD scores > 13 was 8.7% in Flint and 6.2% outside Flint; two years after, the prevalence was 3.3% in Flint and 10.4% outside Flint. Rx Kids exposure was associated with a 9.8-percentage-point reduction in high risk PPD scores (95% CI, -16.8 to -2.8, p = 0.006), reversing the preexisting geographic disparity. Reductions were also observed in moderate PPD risk and mean EPDS scores.

Findings suggest that universal and unconditional perinatal cash support may be an effective upstream strategy for preventing PPD, improving maternal mental health, and reducing place-based disparities.

Postpartum Depression Risk Following Exposure to Universal and Unconditional Perinatal

Cash Transfers

Postpartum depression (PPD) is a common and increasingly prevalent maternal mental health condition with significant consequences for mothers, infants, and families. [1] [2] [3] [4] PPD is associated with adverse maternal health outcomes, impaired mother-infant bonding, and poorer child developmental outcomes. [5] [6] [7] Its burden is not equally distributed, with disparities associated with race and ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and preexisting health conditions. 3, 4 Efforts to address PPD have largely focused on detection and treatment. Professional organizations, including the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists 8 and American Academy of Pediatrics, 2 recommend routine PPD screening during maternal and infant healthcare visits using a validated instrument such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends first screening for maternal PPD at the one-month well-child visit. 2 While clinical screening and treatment are essential, they do not address the socioeconomic drivers of health that can contribute to PPD burden.

Economic hardship is an important contributor to maternal depressive symptoms, in part through the chronic stress and uncertainty that come with financial insecurity. 9 Pregnancy and infancy are particularly vulnerable times for families, with income dropping sharply before childbirth and remaining suppressed during the postpartum year. 10 Financial hardship during this critical period may compound the physical, emotional, and caregiving demands associated with welcoming a new baby. Beyond its consequences for maternal and infant health, 5 PPD also imposes substantial economic costs through increased healthcare expenditures, lost productivity, and adverse developmental outcomes for children, among other costs. 11 Policies that strengthen family economic security during the perinatal period may therefore improve maternal mental health while reducing downstream health and economic costs.

Launched in January 2024 in Flint, Michigan, Rx Kids is the nation's first universal, place-based, unconditional cash prescription program that begins prenatally and extends through infancy. 12 Designed to improve population-level maternal and infant health by reducing the harmful effects of poverty during a critical developmental period, Rx Kids prescribes a one-time $1,500 payment mid-pregnancy, then $500 monthly during infancy. Rx Kids has been associated with measurable improvements in family financial stability, maternal and infant health, and family well-being. [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] Previous survey-based Rx Kids research found a 14.0-percentage-point reduction in positive depression screens using the CES-D-10 among postpartum mothers. 13 This study builds on earlier findings by examining whether exposure to Rx Kids was associated with reduced PPD risk using data from electronic health records, as measured by the EPDS during one-month well-child pediatric visits. Using a quasi-experimental difference-indifferences study design, we evaluated changes in PPD risk before and after Rx Kids implementation within and outside Flint, Michigan. By examining the effects of population-wide unconditional perinatal cash support on PPD risk, this study allows us to examine whether upstream economic policies may be an important tool for improving maternal mental health, complementing clinical care strategies, and advancing maternal and infant health equity.

Methods

Setting

Flint, Michigan, is a majority-minority city and represents a high-risk, structurally disadvantaged setting where poverty, housing instability, and economic stress are strongly linked to adverse maternal and infant health outcomes, with clear geographic disparities in these outcomes when compared with nearby communities and statewide metrics. [20] [21] [22] Hurley Children's Clinic in Flint is a high-volume pediatric primary care clinic and the medical home to 1,226 infants from 2022-2025, 87.4% of whom were born at the affiliated hospital, Hurley Medical Center, and approximately 88% of whom were insured by Medicaid.

Data and Study Population

For this quasi-experimental difference-in-differences study, data were abstracted from electronic health records. The study included 815 mothers who gave birth at Hurley Medical Center between 01/01/2022 and 12/31/2025 and who completed the EPDS at their infant's onemonth well-child visit (i.e., occurring between 3 weeks and 2 months after their infant's birth) at Hurley Children's Center. This study was approved by Hurley Medical Center's Institutional Review Board (IRB #2170872).

Variables

Infant information obtained included sex assigned at birth and age, in days, at the onemonth well-child visit. Maternal information obtained at the time of delivery included race or ethnicity, marital status, age, type of medical insurance, and residential address. Maternal residential address was then geocoded to determine whether the mother lived within the city of Flint (i.e., Rx Kids eligible) or outside the city of Flint (i.e., Rx Kids ineligible). Based on geocoded address (i.e., Flint or non-Flint) and timing of birth (i.e., before or after Rx Kids implementation in January 2024), mothers were classified into four groups for the difference-indifferences analysis: Pre-Rx Kids Flint, Pre-Rx Kids Non-Flint, Post-Rx Kids Flint, and Post-Rx Kids Non-Flint.

The EPDS assesses common depressive-and anxiety-related symptoms and difficulties during the past seven days. 23 Individual items are scored from 0 to 3, and responses are summed for a total score of 0-30, with higher scores reflecting greater symptom severity and PPD risk.

With strong psychometric properties, a cut-off score ≥ 13 has strong specificity (0.95) and adequate sensitivity (0.66) for identifying women who may meet criteria for postpartum depression. 24 A score ≥ 10 has lower specificity (0.84) but greater sensitivity (0.85).

For descriptive analyses, participants were categorized as follows: • Low risk for PPD: EPDS score ≤ 9

Statistical Analysis

To assess the association between Rx Kids implementation and PPD risk, we used a difference-in-differences strategy to estimate the adjusted change in the probability of a clinically significant EPDS score among mothers residing in Flint after Rx Kids implementation relative to the change observed in the temporal and geographic comparison groups. Linear probability models were fitted with a characteristic difference-in-differences interaction term between binary variables for maternal place of residence at the time of delivery (i.e., Flint versus non-Flint) and timing relative to program implementation (i.e., pre-Rx Kids versus post-Rx Kids). In addition to the interaction term, models were adjusted for maternal age, marital status, race, Medicaid insured status, Flint residency status, visit month indicators to account for seasonality, and birthyear fixed effects to account for temporal trends. This analysis was repeated with each of the other measures of PPD (i.e., the binary variable using EPDS scores below or > 10 and the EPDS total score).

The key assumption of the difference-in-differences specification was that outcomes would have evolved similarly for mothers within and just outside of Flint between 2022 and 2025 had it not been for the implementation of Rx Kids (the "parallel trends" assumption). This assumption required that no other interventions occurred contemporaneously with Rx Kids in Flint; there were no other known population-level perinatal interventions targeting only mothers of infants born in the city of Flint between 2022 and 2025. Additionally, our specification required no anticipatory effects and no endogenous selection effects. To the extent that these assumptions hold, our results can be interpreted as causal impacts of Rx Kids. To validate some of our assumptions, we also fit an event study specification for our primary outcome, which allowed us to test our parallel trends assumption in the pre-period and allowed for different treatment effects to be estimated for 2024 and 2025.

Additionally, we defined program exposure based on residency, not enrollment. As such, we took an intent-to-treat approach in our analysis to capture the population-level effect of the program. Over time, Rx Kids has shown high aggregate uptake rates in Flint, near 100% with greater than 90% of participants enrolling prenatally. 25 To preserve sample size, we included all observations after program launch (January 1, 2024), rather than applying weights or excluding the ramp-up period. Due to high take-up rates, it is likely that our intent-to-treat estimates would be very close to estimates of overall treatment effects of the program itself.

All statistical tests were two-sided with a level of significance set at p < 0.05. Standard errors for all analyses were robust to heteroskedasticity. We reported 95% CIs and standard pvalues for all outcomes. All analyses were performed using Stata, version 19.0.

Results

From January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2025, 815 infants born at Hurley Medical Center had a one-month well-child visit at the Hurley Children's Clinic. Of these, 393 were born before January 1, 2024 (184 Flint, 209 non-Flint), and 422 were born on or after January 1, 2024 (211 Flint, 211 non-Flint), based on geocoded residential addresses. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of infants born in Flint and non-Flint, with notable differences in race, maternal marital status, and being Medicaid insured between the two geographies.

High Postpartum Depression Risk

In the two years before Rx Kids implementation, the prevalence of high PPD risk was 8.7% among mothers residing in Flint and 6.2% among mothers residing outside Flint. In the two years after Rx Kids implementation, the prevalence of high PPD risk was 3.3% among mothers residing in Flint and 10.4% among mothers residing outside Flint (Table 2). In the primary difference-in-differences analysis shown in Table 3 and illustrated in Figure 1, Rx Kids was associated with a statistically significant 9.8-percentage-point reduction in the likelihood of screening at high risk for PPD on the EPDS (95% CI: -16.8 to -2.8, p = 0.006), representing a decline of 74.8% relative to the counterfactual scenario in which Rx Kids had not been implemented. Results were robust to alternative model specifications; a logistic regression model yielded estimates that were similar in both direction and magnitude (Supplemental Table 1).

In the event study specification with 2023 set as the reference period (i.e., the year immediately preceding Rx Kids implementation), the pre-intervention estimate was small and not statistically significant (0.4 percentage points; CI: -10.1 to 10.9, p = 0.943), providing support for the parallel trends assumption (Table 3). Following implementation, we observed a marginally significant reduction in high PPD risk in 2024 (-7.5 percentage points; 95% CI: -16.3 to 1.4, p = 0.098) followed by a larger and statistically significant reduction in high PPD risk in 2025 (-12.1 percentage points; 95% CI: -22.3 to -1.9; p = 0.020; Table 3; Supplemental Figure 1).

Moderate Postpartum Depression Risk and Mean EPDS Scores

Repeating the analysis using a binary indicator of moderate or greater PPD risk (EPDS ≥ 10), Rx Kids was associated with a statistically significant 14.2-percentage-point reduction in the probability of screening at moderate or greater PPD risk (CI: -25.0 to -3.4, p = 0.010) (Table 3).

The larger effect observed at the lower threshold suggests Rx Kids may have shifted the overall distribution of PPD symptoms amongst mothers, reducing not only the prevalence of clinically significant symptoms but also the prevalence of more moderate symptom levels. Descriptively, changes in mean EPDS scores generally mirrored changes observed for the binary outcomes (Supplemental Figure 2). Rx Kids was associated with a statistically significant 1.699-point reduction in mean EPDS scores (95% CI: -2.984 to -0.414, p = 0.010) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this quasi-experimental, difference-in-differences study, exposure to Rx Kids was associated with a 9.8-percentage-point reduction in the likelihood of screening at high risk for PPD on the EPDS among mothers of one-month-old infants. Findings were consistent across multiple analyses, with Rx Kids-exposed mothers demonstrating lower rates of both high (EPDS ≥13) and moderate (EPDS ≥10) PPD risk, as well as lower mean EPDS scores, relative to comparison groups. These results suggest that Rx Kids may improve maternal mental health across the distribution of depressive symptoms, rather than solely reducing the proportion of mothers exceeding clinically relevant screening thresholds.

Importantly, these findings also suggest a potential reduction in place-based maternal mental health disparities. Before Rx Kids implementation, mothers in Flint, a community facing substantial socioeconomic challenges, had higher rates of elevated PPD risk than mothers in our comparison communities. Following implementation, this pattern reversed, with Flint mothers demonstrating lower PPD risk than non-Flint mothers (Figure 1). Together, these findings suggest that place-based economic interventions may offer a promising strategy for improving maternal mental health and reducing disparities at the population level.

Interpretation of Findings

One plausible explanation for our findings is that Rx Kids reduced financial stress during a period of heightened economic vulnerability. Pregnancy and infancy are accompanied by increased household expenses, disruptions to employment and income, and growing caregiving demands. 10, 26 By providing unconditional cash support, Rx Kids may reduce the stress associated with meeting basic needs, paying for household expenses, and purchasing infant supplies. In support of this explanation, Rx Kids participant surveys (n=2,985) found that 85% of mothers said the program helped them become more financially secure and 88% of mothers reported reduced stress. 27 Additionally, qualitative studies have documented the close relationship between financial insecurity and maternal mental health along with a sense of relief and mental ease that comes with receiving cash support. 28, 29 The relationship between financial hardship and maternal mental health may also involve biological pathways. Previous research has linked financial stress during the perinatal period to elevated depressive symptoms and dysregulation of physiological stress-response systems. [30] [31] [32] Thus, by reducing financial insecurity and associated stress, cash transfers may influence maternal mental health through both psychological and physiological mechanisms. Preliminary research involving Rx Kids mothers suggests that greater maternal well-being may be associated with healthier postpartum cortisol regulation, a marker of physiological stress response Improved access to prenatal care may represent another pathway. Research has linked inadequate prenatal care with increased risk of postpartum depressive symptoms. 33, 34 Prenatal care provides opportunities to promote healthy behaviors, connect mothers with supportive services, and offer social support during the critical perinatal period. Previous Rx Kids research found that program exposure was associated with increased prenatal care utilization. 15 Finally, Rx Kids may improve maternal mental health by increasing maternal time affluence, or the ability to spend more time on meaningful activities and relationships. 28 In qualitative interviews, Rx Kids mothers described how financial support reduced pressure to return to work and allowed them to recover and focus on their infants. As one mother explained, "I was given that time to bond more... And my focus is strictly on them." Another mother explicitly connected financial security and time at home with not experiencing postpartum depression: "I really didn't go through postpartum depression, because I wasn't worried about having no money... I was just excited to stay home with my baby." 28 Together, these pathways suggest that unconditional perinatal cash support may influence maternal mental health through multiple, potentially reinforcing mechanisms. Although the present study did not directly evaluate these mechanisms, the findings highlight the potential for perinatal economic interventions to address several underlying contributors to postpartum depression simultaneously.

Comparison With Prior Literature

Although numerous studies have examined the effects of cash transfers on mental health, far fewer have focused specifically on parental depression during the perinatal period. To our knowledge, no prior study has evaluated a universal, unconditional perinatal cash transfer program in the United States in relation to postpartum depression (PPD) risk, and we are unaware of any published studies examining the impact of cash transfers on PPD risk using the EPDS. Existing evidence has largely focused on other economic support policies, including expansions of the Child Tax Credit, unconditional cash transfers to mothers with low-income, and paid maternity leave.

The temporary expansion of the federal Child Tax Credit in 2021 provided near-universal eligibility and monthly postnatal cash payments to families with children. Published studies examining its effects on parental depression have produced mixed findings. Some found no significant effects, [35] [36] [37] while others reported reductions in depressive symptoms, particularly among low-income families and racial and ethnic minority populations. [38] [39] [40] [41] [42] Similarly, the Baby's First Years study provided postnatal child cash transfers to 400 mothers with low incomes who were randomized to receive $333 monthly; compared with mothers randomized to receive $20 monthly, those receiving $333 did not experience significant improvements in maternal depression. 43, 44 In contrast, our findings suggest that policies that reduce financial strain during early parenthood may be associated with improved maternal mental health outcomes.

Finally, our findings affirm and build on previous Rx Kids research demonstrating reductions in maternal depressive symptoms using the CES-D-10. 13 The current study finds similar reductions in PPD risk using electronic health record-derived EPDS data, a validated instrument specifically designed to screen for PPD during routine healthcare visits. 2, 23 The consistency in findings across screening instruments and data collection settings strengthens evidence that Rx Kids may improve maternal mental health and suggests that these effects may be detectable not only in research surveys but also in routinely collected clinical data.

Implications

Efforts to address PPD have traditionally focused on detection and treatment within healthcare settings. While these approaches remain essential, our findings suggest that economic policies addressing perinatal financial insecurity may offer an important complementary strategy for improving maternal mental health. Rather than intervening only after depressive symptoms emerge, policies that reduce financial and material hardship during the perinatal period may help prevent or mitigate upstream risk factors for PPD. These findings support a broader approach to maternal mental health that extends beyond clinical care to address the socioeconomic conditions shaping maternal well-being.

The reversal of preexisting disparities in PPD risk between Flint and comparison communities suggests that place-based economic interventions may help reduce geographic and socioeconomic inequities in maternal mental health. By providing universal support within participating communities, Rx Kids offers a population-level approach that does not depend on individual income eligibility or access to mental health services. This model may be particularly relevant for communities experiencing concentrated poverty, where financial insecurity and barriers to healthcare often intersect.

Finally, our findings underscore the importance of recognizing maternal mental health as a key outcome of family economic policy. Incorporating maternal mental health outcomes into evaluations of economic policies could provide a more comprehensive understanding of their health and societal benefits. As policymakers consider strategies to improve maternal and infant health, universal economic support during pregnancy and infancy may warrant inclusion as part of a broader, prevention-oriented maternal health policy agenda.

Future research should examine whether these findings extend to other Rx Kids communities and identify the mechanisms through which cash support improves maternal mental health, including reduced financial stress, improved prenatal care, and increased maternal time affluence. Longer-term studies should assess sustained maternal and child health outcomes and quantify potential downstream healthcare cost savings and broader economic returns. Finally, evaluating the effects of program timing, payment amounts, duration, and universal eligibility could inform the design and expansion of perinatal economic support policies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although the difference-in-differences design, adjustment for relevant covariates, and use of multiple comparison groups strengthen causal inference, residual confounding and unmeasured differences between exposed and unexposed mothers remain possible. In addition, the validity of the difference-in-differences approach depends on the assumption that, in the absence of Rx Kids, PPD risk would have followed parallel trends in Flint and non-Flint. Although event-study analyses supported this assumption, it cannot be definitively established. Second, exposure to Rx Kids was inferred from maternal residence at birth and program timing rather than verified individual enrollment or receipt of payments. Although prior evaluations indicate near-universal program uptake among eligible families, 25 some exposure misclassification is possible. Third, the EPDS is a validated screening instrument that identifies elevated PPD risk but does not establish a clinical diagnosis. 23 Finally, analyses were limited to mothers who attended their infant's onemonth well-child visit and completed EPDS screening, potentially introducing selection bias and limiting generalizability to all postpartum mothers. Findings from Flint may also not fully generalize to other communities with different demographic, socioeconomic, healthcare, or policy characteristics.

Strengths

This study has several important strengths. First, the quasi-experimental difference-indifferences design, supported by event-study analyses and multiple comparison groups, provides a rigorous approach to evaluating the effects of Rx Kids under real-world conditions. Unlike interventions implemented within controlled research settings, Rx Kids is a universal, community-wide program, allowing us to rigorously examine the potential population-level effects of economic support during pregnancy and infancy.

Second, this study leveraged routinely collected clinical data from electronic health records rather than relying on incentivized surveys or self-reported program outcomes. PPD symptoms were assessed using the validated EPDS during routine one-month pediatric wellchild visits, consistent with American Academy of Pediatrics screening recommendations. 2 This approach strengthens the clinical and policy relevance of the findings and demonstrates the value of using existing healthcare data for evaluating the health effects of economic interventions.

Third, the consistency of findings across multiple EPDS outcomes, including moderate and high PPD risk and mean depressive symptom scores, as well as with earlier Rx Kids research using a different depression screening instrument, 13 strengthens confidence in the observed association between program exposure and improved maternal mental health.

Finally, although conducted in a single Midwestern city, the study included a racially and socioeconomically diverse population experiencing intersecting social and structural disadvantages. These characteristics make the findings particularly relevant to broader discussions of maternal health equity, the social and economic drivers of health, and the potential role of place-based economic policies in improving population health.

Conclusion

In this quasi-experimental, difference-in-differences study, exposure to Rx Kids was associated with a 9.8-percentage-point reduction in the likelihood of screening at high risk for PPD during routine one-month pediatric visits. These findings reinforce the idea that PPD is not only a clinical concern but also a population health outcome shaped by the economic and social conditions in which women become pregnant, give birth, and care for their infants. As the first evaluation of a universal, unconditional perinatal cash transfer program in the U.S. to examine PPD risk, this study suggests that economic support during the perinatal period may help improve maternal mental health and reduce place-based disparities.
