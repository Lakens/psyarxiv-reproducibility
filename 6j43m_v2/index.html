Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Prior research in empirical aesthetics has related beauty to visual features like curvature, color, or shape. Although these feature-based accounts have identified reliable visual correlates of beauty, the literature on feature preferences is highly fragmented, with individual features studied for individual stimulus domains. Here, we argue that making further progress hinges on complementing feature-based approaches with approaches aimed at distilling computational principles that underlie the perception of beauty. Recent advances in computational neuroscience can drive this new line of research by linking visual beauty to the coding of visual information in artificial neural networks and the biological brain. We highlight two examples of how this approach has identified overarching computational principles. First, metabolic processing costs, both in artificial and biological vision, are inversely related to perceived beauty, suggesting that beauty may reflect energy-efficient, fluent visual coding. Second, integrative processing in artificial and biological vision captures the efficient spatial representation of image elements across space and predicts beauty ratings. Synthesizing these findings, we derive a neuro-computational framework for assessing computational principles in the perception of beauty, which uses artificial neural networks for exploration at scale and then validates candidate principles using human behavior and brain data. We discuss candidate principles that could be explored using this approach, like spatiotemporal predictability, and delineate how the new insights could be reconciled with feature-based approaches. In conclusion, complementing feature-based studies with studies targeting computational principles promises a comprehensive and mechanistic understanding of why certain visual stimuli are perceived as beautiful, and others are not.

Introduction

In our everyday lives, visual beauty can be found all around us, in artworks and landscapes, and in objects and scenes. One can experience beauty unexpectedly while cycling to work or deliberately in a museum. In both cases, encountering beauty enriches our lives and brings us joy.

How can we explain visual beauty, and which factors determine a stimulus's aesthetic appeal? These questions have been asked since Fechner's first empirical investigations on image preferences in the nineteenth century (Fechner, 1876; Bader, 2018). We have come a long way from these classical studies. Based on decades of empirical research, we now have different theoretical models that characterize the biological basis of visual aesthetics (Leder, 2004; Palmer et al., 2013; Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2014; Redies, 2015). Although these models differ in proposed mechanisms and stress different cognitive processes, they all converge on the notion that sensory processing is the critical backbone: Models assume "perception" (Leder, 2004), "sensory-motor" (Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2014), or "sensory coding" (Redies, 2015) steps, where sensory features are the first stepping stone towards aesthetic appeal.

The importance of visual features in aesthetic appreciation is well established. Studies in empirical aesthetics have identified a range of low-and mid-level visual features associated with aesthetic appeal, including curvature (Corradi et al., 2019; Vartanian et al., 2024), color (Graham et al., 2016; Schloss & Palmer, 2011; Swartz et al., 2024), complexity (Van Geert & Wagemans, 2020), and symmetry (Rhodes et al., 1998; Huang et al., 2018), among others. Visual features can reliably explain domain-specific beauty (e.g., symmetric vs asymmetric faces) and unveil shared aesthetic preferences (e.g., preferences for curved rather than angular objects). More recently, studies have shown that combining multiple features into predictive models can yield powerful predictions of aesthetic judgements for static (Iigaya et al., 2021) and dynamic (Ekinci et al., 2026) stimuli. This shows that multiple visual features are reliably and systematically linked to perceived beauty.

Yet, even though we have learned a lot from these studies, answers to a more general question remain elusive: Why are these specific features preferred? For instance, why is a curved object preferred over a rectilinear one? Why is a symmetric face preferred over an asymmetric one? Though for each of these examples, individual explanations have been proposed (Rhodes, 2006; Bar & Neta, 2006), we argue that there may be overarching computational principles, which are fundamentally ingrained in human visual processing, and which may explain perceived beauty across individual features and across stimulus domains. In other words, they may ultimately at the same time explain why we prefer curved objects and symmetric faces.

In the following, we use computational principles to refer to neural coding motifs in sensory systems. Such neural coding motifs form specific and recurring patterns of neural information processing that characterize or constrain the processing of visual information in the brain. Critically, we ask whether and how we can identify computational principles that are indicative of perceived beauty -without necessarily being tied to individual features or stimulus domains.

A useful starting point for thinking about such computational principles is to ask why the visual system should represent the beauty of visual inputs in the first place. A tractable proposal is offered by processing fluency theories (Reber et al., 2004; Graf & Landwehr, 2015), which posit that beauty arises from enhanced processing efficiency in sensory systems. This idea offers concrete suggestions for candidate computational principles: Neural motifs that facilitate efficient coding in resource-limited sensory systems should be linked to perceived beauty. Computational principles consistent with such efficient coding may include neural motifs that reduce neural competition through integration (Röth et al., 2021), increase efficiency by sharpening representations (Pouget et al., 2000), integrate sensory inputs with predictions based on prior expectations (Rao & Ballard, 1999), or facilitate neural communication through oscillatory dynamics (Fries, 2015; Stecher et al., 2025). While these candidate principles are an arbitrary subset, they can all operate across multiple levels of the visual hierarchy and thus transcend individual feature or stimulus preferences.

As these examples also illustrate, there are numerous candidate principles that facilitate efficient representation in the visual brain, and there are potentially several different neural indicators for each of them. Establishing reliable links between a computational principle and perceived beauty therefore requires tedious, time-consuming, and expensive experimentation. As an alternative, however, modern artificial neural networks (ANNs) offer cheap and high-throughput in silico testbeds, where computational principles can be examined at scale and in turn linked to human beauty ratings (Figure 1A). Once a candidate principle has been identified, it can be computationally benchmarked in an ANN architecture using a set of suitable measures. Variations in these measures can then be linked to variations in beauty ratings across stimuli. After establishing links between a computational principle in an ANN and perceived beauty in human observers, this association can be biologically validated using targeted human neuroimaging. This validation is important, given that ANNs, although in many cases suitable for modelling human vision (Kietzmann et al., 2017; Cichy & Kaiser, 2019; Doerig et al., 2023), can show systematic divergences from human perception and neural processing (Xu & Vaziri-Pashkam, 2021; Bowers et al., 2022).

In the following, we show the feasibility of this framework by highlighting two recent studies that used ANNs, and specifically deep neural networks (DNNs), in conjunction with brain imaging data to identify, benchmark, and validate computational principles that underlie the perception of beauty. Specifically, Tang et al. (2025) investigated metabolic cost (i.e., the relative energy expenditure in artificial and biological vision associated with processing a stimulus), and Nara and Kaiser (2024) investigated integrative processing (i.e., the relative degree of spatial integration among image elements).

Metabolic cost

Inspired by fluency-based theories, Tang et al. (2025) hypothesized that sharper neural tuning and its associated reduction in energy expenditure are indicative of perceived beauty. Consequently, they proposed an inverse relationship between the metabolic cost of neural activation and aesthetic preferences for visual stimuli. Akin to the framework outlined in Fig. 1A, they first benchmarked the proposed association in a DNN model and then validated the association using biological brain data.

Computational benchmarking. For computational benchmarking, the authors used a VGG19 model pre-trained on object recognition (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014). The total number of active filters in the DNN was used as a measure for in silico metabolic cost. This measure was extracted for 4,916 natural images (from BOLD5000; Chang et al., 2019). Across images, metabolic cost in the DNN was negatively correlated with beauty ratings from an independent group of human observers (Fig. 1B), confirming the hypothesis that beauty is linked to the energy efficiency of stimulus processing. An examination of layer-wise metabolic cost across the DNN hierarchy revealed stronger predictions when moving from shallower to deeper layers, suggesting that metabolic costs associated with high-level feature coding are most indicative of visual beauty.

Biological validation.

To validate the inverse relationship between metabolic cost and beauty using biological brain data, the authors used fMRI data from four participants who viewed the same 4,916 images shown to the DNN (Chang et al., 2019). The sum of the beta values for each image and for each region in the brain served as a measure of biological metabolic cost. Confirming the computational benchmarking, beauty ratings across images were negatively correlated with metabolic costs in the scene-selective parahippocampal place area (PPA) and occipital place area (OPA), and the face-selective fusiform face area (FFA) (Fig. 1C). These findings suggest that lower energy expenditure in the human visual system, similarly to lower processing costs in visual DNNs, is indicative of greater perceived beauty. As in the DNN, this association was strongest for complex feature representations in category-selective regions of highlevel visual cortex.

To sum up, this study first hypothesized metabolic cost to be a candidate principle tied to visual beauty, then used DNN benchmarking as a testbed for this association and in turn validated the association using human neuroimaging data. The resulting association between metabolic cost and perceived beauty not only shows the feasibility of the proposed framework. It also highlights a computational principle (energy efficiency) that can operate across multiple stages of the visual hierarchy and thus promises exploratory power beyond individual features and stimulus domains. Further support for this principle comes from a related study that investigated the sparsity of DNN representations for faces and paintings (Dibot et al., 2023).

Integrative processing

Starting from fluency-based considerations, too, Nara and Kaiser (2024) investigated a complementary computational principle that increases coding efficiency: integrative processing. Inspired by human neuroimaging work (MacMains & Kastern, 2011; Kaiser et al. 2014; Kaiser & Peelen, 2018), they hypothesized that the efficient integration of image elements across space would reduce processing demands via a compression of information into holistic representational formats. The consequently lower processing demands should lend greater beauty to images that are more readily integrated across space. The authors thus hypothesized a positive correlation between the degree of spatial integration and perceived beauty across images. The hypothesized association was again benchmarked in a computational model and then validated using human brain data.

Computational benchmarking. For computational benchmarking, the authors used a VGG16 model pre-trained on scene recognition (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014; Zhou et al., 2017). The degree of spatial integration for each image was measured as the signinverted correlation of DNN activity patterns for the whole image and an average of the activity patterns for complementary image halves (generated from opposing checkerboard patterns overlaid on the image). Lower correlations were taken as indicative of more integration (i.e., the whole is differently represented from the halves), whereas higher correlations were indicative of less integration (i.e., the whole is similarly represented to the halves). Across images, the degree of integration in the DNN, and particularly in intermediate to late layers, was positively correlated with beauty ratings from an independent group of human observers (Fig. 1D), confirming the hypothesis that perceived beauty is linked to how efficiently the brain integrates information across whole images. This relationship remained robust across four experiments with different sets of natural images, from landscapes to varied real-world scenes.

Biological validation.

To validate the relationship between integrative processing and beauty using biological brain data, the authors conducted an fMRI experiment where observers passively viewed a subset of natural scene images and image halves. Akin to the DNN analysis, correlations of fMRI response patterns across the whole scene and the two complementary halves were used to quantify integrative processing, with lower whole-to-halves correlations indicating more integration and greater whole-tohalves correlations indicating less integration. Confirming the computational benchmarking, integrative processing was positively correlated with perceived beauty across the visual cortex, with a significant correlation obtained in scene-selective PPA (Fig. 1E). This suggests that the efficient spatial integration of scene features in highlevel visual cortex is linked to perceived beauty.

To sum up, this study applied the proposed research framework to distill integrative processing as a computational principle connected to the perception of beauty.

Integrative processing provides another great example of a computational principle that can operate across feature hierarchies and across stimulus domains. In fact, a more recent study has already shown that integrative processing is similarly, albeit somewhat more weakly, associated with preferences for visual artworks, too (Lin et al., 2026). A neuro-computational framework for identifying overarching principles

Both highlighted studies follow a very similar framework to test and validate candidate neuro-computational principles, combining modern artificial intelligence (AI) technologies for benchmarking and neuroscientific experiments for validation. Their approach exemplifies a new line of research with a hypothesis-driven neurocomputational framework (Fig. 1A). They also highlight the feasibility of this framework for establishing overarching principles in the neural coding of beauty, which transcend the feature preferences identified in previous work. Now, how can we further build on this framework? There are already other candidate principles on the horizon that could be benchmarked and validated using the proposed framework. Spatiotemporal redundancy seems promising in this regard. We know that, already at the photoreceptor level, neuronal signals are reformatted into a decorrelated representational format to decrease redundancy and increase efficiency (Dan et al., 1996). Similar processes operating across the whole visual hierarchy can make use of the predictability of information across space and time. This redundancy reduction could be related to perceived beauty, as decreasing redundancy will yield more fluent processing of the compressed inputs. While this constitutes just one promising example of a candidate principle, many others are readily testable using the framework we highlight here. These could include principles such as representational sharpening (Pouget et al., 2000), prediction-error minimization (Van de Cruys & Wagemans, 2011), and an optimal balance between feedforward and feedback communication (Herz et al., 2020), each constituting a distinct computational mechanism underlying aesthetic processing.

While many of these principles are directly derived from fluency-based theories, other theoretical views may posit different candidate principles. For instance, reward-based models of aesthetic value also emphasize that current and expected future learning predict aesthetic experiences (Brielmann & Dayan, 2022). Other candidates could be derived from pioneer theories of aesthetics, such as Berlyne's psychobiological theory (1971), in which complexity and novelty are related to beauty in a U-shaped curve, and Martindale's cognitive theory (1988), which suggests prototypicality and meaningfulness are determinants of beauty, yielding a fresh look at classically proposed mechanisms.

Moving beyond prediction

When using ANNs in service of distilling computational principles that are biologically derived and interpretable, we can find new explanations for why certain stimuli are preferred over others. This approach is complementary to using ANNs as prediction machines in the context of maximizing accuracy in predicting whether or not a stimulus is preferred. ANNs, like DNNs, naturally excel at this task, with their high-dimensional representations offering powerful predictions of human beauty judgments when directly regressed onto them. For instance, Conwell et al. (2025) showed that a set of 180 different ANN architectures trained on visual recognition could robustly predict human beauty ratings. By now, similar findings have been obtained across a range of studies and domains (Lu et al., 2014; Seresinhe et al., 2017; Jin et al., 2019; Nara & Kaiser, 2024). However, such studies largely rely on "brute-force" approaches, where many features are jointly exploited to enable high-accuracy predictions. Unlike the approach proposed here (Fig. 1A), this essentially leaves the underlying mechanisms hidden in a black box. This does not necessarily mean that such approaches cannot yield insights further down the line, where opening the black box and examining the high-dimensional representations that drive predictions can yield further insights. For instance, a recent study by Soydaner and Wagemans (2026) predicted aesthetic evaluations using human-rated image attributes by employing different machine learning models trained on those attributes. To clarify the importance of each feature, they used an explainable AI technique, Shapley Additive Explanations, by measuring how the prediction performance changes when excluding and including each feature across different combinations of other features. This technique allowed them to assess the contribution of specific features to predicting aesthetic evaluations while exploring whether there is a nonlinear relationship between aesthetic evaluations and network features. In the future, further research along these lines may unify approaches based on first principles and approaches based on maximizing predictive power.

Finally, using ANNs to benchmark candidate computational principles also highlights a critical feature of ANNs in cognitive neuroscience: The approach leverages ANNs as scientific tools for exploring hypothesis spaces (Cichy & Kaiser, 2019; Wichmann & Geirhos, 2023). Here, the power of ANNs as reasonably good models of human visual processing enables cheap and high-throughput testing of hypothesized associations, which in turn can be validated in bespoke human experiments that use targeted paradigms and stimuli. In the future, this computational exploration of putative effects can even be fully automatized into an iterative hypothesis generation and testing loop (Lu et al., 2024; Musslick et al., 2025; Kabra et al., 2026) that automatically produces a set of computational principles that can then be validated.

Closing the loop: relating computational principles and feature preferences

At this point, it is important to stress that we do not advocate for an approach that replaces studies on feature preferences. Such studies are critically important for characterizing the building blocks of visual preferences within and across domains. We rather advocate for a complementary approach that coexists with studies of feature preferences, providing a new starting point for adding explanations for feature preferences on a more abstract level of description.

But how do we go forward and bring these approaches together to distill such explanations? After identifying neuro-computational principles (e.g., metabolic cost or integrative processing), we can use these new insights and directly test whether established feature preferences can be derived from these first principles. Taking the example of metabolic cost, the common reason curved lines are preferred over angular ones, and symmetric faces are preferred over non-symmetric ones may be that they are both coded more fluently, requiring less energy expenditure in neural circuits. Following this example, one could utilize previous stimuli in experiments relating curvature and beauty (or symmetry and beauty), compute the desired measure in an ANN (here: metabolic cost), and test whether it is systematically related to curvature and whether the shared variance in turn explains human beauty ratings. Work along these lines thus has the potential to unite approaches based on computational principles and studies focusing on feature preferences. Some first steps in this direction have been made. For instance, Nara and Kaiser (2024), in their spatial integration study, tested which visual features contributed most to spatial integration in DNNs and found that the integration of high spatial frequency contours was most prominently linked to the prediction of perceived beauty. This was achieved by manipulating the visual feature content of the images while repeating the in silico benchmarking in their DNN models. Moving forward, however, it may turn out to be relatively tricky to relate individual visual features and a stimulus optimally "fulfilling" the computational principles in question: Most everyday stimuli are defined by a myriad of visual features and relevant dimensions, and a stimulus may reach an optimal point in this feature space through intricate interactions between its many features.

Identifying overarching computational principles also promises to reconcile the literature on feature preferences in cases where it does not fully converge. A good example is the relationship between complexity and perceived beauty. While some studies found that complexity relates to beauty following an inverted U-shape (Berlyne, 1971; Nadal et al., 2010), others reported negative or positive linear relationships (Tinio & Leder, 2009; Nadal et al., 2010; Delplangue et al., 2019). If we can identify computational principles that explain why complexity is related to beauty, we can then scrutinize under which conditions certain stimuli and domains produce different types of relationships between complexity and beauty: The different relationships observed in different studies may directly follow from a differential alignment with the more overarching coding motif that relates complexity and beauty in the first place.

Principles of sensory coding and what cognition does with them

Here, our focus has been strictly on perceptual processing and how it relates to beauty. However, it is possible that the framework proposed here offers a fruitful starting point for investigating the cognitive processing related to beauty, too. For one, computations in cognitive systems may also systematically link to perceived beauty. For instance, fluency is not constrained to sensory systems. The fluency of processing at different levels of information processing can also predict cognitive judgments (Oppenheimer, 2008), such as judging fluent statements to have higher truth (Reber & Schwarz, 1999) or believing fluent-named companies in the stock market to have better values (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2006). From an aesthetic perspective, Brielmann and Dayan (2022) suggested that decision-making plays a critical role in our aesthetic preferences, and applying fluency considerations to decision-making processes could be a promising avenue for characterizing computational principles on a cognitive level.

Additionally, cognitive systems may read out sensory beauty by evaluating perceptual representations in line with their overarching properties, such as how compressed and sparse they are. It has been shown that higher-level neural correlates of beauty operate on re-weighted sensory representations (Iigaya et al., 2023). It is conceivable that cognitive systems do not read out visual feature codes directly when assessing beauty in the world. They may rather read out the quality of a representation and whether coding is in line with principles that are shared across sensory inputs that have been associated with beauty in the past (Walther, 2026). Understanding what these principles are would consequently open new ways of thinking about the nature of such high-level representations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the framework proposed here offers a new methodology for understanding the nature of visual preferences. First, the framework focuses on establishing broader principles of aesthetic processing rather than limiting the scope of the explanations to single perceptual features using a single type of content. Second, through recent advancements in ANN modelling, it offers new opportunities to overcome critical challenges in empirical aesthetic research by (i) enabling a fast testbed for computational hypotheses and (ii) distilling computational principles that can be compared with those observed in the human brain. We believe that this approach has the potential to kickstart a fruitful new line of investigations into the neural correlates of beauty, which may ultimately yield explanations for why we assign beauty to the world around us.
