How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). Introduction

Eye movements during reading represent an integral part of psychological research since they allow to infer underlying psychological processes from observable behaviour. Eye movement behaviour alternates between fixations, i.e. phases when the eyes remain largely still to record visual information, and eye movements, also called saccades (Dodge, 1900; Kowler, 2011). To record visual information, the eyes move around four to five times per second during reading (Rayner, 1998). Since most saccades are voluntary, eye movement behaviour is considered a direct and observable indicator of underlying psychological and cognitive processing (Liversedge & Findlay, 2000; Rayner, 1998 Rayner, , 2009)). Analysing reading behaviour can even establish links between psychological processes and the human brain (Choi et al., 2014; Schuster et al., 2016; Turker et al., 2025; Weitin et al., 2024), to the extent that eye movements during reading can be predicted from the activity of neurological structures (Vitu et al., 2023).

In psychology, and especially psycholinguistics and reading research, universality has been a central issue for decades. Chomsky (Chomsky, 1965) demands a linguistic theory that applies to any language, given that similar cognitive processing of all humans must have led to some linguistic universality across languages. Frost (Frost, 2012) extends Chomsky's theory by arguing that not only linguistic theory should observe universalities but also cognitive processing during reading. Frost proposes that finding language-independent universalities in reading behaviour would be required for meaningful cross-linguistic modelling of language processing, thus human eye movement behaviour during reading.

Previously identified language-independent features mostly concern fixation durations and landing positions of saccades. Among others, cross-linguistic similarities have been shown for landing positions in equal-length texts (Parshina et al., 2024), viewing durations of words (Siegelman et al., 2022), effects of word length and frequency on fixation durations and word skipping rates (Kuperman et al., 2024; Sui et al., 2023; Zdorova et al., 2023), and effects of word count, frequency, and length on sentence reading times (Liversedge et al., 2016 (Liversedge et al., , 2024)).

Furthermore, a non-linguistic universality in human eye movement behaviour is that eye movements do not directly land on targets when multiple stimuli are presented in the peripheral field of view (Coren & Hoenig, 1972; Findlay, 1982). Since this effect can also be observed during reading (Vitu, 1991 (Vitu, , 2008;; Vitu et al., 2001 Vitu et al., , 2006)), it also represents a languageindependent universality for landing positions in reading behaviour.

Regarding saccade lengths, only few universalities have been suggested since saccades during reading depend on word length (Juhasz et al., 2008), word frequency (Inhoff & Rayner, 1986; Rayner & Duffy, 1986), and the word's predictability in context (Ehrlich & Rayner, 1981), as well as font size and spacing (Pelli et al., 2007). Universalities concerning saccade lengths have, to our knowledge, only been suggested for inter-word regressive saccades (Schwalm et al., 2025), return sweeps, i.e. movements from the end of a line to the start of the next (Kuperman & Bao, 2026), and relative saccade lengths (Fabian, 2025).

Relative saccade lengths have been suggested as a universality in human eye movement behaviour that applies to any type of eye movement (Fabian, 2025) and not only inter-word regressive saccades or return sweeps. A relative saccade length is defined as the quotient of one saccade's length and the previous saccade's length. Thus, relative saccade lengths express saccade lengths as factors: a relative saccade length of 0.5 indicates that a saccade is half as long as the previous one, while a relative saccade length of 3 indicates that it is three times as long. By their definition, relative saccade lengths do not depend on absolute saccade lengths, which are known to depend on stimulus configuration (Gameiro et al., 2017; Otero-Millan et al., 2013), task (Rayner, 1998; Tatler et al., 2011), and, in the case of reading, textual features (Clifton et al., 2016). Hence, it was suggested that relative saccade lengths might present a universality in eye movement behaviour as they overcome the established dependencies of absolute saccade lengths (Fabian, 2025).

To assess whether relative saccade lengths might present a universality in eye movement behaviour, the initial study on relative saccade lengths (Fabian, 2025) analyses publicly available eye-tracking data from 30 experiments, comprising research on reading, visual search, and scene perception. For reading, six experiments on six different languages are analysed: Danish (Hollenstein et al., 2022), Dutch (Eekhof et al., 2018), English (Black et al., 2019), Estonian (Ots, 2021), Hebrew (Kobo & Schonberg, 2019), and Russian (Ziubanova et al., 2023). By estimating the probability density function (PDF) of relative saccade lengths greater than 1, the study shows that relative saccade lengths observe a power-law-tailed PDF for each of the 30 experiments. Later studies show that the PDF of relative saccade lengths allows for better saccade length predictions than the PDF of absolute saccade lengths (Fabian, 2026a) and that relative saccade lengths enable cross-task comparisons of saccade length consistency (Fabian, 2026b).

Observing power-law behaviour has considerable theoretical implications. While power-law behaviour has been observed for various statistical relationships in nature (Carter & Tsenn, 1987; C. J. Rhodes & Anderson, 1996; Zaburdaev et al., 2015), human behaviour (Bachelier, 1900; Johnson, 2014), and human cognition (Ebbinghaus, 1885; Fitts, 1954; T. Rhodes & Turvey, 2007; Seibel, 1963; Snoddy, 1926), it became central to analysing animal foraging behaviour (Shlesinger, 2006). PDFs of various animals' movements during foraging and preying show power-law behaviour (Austin et al., 2004; Bartumeus et al., 2003; Bertrand et al., 2005; Edwards et al., 2007; Ramos-Fernández et al., 2004; Viswanathan et al., 1996), with the distributions' slope, as indicated by the power-law coefficient α, being highly similar.

Hence, research on animal foraging suggests that search behaviour in which step sizes follow a power-law distribution with an α of 2 is optimal (Viswanathan et al., 1999). Step sizes following this distribution optimise the trade-off between exploitation and exploration, i.e. carefully searching an area with small steps and moving to new areas with large steps.

The insights from animal foraging behaviour have theoretical implications for human eye movement behaviour since eye movement planning optimises a similar trade-off: gaining as much new information as possible while keeping the cost, i.e. saccade length, as low as possible (Ahmad et al., 2014; Najemnik & Geisler, 2005; Wagner et al., 2025). Thus, human eye movement behaviour has also been investigated for optimal step sizes, i.e. saccade lengths.

While absolute saccade lengths show power-law behaviour under some circumstances (Brockmann & Geisel, 2000; Stephen et al., 2009), they also observe different distributions in other experiments (Bogartz & Staub, 2012; Kelty-Stephen & Mirman, 2013; Stephen & Mirman, 2010). Thus, search behaviour during visual sampling does not seem to be consistently optimal since the step sizes are not always power-law distributed. However, the original study on relative saccade lengths reports power-law behaviour for all 30 experiments and an average power-law coefficient α of 1.98 (SD = 0.15) for the PDFs (Fabian, 2025). Since relative saccade lengths express differences between subsequent absolute saccade lengths, this finding suggests that adjustments of absolute saccade lengths during visual sampling may be optimal. Hence, when moving away from absolute step sizes and investigating the step sizes' relations, one can observe optimal search for saccade length adjustments. In other words, while differences between locations in the search space are not optimal in visual sampling, relative differences between step sizes appear optimal as indicated by power-law behaviour in relative saccade lengths.

In this study, we investigate whether the initial study's (Fabian, 2025) results regarding eye movements during reading can be replicated and generalised to other languages. Thus, the goals of this investigation are (1) replicating the results of power-law behaviour for eye movements for the six original languages with further empirical data and (2) investigating whether the finding of power-law behaviour in relative saccade lengths can be generalised to further languages. To this end, we analyse empirical eye-tracking data for 21 languages, which have not been analysed for power-law behaviour before. A successful extension of the results would provide further empirical evidence that the PDF of relative saccade lengths might constitute a universality in human eye movement behaviour. If relative saccade lengths also observe powerlaw behaviour, this would indicate that adjustments of saccade lengths during reading follow optimal search behaviour.

Methods

Data

As data, we use an eye-tracking corpus that provides data on the six languages analysed in the original study (Fabian, 2025) and 15 further languages for a broad extension of the findings.

The Multilingual Eye-Movement Corpus (MECO) was launched with the aim of collecting eye-tracking data during reading for underrepresented languages (Siegelman et al., 2022).

Since then, MECO has seen various additions (Bao et al., 2025; Kuperman et al., 2023 Kuperman et al., , 2025;; Siegelman et al., 2025; Siegelman & Kuperman, 2025). We analyse the data of the first wave of MECO (Siegelman et al., 2022) and the second wave of MECO (Siegelman et al., 2025), including the separately published data for traditional Mongolian (Bao et al., 2025). The data we analyse are publicly available from the respective MECO repositories (Bao et al., 2024; Siegelman & Kuperman, 2020). Each experiment received ethical approval of the responsible institution and was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki and respective local regulations. All participants provided written informed consent for their participation in the experiment and the anonymised publication of their data. A total of 1299 people participated in all experiments. For each language, the data comprise at least 30 participants (mean = 59.05; SD = 26.16), providing us with sufficient data for estimating the PDFs of relative saccade lengths.

The data cover the six languages analysed in the original study (Fabian, 2025): Danish, Dutch, English, Estonian, Hebrew, and Russian. This allows us to directly replicate the results for the six languages with data from new participants. Furthermore, MECO provides eye-tracking data for 15 further languages, enabling us to extend the analyses to various languages and examine whether the original study's (Fabian, 2025) results generalise beyond the six originally investigated languages. Note that the study describing the respective part of the MECO corpus (Siegelman et al., 2025) refers to data on Mandarin as Chinese. We also refer to the data on Mandarin as Chinese for consistency with the MECO study.

Analysis

For the analysis, we strictly replicate the preprocessing, distribution estimation, and evaluation procedures of the original study (Fabian, 2025) to ensure comparability of the results. All preprocessing steps and analyses are conducted in Python.

Preprocessing

The data for all 21 languages are preprocessed identically. We use the raw fixation reports since they represent the unprocessed chronological sequence of recorded fixations, which we need to identify absolute saccade lengths and subsequent relative saccade lengths. Given the chronological sequence of fixations, we determine the Euclidean distance between subsequent fixations to obtain the absolute saccade lengths. As in the original study (Fabian, 2025), saccades are not differentiated according to their direction. Then, each absolute saccade length is divided by the preceding saccade's absolute length to determine the relative saccade length.

Hence, a trial with n fixations yields n-1 absolute saccade lengths and n-2 relative saccade lengths.

For a more stable estimation process, thus more reliable results, we merge the MECO data that cover the same language. Thus, we combine the English data from Great Britain and the USA, German data from Switzerland and Germany, and Spanish data from Chile and Spain.

Similarly, we merge the data from two Indian experiment sites for Hindi and two experiments for Russian. We keep the MECO data for traditional and simplified Chinese separate since the differences between these scripts have been shown to affect eye movements during reading (Yan et al., 2025).

After preprocessing, the data for all 21 languages comprise 3,279,773 relative saccade lengths.

As in the original study (Fabian, 2025), we fit the distributions only to relative saccade lengths from 1 to 20, leaving 1,592,363 relative saccade lengths or 48.6% of the data. Given the varying numbers of participants and experiments per language, the number of available relative saccade lengths between 1 and 20 underlies considerable variance (mean = 72,380; SD = 47,355).

However, the variance does not pose a problem to the analyses since all distributions are compared per language, ensuring that compared distributions are estimated from the same amount of data.

Although MECO provides enough data for estimating and comparing the distributions within each language, the size of some language's data is rather small compared to the datasets used in the original study (Black et al., 2019; Eekhof et al., 2018; Fabian, 2025; Hollenstein et al., 2022; Kobo & Schonberg, 2019; Ots, 2021; Ziubanova et al., 2023). While the original study ensured at least 75,000 observations in each dataset after preprocessing, MECO contains as few as 21,163 observations after preprocessing in the case of Korean. Small datasets could lead to systematically steeper estimated slopes when some rarely occurring large relative saccade lengths are not present in a dataset. When a large relative saccade length is not observed in a dataset, the fitting algorithm considers its probability zero, leading it to estimate a steeper slope.

As the availability of eye-tracking data for many languages, especially underrepresented ones, is limited, we consider the trade-off between language variety and dataset size. Since our goal is to replicate and extend the finding of power-law behaviour for various languages, we opt for an eye-tracking corpus that covers many languages with sufficient data for distribution estimation. However, exact slope estimation might differ from the original study that relies exclusively on large datasets.

Distribution estimation

We estimate the PDF for relative saccade lengths greater than 1. Relative saccade lengths for each of the 21 languages are analysed to determine which PDF best describes them. To this end, we fit four different distributions to the relative saccade length PDF of each language. The four distributions are the ones used by previous studies that estimated distributions of saccade lengths (Bogartz & Staub, 2012; Brockmann & Geisel, 2000; Fabian, 2025; Kelty-Stephen & Mirman, 2013; Stephen et al., 2009; Stephen & Mirman, 2010): gamma, exponential, lognormal, and power-law. Figure 1 shows the four distributions. It is a common procedure to identify power-law distributions or power-law-like behaviour via graphical analyses (Brockmann & Geisel, 2000; Voitalov et al., 2019), where one regards a distribution power-law-shaped if the plotted data follow a straight line when both axes are on logarithmic scale. For a more quantitative assessment, we formulate each of the four investigated distributions mathematically, fit them to the empirical data, and quantitatively assess their fit to determine which distribution fits the data best. Following the original study on relative saccade lengths (Fabian, 2025), we define the gamma distribution as 𝑓(𝑥|𝑘, 𝜃) = 𝑥 𝑘-1 • 𝑒 -𝑥/𝜃 𝜃 𝑘 • 𝛤(𝑘) , exponential as 𝑓(𝑥|𝜆) = 𝜆 • 𝑒 -𝜆𝑥 , lognormal as 𝑓(𝑥|𝜇, 𝜎) = 1 𝑥𝜎√2𝜋 • exp(-ln(𝑥 -𝜇) 2 2𝜎 2 ), and power-law as 𝑓(𝑥|𝛼) = 𝑥 -𝛼.

As in the original study (Fabian, 2025), a shift parameter is added for each distribution which allows the applied fitting algorithm to shift each distribution along the x-axis to achieve the optimal estimate. The shift parameter does not influence the distributions' characteristics.

Given the equation for each distribution and the additional shift parameter, exponential and power-law distributions are fitted via two parameters, while gamma and lognormal distributions are estimated via three parameters. The PDF of relative saccade lengths is discretised for each language to intervals of 0.1. This yields 191 data points in the interval from 1 to 20 per language, which we use to fit the four candidate distributions.

The PDF of relative saccade lengths above 1 is estimated separately for each of the 21 languages, allowing us to determine the best-fitting distribution for each language individually.

The four distributions' parameters are estimated via the Least Squares method (Weisberg, 2005) and the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (Levenberg, 1944; Marquardt, 1963). Levenberg-Marquardt is applied since Least Squares does not converge for the power-law distribution. All distributions are estimated via both methods to avoid systematic deviations caused by applying different estimation algorithms. For each combination of distribution and language, both methods are applied and the fitted parameters achieving higher log-likelihood are used.

When fitting the distributions, all parameters are initialised with uninformed default values.

Using the parameters estimated in the original study (Fabian, 2025) as initial values would provide the fitting algorithms with additional information. However, these initial values were not available to the original study, meaning that their use would not constitute a direct replication of the original study. During fitting, each distribution is evaluated a maximum number of 5000 times for improved convergence of the estimation algorithms. Python's SciPy package is used to estimate the distributions' parameters.

For each language, we assess the estimated distributions' fit. Following previous studies (Bogartz & Staub, 2012; Fabian, 2025), we calculate the log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) (Akaike, 1998), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Schwarz, 1978) for each combination of language and distribution. While log-likelihood only measures the distributional fidelity of the fitted and empirical distributions, AIC and BIC also consider the complexity of the fitted distribution. More complex distributions, i.e. those requiring more parameters, are penalised. While higher log-likelihood values indicate a better fit, scores of AIC and BIC decrease with better fit. Since the results for log-likelihood and BIC do not qualitatively differ from those of AIC, we focus on AIC scores when reporting the results, similar to the original study (Fabian, 2025). The complete results are available in the study's repository.

We focus on AIC scores since they allow us to assess the amount of evidence supporting each fitted distribution. Our definitions and interpretations of AIC follow established procedures and guidelines (Burnham & Anderson, 2002 , 2004). For each language, the fits of the four different distributions can be compared via ΔAIC. By definition, the best-fitting distribution with the lowest AIC has a ΔAIC of zero. For any other distribution, ΔAIC is defined as the difference between the respective distribution's AIC and the AIC of the best-fitting distribution.

According to established interpretations, a ΔAIC less or equal to 2 suggests substantial evidence that a distribution fits the empirical data as well as the best-fitting distribution. If ΔAIC is greater than 10, the evidence for a comparably good fit is negligible. Note that the interpretation of ΔAIC does not depend on the absolute AIC scores since subtracting two AIC scores removes scaling constants from ΔAIC.

Discussion

This study was carried out as replication and generalisation of a previous study that showed PDFs of relative saccade lengths greater than 1 remain stable across tasks and populations (Fabian, 2025). As a part of the results, it was demonstrated that relative saccade lengths during reading follow a power-law distribution for six different languages. The two goals of this study were to ( 1) replicate the finding of power-law behaviour during reading with new empirical data for the same six languages and (2) investigate whether the original study's findings can be generalised to more languages. Using eye-tracking data from the MECO corpus (Bao et al., 2025; Siegelman et al., 2022 Siegelman et al., , 2025)), we pursued both goals with (1) new empirical data for the six previously analysed languages and (2) empirical data for 15 further languages.

First, we aimed to replicate the original study's results, which cover Danish, Dutch, English, Estonian, Hebrew, Russian (Fabian, 2025). As data, we use the eye-tracking data of MECO for the respective six languages. Our results show that the PDFs of relative saccade lengths above 1 follow a power-law distribution for each of the original study's six languages. There is virtually no empirical evidence supporting the three alternative distributions gamma, exponential, and lognormal. Thus, we can confirm the original study's results with our replication based on new empirical evidence. However, for the six languages, our results show an average power-law coefficient α of 2.04 (SD = 0.09), compared to 1.85 (SD = 0.08) reported in the original study. We suspect this discrepancy arises from the MECO corpus' smaller amount of data for each language. While the original study ensured each dataset contains at least 75,000 observations, only two of the six languages, English and Russian, meet this criterion in the MECO corpus. Since large relative saccade lengths only occur with a probability of less than 0.1%, small datasets might not contain any observations of some larger relative saccade lengths. Since the fitting algorithms consider all bins of relative saccade lengths, bins without any observation yield steeper estimated slopes, thus higher power-law coefficients α. Therefore, smaller datasets without any observations of some large relative saccade lengths yield systematically higher power-law coefficients.

Second, we investigated whether the results for the six languages from the original study can be generalised to further languages. MECO provides eye-tracking data for 15 languages beyond the originally investigated six. For each of these 15 languages, the results show the power-law distribution fits the empirical PDF of relative saccade lengths above 1 best. Empirical support for the three alternative distributions is indicated as negligible. Hence, our results show the findings of the previously investigated six languages can be generalised to 21 languages in total. This generalisation includes two non-alphabetic languages (Chinese, Hindi), two alphabetic languages with non-Latin scripts (Korean, Mongolian), and traditional Mongolian as a language that is read vertically. Thus, the data indicate that the PDF of relative saccade lengths above 1 follows a power-law distribution during reading, regardless of the text's language.

With these two findings, this study contributes new empirical evidence that the PDF of relative saccade lengths might represent a universality in human eye movement behaviour.

Furthermore, the results show an average power-law coefficient α of 2.06 (SD = 0.17) for all 21 languages analysed. Thus, the slope of the relative saccade length PDF is close to 2, which has been suggested optimal for search behaviour (Viswanathan et al., 1999). While previous research does not consistently observe power-law behaviour for absolute saccade lengths (Bogartz & Staub, 2012; Brockmann & Geisel, 2000; Kelty-Stephen & Mirman, 2013; Stephen et al., 2009; Stephen & Mirman, 2010), this finding suggests an optimal or near-optimal adjustment process for saccade lengths during reading. As relative saccade lengths capture relations between subsequent saccades, the results indicate that the search for the best-fitting relation between saccade lengths is near-optimal. This finding provides a novel insight into human saccade planning since cross-linguistic influences remained largely unknown. Relative saccade length PDFs appear as a stable statistical property in reading behaviour, describing eye movement behaviour independent of the language being read. Thus, our study provides further empirical evidence that relative saccade length PDFs might constitute a universality in reading behaviour, as was theoretically proposed and demanded by Frost (2012).

Despite the successful replication and generalisation, this study has a few limitations. First, although 21 languages represent a considerable extension from the previously analysed six languages, the vast majority of world languages remains unexplored. Future studies could collect data from further underrepresented languages and generalise the findings of our analyses further. Such extensions could show whether relative saccade length PDFs constitute a stable statistical property or even a universality in reading behaviour. Second, for the six originally investigated languages, the average power-law coefficient of 2.04 estimated in this study slightly deviates from the 1.85 estimated in the original study. We suspect this deviation stems from the fact that the, at least for our purposes, rather small datasets contained in MECO lead to systematically higher power-law coefficients than the large datasets analysed in the original study. Larger datasets would be needed to ensure no systematic differences arise from the process of distribution fitting. Since the aim of this study is to investigate power-law behaviour during reading for various languages, we leave analyses focusing on the exact replication of power-law coefficients with larger datasets to future research. Third, our statements regarding empirical support for the possible universality of relative saccade length PDFs only concern reading behaviour. To provide empirical support for visual search and scene perception, which were also analysed in the original study introducing relative saccade lengths (Fabian, 2025), further studies with more empirical data are needed.

In summary, this study confirms previous results regarding the power-law behaviour of eye movements during reading for six languages and shows that the findings can be extended to a total of 21 languages. Eye movements during reading seem to share a statistical regularity, regardless of the text's language. In contrast to the established differences between reading behaviour for different languages, the power-law-shaped tail of the relative saccade length PDF appears as a unifying statistical property. This statistical property indicates that the adjustments of human eye movement lengths could be optimal independent of viewing context. We hope this study contributes to establishing a common ground in human eye movement behaviour.

Distributions per language

The empirical PDF of relative saccade lengths above 1 is fitted best by the power-law distribution for each of the 21 languages analysed, as well as the traditional and simplified Chinese script. In each case, the gamma distribution fits the empirical data second best.

However, ΔAIC compared to the power-law is greater than 10,000 for the three other distributions for each language, indicating negligible evidence for a comparable fit. Thus, for all languages analysed, the power-law distribution is the best-fitting distribution while gamma, exponential, and lognormal distributions achieve considerably worse fits.

For the power-law distributions, we estimate two parameters: (1) the power-law coefficient α which describes the distribution's slope and (2) the shift parameter. Since the shift parameter is only introduced to ensure optimal fitting and does not represent distributional characteristics, we only report the results for the power-law coefficient α here. We find that the power-law coefficient α is 2.06 (SD = 0.17) across all 21 languages, and 2.04 (SD = 0.09) for the six languages analysed in the original study (Fabian, 2025).
