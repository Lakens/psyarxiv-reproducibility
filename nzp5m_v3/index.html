Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

The distinction between episodic and semantic memory has long been central to theories of autobiographical recall, yet accumulating evidence suggests that this boundary is empirically less clear-cut. Autobiographical remembering is increasingly understood as a constructive process in which episodic and semantic components are dynamically integrated rather than retrieved in isolation. In this review, we adopt the theoretical stance that the relative weighting of episodic and semantic information during recall is flexibly modulated by multiple factors, including age, task demands, temporal distance, emotional salience, and self-relevance. We synthesize findings demonstrating how these factors bias recall toward more episodic or more semantic representations and examine how they interact with one another and with situational context. By outlining these modulating influences, the review aims to better understand the mechanics of the episodic-semantic continuum during autobiographical recall and to clarify implications for how autobiographical memory is studied and interpreted across contexts.

1 Introduction

Autobiographical memory (our ability to recall personal life events) is crucial to the development and maintenance of identity. It draws on both episodic memory, which involves the retrieval of events tied to specific times and places, and semantic memory, which comprises general knowledge, facts, and concepts abstracted from experience (Tulving, 1972). For instance, recall your memories from your high school graduation. Your episodic memory enables you to recall the specific sensory and emotional details of the event: how you felt, who was there, what the weather was like. Simultaneously, your semantic memory contributes to knowledge such as the fact that high school graduations mean the end of secondary education. That example would be semantic memory in terms of general knowledge about the world. Another category is semantic knowledge about yourself, i.e., general facts or knowledge that people have about themselves that is not directly related to a specific event or particular point in time (Renoult et al., 2012). For example, knowledge about one's personal choices can constitute semantic self-knowledge, as it reflects general information about the self rather than a memory of a specific experience. Recollecting personal events is not simply the retrieval of static records. Rather, it is a constructive process in which episodic and semantic knowledge are flexibly integrated to form coherent, meaningful narratives.

Even though episodic and semantic memory are often seen as separate systems, in reality, when someone is recalling autobiographical events, not many memories fall entirely in one of these two categories (Conway, 1996). For that reason, it was suggested that episodic and semantic memory might instead be the two extremes from one and the same continuum (Addis & Szpunar, 2024; Bontkes et al., 2025; Renoult et al., 2012). This view of a continuum is in line with the various declarative memory types relying on different weights of the same behavioral and neural processes and could therefore effectively be viewed as a component process model (Tanguay et al., 2023). Building on this perspective, the central aim of the current review is to examine modulating factors that influence the relative contribution of episodic and semantic components during autobiographical recall, emphasizing that autobiographical memory should be understood as a flexible integration of both rather than a strict dichotomy. We synthesize evidence from empirical studies that include both episodic and semantic measurements collected during an autobiographical memory task to illustrate how episodic and semantic contributions are modulated by factors such as age, temporal distance, task demands, emotional salience and self-relevance. Beyond determining these modulating factors, this review aims to bring them together within a mutual framework for understanding variability in autobiographical recall. Specifically, we examine how the relative contributions of episodic and semantic memory alter across contexts, and whether this perspective helps explain differences in recall without assuming that reduced episodic detail necessarily reflects a loss of episodic information.

The review is structured as follows: first, we outline the conceptual foundations underlying the episodic-semantic distinction and its limitations in real-world autobiographical recall. Next, we discuss mechanisms of interaction between episodic and semantic representations. Then, we examine modulating factors that bias recall toward one or the other. Finally, we address future directions before concluding with a synthesis of current insights.

2 Conceptual foundation

Our current conceptual understanding of memory benefited in a major way from conceptual foundations about the episodic versus semantic distinction as laid out originally by Tulving, 1972, which then subsequently led to increased understanding of how memory relates to decision making (Lalla et al., 2022; Nicholas & Mattar, 2026), the social function of memory (Spreng, 2013), false memories (Gatti et al., 2024) and much more. Episodic memory is specific and focused on reliving a past experience, whereas semantic memory is much more general and focused on generalized knowledge of the world but also of oneself. Semantic memory, therefore, only contains the actual facts, not our sensory or personal subjective experience of these events. Our understanding of episodic and semantic memory has evolved beyond the initial strict systems-based dichotomy, and moved towards the view that episodic and semantic memory are two different states with shared but differentially engaged processes. Thus, the two extremes from the same continuum (Bontkes et al., 2025; Fawns, 2020; Irish & Vatansever, 2020). This continuum has been proposed to vary along dimensions, such as the degree of spatiotemporal specificity (Levine et al., 2002; Tulving, 1972), richness of perceptual and contextual detail (Piolino et al., 2002; St-Laurent & Buchsbaum, 2019), reliance on abstraction or schemas (Bontkes et al., 2025; Fawns, 2020) and/or phenomenological reliving (Rubin et al., 2003).

To outline these interactions in a systematic way, memory phenomena can be organized within a three-dimensional conceptual space defined by three axes: Explicit versus implicit, self-reference versus non-self-reference, and scene versus non-scene (Rubin, 2022). Within this matrix, unique personal events reside in explicit, selfreferential, scene-construction coordinates, whereas abstract facts occupy non-scene, non-self-referential coordinates. Autobiographical memory serves as an ideal test case for this space because it naturally blends event-specific scenes with semanticized personal knowledge. Intermediate phenomena such as "repisodic" or general event memories occupy continuous, mid-point coordinates across the scene and self-reference dimensions, bridging the gap between specific scene recollection and abstract personal understandings (Greenberg & Verfaellie, 2010; Rubin, 2022).

Despite this shift in understanding about episodic and semantic memory, several aspects of this continuum itself are still relatively under-theorized. Particularly, the specific factors that dynamically modulate the balance between episodic and semantic contributions during autobiographical recall and how these various modulating factors interact with one another and with the context is fragmented across domains and methods. What is missing currently is a unifying framework for how these various modulating factors that are shown to be important jointly and dynamically shift episodic-semantic weighting during autobiographical recall. It is important, however, to get a better fundamental understanding of these basic aspects of memory functioning since they shape the form and content of our remembered experiences.

3 Episodic-semantic interaction

Given that episodic and semantic memory are not independent but interact flexibly during autobiographical recall, there is a lot of work investigating how memory retrieval can be supported by this interaction between episodic and semantic memory. More specifically, a substantial body of work has examined how semantic memory can scaffold episodic retrieval and how episodic memory, in turn, can enrich semantic representations. In this context, there are various forms of external aids studied that provide structure during recall, for example, semantic cues (Mace et al., 2019), photographs (Xu et al., 2020), elaborate reminiscing practices (Harris et al., 2022), narrative scaffolding (Cohn-Sheehy et al., 2022) and others. These external aids are thought to guide memory retrieval, activate semantic information or facilitate retrieval of episodic information. Importantly, such external aids likely do not simply increase retrieval success, but might actually shape which memories are accessed and how they are reconstructed. Cues can be involved in multiple stages of memory: at the level of retrieval selection (i.e., biasing which memory is accessed to begin with), at the level of reconstruction (i.e., biasing how the memory is reconstructed) or at the level of consolidation (i.e., biasing long-term representations with for example repeated recall).

Simply by being cued with a semantic concept in the form of a single word might bring to mind specific episodes from your life (Mace et al., 2019). For example, the word "school" can bring to mind specific memories of particular days from your childhood. A cue, no matter whether it is a single word, a description, a photograph or something else, might often not only trigger but also facilitate and enrich memory for personal events. Cues that are more familiar, like a highly familiar contextual cue, might often lead to a higher amount of spontaneous autobiographical memories coming to mind (Robin et al., 2019). These types of semantic scaffolding may organize schemas or allow for easier retrieval when episodic traces are weak.

However, literature suggests that their influence on memory recall of personal events is more complex than a simple overall improvement. Using photos as cues can, especially when repeatedly looked at over time, lead to sharpening of the memory of that photographed event, by emphasizing some details through repetition and forgetting other details due to neglecting them, even if these details were similarly important at the time of the actual event (Fawns, 2020). Besides the fact that certain cues might lead to sharpening of an event and favoring some details over other ones in memory, some cues, especially highly familiar ones, may also lead to more generic memories that in general lack specific episodic detail (Robin et al., 2019). This suggests that cues do not always lead to improved episodic recall. In other words, the same mechanisms that support efficient recall might also increase susceptibility to distortion. Less familiar cues have much more tendency to bring to mind one specific episode in our life in high amount of detail, but highly familiar cues might bring to mind many similar experiences related to that familiar cue and therefore unconsciously trigger more generic thoughts that relate to that cue (Robin et al., 2019). Also, when having to recall various instances of similar personal events in succession, participants start to rely more on semantic memory which suggests that recalling a number of semantically similar events in succession leads to a focus on the generic aspects across these events (Bontkes et al., 2025). Altogether, this shows how episodic recall can be influenced by semantic information.

The other way around is also often described. In other words, besides facilitating episodic recall by semantic scaffolding, semantic memories can be triggered or strengthened by episodic experiences. For example, when associating a certain celebrity to a specific personal experience, one is more likely to remember this celebrity's name, which suggests that episodic (autobiographical) memory can aid the long-term presentation of semantic knowledge (Westmacott & Moscovitch, 2003). The more a certain episodic event is recalled, the more the associated semantic facts might be strengthened and remembered over time. Episodic experiences can also prime related semantic knowledge, activating associated concepts and facilitating later recall of these concepts (Fawns, 2020; Sakaki, 2007). This shows that episodic memory shapes and strengthens semantic networks over time. Children use the fact that episodic events can lead to semantic memory in their daily life a lot, since repeated episodic experiences can become "proto-typical semantic knowledge" over time (Occhionero et al., 2023). Early childhood memories are often fragmented and general which might be the basis of later semantic knowledge (Willoughby et al., 2012). Taken together, this suggests that the influence of episodic to semantic memory is cumulative and relatively slow. This is in contrast to the influence of semantic on episodic memory which is often immediate and strongly task-dependent.

Recently, more research goes beyond using distinct items (like word cues or photos) as external cue for memory retrieval. The practice of using narrative formation as external aid to remember events and experiences can also alter memories. Narratives differ from other external cues in that they impose temporal/causal structure rather than mostly trigger access. Narratives are known to help us remember experiences because our memory has an easier job remembering coherent information compared to fragmented information, and narratives make the memory of a certain experience more coherent (Cohn-Sheehy et al., 2022). Especially when related events are distant in time, narratives help us bridge this distance and create a coherent memory in our mind (Cohn-Sheehy et al., 2022). However, it also has the potential to loop in details that might not have happened but that were introduced by our memory system in order to make the memory of a certain experience more coherent. Specifically, this happens mostly when we introduce false details that are schema-consistent with that particular event (van Kesteren et al., 2012). This need for narrative coherence also becomes obvious in Tversky and Marsh, 2000 in which participants became more structured and coherent over the course of multiple retellings of the same events, however, later retellings in the task became more similar to a retold narrative rather than the original event. It has been suggested that this happens more when adjacently recalled events are semantically similar but dissimilar temporally or spatially, which might mean that we feel the urge to justify differences across adjacent events of our own lives to fulfill our need to bring a coherent life story (Hong et al., 2024).

4 Modulating factors

The relative contributions of episodic and semantic information during autobiographical recall are context-dependent. There is a lot of evidence suggesting that the relative contribution of episodic and semantic influence on recall of autobiographical memories is modulated by a complex interplay of various modulating factors. Here, we will outline how modulating factors like age, task and cuing effects, temporal distance of memories, emotion and self-relevance and individual differences influence the format in which autobiographical memories are being constructed in our mind. Importantly, these factors do not act independently, and their effects on the weighting between episodic versus semantic memory are not simply additive.

4.1 Age

In the empirical studies found, the distribution of episodic and semantic characteristics of autobiographical memories varied considerably across age groups (children, adolescents, young adults, middle-aged adults, and older adults). In (young) children, episodic memory seemed to be reconstructed primarily from semantic knowledge rather than from the retrieval of actual specific episodic details (de Paula et al., 2018; Occhionero et al., 2023). It is not until adolescence that the richness of episodic detail emerges (Gott & Lah, 2014; Willoughby et al., 2012). In older adults, evidence converged to the claim that there is a shift toward semantic reliance and episodic decline (Levine et al., 2002; Melega et al., 2024; Meléndez et al., 2018; Piolino et al., 2002). Indeed, older adults show reduced episodic specificity with less contextual and sensory details and more reliance on personal semantic facts. However, the literature suggests no decrease in accuracy necessarily, and no support for misremembering core events (Fenerci et al., 2024). This suggests that for older adults episodic information about life events is less accessible, but not necessarily remembered wrong. Narrative coherence tends to be preserved in older adults, and sometimes even improves given that older adults rely more on gist-level accuracy during recall (Melega et al., 2024; Setton, Sheldon, et al., 2022). In other words, older adults might not switch to semantic memory because their episodic memory fails, but they rely more on semantic memory because this helps to maintain coherence and accuracy of their memories. Some specific tasks may amplify this distinction between younger and older people, since some tasks are structured to focus on event-specific recall. It is interesting to note that young children as well as older adults seem to rely heavily on semantic scaffolding, but seem to do so for different reasons; in young children this is mostly due to immature binding of information and in older adults this is due to reduced accessibility to episodic detail (Shing et al., 2008).

4.2 Task demands

Studies investigating episodic and semantic contributions to autobiographical memory have used a range of tasks that differ in their retrieval instructions, cueing procedures, probing strategies, and response requirements. These differences are important because tasks do not simply measure the episodic-semantic balance in autobiographical memory, they can also shape or influence that balance in terms of what participants end up reporting (Levine et al., 2002). In particular, the instruction to recall a specific personal event, which is common in autobiographical memory research, encourages episodic retrieval and may constrain the extent to which more general semantic knowledge about oneself is expressed. Consequently, differences in the relative proportions of episodic and semantic details across studies may reflect both differences in actual memory functioning but also differences in task instructions and task demands (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000; Strikwerda-Brown et al., 2021).

The Autobiographical Interview (AI), developed by Levine et al., 2002, and adaptations of this procedure were among the most frequently used methods (Kian et al., 2021; King et al., 2022; Libersky et al., 2023; Mair et al., 2024; Melega et al., 2024; Occhionero et al., 2023; Setton, Mwilambwe-Tshilobo, et al., 2022; Setton, Sheldon, et al., 2022; Thoma and Wechsler, 2021 and others). The AI typically begins with an initial narrative recall period, followed by structured probing. Importantly, these stages place different demands on retrieval: the initial narrative allows participants to retrieve information relatively freely, whereas these subsequent (more specific) probes encourage participants to elaborate on specific aspects. The AI therefore illustrates how different retrieval procedures within the same task can influence which information is produced in the task by the participant. Its scoring system also explicitly distinguishes event-specific episodic details from more general semantic information, allowing researchers to examine how these contributions change across recall conditions. This distinction is particularly relevant when episodic retrieval is less detailed. For example, findings in older adults suggest that probing may elicit semantic information that supports the construction of a coherent account when episodic details are limited (Melega et al., 2024; Setton, Mwilambwe-Tshilobo, et al., 2022). However, an increase in semantic detail following probing should not necessarily be interpreted as a change in the underlying representation of a memory.

Free and cued recall are better understood as retrieval procedures than as mutually exclusive alternatives to structured interviews. Free recall allows participants to retrieve autobiographical information without a specific retrieval cue beyond the initial (usually rather generic) instruction. Studies using relatively unstructured recall procedures (Davis et al., 2021; Duff et al., 2024; Fuentes and Desrocher, 2013; Matijevic et al., 2024; Welch et al., 2025; Westmacott and Moscovitch, 2003; Zelig and Nachson, 2012 and others) can therefore reveal how participants spontaneously combine event-specific details with general knowledge in their narratives. However, free recall does not necessarily elicit a balanced representation of episodic and semantic information: when participants are explicitly instructed to describe a specific event, their responses are still directed towards episodic retrieval. The extent to which semantic information is expressed may additionally depend on the breadth of the instructions and the freedom participants have to elaborate.

Cued recall, in contrast, provides a retrieval prompt that can constrain or direct the search for autobiographical information. The effects of cueing depend on the nature of the cue and the retrieval instructions. Specific, contextually rich cues may cause participants to recall particular specific events, whereas broader or more general cues may lead to the retrieval of more general autobiographical knowledge. Indeed, semantic priming has been shown to influence the type of autobiographical information retrieved (Mace et al., 2019). The Autobiographical Memory Test (AMT), which typically uses word cues and asks participants to retrieve specific personal events, provides another example of how cueing and retrieval instructions work together: although the cue initiates retrieval, the instruction might lead to episodic retrieval. Thus, the episodicsemantic balance cannot be attributed to cue type alone. Other studies have used photographs (Finley & Brewer, 2024) or music (Bloom et al., 2023) as retrieval cues to investigate whether these stimuli facilitate autobiographical remembering. Music, in particular, has been shown to elicit autobiographical memories more effectively than some visual stimuli, such as photographs of faces (Belfi et al., 2016). Nevertheless, greater access to autobiographical memories does not necessarily imply a higher proportion of episodic relative to semantic details. This depends also on how the resulting memories are assessed.

Other paradigms provide complementary perspectives on episodic-semantic interplay. Autobiographical fluency tasks (Dritschel et al., 1992; Huffman & Weaver, 1996; Sheldon & Chu, 2017), for example, typically emphasize the generation of multiple memories within a limited period rather than the detailed reconstruction of a single event. Depending on their instructions and scoring, such tasks may therefore place greater emphasis on structured autobiographical knowledge than on the richness of individual episodic recollections. Recognition tasks (Damjanovic & Hanley, 2007; Westmacott & Moscovitch, 2003), think-aloud procedures (Hutmacher et al., 2025), remember-know paradigms (Pereverseff & Bodner, 2020), and other bespoke tasks or surveys (Bontkes et al., 2025; McDonough & Gallo, 2008; Sheldon et al., 2016) assess different aspects of autobiographical remembering and may impose different requirements on retrieval. Future-thinking paradigms provide a further illustration of the interplay between episodic and semantic memory: constructing plausible future events involves the recombination of episodic details and general knowledge, with semantic knowledge providing a potential scaffold for event construction (Compère et al., 2018; Gott & Lah, 2014). This interplay is therefore not restricted to remembering the past but also contributes to the construction of imagined events.

Taken together, these paradigms suggest that the observed balance between episodic and semantic details depends not only on the characteristics of autobiographical memory but also on what a task asks participants to retrieve. Specific-event instructions and cues that seem to facilitate access to particular episodes may favor the production of episodic details, whereas broader retrieval goals, fluency requirements, and opportunities to elaborate may allow more general autobiographical knowledge to be reported. Probing can further alter the distribution of reported details by encouraging participants to elaborate on aspects of a memory that were not spontaneously retrieved. These effects are especially important when interpreting age-related differences: tasks that provide opportunities for elaboration or allow participants to draw on semantic knowledge may reveal preserved aspects of autobiographical remembering even when event-specific detail is reduced. Accordingly, differences in episodic and semantic output across studies should be interpreted in light of task instructions, cue characteristics, probing procedures, and scoring criteria. More generally, because autobiographical memory experiments commonly instruct participants to retrieve specific events, the episodic emphasis of many experimental tasks should be considered when drawing conclusions about the relative contributions of episodic and semantic memory to autobiographical remembering.

4.3 Temporal distance

The temporal distance of recalled events is naturally of influence on the interplay between episodic and semantic content of autobiographical memories. The original view is that recent memories contain a higher amount of detail compared to remote memories (Levine et al., 2002). However, our review of the literature suggests a more complex pattern of results. Recent memories indeed contain more episodic specificity (Aizpurua & Koutstaal, 2015; St-Laurent & Buchsbaum, 2019), i.e., richer in sensory, perceptual and contextual details. However, remote memories do not only go down in episodic specificity, at the same time they go up in mentioning of personal semantic knowledge and in explaining meaning and relevance (Fenerci et al., 2024; Melega et al., 2024). This suggests that remote memories are not simply loss, but a change in representational format of the memories. Temporal distance modulates the amount of details recalled but also the format, relying more on semantic scaffolding (Finley & Brewer, 2024; St-Laurent & Buchsbaum, 2019). Indeed, remote memories are supported by schematic information (St-Laurent & Buchsbaum, 2019). This might partly be because recalling recent versus remote memories often map onto different goals for recall; recalling recent memories is often for verification of detail while recalling remote memories is more often for meaning or identity. Interestingly, Finley and Brewer, 2024 suggests that remote memories can become more episodic again in certain contexts (for example when cuing in particular ways) and with effort.

Modulation of task demands (see section 4.2) on the episodic-semantic interplay during recall seems to be different for remote memories compared to recent memories. Remote memories are more sensitive to differences in task demands. For example, external aids like photos or life-logging have been shown to shift remote memories somewhat on the suggested episodic-semantic continuum. More specifically, shifting it back towards the episodic end of this continuum (Xu et al., 2020). Importantly, changes in format of remote memories compared to recent memories is also functional. Remote memories are more often recalled in real life for storytelling and general meaning of certain life events. This could be seen as a goal that benefits from semantic abstraction (Grysman, 2017; Mair et al., 2024).

4.4 Self-relevance and emotional salience

Emotion has long been associated with enhanced memory retrieval, particularly through arousal rather than valence (Phelps, 2004). However, these effects appear to be more pronounced for episodic than for semantic details (Waring & Kensinger, 2011). Specifically, emotion may enhance sensory-perceptual information, subjective vividness, and the binding of items to their encoding context (El Haj et al., 2016). This distinction may reflect the fact that episodic memories retain information about the context in which an event occurred, whereas semantic representations are more abstracted from their original encoding context. Self-relevance may further contribute to this effect, as personally significant events tend to elicit stronger emotional engagement. This interaction aligns closely with the Self-Memory System (SMS), a conceptual framework introduced by Conway (2005) that emphasizes the fundamental interconnectedness of the self and memory. Consistent with this account, children retrieve more detailed autobiographical memories for highly self-relevant events, and self-relevance appears to reduce age-related differences in autobiographical memory richness (Kian et al., 2021). However, not all factors that facilitate memory retrieval necessarily enhance episodic specificity. For example, familiar spatial contexts reliably cue autobiographical memories, yet frequently visited locations tend to elicit more semantic and personal-semantic information, whereas less frequently visited locations are more often associated with episodic detail (Robin et al., 2019). This contrast suggests that the effects of retrieval cues on memory content depend on the type of information they make accessible: emotion and self-relevance may favor the retrieval of event-specific details, whereas familiarity and repeated experience may favor more abstract, semantic representations.

4.5 Individual differences

Individual differences may influence not only the accessibility of autobiographical memories but also the balance between episodic and semantic information produced during recall. However, relatively few studies directly examine this relationship. The following evidence suggests that both retrieval conditions and individual cognitive tendencies can shape the composition of autobiographical recollections.

One example concerns gender differences in autobiographical recall. Using the Autobiographical Interview, Fuentes and Desrocher found that women recalled more episodic information than men when provided with high retrieval support, whereas no gender difference was observed under low retrieval support (Fuentes & Desrocher, 2013). This finding suggests that differences in episodic detail production may depend on the conditions under which memories are retrieved, rather than reflecting a general difference in memory performance. It also highlights the importance of distinguishing between the amount of episodic information retrieved and the relative proportion of episodic to semantic details produced.

Individual cognitive tendencies may also influence the accessibility of episodic and semantic information differently. Neufeind and colleagues found that greater thought suppression was associated with faster retrieval of negative episodic memories and reduced retrieval of personal semantic information. Their experimental study further showed that suppressing thoughts about a negative event enhanced subsequent retrieval of negative episodic memories while reducing the retrieval of personal semantic memories (Neufeind et al., 2009). These findings provide more direct evidence that an individual cognitive strategy can differentially influence episodic and personal semantic retrieval, although they do not establish how such effects translate into the relative proportions of these details in extended autobiographical narratives.

Finally, the goals that guide autobiographical recall may influence the balance between episodic and non-episodic information. Mair and colleagues examined this possibility by asking younger and older adults to recall the same autobiographical event either as a good story or as a detailed police witness statement. Both groups produced more general details when instructed to tell a good story, while older adults also produced fewer specific details under this instruction (Mair et al., 2024). These findings suggest that age-related differences in autobiographical narratives may partly reflect communicative goals and preferences, rather than differences in memory functioning alone.

Taken together, these studies indicate that episodic and semantic autobiographical retrieval can be shaped by retrieval support, individual cognitive tendencies, and communicative goals. However, the evidence remains limited, and the effects observed across studies are not directly equivalent: some concern the retrieval of episodic and personal semantic information, whereas others measure the production of episodic and non-episodic narrative details. Further research is therefore needed to establish how individual differences influence the relative proportion of episodic and semantic details produced during autobiographical recall.

5 Future directions

Summarizing across all these modulating factors, the following pattern emerges. Older adults rely more on semantic scaffolding when recalling, especially when recalling remote events, partly due to reduced accessibility to episodic detail (Levine et al., 2002; St-Laurent & Buchsbaum, 2019). At the same time, some tasks (in particular the autobiographical interview task that is often used in this niche field) tends to emphasize event-specific probing during recall which might simply amplify the lack of episodic accessibility in older adults. When recall is based on different types of cues compared to the autobiographical interview, like photos (Finley & Brewer, 2024) or music (Bloom et al., 2023) or narrative framing, older adults show preserved accuracy and coherence during recall of events, despite the production of less episodic detail (Mair et al., 2017). These findings suggest that age-related changes in autobiographical memory reflect a context-dependent reweighting of episodic and semantic contributions during autobiographical memory recall, rather than a uniform decline in episodic memory or a simple shift toward semantic information.

Most empirical studies that we could find that explicitly investigated episodic and semantic components of autobiographical recall were in a single age group, with only the minority of studies directly comparing various age groups. As with many topics in psychology research, most research has been conducted in young adults, but also some studies included children and/or older adults. Longitudinal studies tracking episodicsemantic interplay across the lifespan were sparse and clearly a valuable avenue for future research. Developmental patterns demonstrate that the role of episodic and semantic memory alters as people age. Still, a majority of studies only consider specific age groups. To see how semantic frameworks, emotional salience, and time interact from childhood to old age, lifespan or long-term studies are essential. This is especially relevant during phases of identity creation and life reflection. With regards to the age of the memories recalled themselves, there was a nice spread of studies investigating primarily recent memories, primarily remote memories or both recent and remote memories. Also here, the need for longitudinal studies that investigate the same memories in the same participants in recent and subsequent remote sessions became obvious.

There were a few, relatively more recent, studies using naturalistic and technologymediated recall tasks (Fawns, 2020; Hutmacher et al., 2025). These studies have the potential to study memory in an ecologically valid way, and in particular how it is embedded in people's digital lives. The ability to easily integrate subjective measures like vividness and emotional intensity at the actual original moment of the event (instead of afterwards when mentally revisiting the event in the lab) allows for less biased results. It would be useful to then also combine this with investigating the interaction with emotional and social factors in real-world autobiographical recall. For example, longitudinal studies of positive, negative, and mixed emotional events may indicate if these differentially influence the balance between specific episodic details to general semantic understanding. Due to recent developments in portable neuroimaging methods like fNIRS, this naturalistic approach would even allow for multi-method approaches in which digital tools are combined with portable neuroimaging methods and additional behavioral measures. This might help to clarify if lab-based recall may misestimate the episodic-semantic balance during recall, given that it usually relies on inferred (post-hoc) recall and post-hoc reconstructed emotion.

Another useful avenue of future research would be to use computational modeling to investigate how semantic scaffolding supports episodic retrieval and vice versa. As becomes clear in section 3, there are a lot of insights about semantic-episodic mechanistic interactions, but computational modeling would allow for more detailed insights here. Computational models have the potential to enlarge our mechanistic understanding about the trade-off between specificity and generalization during repeated recall over and above what empirical studies based on human data can do. Additionally, most of the research has been correlational in nature. Still, there is very little experimental evidence of studies that deliberately changes semantic support to investigate its effects on episodic detail recall. Subsequent studies may use such controlled paradigms, which change the semantic context before the retrieval, in order to find out the time and the way in which semantic knowledge helps or hinders episodic memory. For instance, giving schema-consistent as opposed to schema-inconsistent situational cues multiple times across a longer period of time might help to clarify the causal role of semantic influence on episodic specificity and memory accuracy.

It is clear that some individual differences discussed, like communication goals, influence the episodic-semantic continuum during autobiographical recall. However, given the fact that empirical research in this direction is sparse, there is a lot still to investigate. Individual cognitive and emotional traits, including personality, emotion regulation strategies, personal stories, and culture, are often not considered. These individual differences might explain heterogeneity that is currently being attributed to age or task effects. Further studies should investigate how these factors influence the connection between episodic and semantic contributions, as well as the persistence of autobiographical memories.

Lastly, what is currently missing in the field are studies that explicitly manipulate multiple of these modulating factors at the same time, either with experimental designs or with computational models. This would explicitly test whether their effects are additive or somehow interactive.

6 Conclusion

In this review, we synthesized across empirical work using episodic and semantic memory measures during autobiographical recall to outline that episodic-semantic interplay is dynamic and context-dependent. Episodic and semantic memory are not competing systems, but rather two extremes on the same continuum. Age-related, task-related, emotional and contextual differences in autobiographical memory reflect context-dependent reweighting of episodic and semantic contributions, not a simple loss of memory or take-over by semantic memory. Future research that integrates lifespan perspectives, ecologically valid methods, and mechanistic modeling will be crucial for understanding how episodic and semantic contributions jointly shape remembered experiences.
